[P1.1] Post-authoritarian and post-conflict societies do not face a choice between stability and accountability: broad amnesties trade silence for fragile, short-term quiet, while truth commissions with public testimony build the durable, legitimate stability these societies actually need. First, public testimony validates victims, a non-negotiable foundation for a new social contract. Broad amnesties demand that those who suffered the worst atrocities swallow their pain to avoid unrest, delegitimizing the new state in the eyes of its most harmed citizens. South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission gave Black families whose loved ones were tortured and killed by apartheid security forces a public platform to share their stories, restoring dignity that a blanket amnesty would have denied entirely, and building public trust in the transition rather than eroding it.
[P1.2] Second, public testimony creates a shared, credible historical record to prevent future conflict. Amnesties enshrine historical erasure, letting each faction cling to its own narrative of grievance that can reignite violence years later. Argentina’s 1983 truth commission documented 9,000 forced disappearances, dismantling the military’s false narrative that it had acted to protect the country from subversion—a record that would have been impossible to build under a blanket amnesty, and that laid the groundwork for the country’s later efforts to hold senior junta leaders accountable.
[P1.3] Third, truth commissions offer a more pragmatic balance of justice and reintegration than blanket amnesties. Rather than granting unconditional immunity to all perpetrators, including those who committed the worst crimes, commissions can condition amnesty on full, truthful disclosure, allowing low-level participants to reintegrate into society while reserving prosecution for senior offenders. This avoids the destabilizing risk of mass trials, while rejecting the impunity that lets worst offenders retain power and plot a return to authoritarian rule.
