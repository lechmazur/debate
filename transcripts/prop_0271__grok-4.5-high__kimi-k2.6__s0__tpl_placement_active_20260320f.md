[P1.1] Parents bear primary responsibility for their children's development, yet app makers currently leave them with blunt or incomplete tools against the most powerful force on children's devices: algorithmic recommendation engines. These systems are engineered to maximize time on platform by continuously feeding content tailored for engagement, not age-appropriateness or well-being. Infinite scroll, autoplay, “for you” feeds, and related videos pull kids into longer sessions, denser stimuli, and content that escalates faster than any parent can monitor.
[P1.2] Today's parental controls rarely let caregivers simply block or delay those features at the source. Screen-time limits and app restrictions still leave the recommendation engine on by default once the app is open—suggesting next videos, friends, trends, and challenges optimized for retention. Filters lag. Kids invent workarounds and secondary accounts.
[P1.3] The result is parents fighting a rear-guard battle against opaque systems optimized for attention extraction, not parental intent.
[P1.4] More power means default-off recommendations for minors' accounts, clear toggles to disable algorithmic ranking in favor of chronological or parent-curated feeds, delayed rollout of high-engagement features until parental unlock, and device-level enforcement apps cannot easily evade. This restores balance so parents introduce algorithmic persuasion deliberately—the same way they control books, television schedules, or outdoor free play.
[P1.5] Without it, companies decide the intensity of influence by commercial default. Parents already decide curfews and diets. Algorithmic feeds shape attention, social comparison, and worldview far more continuously. Families should hold the stronger lever to pause that engine until they judge their child ready.
[P1.6] Authority follows responsibility; responsibility for children's minds remains with parents.

[C1.1] The proposition promises parents more control, but it is really a license for platforms to shift blame. App makers should be held accountable for safe defaults, not allowed to bury dangerous design choices behind an ever-expanding dashboard of parental toggles. Expecting mothers and fathers to become systems administrators for every app their child touches substitutes the illusion of control for structural protection.
[C1.2] Stripping away recommendation algorithms does not remove harmful content; it simply removes the filtering layer that suppresses it. A chronological or parent-curated feed has no automated way to deprioritize self-harm, extremism, or age-inappropriate material. The same systems that maximize engagement also enforce community guidelines, demote unsafe content, and adapt to emerging harms faster than any caregiver reviewing posts by hand. Without algorithmic ranking, the worst material becomes just as accessible as the benign—only now there is no automated mechanism pushing it down the queue.
[C1.3] The motion also rests on a category error. Algorithms are not a detachable "feature" like autoplay; they are the infrastructure of search, spam filtering, duplicate detection, and even chronological ordering itself. A truly algorithm-free feed would be unusable—filled with bots, scams, and unfiltered garbage. What the proposition actually targets is engagement optimization, yet it proposes fighting that by giving parents crude switches over complex systems their children already routinely circumvent.
[C1.4] When a twelve-year-old creates a secondary account or borrows a friend's device, parental toggles on the home tablet become irrelevant.
[C1.5] Genuine protection comes from mandatory platform design that puts child welfare ahead of engagement by default, not from converting parents into part-time IT security staff. Giving families more switches to flip lets companies off the hook while leaving children exposed.
