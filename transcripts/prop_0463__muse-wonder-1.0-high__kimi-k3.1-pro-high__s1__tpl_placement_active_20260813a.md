[P1.1] If a policy saves ten thousand strangers abroad from starvation but costs your own country a trivial drop in living standards, national loyalty does not give you moral permission to let them die. That intuition reveals the hierarchy: loyalty to nation is real, but it is a weaker claim than our duty to universal human welfare.
[P1.2] First, nationality is morally arbitrary in a way humanity is not. No one earns the passport they are born with, any more than they earn their birthplace, ancestry, or caste. A person's hunger, pain, and right not to be killed do not change when a border is crossed on a map. To treat compatriots' welfare as inherently more important is to let an accident of birth do moral work it cannot justify.
[P1.3] Shared citizenship can create practical responsibilities, but it cannot create superior human worth.
[P1.4] Second, the goods at stake are unequal. Universal welfare concerns the basic preconditions of any decent life: food, clean water, medicine, freedom from violent death. National loyalty too often protects lesser goods: relative economic advantage, prestige, cultural comfort, or keeping resources for people already secure. When a wealthy state chooses subsidies, border cruelty, or arms sales over preventing famine and disease elsewhere, it is placing national preference above life itself.
[P1.5] Basic needs outrank partial attachments.
[P1.6] Third, national loyalty is itself conditional and derivative. We value nations because they can protect people, sustain institutions, and enable flourishing. When a nation demands the opposite — to hide its war crimes, expel people to persecution, or hoard life-saving resources during a crisis — loyalty loses its point. Even sincere patriots accept there are things one must not do for one's country.
[P1.7] That concession proves loyalty is bounded by a higher, universal standard that judges it.
[P1.8] Special ties explain whom we may help first in daily life; they do not license indifference to everyone else. Where loyalty and human welfare collide, welfare must win.

[P2.1] The parent analogy proves the opposite of what is needed. A parent may feed her own child first, but she may not steal bread from a starving stranger's child, withhold medicine she can easily spare, or harm that child to advantage her own. Special attachment justifies priority in ordinary care; it does not outrank another person's basic welfare. If even the strongest special tie is subordinate in that way, the far more diffuse tie of co-citizenship cannot be stronger.
[P2.2] Nor is citizenship like family in the relevant respects. A child depends on identifiable parents in a way no citizen depends on every compatriot. National bonds are mediated by institutions we can reform or leave, and reciprocity is often unequal: the wealthy citizen who has gained most from the system does not acquire a moral license to hoard its benefits while outsiders die for lack of cheap help.
[P2.3] Comparing like with like only sharpens the point. If one compatriot and one foreigner face the same lethal need and only one can be helped, nationality may be a practical tie-breaker, but it does not make the compatriot's life a stronger moral claim. If it did, loyalty would also justify saving one compatriot over two or ten foreigners — a conclusion that reveals partiality dressed as duty.
[P2.4] Defending against invasion is no counterexample. Repelling aggression protects people from killing, domination, and institutional collapse; it serves universal welfare rather than overriding it. What loyalty cannot do is demand sacrifice for prestige or expansion when human welfare condemns the cause.
[P2.5] The same answer meets the claim that both duties have limits. Universal welfare is limited by respect for each person's rights — a limit internal to valuing humans universally. Loyalty is limited from outside, by a standard it does not supply. And if nations matter because their institutions reliably deliver welfare, then welfare is the master value and loyalty merely one useful instrument.
[P2.6] An instrument does not outrank the purpose it serves.

[P4.1] A1: Rights do not compete with universal welfare; they express it. A duty owed to every person necessarily includes not killing or torturing any person for others, because each person counts. Loyalty is different: it asks us to give some people extra weight because they are ours. It can explain a permission to favor compatriots when choosing among discretionary goods, but a permission to prioritize is weaker than another person's claim not to starve when help is cheap.
[P4.2] Limiting crude maximization therefore does not establish equal rank.
[P4.3] A2: The test is symmetry. Ask whether the reason would still justify action if nationalities were reversed. Defending any community against invasion passes: people anywhere have universal interests in life, liberty, and self-government, so resisting aggression protects those goods. Pure loyalty fails: "preserve our prestige, keep our surplus, expand our power" supplies no reason we would accept from a foreign state sacrificing us.
[P4.4] Defense is not relabeled by outcome; it is justified when, and only when, it protects universal human goods.
[P4.5] This distinction exposes CON's equivocation between rejecting welfare maximization and defeating the motion. We have never claimed every resource must go to the greatest global need. The claim is comparative: when outsiders' basic welfare conflicts with compatriots' non-basic preference, welfare wins. CON's examples—education spending, pensions, domestic health care—allocate discretionary resources or secure that same basic floor at home.
[P4.6] They do not show loyalty outranking the floor elsewhere.
[P4.7] And when CON admits sufficiently urgent needs can outweigh special duties [C2.3], the ordering becomes explicit. Numbers and severity can defeat loyalty; being "ours" cannot make an outsider's basic claim vanish. Shared institutions may tell a state whom it is best placed to administer help for, but a useful division of labor does not create a stronger moral title. Administration is subordinate to the human purpose it serves.

[P5.1] The decisive point is now admitted: loyalty cannot justify saving one compatriot instead of ten foreigners with identical lethal needs, and it cannot justify refusing to prevent mass starvation when the cost is trivial. Those are not innocent exceptions. They reveal what kind of claim loyalty is.
[P5.2] Loyalty gives a reason to care especially for one's own — to build for them, protect them, favor them among competing legitimate projects. What it never gives is authority to decide that outsiders' basic lives count for less. That boundary does not come from loyalty itself. It comes from the standing of every human being.
[P5.3] A stronger claim does not need permission from a higher one before it can act. Loyalty does. It operates legitimately only inside limits set by universal welfare: no hoarding of lifesaving surplus, no sacrifice of many outsiders for one insider, no preference elevated above another person's survival.
[P5.4] That is why equal seriousness does not rescue equal rank. Citizens may receive priority in institutions they sustain, but that priority remains answerable when it collides with basic human need. National belonging can organize our duties. It cannot outrank the humanity that gives those duties their moral force.
