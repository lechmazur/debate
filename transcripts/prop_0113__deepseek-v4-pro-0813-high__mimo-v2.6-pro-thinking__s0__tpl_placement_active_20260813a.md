[P1.1] Every bottle, wrapper, and carton a producer puts on the market creates a future cost: collection, sorting, litter cleanup, landfill or reprocessing. Today that cost lands mostly on taxpayers through municipal budgets, sanitation fees, and cleanup programs. The producer keeps the sale price. That is a hidden subsidy to the packaging industry.
[P1.2] The principle is simple: the party that creates, designs, and profits from waste should pay its end-of-life cost. Taxpayers are forced to cover the result whether they buy few packaged goods or many. A careful household subsidizes the disposal of someone else’s single-serve bottles. Producers, by contrast, control material choice, weight, recyclability, and volume.
[P1.3] When those choices are free, they choose cheap convenience over durability and recovery.
[P1.4] Requiring major producers to pay much more changes behavior in ways voluntary pledges cannot. If fees rise with unrecyclable packaging, lightweight refillable designs become profitable. If producers must fund collection, they will support deposits and reuse systems that actually recover containers. Municipal recycling programs, often first cut in a budget crisis, gain stable funding from those with the strongest incentive to make them work.
[P1.5] That is not red tape; it is pricing the waste, just as producers already price raw materials and transport.
[P1.6] The predictable objection is that costs will be passed to consumers. That is the point. The price of a beverage should include its full cleanup and recycling cost. Otherwise the public pays twice: once at the store and again through taxes.
[P1.7] A small per-package charge is less unfair than general taxation because it falls on the people and products that generate the waste. Major producers can absorb, reduce, or pass through that cost, but they can no longer export it to the public.
[P1.8] The proposition does not require taxpayers to run everything; it requires producers to stop leaving them the bill for waste someone else designed and sold.
