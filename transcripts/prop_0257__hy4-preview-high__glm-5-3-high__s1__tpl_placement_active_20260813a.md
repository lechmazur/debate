[P1.1] When a storefront says "Buy now" and charges a one-time price, the ordinary meaning is that the buyer gets the thing. A digital purchase is usually the opposite: consumers pay an ownership price and receive a revocable permission slip. That mismatch is the case. In a physical shop, twenty dollars buys a book you can keep, lend, resell, or bequeath.
[P1.2] Digitally, it often buys access a platform can suspend, delete, or degrade because a license expired, a server closed, or an account was flagged. The transaction says sale; the fine print says rental.
[P1.3] Meaningful ownership does not mean owning copyright. It means the basic incidents of owning a copy: durable access independent of one company’s cooperation, backups, use on devices you lawfully own, lending or resale, and inheritance. Platforms may need flexibility to run stores and honor upstream contracts. They should not need the power to confiscate what a customer already paid for.
[P1.4] Revocable licenses make paid-for culture fragile. Amazon once deleted Kindle copies of Orwell's 1984 after a rights dispute. Ebook stores have closed and left purchased ebooks stranded. Always-online games can become unplayable when servers shut, stranding buyers.
[P1.5] An account dispute can erase a decade of music or film purchases. These are private losses and public hazards: licensed-only works are hard to preserve, so revocable access can become cultural disappearance.
[P1.6] The real question is who bears the risk of a broken license. Today consumers do, after paying purchase prices, while enjoying fewer rights than a paperback. A sale should transfer a copy, not rent access. Require pass-through rights: perpetual use, personal backup, transfer, inheritance, and continuity if a store exits.
[P1.7] If the opposing side defends a system where every paid acquisition can be revoked at will, it must explain why "purchase" should mean so little. The ordinary deal should win: pay once, keep the copy.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like common sense until you notice what digital goods actually are: copies that never wear out, never degrade, and can be duplicated at zero cost. The first-sale doctrine our paperback intuition rests on was never a property right in the content—it was a concession to physics. A used book store works because a physical copy can only be in one place. A "used" digital file is an unlimited stream of perfect new copies.
[C1.2] Mandate digital resale and inheritance, and you haven't modernized ownership; you've legalized uncontrolled distribution with one buyer funding infinite downstream "owners." Every creator market that depends on selling copies would be repricing against a flood of permanent, pristine second-hand goods. That's not consumer protection; it's demolition.
[C1.3] Second, the license model is what pays for the goods consumers enjoy. Because platforms control distribution, they can offer things ownership never could: redownload your library on any new device, cloud saves, patches that fix what you bought, family sharing, instant refunds. When my console died, my purchased games reappeared on the next one because they were licensed to an account, not welded to a physical disc. Ownership of a scratched disc or a hard drive crash looks far more fragile than the deal the proposition mocks.
[C1.4] Third, the horror stories are real but rare, and they argue for better consumer-protection rules—refunds, notice before delisting, escrow on shutdown—not for importing property law wholesale. A regulation targeted at actual abuses beats a regime that would also break DRM, mandate file escrow, and hand every purchaser a distribution right.
[C1.5] The proposition must own its tradeoff: the "incidents of ownership" it lists—transfer, lending, resale—are, for bits, reproduction. Enforce them and you don't get a fairer marketplace; you get higher prices, weakened creators, and DRM wars fought even harder. Revocable licenses, with sensible guardrails, deliver durable access without dismantling the economics that made twenty-dollar books and games possible in the first place.
