[P1.1] Commodity windfalls have one defining feature: they are temporary, lumpy, and violently unpredictable. Any management tool has to be judged against that fact. A sovereign wealth fund is built for it. Direct cash distribution is built to ignore it.
[P1.2] Start with the price cycle. If a government mails every citizen a share of this year's oil or copper revenue, household incomes rise and fall with the commodity index. When prices double, the country floods with cash it cannot absorb: imports surge, rents and food prices climb, the currency appreciates, and farmers and manufacturers are priced out of their own markets. That is Dutch disease delivered directly to the doorstep.
[P1.3] Then prices crash, and the cheques shrink. Families who took on debt or school fees against last year's payment are stranded. Mongolia's copper-boom handouts around the start of the last decade followed roughly this arc: inflation on the way up, fiscal crisis on the way down. A fund breaks that chain.
[P1.4] Chile banks copper revenue above a long-run reference price and draws it down in slumps; Botswana did the same with diamonds for decades. Spending is smoothed, so the economy is smoothed.
[P1.5] Second, ownership across time. Minerals are a one-time inheritance. Distributing the proceeds to whoever holds citizenship during the boom liquidates the principal and leaves the next generation an empty hole. A fund converts a depleting asset into a permanent one.
[P1.6] Norway's fund now earns more annually than the oil that built it ever did in a typical year.
[P1.7] Third, politics. Cash transfers tied to raw revenue become entitlements the state cannot cut when prices fall without riots, so governments borrow to keep paying. A fund with a published withdrawal rule is one auditable institution that opposition parties, courts, and the press can watch.
[P1.8] And note what the fund does not exclude. Alaska pays its citizen dividend from fund earnings, not from raw production. Distributions can sit downstream of a fund. The reverse is impossible: once the money is handed out, there is nothing left to manage.
