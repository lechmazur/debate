[P1.1] Cultural integration is not asked as a favor; it is the price of a shared civic life. When newcomers settle, they enter institutions built on specific rules: courts that decide by evidence and legal argument, schools where parents and teachers deliberate, hospitals where instructions must be understood, elections whose losers accept the result. Those institutions run on a common public language and core civic norms. Treating them as optional is like joining a hospital and refusing the language of its charts: daily operations break down, errors multiply, and trust collapses.
[P1.2] At stake is reciprocity. Immigrants gain access to rights, markets, and public services that were created and paid for by a political community. The fair return is not abandoning one’s heritage; it is accepting the operating rules that let everyone use those goods equally. A person can keep their faith, food, family customs, and home language while still committing to freedom of speech, equality before the law, rejection of violence, and the equal-rights and due-process norms that make pluralism safe.
[P1.3] Diversity flourishes in private life; it cannot be a license to opt out of the public framework that protects it. A shared public language does not abolish mother tongues; it lets neighbors argue as fellow citizens instead of bargaining through permanent interpreters.
[P1.4] The practical cost of optional integration is fragmentation. Where language enclaves are permanent and civic norms are merely suggestive, parallel societies form: children fall behind because parents cannot navigate schools, workers are trapped in informal ethnic economies, and disputes retreat into unaccountable community arbitration. That weakens the social trust any large, diverse society depends on. Expecting newcomers to learn the common language and embrace core norms is therefore not assimilationist bullying.
[P1.5] It is the condition that keeps membership legitimate, secures equal opportunity, and protects newcomers themselves from isolation. Integration cannot be optional if membership is to mean anything.

No one disputes that immigrants must follow criminal law, pay taxes, respect court orders, and accept equal rights. Those duties are not optional for citizens either. But once “core civic norms” become something immigrants must “adopt” as a condition of belonging, the state is no longer enforcing conduct; it is policing identity.
[C1.2] That is where the real clash lies.
[C1.3] PRO’s reciprocity argument [P1.2] actually cuts against them. Immigrants contribute labor, taxes, care, entrepreneurship, and military service. A society that demands adoption owes something back: language classes, interpretation in hospitals and courts, anti-discrimination enforcement, and honest access to housing and work. A shared public language is valuable, but it is a public good to be built, not a loyalty exam to be passed.
[C1.4] When a patient cannot explain symptoms, the answer is not to lecture her about charts; it is to provide an interpreter and teach English. The first saves lives; the second may follow.
[C1.5] Nor does optional cultural integration mean opting out of civic life. People can keep their religion, dress, cuisine, and mother tongue while voting, serving on juries, sending children to school, and debating neighbors. In fact, that is how integration usually works: through contact, work, school, and shared institutions—not through coerced conformity. Demanding adoption often produces the opposite: enclaves hardened by suspicion, and immigrants treated as permanent probationers.
[C1.6] The motion’s real demand is asymmetric: newcomers must change; institutions need not.
[C1.7] The strongest societies do not require newcomers to erase themselves to belong. They require everyone—native and newcomer alike—to obey the same laws, and then they build common ground. Membership means rights and responsibilities, not sameness. Expectation without support is not integration; it is exclusion with a patriotic vocabulary.
[C1.8] Cultural integration should be invited, funded, and made easy—not commanded as the price of membership.
