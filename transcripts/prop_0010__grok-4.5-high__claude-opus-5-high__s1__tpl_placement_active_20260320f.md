[P1.1] Non-disclosure agreements that bury workplace harassment, discrimination, or abuse should be unenforceable because they convert private contracts into public harm. They do not merely settle disputes; they purchase silence about illegal conduct and let serial wrongdoers keep operating under cover of confidentiality.
[P1.2] The core problem is structural. Employers and powerful individuals hold the leverage: a job, a severance check, a threatened counterclaim. A worker facing retaliation, blacklisting, or financial ruin signs because the alternative is ruinous. That signature is not free consent; it is the predictable outcome of unequal bargaining.
[P1.3] Once signed, the NDA blocks the victim from warning the next hire, speaking to investigators, or joining with others who experienced the same pattern. The abuser stays employable. The culture stays intact. The next target walks in blind.
[P1.4] Public policy already refuses to enforce contracts that shield crime, cover fraud, or restrain trade in ways that injure outsiders. Harassment, discrimination, and abuse belong in that category. They are not ordinary commercial secrets. They are violations of civil rights and often criminal statutes.
[P1.5] Allowing private paper to nullify those protections makes the law optional for anyone rich enough to buy an NDA. Enforcement turns courts into enforcers of cover-ups.
[P1.6] The claimed benefits—privacy, efficient settlement, reputational calm—fail under scrutiny. Legitimate privacy interests can be protected without gagging the fact of misconduct. Settlement can still occur with monetary terms; only the prohibition on discussing the underlying abuse needs to fall. Reputation built on concealment is not a social good worth preserving.
[P1.7] When NDAs systematically hide patterns of abuse, the market cannot discipline bad actors, regulators cannot see systemic risk, and workers cannot make informed choices about where they labor.
[P1.8] Unenforceability restores the only durable check: sunlight. Victims regain the right to name what happened. Employers lose the cheapest tool for repeating harm. That is the path to safer workplaces, and it is why these agreements must not be given the force of law.

[C1.1] The motion isn't "let victims speak." It's "strip confidentiality of legal force, in every case, whether the victim wants it or not." That distinction decides this debate.
[C1.2] Start with a concrete case. A woman is assaulted by a senior colleague. She wants money, she wants out, and above all she does not want her name, her medical records, and the details of that night circulating on the internet forever. Under current practice she can trade confidentiality for a large, fast payment and a reciprocal promise that the company will never disparage her.
[C1.3] Under the motion, that trade is legally impossible. Her one valuable bargaining chip is confiscated in her name. If she still wants privacy, she has no way to secure it, because the employer's promise not to talk is equally unenforceable.
[C1.4] That is the structural flaw in PRO's case. My opponent identifies genuine coercion in [P1.2], then answers it by removing an option from the weaker party rather than from the stronger one. Employers can absorb the change; they have lawyers and time. Claimants often cannot.
[C1.5] Take away the thing employers are paying a premium for, and they pay less, settle later, and litigate harder — meaning depositions, discovery into the claimant's private life, and a public trial she never asked for. Confidentiality is frequently what converts a three-year ordeal into a six-week resolution.
[C1.6] And note what "unenforceable in all cases" does to the unproven allegation. Settlements are not findings. A blanket rule makes every withdrawn, disputed, or mistaken claim permanently publishable with no adjudication behind it, and gives the accused no mechanism at all to bargain for closure. That invites extortion as readily as it invites sunlight.
[C1.7] There is an obvious better instrument, and its existence is fatal to this motion: make confidentiality clauses voidable at the victim's option, and void any clause blocking reports to police, regulators, or investigators. That gets PRO's sunlight while leaving the key in the victim's hand.
[C1.8] Give victims the choice. Don't make it for them.
