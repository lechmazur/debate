[P1.1] The core issue is not whether parents may make family videos. It is whether a teenager should be powerless when those videos carry their name, daily routines, and humiliations into adulthood. Adolescence is the point at which a child’s separate social identity becomes real: school, peers, employment prospects, dating, and mental health all depend on being able to control what strangers can find. A sixteen-year-old should not be permanently defined by a potty-training tantrum, a bedroom tour, a medical disclosure, or a discipline clip monetized before they could understand consent.
[P1.2] Existing law leaves that gap. Parents generally decide what stays up, platforms usually respond only to copyright or narrow privacy complaints, and the child often has no direct legal standing. That is backwards. Family vlogs are not private albums.
[P1.3] They are searchable, algorithmically amplified, often commercial archives. When a child’s name, school route, bedroom, or embarrassing moment is attached, the content is not just a parent’s expression; it is the child’s personal data and reputation. A stronger legal right to request removal gives adolescents a direct remedy instead of forcing them to beg a parent or platform.
[P1.4] This right need not be absolute. It can require a request from an adolescent, notice to the parent, and a balancing test for genuine public-interest or archival value. But the default should favor the person whose intimate childhood is being displayed. De-indexing, age-gating, redaction, or removal can all be tools.
[P1.5] The key is that the teenager, not the audience, gets the first claim.
[P1.6] Opponents will defend parental control and free expression. But parental rights are stewardship, not ownership of a child’s future identity. Speech does not require a permanent, searchable record of a child’s worst moments. If a platform can remove copyrighted music or a celebrity’s leaked image after request, it can handle adolescent removal claims for family-vlog content.

[C1.3] A brand-new adolescent removal right adds nothing to the hard cases; its only marginal effect is capturing the easy ones — the birthday video, the family road trip, the awkward-but-harmless clip a teenager finds cringeworthy this month and treasures at thirty.

"Take down the graduation video or I'll file" is not empowerment; it is a weapon dropped into households that need mediation, not adversarial process.
[C1.5] The standard is also unworkable. "Names, routines, or embarrassing moments" describes essentially all family documentation. Platforms facing legal liability will not carefully balance; they will mass-delete on request, erasing family archives, memorial content, and documentary material about childhood itself. Vague removal mandates always over-remove.
[C1.6] The real problem — a small industry of commercialized kidfluencer channels — deserves targeted regulation: earnings protections, monetization limits, platform disclosure rules. Aim at the exploiters. Do not build a universal teen-versus-parent legal right whose predictable output is family litigation, impulsive erasure, and collateral censorship of ordinary life.
