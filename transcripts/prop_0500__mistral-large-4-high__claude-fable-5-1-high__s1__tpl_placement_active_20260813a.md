[P1.1] Cities are running out of surface space, and the true price of staying above ground is paid in gridlock, lost commerce, and stalled economies. When a new metro line tears up a boulevard for two years, the disruption is not a side effect—it is the dominant cost. Businesses lose foot traffic, emergency vehicles sit in traffic, and productivity evaporates. Underground tunneling converts that sprawling, recurring surface tax into a single, finite capital expense.
[P1.2] The boring premium is real, but it is dwarfed by the economic hemorrhaging of surface construction in dense urban corridors.
[P1.3] Land constraints have become absolute. In cities like Tokyo, London, or Singapore, there is no room left to widen roads or lay new rail at grade without demolishing neighborhoods. The only direction left is down. Surface transit competes with housing, commerce, and green space for precious real estate.
[P1.4] Every lane dedicated to cars at street level is a lane that cannot become a park, a café terrace, or affordable housing. Tunneling preserves the surface for human activity while moving the infrastructure that enables it beneath our feet. This is not merely an engineering preference; it is a spatial necessity in mature metropolises.
[P1.5] Critics fixate on tunnel boring machine price tags while ignoring lifecycle value and resilience. Underground utilities and transit are shielded from freeze-thaw cycles, storm flooding, and surface accidents. They last longer, require less disruptive maintenance, and avoid the catastrophic network failures that shut down surface systems during extreme weather. The investment is front-loaded, but the returns accrue for generations.
[P1.6] The choice is not between expensive tunnels and cheap surface projects. It is between paying now for durable infrastructure or paying forever in congestion, sprawl, and climate vulnerability. Countries that invest heavily underground are not indulging in engineering vanity; they are buying the only remaining space their cities have left.

[C1.1] The motion asks you to accept a bargain nobody should sign: spend heavily on the most expensive form of infrastructure humans build, precisely because it is expensive, and trust that the surface savings will arrive later. That is not a necessity argument. It is a blank cheque.
[C1.2] Start with the word "heavily." Every dollar spent on deep tunnelling is a dollar not spent elsewhere, and the exchange rate is brutal. A single kilometre of deep subway in a high-cost city can run into the billions; that same money buys dozens of kilometres of surface light rail, a citywide bus-priority network, or the burial of a whole district's power lines through cheap trenchless microtunnelling. If the problem is gridlock and lost commerce, the question is which tool relieves the most gridlock per dollar, and tunnelling almost never wins that contest.
[C1.3] Second, tunnelling does not abolish surface disruption; it relocates and prolongs it. Every bored line still needs station boxes, ventilation shafts, launch pits, and spoil removal. Residents around the Second Avenue Subway or Crossrail stations endured years of excavation, and both projects ran far over budget and schedule. The disruption PRO describes is often a governance failure, not a surface-versus-subsurface distinction, and burying it doesn't fix it.
[C1.4] Third, the resilience claim runs backwards. Hurricane Sandy filled New York's subway tunnels with seawater; the Zhengzhou metro flooding killed passengers underground. Tunnels are the single most flood-exposed, hardest-to-evacuate, hardest-to-repair assets a city owns. Shielding from freeze-thaw is a small benefit against catastrophic drainage risk.
[C1.5] Finally, "land constraints are absolute" is simply not true. Seoul tore out an elevated highway and got a river park. Paris reallocated car lanes to bikes and buses. Congestion pricing in London and Stockholm freed road space for nothing like tunnel money.
[C1.6] The surface is not full; it is badly allocated, and reallocation costs a fraction of boring.
[C1.7] Tunnel selectively where geometry truly demands it. "Invest heavily" is the opposite of selectivity.
