[P1.1] Young consumers today navigate a beauty landscape where a "calming" cream sits beside a "treating" serum, where "glow" promises and "healing" language blur into one feed, and where the person selling the product is also the person young users trust for lifestyle advice. That trust is precisely why the distinction between medical, cosmetic, and wellness claims matters—and why requiring clarity is not burdensome regulation but basic consumer protection.
[P1.2] The three categories carry materially different risks. A medical claim implies treatment of a condition—acne, eczema, rosacea—and triggers an obligation toward evidence and professional oversight. A cosmetic claim speaks to appearance—smoothing texture, evening tone. A wellness claim gestures vaguely toward wellbeing—stress relief, balance, self-care.
[P1.3] When influencer brands collapse these categories, young buyers cannot assess what a product can actually do. They may treat a cosmetic as medicine, delaying real care. They may read a wellness promise as clinical proof, trusting an aromatic serum to fix what a dermatologist should examine. They may overuse active ingredients marketed as "treatments" when they are really aesthetics products, and damage skin that was never diseased.
[P1.4] The harm is asymmetric because the audience is young. Teenagers and young adults are still developing the literacy to parse marketing language, and influencers blur the line by design—borrowing clinical vocabulary to make cosmetic products sound therapeutic while avoiding the evidentiary standards that therapeutic claims would require. That is not free speech; it is regulatory arbitrage that exploits a gap.
[P1.5] Requiring clear distinction does not silence anyone. Brands can still sell. Influencers can still endorse. The only thing that changes is that a young buyer knows whether a product is medicine, makeup, or mood—and that knowledge changes how they use it, how much they spend, and when they go to a doctor instead of a checkout page.
