[P1.1] Start with what card rates actually are. They are not actuarial prices. When a borrower's credit score improves, their APR usually doesn't fall; when the prime rate rises, it jumps within a billing cycle or two. Rates cluster in the twenties across wildly different risk profiles, because the industry's profit engine is the persistent revolver, not the accurately priced risk.
[P1.2] A cap therefore doesn't only bite the riskiest tail — it strips out margin extracted from people who were never as risky as their price implied.
[P1.3] Second, the harms are not symmetric. Being denied a card costs someone an option. Being carried at twenty-eight percent costs them years. A few thousand dollars, paid at the minimum, can outlast the car it bought, and the interest can exceed the original balance.
[P1.4] That is not credit access; it is a slow transfer of a household's future income to a lender, and it ends in charge-offs and bankruptcy anyway — the very risk the rate was supposedly compensating for.
[P1.5] Third, exclusion under a cap is partial, not exile. Lenders respond with smaller lines, secured cards, tighter underwriting, and credit unions — which have long operated under a federal rate ceiling in the high teens and still serve working-class members. The military lending cap shows a ceiling can coexist with a functioning market.
[P1.6] Finally, weigh the principle CON must defend: that any price is acceptable if it keeps a product available. We reject it for payday loans and for gouging after a hurricane. And notice who pays: households trapped in revolving debt fund the rewards of affluent customers who never pay a cent of interest.
[P1.7] The cost of this cap is a smaller credit line. The cost of refusing it is a generation financing groceries at thirty percent.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to accept a familiar bargain: make the price look fair by making the product disappear for the people who need it most. For a household facing a car repair, a medical bill, or a gap between paychecks, a card is a bridge.
[C1.2] If the lawful price is capped below what that risk costs to serve, the bridge is pulled up. The borrower does not stop needing $800; she substitutes. She pays an overdraft fee, takes a payday loan, pawns a tool, or misses a bill. Those alternatives often carry a higher effective cost and a faster path to default than the card she was denied.
[C1.3] PRO says exclusion is partial, not exile. But the motion's own "even if" concedes the harm and asks us to wave it through. Partial exclusion is still concentrated on the marginal borrower, the person with thin credit or a past delinquency. And a cap does not merely trim the riskiest tail.
[C1.4] It compresses risk pricing across the book, so lenders protect themselves with smaller lines, annual fees, harsher terms, and tighter underwriting. The safe-looking APR can conceal a worse product.
[C1.5] Prices are information. A ceiling that ignores risk does not abolish risk; it moves it. Lenders exit the segment, competition shrinks, and the remaining suppliers ration by credit score. The borrowers left out are exactly those the cap claims to help.
[C1.6] The right comparison is not 28% versus zero. It is a regulated card versus an unregulated payday rollover, a bounced rent check, or no repair at all. A cap can win the press release and lose the household. Reject it.
