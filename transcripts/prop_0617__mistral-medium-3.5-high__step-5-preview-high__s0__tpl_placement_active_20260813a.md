[P1.1] Dominant digital platforms like Google, Facebook, and Amazon already command market power that distorts competition. Their acquisitions frequently eliminate emerging threats—Facebook’s purchase of Instagram removed a direct competitor before it could mature, while Google’s DoubleClick acquisition entrenched its advertising dominance. These aren’t isolated cases but a pattern of absorbing potential challengers to maintain dominance.

Without a presumption of anticompetitiveness, enforcement remains slow and ineffective. [P1.3] Digital markets’ unique characteristics—network effects, data advantages, and near-zero marginal costs—make them uniquely prone to monopolization. A rebuttable presumption aligns with established merger control principles, adapting them to platform realities. The ‘substantial benefit’ threshold must be rigorous: only concrete, demonstrable gains that couldn’t be achieved through competition should overcome it.
[P1.4] The current system has failed. Dominant platforms continue acquiring rivals with impunity, harming consumers through reduced choice, higher prices, and stifled innovation. This presumption is necessary to restore competitive balance.

[C1.1] A presumption of anticompetitiveness is not a legal standard; it is a legal shortcut that abandons the requirement to prove harm. The proposition asks regulators to invert the fundamental principle of competition law: that a merger is lawful until shown to substantially lessen competition. Dominance is not an offense; it is a conclusion that requires evidence of exclusionary conduct. In digital markets, dominance is itself contested.
[C1.2] Amazon faces Walmart, Shopify, and direct-to-consumer entrants. Google competes with Amazon and Meta for advertising dollars. Labeling these firms dominant enough to trigger a presumption freezes competitive analysis at the threshold.
[C1.3] “Substantial consumer benefit” is a phantom threshold. It demands that firms prove a negative—that gains could not have arisen organically—while regulators retain no obligation to identify actual competitive injury. Consider Google’s acquisition of Android. A marginal mobile operating system was scaled into a global competitor to Apple’s iOS.
[C1.4] Consumers gained a free, open ecosystem. Under the proposed presumption, the burden would have fallen on Google to prove that Android could not have succeeded alone—a nearly impossible evidentiary task that ignores how startups actually scale. Similarly, Facebook’s acquisition of Instagram brought photo-filter technology and Stories to hundreds of millions of users; the counterfactual—an independent Instagram with Facebook’s infrastructure and capital—is speculation, not evidence.
[C1.5] Nor does information asymmetry justify the shift. If regulators genuinely cannot distinguish benign from harmful mergers after investigation, they certainly cannot calibrate a rebuttable presumption before one. Uncertainty will not deter only bad deals; it will chill the venture-capital ecosystem that funds platform challengers. Founders build companies to be acquired; remove that exit, and you reduce future entrants, not protect them.
[C1.6] Existing law already blocks anticompetitive mergers where harm is plausible. The motion’s real function is to make blocking the default, not because evidence supports it, but because dominance is treated as guilt. That is a policy preference, not antitrust analysis.

[C4.5] These answers expose the motion's core defect. PRO now concedes Android created value yet still classifies it as harm because it eliminated a "pathway" to a neutral platform. [C4.6] The motion does not merely shift a burden; it replaces evidence with narrative.
