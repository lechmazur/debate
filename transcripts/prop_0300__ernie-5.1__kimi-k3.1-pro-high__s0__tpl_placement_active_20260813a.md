Formal apologies are not legal instruments; they are acts of political and ethical recognition. When legal advisers flag compensation risks, they are measuring the wrong thing. The real risk is not the cost of reparations—it is the corrosive, generational damage of silence.
[P1.2] Consider Australia's 2008 apology to the Stolen Generations. Legal teams warned of liability. The apology was issued anyway. It did not trigger a floodgate of crippling lawsuits; instead it became a foundation for healing programs and trust-building with Indigenous communities.
[P1.3] The fear was inflated, the moral act was necessary, and the outcome vindicated the choice.
[P1.4] Contrast this with states that have refused to apologize—Japan's decades-long ambivalence toward Korean comfort women, Turkey's denial of the Armenian genocide. [P1.6] What cannot be managed is the loss of legitimacy that comes from a state too timid to say it was wrong.

[P2.2] The trustee argument collapses under its own weight. Yes, states serve taxpayers—but taxpayers are also the descendants of perpetrators and the inheritors of moral debt. A government that treats legal risk as trumping moral recognition is not protecting its citizens; it is privileging one generation's convenience over another's dignity. And the claim that "be more willing" lacks limiting principle is disingenuous.
[P2.3] The motion does not demand reckless apology—it demands that states not let legal caution become the default veto. Every apology still requires judgment; the proposition simply says that judgment should start from a presumption of accountability, not a presumption of silence.
[P2.4] CON's symbolic absolution point is their strongest, but it cuts both ways. If states routinely apologize and then litigate, the political backlash and reputational cost would be severe—far more constraining than any legal warning. No government survives long by saying sorry and then fighting in court; the apology creates its own accountability pressure.
[P2.5] And dismissing Australia as a one-off ignores what actually happened: the feared litigation flood never materialised, while healing programmes did. That is not luck—it is what tends to occur when states choose recognition over evasion.
[P2.6] The real question is not whether risks exist but whether a state should let those risks determine whether it acknowledges harm. CON says wait, assess, then maybe apologise. I say the waiting itself is the harm, and the assessment only becomes possible once acknowledgment opens the door.
