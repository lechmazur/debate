[P1.1] NDAs covering workplace harassment, discrimination, or abuse are not ordinary settlements. They are gag orders that let an employer buy silence about illegal conduct. The right question is not whether secrecy is convenient for the parties; it is whether the legal system should enforce a promise that hides abuse and protects repeat offenders. The answer is clear: enforceable NDAs trade the safety of future workers and the integrity of anti-discrimination law for one-time payment, while doing nothing to stop the harm.
[P1.2] Freedom of contract cannot carry the weight its defenders place on it. A harassed or abused employee does not face her employer as an equal bargainer. She may be afraid, dependent on the job or a recommendation, and unrepresented or outgunned. When she signs an NDA to get money she needs, she is not exercising autonomy; she is surrendering the right to speak so the company can avoid scrutiny.
[P1.3] The victim is muted, the manager is retained, and the next employee walks into the same risk unwarned.
[P1.4] Secrecy also disables deterrence. Harassment and discrimination law depend on patterns becoming visible so regulators, shareholders, coworkers, and future hires can respond. NDAs atomize each case into a private settlement. Picture one manager who gropes a subordinate and also blocks a Black employee’s promotion with slurs; both workers settle confidentially, he keeps the job, and two years later the company says it was shocked.
[P1.5] The surprise was manufactured by enforceable secrecy.
[P1.6] Defenders will argue that NDAs deliver privacy, quicker compensation, and closure. But unenforceability does not abolish settlement or damages. It removes only the gag. A victim who wants privacy can still stay silent; she should not be bound in advance, when desperation distorts choice, to protect the perpetrator’s reputation.
[P1.7] Efficiency bought by suppressing evidence of ongoing abuse is not worth defending. Where the facts are harassment, discrimination, or abuse, NDAs should be void.

[C1.1] Confidential settlements are not immunity. Only if every one were a purchased cover-up would a blanket ban make sense. Most are not. Harassment, discrimination, and abuse stay illegal whether the civil case is public.
[C1.2] What this motion voids is the complainant’s ability to trade a reliable promise of discretion for faster money, privacy, and an exit. Saying she may still choose to be quiet does not replace that bargain.
[C1.3] Employers do not pay a confidentiality premium for silence they cannot enforce. Strip the remedy for breach and the bargain becomes a hope. The person who most needs her name kept off the public record loses the term that made settlement rational. The claim that she can stay silent anyway ignores the price of that silence.
[C1.4] Voluntary quiet is free to the company. Contractual quiet is what funds the check. Unenforceability therefore does not “remove only the gag.” It removes the consideration behind early resolution and pushes more people into depositions and a docket they did not want.
[C1.5] Unequal bargaining justifies policing bad terms, not erasing every confidential release. Terms that bar reports to police, regulators, or civil-rights agencies, or that conceal an ongoing threat, should be void. Those limits warn the next worker without confiscating the complainant’s leverage. This motion goes further.
[C1.6] It voids the NDA even when she demanded it, even when reporting rights are preserved, and even in a one-off case. That is paternalism: her preferred remedy is worthless because strangers might have preferred a warning.
[C1.7] Deterrence does not require every settlement to be a press release. Agencies can still see patterns, shareholders can still sue, and criminal conduct remains reportable. What secrecy buys is an end to one nightmare without making her a public exhibit. A categorical ban does not stop abuse.
[C1.8] It makes the abused person a worse bargainer at the moment she most needs leverage.
