[P1.1] When an employer hires a worker, they purchase labor, time, and skills. They do not purchase the worker's private life, personal conscience, or fundamental autonomy. Yet without robust legal protections, employers hold the unilateral power to strip people of their livelihoods over lawful, off-the-clock choices that have zero bearing on their work.
[P1.2] This unchecked power creates profound societal harms. When an accountant, an engineer, or a retail clerk can be fired simply for attending a political demonstration, making lawful lifestyle choices, or expressing an opinion online that displeases management, basic civil liberties are compromised. If economic survival requires round-the-clock conformity to a boss's preferences or fear of public controversy, freedom of speech and civic engagement become luxuries reserved only for the wealthy.
[P1.3] In the digital age, this vulnerability has exploded. Ubiquitous surveillance, social media, and online visibility have erased the natural boundary between the office and the home. Individual workers possess virtually no bargaining power against corporate employers to negotiate protection for their private lives. Relying on employer goodwill is an illusion; without statutory guardrails, corporations will consistently sacrifice workers to appease vocal online mobs or enforce arbitrary corporate culture standards.
[P1.4] Crucially, the proposition contains a built-in standard of fairness: it protects conduct that *does not materially affect job performance*. This ensures employers retain full authority to address genuine business disruptions, workplace safety threats, or direct operational conflicts. What it eliminates is the abusive power to act as moral wardens over harmless private behavior.
[P1.5] A truly free society cannot permit corporate dominion over human life beyond the workplace door. Stronger legal protections establish a necessary and overdue boundary: your employer owns your labor during the shift, not your freedom when you clock out.

It includes whether clients still want to deal with the firm, whether coworkers can function together, and whether a role maintains the public trust it requires. A counselor can keep perfect case notes while a lawful off-duty blog destroys patient confidence. A sales rep can hit every number while lawful but inflammatory public conduct makes the firm radioactive to key accounts.
[C1.2] The harm is real even before it shows up in a productivity metric.
[C1.3] This turns every termination into a contestable lawsuit. A fired worker can always claim the real reason was their off-duty politics, side business, or lifestyle, while the employer says it was judgment, reputation, or cohesion. Because the statute’s key term is vague, small and mid-sized employers must choose between paying settlements or paying lawyers. The rational response is less freedom, not more: more monitoring, more documented pretext, fewer hires who have any visible public life, and a strong incentive to manage out anyone controversial before the protection attaches.
[C1.4] It also transfers employment decisions to institutions least able to make them. Judges and juries would decide after months or years whether a lawful off-duty act harmed a school, a clinic, or a construction crew. That standard will shift by region and sympathy. The result is not a clear boundary between work and private life; it is a slow, expensive, unpredictable litigation lottery imposed on ordinary employment.
[C1.5] The baseline is not lawless exploitation. Anti-discrimination laws, contracts, and reputational pressure already limit the most indefensible firings. What this proposal adds is a broad veto over the legitimate need to manage trust, risk, and workplace function. It promises autonomy but delivers rigidity, hidden screening, and more conflict, not more freedom.
