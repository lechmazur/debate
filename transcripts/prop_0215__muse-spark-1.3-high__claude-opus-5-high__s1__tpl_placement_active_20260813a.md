[P1.1] When an institution takes children from families, forbids their language, and disciplines them into a new faith and culture, apology alone does not settle the account. Payment is part of acknowledgment.
[P1.2] Residential schools and forced assimilation were not only state projects. In Canada, Australia, Ireland and elsewhere, churches and religious orders sought the contracts, ran the buildings, set the daily rules, hired the staff, kept the children, and shaped the ideology that justified taking them. They were central actors, not hired hands. Where the state delegated coercive care, the church accepted moral and practical authority over vulnerable lives.
[P1.3] Responsibility must follow that centrality.
[P1.4] Second, these are continuing bodies. A diocese or order is not a group of long-dead individuals; it is a legal person that owns land, schools, hospitals and investments, often accumulated across the very decades of abuse. Continuity of property means continuity of obligation. Asking the order to contribute is not punishing today's worshippers for ancestral sin.
[P1.5] It is asking the same institution that acted then to answer now from its institutional resources.
[P1.6] Third, leaving the bill to the state alone is unjust. That forces general taxpayers — including survivors and their descendants — to pay for harms from which churches gained members, funding, labor, land and status. Shared perpetration requires shared repair.
[P1.7] Contribution does not mean confiscation or ruin. The proposition is conditional and proportionate: when central, contribute to a reparative fund for survivors' health, trauma care, education, language revival and truth work. Courts and commissions can assess ability to pay and protect genuine charitable work.
[P1.8] Finally, money matters morally. A church that preaches repentance knows repentance without restitution is hollow. Financial contribution turns words into material repair and gives reconciliation credibility.
[P1.9] Power exercised, benefit received, harm done: that is why former state churches and orders should pay.

[C1.1] Nobody here will defend what the churches did. The question is not whether they were guilty. It is whether a standing obligation to write cheques actually repairs anything — and the historical record says it does the opposite.
[C1.2] Start with who pays. A diocese has no earnings. It has collection plates, and buildings that house food banks, hospices, addiction services and elderly nuns living on pensions. When you levy an order today, the money comes out of tomorrow's parish services and out of the pockets of current congregants — often poor, often immigrant, in Canada frequently Indigenous Catholics themselves.
[C1.3] That is not the perpetrator paying. That is a third group of the not-wealthy subsidising a moral accounting they had no part in. Compare that to the state, which has taxing power, borrowing power, and can pay survivors in full this fiscal year.
[C1.4] Then look at what financial liability actually did. In Canada, Catholic entities signed on to a "best efforts" fundraising commitment of roughly twenty-five million dollars and delivered a small fraction of it, after litigation and restructuring released them. In Ireland, religious congregations negotiated an indemnity that capped their exposure while the state's bill ballooned many times over — and much of what they handed over was property of contested value. Decades of survivors' lives were spent watching lawyers argue about corporate identity.
[C1.5] That is the deep flaw. The moment you make an institution financially liable, you give it a legal duty to minimise exposure. Its lawyers tell it to fight, to dissolve corporations, and above all not to open the archives. The single thing survivors and families have most needed — burial records, school registers, staff files, names — is exactly what a defendant is advised to withhold.
[C1.6] PRO's mechanism buys a modest, delayed, litigated cheque at the price of the truth.
[C1.7] Demand the records. Demand the land, the apology, the transfer of control. Let the state, which compelled attendance and wrote the law, pay the compensation promptly and in full.
