[P1.1] When a worker has spent five, seven, ten years picking crops, framing houses, staffing nursing homes, or writing code under a temporary status, the country has already made its choice. It has accepted their labor, their taxes, their rent payments, and their integration into communities. The only question left is whether to treat that person as a member or as a deportable input. The proposition picks membership, and it does so for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, economic realism. Integrated workers are not interchangeable with hypothetical replacements. They have firm-specific skills, language fluency, client relationships, and local know-how that employers cannot rebuild overnight. Stricter return enforcement against this group is essentially destroying productive capital the economy has already paid to build.
[P1.3] Aging societies in Europe, East Asia, and North America cannot afford that destruction, especially in care work, agriculture, and construction, where labor shortages are structural, not cyclical.
[P1.4] Second, rule-of-law done right. Critics frame enforcement as upholding law; long-term status upholds it better. Workers trapped in temporary categories are precisely the population most vulnerable to wage theft, unsafe conditions, and abusive employers, because complaint means deportation. Giving integrated workers durable status converts a shadow labor market into a regulated one.
[P1.5] Employers compete on productivity, not on who can most easily threaten a visa.
[P1.6] Third, basic proportionality. A person who has worked, paid taxes, and broken no serious laws for years has built a life the state tacitly authorized. Uprooting that life is a severe penalty. Enforcement resources are scarce; spending them on the already-integrated, rather than on trafficking networks or genuinely recent unauthorized entry, is bad triage.
[P1.7] The clash in this debate is simple. CON must argue that removing settled, productive, law-abiding workers produces enough benefit, in deterrence or sovereignty signaling, to outweigh the economic loss, the rights cost, and the misallocation of enforcement. That trade does not pencil out. Prioritize the people the economy has already absorbed.
