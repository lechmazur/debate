[P1.1] Generative AI training is already happening at massive scale, on millions of copyrighted works, whether or not the law blesses it. The real question before us is not "should copying ever occur without individual permission" — that ship has sailed technically and economically. The real question is: given that mass ingestion of copyrighted works is the industrial reality of this technology, what regime actually gets creators paid and gets the technology built in the open, rather than in the shadows?
[P1.2] A compulsory licensing scheme is the only structure that answers both halves of that question at once, and that is why the proposition should win.
[P1.3] Consider the alternative to compulsory licensing: a pure permission regime, where every publisher, label, and studio must individually negotiate with every rightsholder before training. In practice, this does not produce a flourishing market of bespoke deals. It produces exactly what we've seen since 2022: opaque scraping, litigation in a dozen jurisdictions with inconsistent outcomes, and enormous compliance costs that only the biggest players like Google, Meta, and OpenAI can absorb. Individual permission at internet scale is not a real mechanism — it's a fiction that entrenches the largest incumbents while leaving working musicians, midlist authors, and small labels with nothing but the right to sue and lose.
[P1.4] Compulsory licensing has already solved a structurally identical problem: radio play, mechanical reproduction of songs, cable retransmission. In each case, individual negotiation was impossible at scale, so the law created a statutory rate, paid into a collecting body, distributed to rightsholders. Songwriters get paid every time a song airs on radio without ever picking up the phone. That system isn't perfect, but it beats the counterfactual of unlicensed use with occasional lawsuits, or a market so gridlocked that broadcasting nearly ground to a halt in the early twentieth century.
[P1.5] Compulsory licensing here means every ingestion generates a payment, distributed transparently, to everyone whose work is used — not just those with the leverage to negotiate. That is the case I intend to hold CON to.

[C1.1] Compulsory licensing severs the core link between ownership and control. Once a publisher or studio may ingest any work merely by paying a statutory fee, rightsholders lose the ability to refuse uses they find objectionable—derivative styles that dilute their brand, political messaging they reject, or training sets that compete directly with their own future output. That loss of veto is not a minor administrative detail; it is the practical erasure of the exclusive right the copyright statute is meant to protect.
[C1.2] A statutory scheme also cannot price the input correctly. The value of any single work to a particular model is highly context-dependent and changes with every new release, every shift in training technique, and every market niche the model targets. A flat or even tiered rate decided by regulators or collecting societies will systematically overpay low-value material and underpay breakout hits or culturally foundational works. Creators who would have commanded premium terms in direct negotiation receive the same check as everyone else, flattening the incentive gradient that actually drives new creation.
[C1.3] Historical analogies to mechanical licenses or radio performances do not rescue the proposal. Those regimes addressed repeated public performances of already-fixed copies whose marginal harm was small and measurable. Training a generative model, by contrast, produces an entirely new competing product that can generate unlimited close substitutes. The scale of displacement is not comparable, and the risk of permanent market substitution is far higher.
[C1.4] Imposing a compulsory regime on that activity therefore risks locking in an industrial subsidy for one technology at the direct expense of the very authors whose labor it consumes.
[C1.5] The workable alternative is straightforward enforcement of existing rights plus voluntary licensing markets. Where transaction costs are high, creators can still pool rights through existing collecting societies without stripping them of the initial decision whether to participate at all. Forcing participation by statute is not a solution to friction; it is a reallocation of bargaining power away from owners and toward the largest technology firms.
