[P1.1] The transition from physical media to streaming was sold as an era of unprecedented access. Instead, it has enabled an unprecedented form of cultural erasure. In the era of home video, when a studio cancelled a project, physical copies remained in circulation, in libraries, and on living-room shelves. Today, streaming platforms exercise total vertical control: they produce the work, control the sole distribution channel, and lock away the master files.
[P1.2] When a platform purges an original series to claim a tax write-down or dodge residual payments, that work does not simply switch storefronts—it ceases to exist for the public.
[P1.3] This practice inflicts two severe harms that market mechanisms fail to correct. First, it subordinates cultural preservation to balance-sheet engineering. Consumers pay recurring subscription fees under the expectation of catalog access, yet platforms routinely erase completed works like *Willow* or *Westworld* purely to claim immediate tax amortization benefits. Public access and artistic history become collateral damage to corporate tax avoidance.
[P1.4] Second, it exploits creative labor. Writers, actors, and technical crew members accept compensation structures tied to residuals, public visibility, and portfolio proof. Disappearing a project eliminates their future earnings and erases their professional record, all so a conglomerate can offset unrelated corporate debt.
[P1.5] The proposition does not demand that platforms preserve every title in perpetuity at massive expense. It merely establishes a modest, mandatory availability window. If a company commissions, produces, and profits from an original work, it must not be permitted to instantly vault that work into oblivion as a tax-avoidance vehicle. A minimum availability requirement restores the fundamental social bargain between audiences, creators, and the digital distributors that profit from their culture.

[C1.1] A mandate to keep every original on its home platform would not preserve culture. It would make culture riskier to create, pricier to distribute, and harder to move where audiences actually want it.
[C1.2] Streaming originals exist because a company fronts production and marketing costs before knowing if anyone will watch. That gamble depends on flexibility afterward: to rotate a catalog, renegotiate music and guild obligations, retire a flop, license a title to another service, or recognize a loss when a project has failed. Force the platform to keep hosting it for a fixed period and you convert a creative risk into an open-ended liability. The rational response is not heroic preservation.
[C1.3] It is fewer pilots, safer franchises, lower budgets, and quicker cancellation before a work ever qualifies as an original.
[C1.4] Keeping a title “available” is also not free or simple. It means servers and bandwidth, accessibility features, regional compliance, residuals, and underlying rights that may expire or become disputed. For a heavily watched series those costs make sense. For a series almost no one finishes, they are a tax on every subscriber to maintain a digital shelf almost no one visits.
[C1.5] Platforms will pass that cost through higher prices, more ads, or cuts to new production — precisely the access and labor harms the mandate claims to prevent.
[C1.6] Nor is removal from one service the same as erasure. A title pulled from its original platform can be licensed elsewhere, sold on disc or digitally, placed with an ad-supported streamer, or archived by its rights holder. A rigid host-it-here rule blocks those second lives by tying the work to one distributor instead of letting it find an audience elsewhere.
[C1.7] Disappointing rotations are a reason to criticize a service, demand clearer catalog notices, or cancel a subscription. They are not a reason for the state to conscript private catalogs into museums and punish the very risk-taking that created the work.
