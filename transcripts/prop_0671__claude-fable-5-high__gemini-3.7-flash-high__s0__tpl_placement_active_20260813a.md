[P1.1] Picture a first grader learning the letter "b." On paper, her hand traces its curve, her fingers feel the pencil's resistance, her brain binds shape to motion to sound. On a tablet, she taps a picture and an animation rewards her. Only one of these builds the neural architecture of literacy — and that difference is the heart of this debate.
[P1.2] The motion is deliberately modest. It does not ban technology; it says early grades — roughly ages five to nine — should lean paper-first, with one-to-one devices available later. So the question is narrow: when children are building foundational reading, writing, and attention skills, which medium serves them best?
[P1.3] Three reasons paper wins that window. First, mechanism: handwriting engages motor memory in ways tapping cannot, and research on reading has repeatedly found comprehension of substantive text tends to be shallower on screens — the "skimming" mode screens invite. Young children, still forming reading habits, are most vulnerable to learning that shallow mode as their default.
[P1.4] Second, attention. A tablet is a slot machine sitting on a desk. Adults with fully developed prefrontal cortexes struggle to resist notifications and games; asking a six-year-old to self-regulate against engineered distraction is not education, it's an ambush. Paper has no pop-ups.
[P1.5] Third, opportunity cost and equity. American children already average hours of daily recreational screen time at home. School is the one institution that can guarantee every child — rich or poor — sustained practice with deep reading, handwriting, and face-to-face conversation. Screens are abundant; those experiences are now scarce.
[P1.6] Schools should supply what homes increasingly cannot.
[P1.7] Finally, the asymmetry of error. If we go device-light and are wrong, children learn digital skills at ten instead of six — a trivial delay, since interfaces are designed to be learnable in minutes. If we saturate early classrooms with screens and are wrong, we've weakened literacy foundations during an unrepeatable developmental window. Prudence points one way.

[C1.4] PRO worries about digital distraction, but the true asymmetry of error runs in reverse.
