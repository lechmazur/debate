[C1.1] PRO’s core error is framing this as a choice between dynamic pricing and the mass cinema habit, when rigid flat pricing is the far greater threat to that habit. Theaters have faced a steep attendance drop since 2019, with dozens of small chains closing annually because licensing fees, labor costs, and maintenance outpace flat-rate ticket revenue. If dynamic pricing lets even a small share of these theaters stay open, it preserves far more access to the shared cultural experience PRO claims to value than a model that drives operators out of business entirely.
[C1.2] Second, PRO’s comparison to concert surge pricing is apples to oranges: concert venues have fixed, limited total capacity, while multiplexes have thousands of flexible seats across showtimes. Dynamic pricing for standard seats doesn’t just raise peak prices—it drops prices for underbooked off-peak showings, pulling in price-sensitive moviegoers who would have stayed home otherwise. A family that can only afford a discounted 2pm weekday showing is just as much part of the mass habit as someone paying full price for a Saturday 7pm slot; dynamic pricing expands the pool of both, rather than rations access.
[C1.3] Third, PRO cites predictable off-peak discounts and loyalty deals as a viable alternative, but those tools only work if theaters can cover their fixed costs, which dynamic pricing revenue makes possible. PRO also claims dynamic pricing punishes loyalty, but nearly all major chains pair it with loyalty programs that give members early access to tickets at standard, fixed prices before any demand-based adjustment applies. The only people who face peak premiums are last-minute casual viewers who are far more likely to abandon a trip entirely if their preferred showing is sold out than to adjust their plans.
[C1.4] Dynamic pricing doesn’t penalize planned, loyal attendance—it monetizes spare, last-minute seats that would otherwise go empty, turning wasted capacity into revenue that keeps theaters running for everyone.
