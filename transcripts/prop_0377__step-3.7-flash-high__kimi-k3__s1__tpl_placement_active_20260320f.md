[P1.1] Equality of opportunity fails not when people earn high incomes through current work, but when unearned, intergenerational wealth lets a small class of families buy life outcomes regardless of merit. Dynastic wealth is the quiet killer of equal opportunity, because it compounds across generations without any requirement of effort or talent from the heirs who receive it. Consider the gap between a top 1% annual earner and a trust fund heir. A software executive making $2 million a year still earns that income through active labor, and pays income and payroll taxes on it every year.
[P1.2] A 21-year-old who inherits a $10 million portfolio, by contrast, can generate $400,000 a year in passive income without working a single hour, while their low-income peer takes on student debt and works part-time to cover textbooks. The inheritance heir never has to compete on merit for internships, housing, or business loans: their wealth eliminates risk entirely. This advantage compounds over time. The Walton family, heirs to the Walmart fortune, now hold a combined net worth larger than the bottom 40% of the U.S. population, none of it earned through their own work.
[P1.3] Current tax law makes this worse: U.S. federal estate tax exemptions top $13 million per parent, letting couples pass $26 million tax-free to heirs, and unrealized capital gains in estates receive a full step-up in basis, meaning trillions in wealth are never taxed at all as they pass between generations. Aggressive inheritance taxation doesn’t punish success — it punishes the luck of birth that has nothing to do with merit. It levels the playing field far more effectively than raising taxes on high annual incomes, because it targets the root of dynastic advantage: the ability to pass unearned wealth down to lock in privilege for generations.
[P1.4] Equality of opportunity demands that your life chances depend on your effort, not your parents’ bank accounts.
