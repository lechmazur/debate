[P1.1] Public institutions tasked with fostering place identity too often default to one-off prestige mega-events, but this is a categorical error. Place identity is not a static brand to be imposed from above, but a living, repeated practice forged through consistent, meaningful participation by the people who call a place home. One-off prestige mega-events—whether temporary world expos, one-time international sports spectacles, or flashy corporate-sponsored cultural showcases—prioritize external validation and short-term tourist revenue over the slow, steady work of building community belonging.
[P1.2] They are inherently extractive: they divert public funds to temporary infrastructure, disrupt local routines, and center outsider audiences over residents.
[P1.3] By contrast, creator-led local recurring events align exactly with the core goal of place identity. When local artists, small business owners, and cultural organizers design annual or seasonal events, they embed local history, values, and shared references into the programming. These events are not one-time spectacles, but regular touchpoints that strengthen social ties across neighborhoods. They let residents see their own stories reflected in public space, rather than being sidelined for a generic, prestige-friendly narrative designed to impress outsiders.
[P1.4] Public institutions that fund these events directly support the community members doing the work of identity-building, rather than outsourcing it to external event firms.
[P1.5] Mega-events boost global visibility, but visibility is not identity. A place known worldwide for a one-off event that displaced local residents and left vacant stadiums has no stronger place identity than it did before—only a hollow marketing tagline. Recurring local events compound their impact over time: each iteration deepens resident attachment, attracts like-minded newcomers, and creates a self-reinforcing cycle of community investment that no one-off spectacle can match. For public institutions, the choice is clear: fund the events that build belonging, not the spectacles that sell a hollow version of it.
