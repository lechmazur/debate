[P1.1] Direct revenue sharing ended amateurism. When a university cuts a check from ticket sales and television money for playing football or basketball, that athlete is not just a student promoting a local car dealership. That is labor paid for value created. Governing that pay with handshake NIL deals and ever-shifting compliance memos is a fiction that serves everyone except the athletes.
[P1.2] Only formal collective bargaining fits the new reality because only it gives both sides a binding, enforceable deal. Bargaining can set minimum shares, standard contract protections, guaranteed medical coverage for injuries, limits on practice time and offseason demands, clear transfer and eligibility rules, and a grievance process when a coach cuts a player or withholds pay. The current system offers none of that: star quarterbacks get secret collective promises that vanish, linemen get nothing, women athletes get a fraction, and anyone injured or benched has no recourse.
[P1.3] A freshman cannot negotiate practice limits or concussion care alone against a program worth a hundred million.
[P1.4] Schools need this too. Without a union contract, every salary cap, transfer restriction, or eligibility limit is an antitrust lawsuit waiting to happen. A collectively bargained agreement solves that by letting schools and players jointly set the rules, just as professional leagues do. That means predictable budgets, real competitive balance, and an end to bidding wars run by boosters in the shadows.
[P1.5] The alternative is to treat revenue athletes as the only adults in a multibillion-dollar industry denied a voice in their working conditions. Graduate assistants can unionize, coaches have agents and guaranteed contracts, administrators negotiate salaries. Denying the same to the players whose bodies produce the product is exploitation dressed up as education. Bargaining does not destroy college sports; it rescues it by replacing chaos and paternalism with transparency, fairness, and labor peace.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a symptom for the cure. Athletes at revenue-sharing schools deserve enforceable pay, medical protection, and a real grievance process. They do not need to become a bargaining unit to get them.
[C1.2] Formal collective bargaining is the wrong instrument because college athletics is not one workplace. The same motion sweeps together public universities, private colleges, HBCUs, women’s teams, Olympic sports, scholarship athletes, walk-ons, minors, and international students whose visas restrict compensation. Labor law differs by state; many public employees have no federal right to bargain; Title IX forces benefits across teams; tax and eligibility rules treat aid differently. A campus-by-campus union model would produce a patchwork of contracts, injunctions, and competitive imbalance rather than the uniform rules the PRO side claims to want.
[C1.3] It would also make athletes worse off in practice. Exclusive representation means one union speaks for everyone; a senior football player’s priorities can bind a freshman volleyball player. The remedy becomes strikes and lockouts, which punish the athletes who cannot afford a lost season and the teammates left behind. In right-to-work or public-sector states, leverage and legality vary campus to campus.
[C1.4] A no-strike promise buys labor peace by silencing the players.
[C1.5] The enforceable core of the PRO case—guaranteed medical coverage, limits on practice, transparent NIL, transfer and eligibility rules, a neutral grievance process—does not require collective bargaining. It requires direct revenue sharing under clear conference or federal rules, standardized athlete contracts, independent enforcement, and separating NIL marketing from pay-for-play. A union contract is not an antitrust shield; patchwork bargaining invites more litigation, not less. That is not “ad hoc NIL”; it is a defined system with teeth.
[C1.6] The choice is not union or chaos. It is adversarial labor relations or enforceable athlete rights. College sports need the second, and the motion as written chooses the first.

[C2.1] They keep framing this as a choice between paternalism and power, but they miss the third option: enforceable rights written into law. A federal athlete bill of rights or conference-mandated standards with real penalties gives players guaranteed medical coverage, practice limits, and grievance arbitration without forcing them into an adversarial labor relationship where leverage means withholding their labor.
[C2.2] Their answer to the heterogeneity problem is separate bargaining units, but they ignore Title IX. If the football team bargains for revenue shares and enhanced medical benefits while the women's basketball team bargains separately for lesser terms, the school faces immediate Title IX liability for unequal treatment. You cannot differentiate compensation across teams at the same institution without cross-subsidization, turning every negotiation into a zero-sum fight where one team's gain becomes another team's liability. That is not solving the patchwork; it is creating a compliance minefield.
[C2.3] They cite professional leagues as the model, but the NFL is one employer negotiating with one union across state lines. College sports is hundreds of separate employers—public, private, large, small—each bound by different state laws and Title IX rules. You cannot transplant a league-wide CBA model onto a system where Alabama and a smaller school would be bargaining under identical structures despite having nothing in common.
[C2.4] On no-strike clauses, they call it a trade, but it is a rigged one. Under direct revenue sharing, athletes receive guaranteed compensation and protections as conditions of participation. Under collective bargaining, those same protections must be extracted through the threat of strikes that cost athletes their season, their development, and their draft stock. The athletes who can least afford a lost year hold the bag when the union calls a walkout.
[C2.5] Finally, they claim bargaining shields schools from antitrust suits, but a CBA does not automatically confer antitrust immunity. That requires legislation or court approval. Without it, a bargained salary cap is still illegal price-fixing. They promise a legal shield that bargaining does not provide, while statutory reform delivers enforceable rights without the strike risk.
