[P1.1] The proposition is not that partition is ideal. It is that every realistic alternative is worse, and that two states remain the only framework that can convert a zero-sum national conflict into two imperfect but survivable political communities.
[P1.2] Start with the alternatives. A single democratic state would require one electorate to decide borders, refugees, security, symbols, and Jerusalem while carrying decades of bloodshed and mutually exclusive national narratives. That is not a recipe for liberal democracy; it is a recipe for permanent constitutional crisis, communal veto, and likely organized violence. A single state with unequal rights is not peace; it is institutionalized domination and global isolation.
[P1.3] The status quo—occupation punctuated by wars—does not hold: it radicalizes each generation, erodes governance, and keeps the region on a hair trigger. Fragmentation into isolated cantons would reproduce Gaza’s incentives at larger scale: no sovereignty, no accountability, endless low-grade war.
[P1.4] Two states are least bad because they answer the core political need of both peoples: self-determination without one side having to live permanently under the other’s veto. Israel gains recognized borders and security coordination; Palestinians gain sovereignty, international legitimacy, and a mechanism for national development. Difficult issues—Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, demilitarization—do not disappear, but they become negotiable trade-offs rather than existential struggles over one state’s identity.
[P1.5] Achievability matters more than optimism. Even after repeated failures, the two-state framework still has the broadest diplomatic backing, prior negotiated parameters, regional acceptance, and a plausible sequence: phased withdrawal, international guarantees, demilitarization, land swaps, and end-of-claims finality. Declining trust is an argument for stronger enforcement, not for abandoning the only structure that both sides and key regional actors can still inhabit. If trust is shallow, the answer is third-party monitoring and reciprocal phased steps, not a leap into arrangements that demand even more trust.
[P1.6] The task is not to find a perfect peace. It is to choose the least-bad path that leaves both peoples a future.
