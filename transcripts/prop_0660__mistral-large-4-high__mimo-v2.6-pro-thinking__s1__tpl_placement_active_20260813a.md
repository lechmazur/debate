[P1.1] The proposition rests on a false trade-off. We are told that disconnecting damages productivity in knowledge industries, as if cognitive work were a factory line where output scales with hours logged. It does not. Knowledge work depends on sustained attention, creative problem-solving, and error-free judgment—capacities that degrade predictably under chronic hyperconnectivity.
[P1.2] Right-to-disconnect laws directly improve worker welfare by establishing enforceable boundaries against the expectation of perpetual availability. In software engineering, consulting, finance, and research, the norm of answering emails at midnight or on weekends does not create value; it creates burnout. Burnout is not a personal failing but an occupational hazard that erodes mental health, increases anxiety, and drives skilled workers out of the industry entirely. When a jurisdiction mandates that employees may ignore after-hours contact without penalty, it converts recovery time from a privilege into a right.
[P1.3] That is a concrete welfare gain: sleep, family time, and psychological safety.
[P1.4] The productivity objection misunderstands how knowledge work produces value. A fatigued programmer writing code at 2 a.m. introduces bugs that cost exponentially more to fix than the hours saved. A consultant drafting strategy documents while sleep-deprived produces lower-quality analysis than one who rests and returns fresh. The "productivity" of constant connectivity is an illusion—it measures presence, not output quality.
[P1.5] Sustainable productivity requires cognitive recovery, which is precisely what these laws protect.
[P1.6] Even if one grants a modest short-term coordination cost—a delayed email, a meeting scheduled for morning rather than midnight—that cost is trivial compared to the welfare improvements and the long-term productivity gains from reduced turnover, lower healthcare costs, and sustained creative capacity. The choice is not between welfare and productivity; it is between short-term presenteeism and long-term value creation. The law chooses the latter.

[P2.1] CON confuses coordination with exploitation. The "relocation" CON describes—compressed deadlines and intensified days—is not caused by the boundary itself, but by firms refusing to internalize coordination costs. That is a management choice the law exposes, not a harm it creates.
[P2.2] The flexibility argument collapses on inspection. The parent who answers emails after bedtime retains that ability under a right-to-disconnect law; the law removes only the employer's power to demand it. CON calls this rigidity, but it is the difference between voluntary choice and coerced availability. Without boundaries, flexibility flows upward to management while workers absorb the cost.
[P2.3] The claim that work shifts to the least-leveraged workers gets the causality backwards. Managers redistribute after-hours burdens onto junior staff precisely because no one has a right to refuse. A statutory floor protects those with the least bargaining power; it does not create the inequality, it corrects it.
[P2.4] Finally, CON equates responsiveness with productivity. Midnight output is not value creation; it is deferred maintenance paid in cognitive capital. Firms that lose deals over a 6 p.m. email have built models dependent on unsustainable extraction. The law forces a healthier equilibrium—adequate staffing, realistic deadlines, output that does not require burnout.
[P2.5] The productivity "damage" is merely the correction of phantom output, while welfare gains—reduced burnout, retention, mental health—are structural and permanent.
