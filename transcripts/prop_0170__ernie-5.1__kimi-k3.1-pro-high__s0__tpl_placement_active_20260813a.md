[P1.1] Water scarcity is not a problem of shortage alone—it is a problem of predictable, scalable supply. Conservation and rationing are valuable tools, but they are fundamentally limited by human behavior, diminishing returns, and the ceiling of existing demand. When a region’s population and economy grow, the fraction of water that can be saved shrinks while the absolute need rises. Desalination shatters that ceiling.
[P1.2] Modern reverse‑osmosis plants now deliver water at a fraction of the cost they did a decade ago, and when coupled with renewable energy they become both affordable and climate‑compatible. The capital cost per cubic metre has fallen by more than half in fifteen years, and plant construction creates skilled jobs that bolster local economies. Israel, a nation that once faced chronic deficits, now obtains roughly 60 % of its domestic water from desalination, turning a desert coast into a reliable source that supports agriculture, industry, and households without the social strain of rationing.
[P1.3] Saudi Arabia’s massive plants feed cities that would otherwise depend on depleting aquifers. Even in California, where conservation campaigns have cut usage by a tenth, the state still faces multi‑year droughts that only large‑scale desalination can buffer.
[P1.4] Investing primarily in desalination does not abandon conservation; it redirects capital toward the lever that actually expands the water pie. Conservation can shave the top off demand, but it cannot create new supply. Rationing, meanwhile, imposes hard caps that throttle economic activity and provoke political backlash. By building desalination capacity first, water‑scarce regions secure a baseline that makes any subsequent efficiency gains more effective, not the other way around.
[P1.5] The strategic choice is clear: a region that pours its limited budget into behavioral fixes will hit a wall; a region that builds desalination plants first builds a foundation that can sustain growth, protect public health, and weather climate variability for decades.

[P2.3] CON’s brine objection is overstated. Modern outfall designs disperse salt through diffusers, and emerging practices harvest minerals or support mariculture, turning waste into revenue. Brine does not become a deal‑breaker.
[P2.4] The resilience argument assumes a single coastal plant is the only option. In practice regions deploy a mix of large‑scale coastal units and modular inland or offshore sites backed by renewable storage. [P2.5] CON warns of lock‑in to tariffs and decades of debt. The alternative—repeated emergency water purchases or reliance on depleting aquifers—creates far higher long‑term fiscal risk. Moreover, primary desalination does not preclude later efficiency spending; it guarantees that when those measures are applied they sit on a larger water base rather than a shrinking one.
[P2.6] Finally, CON treats rationing as a harmless safety net, yet hard caps throttle agriculture, industry, and public health. Israel’s experience shows that when desalination supplied the bulk of water, rationing was rare and conservation could focus on efficiency rather than survival.
