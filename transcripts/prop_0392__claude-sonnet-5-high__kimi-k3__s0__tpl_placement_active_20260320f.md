[P1.1] Imagine a blind veteran trying to book a medical appointment through a hospital's website, and the date picker cannot be operated with a screen reader. Not once, but every month, for years, because the hospital knows the workaround: a phone line that puts him on hold for forty minutes. This is not a hypothetical edge case. It is the daily texture of life for tens of millions of disabled people who are told, in effect, that public digital infrastructure was built for everyone except them.
[P1.2] The proposition is narrow and reasonable: fines only for repeated failures of basic accessibility standards. Not novel design, not perfection, not every edge case — basic standards, like alt text on images, keyboard navigability, and captioning, that have existed as settled technical guidance for over a decade. And only after repetition, meaning the organization had notice and chose not to fix it.
[P1.3] Here is the core argument: without meaningful financial consequence, accessibility will always lose the budget fight. Every product team faces trade-offs, and inside that trade-off, accessibility is invisible to the majority of users and therefore invisible to revenue. A company can ignore a broken screen-reader experience for years and suffer zero quarterly earnings impact. That is not an accident of neglect; it is a rational response to incentives as currently structured.
[P1.4] If you want organizations to treat accessibility as non-negotiable, you have to make ignoring it cost money, because that is the only language enforced by boards and shareholders.
[P1.5] Compare this to every other domain where basic safety or rights compliance is treated as optional until it is expensive: workplace safety, fire codes, food handling. Nobody argues those fields would fix themselves through general goodwill. Voluntary compliance frameworks for digital accessibility have existed for over twenty years, and the web is still overwhelmingly inaccessible by independent audits year after year. Voluntary asks were tried.
[P1.6] They failed. Fines are not punitive overreach — they are the correction of a market failure that goodwill alone could never fix.
