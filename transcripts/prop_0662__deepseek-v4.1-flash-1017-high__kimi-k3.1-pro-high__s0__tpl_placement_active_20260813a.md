[P1.1] Social insurance is supposed to follow work, not the payroll software a firm happens to use. Platform work is work: rides, deliveries, care tasks, and freelance services are coordinated, priced, and paid by platforms that already record the transactions. Excluding those earnings from unemployment insurance and social-insurance eligibility is not neutral. It is a subsidy for a business model that shifts risk onto workers and taxpayers.
[P1.2] The proposition fixes this without forcing every platform worker into a traditional employee box. Standardized earnings records can do the administrative work: platforms report gross payments and relevant work time or task volume in a common format; contributions accrue pro rata; eligibility is calculated on aggregated earnings across platforms. A driver splitting hours across two apps should not be invisible to unemployment insurance because no single app sees the whole picture. Aggregation fixes that.
[P1.3] A worker who loses access to the platform—through deactivation, demand collapse, or injury—should have a record that follows them, just as wages follow a conventional employee.
[P1.4] This is administrable precisely because platforms are data-rich. They already generate payout and tax records; standardization turns fragmented private data into portable social-insurance records. It also reduces evasion and misclassification, because earnings cannot disappear into a contractor label. Firms competing with traditional employers should not get a cost advantage by externalizing injury, unemployment, and retirement risk.
[P1.5] The alternative is worse: workers fall back on public safety nets when platform work fails, while platforms keep the flexibility and the margin. Social insurance is insurance, not charity; the point is to pool risk across all work. If a government can record the earnings, it can require the contribution. Standardized records are the least intrusive way to make the social contract keep up with the labor market.

[C1.1] PRO treats a record of payment as proof of an insurable employment relationship. That is the central mistake. Unemployment insurance does not ask only what someone earned; it asks whether work was lost involuntarily, whether the worker is available for work, and which contributions financed the risk. Other social-insurance programs use still different contribution periods and earnings bases.
[C1.2] A standardized earnings file cannot settle those questions, yet the motion makes it the gateway to eligibility across them.
[C1.3] The file itself is far less reliable than PRO suggests. Gross platform payouts are not wages: a driver’s payment may include amounts offset by fuel, vehicle, and other costs, while a care worker’s economics are entirely different. “Work time” is equally unstable. Someone can be logged into two apps simultaneously, available on both but performing a task on neither—or on one. Counting logged hours twice inflates attachment to work; counting only completed tasks penalizes workers whose jobs involve waiting.
[C1.4] Platforms are data-rich, but their data were designed to run marketplaces, not determine public benefits. Standardization would convert inconsistent private metrics into apparently authoritative government records, with workers bearing the burden of correcting them.
[C1.5] The financing dilemma is just as serious. If counting earnings triggers contributions, platforms can respond through lower worker payouts, higher consumer prices, or tighter access for marginal workers. If governments instead expand eligibility without corresponding, sustainable contributions, existing insurance funds subsidize a newly covered risk. PRO’s casual “pro rata” formula does not resolve who owes what when work overlaps across several apps, conventional employment, and self-employment.
[C1.6] Nor is exclusion from this particular mandate a declaration that platform workers deserve no protection. Governments can improve access through existing tax records, portable individual accounts, voluntary contribution arrangements, or specifically designed self-employment coverage, each with eligibility rules suited to the program. Those approaches do not require building a universal reporting infrastructure before its categories are even coherent.

The motion therefore mandates the record before establishing that it answers the questions different insurance programs actually ask.
[C2.2] The proposed repairs expose, rather than solve, the problem. “Net earnings” require allocating expenses across platform and non-platform work; standardized deductions can overstate one worker’s resources and understate another’s. Replacing hours with paid tasks avoids double-counting logins but makes waiting time invisible. Deduplication through a common worker identifier links activity across marketplaces. This is no longer simple payout reporting; it is a new adjudication and surveillance infrastructure whose errors feed directly into contributions and benefits.
[C2.3] Nor do pro rata earnings allocate risk. Suppose a driver earns through two apps and conventional employment, then loses platform access while demand remains available elsewhere. An earnings formula can divide revenue; it cannot establish which platform financed which unemployment risk, or why that base should carry across programs with different eligibility rules. Calling financing a “design choice” leaves the motion’s hardest burden unanswered.
[C2.4] The claim that the alternative is “no record, no contributions, no eligibility” is false. Existing tax and payment records can support specifically designed self-employment coverage; portable accounts can be funded without treating every platform payment as an insured wage. If PRO instead defines those tax records as the motion’s standardized records, then the motion adds no cross-platform task reporting and cannot deliver the system just defended.
[C2.5] Finally, lower payouts are not a harmless trade for accruing protection. A worker can bear an immediate loss yet still fail later contribution, involuntariness, or availability tests. Certain costs now cannot be justified by contingent eligibility later. Governments should design and finance coverage around defined risks—not mandate a deceptively portable earnings file and discover its insurance meaning afterward.
