[P1.1] The question before us is what actually changes a young couple's decision about having children. I'll argue that the answer lies in the two largest recurring costs of family life—housing and childcare—not in a one-time payment that vanishes into a few months of expenses.
[P1.2] Start with the economics. A one-off baby bonus, even a generous one, is a rounding error against the lifetime cost of raising a child in Seoul, Tokyo, or Shanghai. Raising a child to adulthood in these cities costs a fortune dominated by two line items: housing space and care. A bonus equivalent to a few thousand dollars cannot offset a housing market where a family-sized apartment is priced at ten to fifteen times annual income.
[P1.3] Bonuses fail because they misprice the problem. They treat childbearing as a one-time decision cost, when it is actually a decades-long stream of expenses.
[P1.4] Second, bonuses fail behaviorally. If money were the binding constraint, Hungary's sustained baby subsidies would have produced a durable fertility recovery; instead, gains have been modest and partly attributable to timing—couples having children earlier, not more children overall. Japan's repeated cash grants have coincided with decades of decline. East Asian fertility fell even as governments sprinkled cash, because the deeper deterrents—cramped homes, brutal work hours, and scarce affordable daycare—were untouched.
[P1.5] Third, housing and childcare attack the actual bottlenecks. Housing affordability changes the feasibility calculation: couples delay marriage and children largely because they cannot secure a home suitable for raising a family. Universal, high-quality childcare removes the motherhood penalty in careers, which is among the strongest drivers of East Asian women's reluctance to have children. France and the Nordic countries sustain comparatively higher fertility on the back of exactly this infrastructure, not lump sums.
[P1.6] South Korea's own experiences with cash incentives show public money spent without shifting the structural math.
[P1.7] The proposition asks which raises birth rates *more*. Bonuses are politically visible but behaviorally weak; structural affordability changes the constraints families actually face. That is where East Asian governments will move the number.
