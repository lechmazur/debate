Surveys may simply reflect this shift in priorities rather than a systemic breakdown. The loss of casual "third place" interactions stems from changing social norms, not a solvable policy gap that zoning or funding can fix.
[C1.2] Public policy cannot manufacture genuine human connection. Governments can build cafes and mandate workplace social hours, but these produce awkward compliance, not authentic friendship. The UK's minister for loneliness targets a specific public health vulnerability—elderly isolation—but extending this to general adult friendship medicalizes normal life, treating natural social variation as a pathology requiring state management. Worse, it sets a precedent: if friendship is fair game, what personal relationship is not?
[C1.3] Dating? Parenting? The state lacks the legitimacy to intervene in such intimate spheres.
[C1.4] The public costs PRO cites are misattributed. Comparing loneliness to smoking conflates correlation with causation; loneliness often reflects underlying mental health or economic issues, not a lack of friendship infrastructure. Addressing those root problems directly is more effective than the blunt instrument of social engineering. Adults already have agency to build friendships—joining clubs, prioritizing time with others.
[C1.5] Policy should remove barriers like mental health access, not attempt the impossible task of legislating bonds that only flourish when freely chosen.
[C1.6] Most fundamentally, friendship requires mutual desire. No amount of benches, shorter commutes, or mandated social time can create that. The PRO's solutions ignore this truth, offering structural fixes for what is essentially a human, not a systemic, challenge.
