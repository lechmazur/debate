[P1.1] Public health systems should fund mitochondrial replacement therapy for a narrow, high-risk group: people with known severe inherited mitochondrial disease who, after counseling, want a genetically related child without transmitting that disease. This is not enhancement. It is prevention of a catastrophic childhood illness.
[P1.2] Mitochondria supply energy to brain, heart, and muscle. When inherited mitochondrial DNA is severely damaged, medicine often cannot repair the cause; it manages seizures, feeding failure, weakness, organ decline, and palliation. A child with a disorder like Leigh syndrome may lose movement, vision, and swallowing; parents may watch one child die while fearing the same risk in the next pregnancy. MRT keeps the parents’ nuclear DNA while using donor mitochondria with functioning energy machinery.
[P1.3] It cannot guarantee a healthy child, but it can greatly reduce transmission risk.
[P1.4] That is squarely public health. Systems already fund vaccination, prenatal screening, and newborn screening because prevention is cheaper and more humane than rescue. Severe mitochondrial disease can mean years of specialist care, hospital visits, equipment, drugs, emergency treatment, and parental caregiving. A regulated course of assisted reproduction, with screening and follow-up, is a reasonable public expense against irreversible harm.
[P1.5] It also prevents a two-tier system in which affluent families can travel for MRT while others must accept preventable tragedy or options that do not meet their family-forming interests.
[P1.6] The objections point to governance, not refusal. Safety worries justify licensed centers, strict eligibility, informed consent, disclosure of residual mitochondrial carryover, and monitoring. Germline worries justify transparent registries and a ban on enhancement. Worries about donor eggs, adoption, or embryo selection justify counseling about alternatives, not pretending those alternatives are equivalent for every patient.
[P1.7] A system that pays for a child’s suffering after birth but not for a carefully governed chance to prevent it has its priorities backward. The state should make this prevention available by need, under public oversight, rather than leave it to wealth, luck, and foreign clinics.
