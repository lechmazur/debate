[P1.1] Infertility is a medical failure of the reproductive system, not a consumer preference. Blocked tubes, absent ovulation, severe endometriosis, and impaired sperm production are dysfunctions of the body. Healthcare exists to diagnose and treat exactly that kind of failure. Classifying IVF and related care as basic healthcare applies a principle we already accept for every other organ: when disease blocks a normal human function, treatment belongs in the core system, not in a luxury market.
[P1.2] The alternative is a wealth test for parenthood. These treatments work for a substantial share of patients, but they are costly. Left to private payment, a teacher with the same diagnosis as a banker faces radically different odds of having a child. That is not neutral budgeting.
[P1.3] It sorts families by income at the point of a recognized medical need. Basic coverage is how a health system refuses that sorting.
[P1.4] “Not immediately life-saving” is not a reason for exclusion. Systems already fund cataract surgery, joint replacement, antidepressants, and fertility preservation after cancer because medicine is also about restoring function and relieving serious, lasting harm. Infertility brings grief, anxiety, and relationship strain that persist for years. Ignoring it while covering other non-fatal conditions is an arbitrary line drawn around one organ system.
[P1.5] Nor does coverage require promising a baby to every patient. Basic care already includes treatments with incomplete success rates—chemotherapy, joint repair, addiction treatment—because partial restoration of health still counts. What matters is that the intervention addresses a disease, that benefits are real for many who receive it, and that denial falls hardest on those least able to pay.
[P1.6] Countries should treat infertility care, including IVF, as basic healthcare because the condition is medical, the suffering is concrete, effective treatment exists, and exclusion turns a biological misfortune into a class privilege. “Basic” means essential to equal access, not cheap and simple.

[C1.1] Calling infertility a medical condition does not settle what belongs in basic healthcare. Basic is not a label for everything medicine can diagnose; it is the guaranteed core a country promises everyone when money, staff, and time are finite. That core should protect the health of the existing patient, work reliably enough for its cost, and have limits that can be defined fairly. IVF fails those tests.
[C1.2] First, IVF does not restore reproductive health. Blocked tubes remain blocked, impaired sperm production remains impaired, and after treatment the patient is still infertile. Its measure of success is not a healthier adult but the birth of another person. That differs categorically from cataract surgery or joint replacement, which repair the patient's sight or mobility.
[C1.3] IVF bypasses dysfunction to pursue a deeply valued life project: having a biological child. Sympathy for that project does not make its fulfilment a guaranteed medical entitlement.
[C1.4] Second, IVF is expensive, uncertain, and open-ended. One cycle can consume resources that would fund many routine operations or years of chronic-disease care, yet often produces no pregnancy and invites another cycle. Success declines with age, while emotional pressure makes stopping rules feel cruel. As basic care, it creates an entitlement with no natural endpoint: how many cycles, at what age, with donor eggs or surrogacy?
[C1.5] Any cap reintroduces arbitrary lines; no cap is unaffordable.
[C1.6] The medical parts of infertility care already belong in the basic system: diagnosis, treatment of endometriosis, infection and hormonal disorders, and surgery that restores function. What the proposition adds is not that care, but a guarantee for a costly technique to create a child. Reclassification will not end sorting by wealth—the better-off will still buy extra cycles and private clinics—but it will divert a guaranteed budget from care protecting existing patients. Limited support may be compassionate policy; IVF is not basic healthcare.

[C2.4] Protocols do not rescue the classification. Transplant and cancer limits ration scarce, often life-preserving treatment for existing patients against clear clinical endpoints. An IVF cap must still decree two cycles or three, age forty or forty-three, this prognosis or that, while spending heavily for a modest chance per attempt. Calling those cutoffs “evidence-based” [P2.5] does not make the entitlement basic; it only administers an open-ended desire with better paperwork.
[C2.5] As a basic right, each boundary will also be lobbied as discrimination.
[C2.6] Finally, a public first course does not equalize parenthood; it socializes the most expensive first step while private patients still buy more attempts. That may be a subsidy worth debating.
