[P1.1] Modern science operates on a foundational rule: utility never overrides consent. If a university researcher dug up a local cemetery today because the skeletons held valuable data about nineteenth-century disease, they would be prosecuted, not published. Yet museums and universities continue to hold the ancestral remains of Indigenous and colonized peoples—acquired through grave robbing, battlefield looting, and colonial subjugation—on the grounds that studying them is useful. We cannot defend a two-tiered system of human dignity where the dead of dominant societies are granted legal protection and peace, while colonized ancestors are reduced to permanent institutional property.
[P1.2] Allowing researchers to block or delay return by claiming "scientific value" creates a loophole for indefinite retention. Science is never finished; someone can always argue that a better DNA sequencing technique or imaging tool will exist ten years from now. Under that logic, remains are never returned. In practice, institutions across the world have kept thousands of ancestral remains packed in basement storage boxes for generations—often completely unstudied—hoarding them as biological capital for hypothetical future projects.
[P1.3] Mandating prompt return, even over researcher objections, is the only way to break this cycle of bureaucratic stalling.
[P1.4] Finally, prompt repatriation does not mean the end of discovery; it means the end of coerced research. For descendant communities, keeping ancestors in drawers is not a harmless archival practice, but an ongoing spiritual and cultural trauma. When institutions return remains unconditionally, they replace colonial possession with mutual respect. Once their sovereignty is recognized, descendant communities frequently choose to partner with geneticists and anthropologists on collaborative studies that benefit both science and the community.
[P1.5] The real choice in this debate is not between scientific progress and cultural sensitivity. It is whether museums and universities will finally apply the basic ethics of human consent to the people history treated as specimens.
