[C1.1] The proposition asks us to treat social media as the engine of the crisis, but the evidence supports a different order of causation: social media is where deeper problems become visible, amplified, and worsened, not where they originate.
[C1.2] Start with what predates the smartphone era. Adolescent anxiety and depression were already climbing in many countries before social media adoption, and economic precarity, family breakdown, academic pressure, and inadequate mental-health care are measurable, long-standing drivers. These are not background noise; they are the primary variables. When researchers control for baseline adversity, family conflict, and socioeconomic status, the independent effect of social media shrinks substantially, often to small or null effects, especially for boys.
[C1.3] The correlation is real, but it largely reflects that troubled teens and troubled families are both heavy users and heavy sufferers.
[C1.4] The timing argument cuts both ways. Smartphones arrived during the 2010s alongside austerity policies, rising housing costs, gig-economy instability, climate anxiety, and a youth mental-health care system already collapsing under demand. Attributing the crisis to the device lets policymakers avoid the harder truth: we have underfunded schools, clinics, and family supports for decades.
[C1.5] There is the selection problem. The proposition assumes platforms cause distress, but distressed teens also seek connection online, using social media more intensively precisely because offline life is painful. Reverse causation is not a footnote; it is a serious threat to the causal claim. When longitudinal studies track within-person changes, the effects of social media on well-being are typically small and inconsistent, while the effects of poverty, abuse, and family conflict remain large and stable.
[C1.6] Social media is not benign; cyberbullying and comparison are real harms, but calling it the primary driver misidentifies the lever. Restricting access treats a symptom while ignoring the disease. For many isolated teens, especially marginalized youth in unsupportive homes, online spaces are a lifeline, not a toxin. The crisis is real, but its primary drivers are material and structural: inequality, instability, and untreated trauma.
[C1.7] Social media is the screen on which those problems play out, not the projector.
