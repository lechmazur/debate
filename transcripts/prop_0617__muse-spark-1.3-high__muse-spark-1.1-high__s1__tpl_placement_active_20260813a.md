[P1.1] Dominant digital platforms don't maintain power by out-innovating rivals. They buy the future before it can threaten them. Network effects, massive data feedback loops, and ecosystem lock-in mean each acquisition is not just an asset purchase, it is another brick in a moat. A search giant buys mapping, video, ad tech and AI startups until no newcomer can reach users without passing through its tollbooth.
[P1.2] The record is a graveyard of missed warnings. Instagram and WhatsApp looked small when Facebook bought them; today they anchor social dominance. Google's purchases of YouTube and its ad-tech stack helped turn a search lead into control of the pipes of digital advertising. Regulators waved these deals through because they bore the impossible burden of proving future competition harm with present certainty.
[P1.3] That standard does not protect competition, it protects incumbents.
[P1.4] The information is upside down. Only the platform knows whether that startup is a cute feature or a mortal threat — it has the threat memos, the user overlap data, the integration plans. Forcing a resource-strapped agency to divine that future from outside guarantees false negatives. Shifting the burden puts proof where evidence lives.
[P1.5] If a deal truly serves consumers, the firm can demonstrate it: better products, real efficiencies, interoperability commitments that survive scrutiny.
[P1.6] This is no ban and no attack on mergers generally. It applies only where dominance plus acquisition risk is highest, and it leaves a clear escape hatch: prove substantial consumer benefit. Weigh the errors. Blocking or conditioning a harmless startup purchase costs delay and paperwork.
[P1.7] Approving a killer acquisition costs a decade of locked-in monopoly, higher ad taxes passed to shoppers, and innovation that never appears. In markets that tip fast and un-tip almost never, precaution is not bias. It is the only pro-consumer policy.

[C1.1] Presuming every acquisition by a dominant platform is anti-competitive until proven beneficial sounds like precaution. It is prohibition by another name, and it flips the basic rule of competition law: the government proves likely harm, not the company proves its innocence.
[C1.2] Most platform acquisitions are not moat-building. They are how innovation scales. Android was a tiny startup when Google bought it in 2005. Without Google's capital, distribution and engineering there is no mass-market alternative to iPhone.
[C1.3] YouTube in 2006 could not stream globally without Google's data centers. Consumers got more competition, not less. Under [P1.5]'s test, Google could not have proven "substantial consumer benefit" in advance with certainty, so under PRO's rule those deals die and with them the benefit.
[C1.4] PRO's history from [P1.2] - Instagram, WhatsApp - is hindsight bias. Instagram had 13 employees and no revenue. WhatsApp was one of dozens of messaging apps. Regulators look foolish in retrospect because the future is uncertain.
[C1.5] That uncertainty is exactly why startups exist. Founders and venture investors take risk for an acquisition exit. If you close that exit, you don't save future rivals, you starve them. Early funding collapses when the path to scale via a larger platform is presumptively illegal.
[C1.6] The error trade-off in [P1.7] is therefore backwards. PRO says blocking a harmless deal costs only paperwork. Tell that to users who got Google traffic data in Waze, or to publishers when ad-tech got integrated. A false positive denies scale economies, leaves products fragmented and worse, and hurts consumers directly.
[C1.7] And dominance is not permanent. Myspace fell to Facebook, Yahoo to Google, BlackBerry to Android. Markets that tip can un-tip when efficient scaling is allowed.
[C1.8] "Prove substantial consumer benefit" is unworkable. Benefits are future, diffuse, and hard to quantify at filing; harms are easy to imagine. No counsel will clear a $20 million acqui-hire under that burden, so deals won't be tried. Keep the burden where tools exist - on agencies with subpoena power to show likely harm - not a presumption of guilt.
