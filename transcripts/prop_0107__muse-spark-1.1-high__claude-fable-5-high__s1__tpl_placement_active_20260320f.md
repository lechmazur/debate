[P1.1] Carefully screened adolescents with persistent gender dysphoria are not kids who questioned for a week. They are teenagers with years of consistent, insistent distress about their bodies, evaluated by mental health professionals, pediatricians and endocrinologists over months, often years, where other mental health conditions have been addressed. For them, puberty is not a neutral pause - it is an active injury.
[P1.2] Unwanted pubertal development - voice deepening, breast growth, facial hair, menstruation - cements the very source of dysphoria every day in the mirror. It drives sky-high rates of depression, anxiety, self-harm and suicidal ideation that do not respond to therapy alone because therapy cannot change the body. Gender-affirming medical care - puberty suppression to prevent further incongruent changes, then hormones when appropriate - directly interrupts that mechanism. It buys time and it aligns the body with a stable identity.
[P1.3] When that care is provided under guidelines, mental health improves. Clinics consistently report drops in depressive symptoms, less anxiety, better functioning at school, and markedly lower rates of suicidal thoughts after initiation of care. The young person can go out, be seen as themselves, and develop rather than hide.
[P1.4] The other side of the ledger - long-term regret and irreversible harm - is remarkably small in this screened group. Detransition and regret rates in cohorts following the Dutch-model protocols are consistently low, in the low single digits, and often driven by social pressure rather than changed identity. Medical risks like bone mineral density changes or fertility implications are real but monitored, mitigated, and discussed in informed consent - just as with many pediatric treatments that carry risk.
[P1.5] Denying care does not avoid harm. It guarantees the harm of an irreversible, unwanted puberty and the psychological collapse that follows. For this narrow, well-evaluated group, benefits clearly outweigh harms.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to weigh a benefit against a harm, so notice the asymmetry at its heart: the claimed benefit is measured in short-term mood scores; the harm is permanent — sterility, lost sexual function, altered bone and brain development in bodies that can never be given back. Those are not commensurate risks, and a proposition asking us to trade one for the other needs strong evidence. It does not have it.
[C1.2] First, the evidence for mental-health improvement is strikingly weak. The studies clinics point to are small, uncontrolled, and short — typically one or two years, with substantial loss to follow-up. When national health authorities in the UK, Sweden, and Finland commissioned systematic reviews of this literature, they independently concluded the evidence was of very low certainty and pulled back from routine adolescent medicalization. These are not culture-war actors; they are cautious public health systems that read the data and found the improvements too small and too confounded — by concurrent psychotherapy and regression to the mean — to justify irreversible intervention.
[C1.3] Second, "carefully screened" is doing impossible work. The original Dutch protocol screened for childhood-onset, persistent dysphoria with minimal comorbidity. Today's clinical population is dominated by adolescent-onset cases, disproportionately natal females with depression, autism, and trauma histories — a group the founding research never studied. No screening tool exists that reliably distinguishes an adolescent whose dysphoria will persist from one who would desist through puberty, and historically many did desist.
[C1.4] Third, blockers are not a pause. Nearly every child placed on them proceeds to cross-sex hormones, suggesting the intervention consolidates the very outcome it claims to merely delay — while foreclosing the natural resolution puberty itself often brings.
[C1.5] Finally, low regret figures are an artifact of short follow-up: regret typically surfaces after a decade, and detransitioners rarely report back to the clinics counting them.
[C1.6] Unproven benefit; permanent, undercounted harm. The motion fails on its own terms.
