[P1.1] Cable news gave partisans a mirror. Social media gave them an enemy, an audience, and a scoreboard. That is the difference the motion turns on.
[P1.2] The polarization that matters here is not careful disagreement about tax rates; it is affective — the conviction that the other side is stupid, evil, or a threat to the country. Cable news certainly fed that among people who deliberately tuned in. But it was opt-in, scheduled, and edited. You had to want it, at eight o'clock, and a network with lawyers and advertisers decided what aired.
[P1.3] Social media removed every one of those filters and made political hostility ambient: it arrives while you are looking at your cousin's baby photos.
[P1.4] Two mechanisms do the work. First, engagement optimization rewards moral outrage, and the cheapest outrage is a screenshot of the worst person on the other side. Under cable, the out-group's spokespeople were professionals; under social media, your feed's picture of a Republican or Democrat is a stranger's most deranged post, algorithmically promoted precisely because it made you react.
[P1.5] Second, identity: cable was something you watched, social media is something you are. Likes and quote-posts turn contempt into a public performance your friends reward.
[P1.6] It also rewired elites. Cable created pundits; social media made every legislator their own producer, paid in attention for the clip that humiliates an opponent. Governing quietly became a losing strategy.
[P1.7] My opponent will say polarization predates Facebook. True — and irrelevant to a comparative claim. The question is which did more. Cable sorted a self-selected audience of the already-engaged.
[P1.8] Social media reached nearly everyone, including people who never sought news, and it now distributes cable's own clips. Cable polarized its audience. Social media polarized the country.
