[P1.1] Geography is not a policy choice, and it should not function as a life sentence. Under the current arrangement, whether a state bears the overwhelming weight of asylum processing depends on which way a coastline faces or where a land border happens to sit. Italy, Greece, and Malta did not choose to be entry points any more than landlocked Slovakia or Hungary chose to be sheltered from arrivals. Yet the current system treats accident of geography as if it were a deliberate assumption of responsibility.
[P1.2] That is the core injustice burden-sharing quotas exist to fix.
[P1.3] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.4] First, functional collapse. When frontline states absorb the bulk of arrivals without proportional support, reception systems buckle: overcrowded camps, backlogged asylum courts, and processing delays measured in years rather than months. This is not merely unfair to the states involved, it is disastrous for the asylum-seekers themselves, who wait in limbo, and for the EU's own credibility as a rules-based bloc when its stated legal obligations under the Common European Asylum System go unmet in practice.
[P1.5] Second, incentive collapse. Leaving frontline states to cope alone incentivizes exactly the behavior critics condemn: waving migrants through to secondary countries, under-registering arrivals, or straining the Dublin Regulation's already-fragile "first country of entry" principle. Quotas remove the incentive to dodge responsibility by ensuring no single state faces a structurally unmanageable load in the first place.
[P1.6] Third, solidarity is the EU's foundational premise. The euro, the single market, structural funds—all rest on the idea that shared institutions require shared burdens. A union that demands solidarity on regulatory harmonization but refuses it on humanitarian emergencies is incoherent. If Poland benefits from EU cohesion funds, it cannot simultaneously claim total immunity from EU asylum obligations.
[P1.7] Quotas need not be rigid headcounts alone; they can include financial contributions or resettlement flexibility. But the principle must hold: distribution should track capacity and fairness, not coastline. That is the case CON must answer.

[C1.1] The injustice PRO describes is real. But burden-sharing quotas do not solve this problem. They replace one arbitrary unfair allocation with a different arbitrary unfair allocation, while making every underlying failure worse.
[C1.2] There exists no neutral, fair formula for quota weight. A headcount quota treats a refugee with existing family in Sweden exactly the same as one placed alone in rural Latvia. It counts each arrival identically regardless of whether a country has 4% unemployment or 19%, robust integration systems or none. Every past attempt to draft these rules collapsed into ugly political haggling, not solidarity.
[C1.3] Worse, quotas reverse the very incentives PRO claims to fix. If every registered arrival will automatically be reallocated across the bloc, frontline states gain nothing by investing in proper reception or fair processing. They gain even less by cooperating on returns of rejected claimants or external border security. Every state’s rational best move becomes quietly waving people onwards before official registration: exactly the dysfunction PRO rightly condemns.
[C1.4] This is not abstract. The 2015 mandatory quota trial did not reduce camp overcrowding, clear backlogs, or build trust. It produced years of legal challenges, mass refusal to accept relocations, and deepened division across the EU far more than the original burden ever did.
[C1.5] Solidarity is not forcing populations to accept resettlement they reject. It is delivering frontline states the processing staff, border resources, and return support they actually requested. Quotas do not fix unfair geography. They just spread the failure evenly.
