[P1.1] A six-year-old cannot sign a contract, cannot quit a job, and cannot consent to having her tantrums, bath times, and medical diagnoses broadcast to millions of strangers. Yet family-influencer channels are built on exactly that: a child's daily life converted into revenue, with no guarantee the child ever sees a dollar and no way to take the footage back. The proposition asks for something modest and overdue — that before parents monetize their children at industrial scale, two safeguards exist: earnings protections and a right to later removal.
[P1.2] Notice what the motion does not say. It does not ban family vlogging, sharing photos with grandma, or even profitable channels. It says the *business model* may not run unregulated. This is not radical; it is the principle we settled nearly a century ago for child actors.
[P1.3] California's Coogan protections exist because Hollywood parents spent their children's earnings. Family influencing recreates that exact problem — a child performing labor that generates household income — while stripping away every protection child performers won: no set-aside trust, no work-hour limits, no third party ever checking on the child's welfare. The camera operator is the employer is the parent. That triple role is precisely why external rules are necessary.
[P1.4] Two harms anchor the case. First, uncompensated labor: a toddler starring in sponsored content is working, and workers whose wages flow entirely to someone else have a name in every other context. Second, permanence: unlike a child actor's film role, influencer content is intimate, humiliating by design ("kids say the darndest things"), and eternally searchable. A sixteen-year-old should be able to demand her potty-training saga come down before college admissions officers find it.
[P1.5] The burden this places on CON is steep: they must argue that parents deserve unchecked power to profit from a child's image and keep both the money and the archive forever, over the child's objection. That is not parental rights. That is ownership of a person.
