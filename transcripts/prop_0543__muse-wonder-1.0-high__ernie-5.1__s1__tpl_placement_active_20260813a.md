[P1.1] For a minor signing up to a large platform, the safest choice should be the starting point, not a maze buried three menus deep. Privacy-by-design means exactly that: accounts private by default, direct messages limited to approved contacts, location off, tagging and search discoverability restricted, and no targeted advertising or profiling built from a child's data. A teen or parent can loosen them, but the company cannot start by exposing the child.
[P1.2] That design measurably reduces harm because so much harm needs access first. A stranger cannot groom, threaten, or solicit images from a child he cannot find and message. A bully's screenshot travels less far when resharing and public indexing are off. Advertisers and data brokers cannot build a profile they are not allowed to collect.
[P1.3] Location stalking, unwanted sexual contact, and viral humiliation do not disappear, but their pathways narrow — and platforms can count drops in stranger contacts, reports, and exposures.
[P1.4] A mandate is necessary because voluntary privacy loses to the business model. Open profiles, frictionless contact, and rich data make networks grow faster and ads sell better. Every platform fears that if it slows sign-up or limits discovery, a rival will not. Teenagers predictably accept whatever default appears; that is why defaults are powerful.
[P1.5] Expecting a thirteen-year-old to out-negotiate an interface designed by thousands of engineers is not freedom, it is abdication.
[P1.6] This is not censorship in any significant sense. No post is banned, no viewpoint is penalized, no topic becomes unspeakable. A minor can still speak, create, and be heard by friends and followers they choose; they simply are not automatically broadcast to every adult, scraper, and advertiser on the internet. Regulating who initially can contact a child and what data a company harvests is child safety and consumer protection, not state control of ideas.
[P1.7] Large platforms have the users, engineers, and profits to do this. Government should stop letting them make exposure the price of participation.

[C1.3] The “measurement” the PRO touts is therefore a narrow, optimistic proxy that ignores the dominant harm pathways.
[C1.4] Second, the claim that this is “without significantly increasing censorship” is the proposition’s Achilles’ heel. When a teenager’s posts cannot be indexed, shared, or amplified by algorithmic recommendation, the reach of their speech is curbed as effectively as any content takedown. The line between protecting a child and silencing a young voice is drawn by the same code that decides who can see a post, and the resulting opacity makes it impossible to audit whether a post was truly “private” or merely buried.
[C1.5] That is a form of censorship that scales with every default setting—a quiet, systematic restriction on the public sphere.
[C1.6] Moreover, a one‑size‑fits‑all mandate forces platforms to adopt blunt tools that ignore family diversity, pushes minors toward less regulated corners of the internet where safety is far worse, and imposes costly compliance that will be passed to users. The proposition’s promise of harm reduction without censorship is, on balance, an illusion.
