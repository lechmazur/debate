When a carrier prices a cabin bag separately, the traveler with only a small personal item pays for less of the weight, fuel, and bin space she uses. Mandating one included cabin bag forces her fare to subsidize the passenger with a roller bag.
[C1.2] That cross-subsidy is both regressive and inefficient: the budget weekend student pays more so the business traveler's bag allowance stays hidden in the fare.
[C1.3] The opponent's price example cuts the other way. If airline A quotes $90 and adds $25 only when a bag is taken, while airline B quotes $105 with the bag included, the bagless traveler is plainly better off at A. The real defect is display, not bundling: fare-comparison rules and total-price disclosure can make the $115 price visible without outlawing the $90 product. A mandate would destroy the no-frills fare segment that has made short-haul travel cheaper for millions.
[C1.4] Cabin bags also impose genuine costs. Weight raises fuel burn, overhead bins are scarce, and gate checks for noncompliant bags delay boarding. [C1.5] Finally, airlines already compete on whether a bag is included. That is product differentiation, not fragmentation. A regulator-defined "reasonable" allowance would freeze one model into law, invite disputes over size limits on regional jets and connections, and push airlines to recover revenue through seat fees or higher base fares. The correct remedy is better disclosure, not mandatory re-bundling.
