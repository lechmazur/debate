[P1.1] Schools prioritize abstract academic skills that most students will never apply after graduation, while ignoring universal, daily skills every learner will need within months of walking the stage. The motion fixes this glaring gap: cooking, basic home repair, and personal finance must be core, mandatory requirements, not optional electives reserved for students who happen to seek them out or whose schools have extra funding to offer niche electives.
[P1.2] Optional status for these skills is an equity failure. If a student’s family cannot afford private enrichment classes, or their parents work multiple jobs and lack time to teach them how to unclog a drain, file taxes, or cook a balanced meal, they are left to fend for themselves, often making costly, avoidable mistakes. Mandating these as core ensures every student, regardless of income or family circumstance, gains the tools to navigate independent adulthood without punitive disadvantage.
[P1.3] The harm of omitting these skills is concrete and widespread. Young adults regularly drown in avoidable credit card debt because they never learned how compound interest works, rely on processed fast food because they cannot prepare a simple nutritious meal, and pay hundreds of dollars for minor home fixes they could handle with basic know-how. These are not niche hobbies—they are the bare minimum for self-sufficiency, and failure to teach them leaves new graduates vulnerable to predatory lenders, unhealthy lifestyles, and unnecessary financial strain.
[P1.4] These skills do not displace traditional core subjects; they reinforce them. Measuring ingredients sharpens fraction literacy, budgeting builds practical math skills, basic repair applies physics concepts to real-world scenarios. Making them core does not dilute education, nor does it crowd out advanced coursework for students who want it. It makes learning more relevant, immediately useful, and accessible to every student, not just those pursuing four-year degrees.

[C1.1] Treating cooking, repair, and finance as core curriculum imposes catastrophic opportunity costs on an already overstretched school day. Schools exist to cultivate transferable cognitive frameworks—critical reasoning, mathematical abstraction—that prepare students for an unpredictable economy. Converting scarce instructional hours into vocational checklists forces trade-offs against the very literacy foundations that make self-teaching possible later. A teenager who understands exponential functions can decode any mortgage; one who memorizes "how to change a tire" in a classroom simulation remains helpless when facing a hybrid vehicle's electronic systems.
[C1.2] Core status doesn't add capacity; it cannibalizes it.
[C1.3] The equity argument collapses under unfunded mandates. When jurisdictions declare home repair "core," affluent districts hire licensed contractors and build workshop facilities, while underfunded schools press English teachers to supervise screwdriver demonstrations with dull tools. The result is a two-tier system where wealthier students gain genuine competence while poorer students receive degraded academic time plus useless seat-time in sham practical courses. Optional extracurriculars allow targeted resources to flow where interest exists; compulsory core status spreads mediocrity thin.
[C1.4] The pedagogy is developmentally mismatched. Personal finance taught to fourteen-year-olds without incomes becomes abstract play-acting forgotten by twenty-one; basic repair requires infrastructure—fully equipped shops, liability insurance—that most buildings lack. Optional programs attract motivated learners who practice at home; mandatory enrollment forces disengaged students through shallow, checkbox exercises that satisfy graduation requirements while building no durable skill. You cannot standardize self-sufficiency across heterogeneous communities via state curriculum any more than you can mandate "core" lessons in emotional resilience.
[C1.5] Finally, bureaucratizing practical wisdom destroys its value. When repair becomes a tested standard, districts buy prefabricated "safe" modules—how to hang a picture frame, not how to troubleshoot a circuit—while avoiding complex financial calculations that might annoy political interests. Schools excel at abstraction precisely because it travels well; they fail at context-specific know-how because it resists standardization. Keep these skills optional, community-based, and responsive to actual student need rather than trapping them in the rigid machinery of core requirements.
