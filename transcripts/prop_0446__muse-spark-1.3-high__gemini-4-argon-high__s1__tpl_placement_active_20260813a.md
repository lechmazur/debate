[P1.1] Unabated coal power is the single easiest large cut in global emissions, and the one most crippled by inaction elsewhere. A kilowatt-hour from coal emits roughly twice the carbon of gas and far more air pollution, while alternatives are now cheaper in most major markets. Leaving each country to decide alone guarantees delay: no finance minister wants to close plants first, pay the transition costs, and watch neighbors undercut them with cheap coal electricity and steel.
[P1.2] A binding agreement with a common timeline breaks that logic. Binding does not mean world police; it means reciprocal commitment with consequences for reputation, trade measures, and access to climate finance. That reciprocity is what unlocks investment. When manufacturers, utilities, and banks know coal has a firm end date across the G20 and other large economies, capital stops flowing to life extensions and starts flowing to renewables, storage, grids, and retraining.
[P1.3] Voluntary pledges have not done that because they can be quietly abandoned when elections change.
[P1.4] Common does not mean identical. Differentiated support is the core of the deal: wealthier economies move faster, contribute finance and technology, and help coal-dependent regions build replacement capacity and protect workers and communities. That is fairer than the status quo, where poor communities bear the floods, heat, and smog while rich importers lecture them about ambition without paying for it.
[P1.5] And this is only unabated coal power, not all uses overnight, not a ban on abatement research. Countries keeping genuinely captured emissions would comply; countries pleading energy poverty would receive help to leapfrog to clean power rather than lock in forty years of fuel imports and stranded assets. Britain went from dominant coal to near zero in a decade; others can move faster with support.
[P1.6] The choice is orderly, financed phaseout together, or disorderly climate damage alone.
