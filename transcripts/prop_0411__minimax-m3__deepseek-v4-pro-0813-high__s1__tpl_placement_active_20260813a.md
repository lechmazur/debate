[P1.1] A wheelchair user standing at a bus stop while accessible buses drive past is not a transportation problem. It is a civil rights failure dressed up as a scheduling issue. The proposition before us is modest: when public dollars build transit networks and private platforms move the public for hire, the people who fund them cannot be left waiting twice as long, stranded on rainy corners, or told to schedule their lives around a separate, slower system. That is the standard this debate must answer.
[P1.2] Three reasons settle it.
[P1.3] First, the demand is for equal access, not preference. Roughly one in seven adults reports a mobility disability serious enough to affect daily life. Many cannot use stairs, narrow aisles, or high boarding steps at all. Standard service is not a luxury for them; it is the only service that works.
[P1.4] A two-tiered system is not "close to standard" by any honest reading. It is a separate, inferior system with a different name.
[P1.5] Second, the technology is already proven. Low-floor buses, ramps, securement areas, and wheelchair-accessible ride-hail vehicles have existed for decades. Uber and Lyft have run WAV programs in major cities. Paratransit operations demonstrate the logistical know-how.
[P1.6] The question is no longer whether it can be done; it is whether operators will be required to do it.
[P1.7] Third, cost cannot be the shield. Vehicles that accommodate wheelchairs cost somewhat more, but the marginal expense is small against multi-billion-dollar transit budgets and platform revenues. The real cost of non-accessibility is borne by disabled riders in lost jobs, missed medical appointments, and social isolation. We do not let operators off the hook for brakes because airbags are expensive.
[P1.8] We should not let them off the hook for accessibility because retrofitting costs money.
[P1.9] The opposition will object that "close to standard" is vague, or that strict requirements burden small operators. Vague is exactly what existing accessibility law already requires, and courts enforce it. As for small operators, exemptions historically became loopholes. The proposition does not permit them, and neither should this debate.

[C1.1] The motion sounds like equality, but it actually imposes a rigid wait-time mandate that will divert scarce resources from the riders who need them most. The right question is not whether wheelchair users deserve reliable service—they plainly do—but whether a blanket "close to standard" requirement is the best way to deliver it. It is not.
[C1.2] First, comparable wait times across fixed-route transit mean every route and every spare bus must be wheelchair-ready at all times. For a small or mid-sized agency, that is not a paperwork change. Accessible vehicles cost more and require more maintenance, so agencies forced to spread them across every route will cut frequency or drop low-ridership routes. The result can be worse mobility: the accessible bus now comes every hour instead of every thirty minutes, or the route disappears entirely.
[C1.3] That harms disabled and non-disabled riders alike.
[C1.4] Second, the ride-hail portion is even less workable. Drivers are generally independent contractors who cannot be commanded to buy specialty vehicles. If platforms must guarantee wheelchair-accessible vehicle wait times comparable to ordinary cars, they will not magically create a large accessible fleet; they will shrink service areas, raise prices, or exit smaller markets. That shrinks access rather than expanding it.
[C1.5] Third, the motion rejects the more flexible approach already built into disability law: complementary paratransit paired with steadily upgraded fixed-route service. Its "close to standard" language also invites years of litigation over what counts as close, standard, or convenient, while actual improvements stall.
[C1.6] Cost is not a shield; it is a budget. Every dollar spent running largely empty accessible buses on sparse routes is a dollar not spent on paratransit trips, station elevators, or subsidized wheelchair-accessible rides. The humane target is maximum real mobility, not symbolic wait-time parity.

[P4.3] A2: The motion does not require overnight fleet replacement. It requires wait times and convenience comparable to standard service. [P4.4] ADA paratransit itself launched on exactly that model — phased, deadline-driven, non-optional. Calling that "a different, weaker rule" is precisely backwards; it is the rule. Voluntary phase-ins are exactly what built the present two-tier system the proposition targets for replacement.
[P4.5] The decisive reason this debate resolves for PRO: every "flexible, funded" alternative the opponent offers is, in practice, a permission slip to defer. The proposition forecloses that deferral by making accessibility the default rather than a chronic aspiration. With a mandate in force, the budget conversation inside agencies and platforms shifts from "should we do this?" to "how do we deliver it?" — and on every prior accessibility mandate in living memory, that second question reaches a fundamentally different answer than the one the country is living with now.
[P4.6] Equality built into the system beats equality left to the goodwill of operators who have already shown, in plain evidence on every inaccessible corner, exactly where their goodwill ends.
