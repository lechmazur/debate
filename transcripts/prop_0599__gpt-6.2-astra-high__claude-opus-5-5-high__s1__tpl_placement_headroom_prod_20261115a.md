[P1.1] A retired worker who returns home across a border should not have to travel back just to prove to a pension office that she is the person on its books. She earned the pension; what she lacks is proof the office can accept. A national digital ID cannot solve that problem by itself. Recognition requires agreement on who can issue a credential, what it certifies, and how another country can check it.
[P1.2] The African Union should set those terms, not collect everyone’s records. Under a common framework, a local office could verify the pensioner’s identity in person and give her a digitally signed attestation the pension office can authenticate. Her pension file stays where it is. The same principle works within borders: a parent proving a child’s age for school need not hand over a copy of a household register.
[P1.3] Paper credentials verifiable offline and a staffed appeals process should be part of the standard, so a lost phone or a misspelled name does not become a denial of service.
[P1.4] Surveillance is a real cost. Even without a central database, authorities could scan credentials at checkpoints and log encounters that once left no trace. That is a reason to prohibit a common tracking number across services, audit participating agencies, and sanction unauthorized collection. It is not a reason to leave each agency demanding whatever documents it likes.
[P1.5] Even if those safeguards only partly work, the choice is not between risk and safety. Fragmented systems already expose personal records while charging people in fares, lost wages, and missed payments to prove the same fact twice. A framework that lets proof travel instead of requiring people to travel is worth the residual risk.
