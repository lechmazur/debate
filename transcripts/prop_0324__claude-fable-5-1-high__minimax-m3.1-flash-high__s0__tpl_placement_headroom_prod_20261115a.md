[P1.1] Place identity is not what a city shows the world for seventeen days. It is what residents recognize as theirs, year after year, without being asked. That is the test both models must pass, and only one does.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. Identity forms through repetition and ownership. A recurring festival run by local musicians, cooks, or artists embeds itself in calendars, childhoods, and neighborhood routines. The twentieth edition means something the first never could, because people have grown up inside it.
[P1.3] Edinburgh's Fringe began as uninvited local companies performing on the margins; today it defines the city more than any single sanctioned spectacle ever has. Notting Hill Carnival, Oktoberfest, Pamplona's running of the bulls, Fête de la Musique: none was launched as a prestige project, and all are now inseparable from their places.
[P1.4] Mega-events run the opposite logic. They are addressed outward, to broadcasters and dignitaries, and they are structurally temporary. Athens, Montreal, and Rio each inherited stadiums that now sit underused or decaying, along with debt that outlived the memory of the games. The branding was rented; the identity never transferred, because residents were spectators to their own city's performance.
[P1.5] Risk is the second difference. A single mega-event concentrates a public budget on one date and one outcome. If it rains, if a contractor collapses, if the political climate shifts, the entire investment evaporates. A portfolio of community events spreads that money across dozens of experiments, lets weak ones die cheaply, and lets strong ones compound.
[P1.6] Public institutions are stewards of scarce funds, and compounding returns beat lottery tickets.
[P1.7] Finally, authorship matters. When creators lead, the event reflects what a place actually is rather than what consultants decided it should project. Residents defend what they built. Nobody volunteers to maintain a velodrome.
[P1.8] So the motion asks only for a preference, and the preference is clear: fund the things people will still be doing in thirty years.

[C1.1] Recurring, creator-led events can create continuity and participation, but institutions should not prefer their form simply because it is local or repeated. The decisive variables are who gains control, whether residents share the narrative, and what legacy remains; these do not map neatly onto event size or duration.
[C1.2] The opponents’ examples blur their distinction. Edinburgh’s Fringe is now a large tourism industry, while recurring carnivals and beer festivals can be professionalized and marketed to visitors. Repetition does not guarantee grassroots ownership. Nor does local authorship guarantee inclusion: an established festival may celebrate one neighborhood’s habits as the whole city, alienating workers, newcomers, or residents who do not recognize themselves in it.
[C1.3] A recurring institution can reproduce exclusion as effectively as belonging.
[C1.4] Conversely, a one-off mega-event can create a shared civic reference point that later festivals inherit. Barcelona’s 1992 Olympics became shorthand for urban transformation and offered residents a common story, even though the spectacle was temporary. The issue was not its duration, but whether civic participation shaped its meaning and whether infrastructure and memories continued to serve place identity. A locally meaningful, well-governed event may outperform a permanently commercialized festival in authenticity and legacy.
[C1.5] Risk is not decisive either. Recurring events require promotion, management, security, venues, and subsidies; they are not always cheap experiments that can simply “die.” A major event may leave parks, transit, arts capacity, or a civic organization. Conversely, a festival can lose value when sponsorship or neighborhood demographics change.
[C1.6] Institutional choices should therefore turn on participation, representation, and transferred legacy, not a categorical ranking of formats. Support recurring community creativity when it is genuinely participatory; support a major event when it creates durable benefits and shared ownership. The proposition substitutes frequency for legitimacy and localism for inclusion.

[C2.1] The claim that format “predicts” outcomes is precisely what PRO has not established. The examples offered show that some recurring festivals became beloved and some mega-events left wasteful legacies; they do not prove that repetition causes inclusion or that size prevents it. Indeed, Barcelona concedes the mechanism PRO claims matters: the spectacle succeeded because residents and civic organizations gave it meaning. A funded mega-event can therefore create a shared reference point without displacing local creativity.
[C2.2] PRO also turns inclusion into a contest with an implausible winner. Funding “twenty events” does not guarantee representative programming. It may divide attention, reward established organizers, and produce a competitive calendar in which commercially successful events capture the narrative. Recurring events have cumulative advantages precisely because repetition creates familiarity; that can deepen inclusion, but it can also make one group’s habits appear synonymous with the whole city. “Creator-led” does not mean participatory: nonprofit elites, professional associations, and commercial operators can all be local creators.
[C2.3] The cost comparison is equally overstated. A dead festival does not leave “a vacant weekend” when public grants have already subsidized venues, marketing, security, and organizational capacity. Recurring commitments create path dependence: organizers expect next year’s support, and institutions face political pressure to renew it. A one-off event, by contrast, has a defined end and can be evaluated before permanent commitments replace it.
[C2.4] Nor is mega-event exclusion inherent. Publicly planned events can include free local programming, neighborhood representation, accessible public spaces, and participatory cultural programming. Their temporary nature can even permit experiments without locking a city into a permanent identity brand.
[C2.5] Public institutions should prefer recurring community events only when they are demonstrably participatory, representative, and fiscally sustainable. Neither “local” nor “recurring” guarantees those conditions. The responsible institutional rule is not to substitute a preferred format for evidence, but to refuse the categorical ranking PRO demands.
