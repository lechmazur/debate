Execution does not restore victims, and imprisonment does not trivialize them. Life without parole can express society’s strongest condemnation through permanent incarceration. CON says society must not “grant a life” to murderers, but abolition grants nothing: it refuses to let the state take a life when permanent imprisonment already delivers accountability.
[P2.3] A moral boundary can be drawn by a sentence; it need not be written in another death.

That is an argument for communicating restrictions, monitoring, and securely managed imprisonment—not proof that killing is necessary. Otherwise, their logic extends to any prisoner suspected of influencing others, including people never convicted of a capital crime. Execution then becomes punishment for a forecast of future dangerousness, precisely the expandable power abolition prevents.
[P2.5] If secure custody works, execution is redundant; if prison authorities cannot control communications, the remedy is to fix custody, not kill every prisoner who might exploit its failures.
[P2.6] On deterrence, CON effectively concedes the weakness: the evidence is “debated,” so they retreat to an “unmistakable message.” But a message is not a demonstrated prevented atrocity. CON cannot justify irreversible killings with the possibility that “at least some” unknown offenders might respond differently.
[P2.7] Finally, calling wrongful executions “exceedingly rare” does not make them correctable. “Overwhelming evidence” and exhaustive appeals are judgments made by the same fallible institutions, often amid exceptional pressure. Appeals can free a living prisoner; they cannot revive anyone. CON asks us to accept that risk to purchase symbolism and speculative deterrence. Permanent imprisonment protects the public, condemns the crime, and preserves correction.
