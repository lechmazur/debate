[P1.1] There is a strange kind of copyright debate in which the rule designed to nurture culture is, in practice, erasing it. That is the clash this motion targets, and it deserves to be won on its narrow, precise terms: nonprofit digital libraries should be exempt from traditional copyright enforcement when they act to preserve humanity's digital heritage.
[P1.2] Digital heritage is not like physical heritage. A book on a shelf survives whether or not anyone reads it. A web page survives only if someone chooses to copy it. When a domain expires, a server is decommissioned, a format is abandoned, or a rights-holder disappears, the work is simply gone, often irreversibly.
[P1.3] The practical effect of enforcing copyright against nonprofit preservation efforts in this environment is not the protection of creators. It is the deletion of record.
[P1.4] Libraries have always occupied a privileged position in copyright law because the alternative, locking knowledge behind paywalls and the luck of survival, is worse for the public. The Internet Archive and its peers are doing for the born-digital and digitized record what the great research libraries did for print. Their controlled lending model is a deliberate analog to physical borrowing: one copy serves one reader at a time, the market is not displaced, and orphan or out-of-print works, the bulk of what is at risk, find readers they otherwise never would.
[P1.5] Two further points should be conceded early so the debate can move forward. First, the exemption is justified only for nonprofits acting in a preservation capacity, not for commercial re-distribution dressed up as archiving. Second, authors and publishers retain every meaningful commercial right: first publication, exclusive windows, paid licensing, derivative markets. What they lose is the ability to veto, indefinitely, the nonprofit preservation of works they have already chosen to make available, and which would otherwise vanish.
[P1.6] The question is therefore not whether copyright matters. It is whether copyright should be the instrument that determines which parts of human cultural memory survive. The motion says no, not when a nonprofit library is willing [clipped]

[C1.5] It destroys that balance entirely. It turns every nonprofit digital archive into an unaccountable gatekeeper that gets final say over what work survives, and in what form, over the objection of the very people who made it. That is not how libraries worked. That is not how heritage should work.
