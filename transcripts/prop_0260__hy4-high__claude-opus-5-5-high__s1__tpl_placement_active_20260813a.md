[P1.1] The central harm is not that advertising exists; it is that ads and trackers are imposed before meaningful consent. Someone who looks up a medical symptom, checks a bank balance, or reads about addiction can be followed by invisible third parties into news sites, games, and apps. No ordinary user can identify those actors, audit code, or renegotiate after each update. Pop-up consent and buried settings make refusal homework, not a real choice.
[P1.2] A legal duty to offer robust, opt-out ad and tracker blocking is the narrow remedy. It does not ban ads, subscriptions, or contextual and personalized ads chosen freely. It requires browsers and operating systems to give every user a clear, maintained control over ads and cross-context tracking. Opt-out preserves liberty: ads can still be allowed, but refusal of ads and tracking must be technically effective.
[P1.3] System-level coverage matters because partial tools leave predictable holes. Browser extensions miss native apps, OS ad IDs, background calls, and identifier tricks; OS settings miss web scripts, pixels, fingerprinting, and link decoration. A duty on both sets one floor: ad domains, tracker IDs, fingerprinting aids, and cross-context sharing are blocked after refusal, with narrow exceptions for core service and user-approved first-party ties.
[P1.4] Voluntary privacy fails because the gatekeepers are conflicted. Vendors profit from ads, app ecosystems, data partnerships, and publisher goodwill; controls can be hidden, fragmented, weakened, or wrapped in dark patterns. Extensions demand expertise and leave children and ordinary users exposed. Law makes refusal a standard product feature and stops firms profiting from confusing privacy.
[P1.5] Ad-funded content does not require taking data or attention before permission. Contextual ads, first-party relationships, subscriptions, and genuine opt-ins remain available. A model that depends on covert tracking and unavoidable ads is not free; it is an unpriced tax on autonomy, security, and time. A robust opt-out lets the device owner see that cost and decide.
[P1.6] That is what makes a personal computer personal.

[C1.1] The harm PRO describes is surveillance: someone searches a symptom and gets followed. The remedy PRO defends is something else entirely: a legal mandate that every browser and operating system block ads by default for everyone. That mismatch decides this debate.
[C1.2] First, the motion kills the very alternatives PRO promises survive. "Opt-out" means blocking is on unless the user switches it off. PRO says contextual ads remain available [P1.2], but a system-level ad blocker does not ask whether an ad is contextual. It blocks the banner on a local newspaper that knows nothing about you, just as it blocks the cross-site tracker.
[C1.3] Defaults decide outcomes: most people never change settings. So the realistic result is that ad revenue for the open web collapses overnight, and the readers who cannot afford ten subscriptions lose access first. A "free choice" engineered so that almost no one chooses ads is not neutral. It is a funding cutoff by statute.
[C1.4] Second, the motion hands the ad market to the gatekeepers PRO calls conflicted [P1.4]. Someone must decide, at the system level, what counts as an "ad" or a "tracker." That someone is Apple, Google, and Microsoft. PRO's own exception for "user-approved first-party ties" [P1.3] is the loophole: giant platforms where you are logged in all day keep their first-party data and in-house ads, while the independent publisher relying on third-party ad networks gets blocked.
[C1.5] We have already seen platform privacy changes that hurt rival ad businesses while the platform's own advertising grew. PRO's law makes that dynamic mandatory and universal.
[C1.6] Third, "robust" system-level blocking is a blunt instrument. Fraud detection, payment verification, analytics that fix broken pages, and accessibility tools routinely rely on scripts that look like tracking. A legally mandated blocklist on every device breaks things unpredictably, and users blame the site, not the law.
[C1.7] The better answer targets the actual harm: ban the sale of sensitive data, enforce universal do-not-track signals, outlaw fingerprinting. Regulate surveillance directly, and leave a free, ad-supported web standing.
