[P1.1] When a phone's battery degrades after two years, the owner faces a choice engineered by someone else: pay the manufacturer's authorised centre, or throw the device away. Independent technicians — the shops in every Indian market that already replace screens and batteries daily — are locked out by paired parts, glued casings, and service manuals that simply aren't sold. Ownership that ends at the receipt isn't ownership.
[P1.2] This is a structural market failure, not a complaint about bad service. The firm that profits from your next purchase controls whether your current one survives. India's voluntary right-to-repair portal asks manufacturers to publish manuals; it cannot compel them, and the incentives run the other way. Only law changes the incentive.
[P1.3] And note what's already true: repair happens anyway, in a grey market of scavenged parts and guesswork. The choice isn't repair versus no repair — it is licensed, documented, warrantable repair versus the improvised kind.
[P1.4] The proposition is narrow and workable. Access to parts, tools and documentation on fair commercial terms — not free intellectual property, not source code, not a mandate to publish chip designs. Manufacturers may charge for parts and recover reasonable costs. Genuine safety limits, like lithium cells or high-voltage assemblies, justify training requirements, not blanket refusal.
[P1.5] Security concerns justify authenticated pairing, not permanent exclusivity. Europe and several American states have already written versions of these rules; India would be adapting, not inventing.
[P1.6] Weigh what follows: households stretching a phone from two years to five, sharply reduced e-waste, and hundreds of thousands of repair livelihoods made legal instead of precarious. Against that, my opponent must defend a manufacturer's veto over your property after you've paid for it. Ask for the principle justifying that veto — because 'innovation' has never required keeping a screwdriver out of the owner's hands.

[C1.2] The motion is silent.
[C1.3] Its breadth is fatal. “Consumer electronics” covers feature phones, televisions, wearables, and connected cameras, with radically different risks and repair economics. A uniform obligation means deciding which parts must be stocked, for how long, which tools are necessary, and what documentation suffices. Those engineering judgments become litigation. Global firms can absorb compliance teams; small Indian assemblers and importers face higher fixed costs, fewer models, or exit.
[C1.4] Consumers pay through higher prices and reduced choice, especially buyers of inexpensive devices.
[C1.5] Nor does access create competent repair. Supplying genuine parts does not license technicians, guarantee workmanship, or allocate liability. [C1.6] Nor is security cured by “authenticated pairing” when the state compels wider distribution of tools and credentials. PRO’s grey-market dilemma is false: authorised inputs alone do not produce safe, accountable repair.
[C1.7] The environmental dividend is also speculative. Compulsory production and years of inventory for obsolete models can create waste, while uneconomic repairs still end in replacement. India should punish deceptive warranty restrictions, challenge abusive tying, and adopt product-specific rules where repair is demonstrably safe and worthwhile. A blanket entitlement sacrifices safety, security, affordability, and innovation for a slogan.
[C1.8] That is not ownership protected; it is product design nationalised without a workable governing rule.
