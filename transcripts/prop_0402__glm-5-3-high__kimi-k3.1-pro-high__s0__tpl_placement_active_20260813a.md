[P1.1] A voucher is a rent payment. That is the heart of this debate. When a tenant hands a landlord a check drawn on public funds, the money is green, it clears, and it arrives — often more reliably than wages do. Rejecting that tenant is not a business judgment about income; it is a judgment about the *source* of income, which happens to signal poverty, disability, family status, or race.
[P1.2] The proposition simply says landlords can't use that signal as a proxy when the tenant is otherwise qualified.
[P1.3] Notice what the motion does not do. It does not force landlords to accept unqualified tenants. It does not set rents. It does not cap deposits or remove screening for credit, references, or lease history.
[P1.4] It removes exactly one ground for exclusion: "I won't take your money because of where it comes from." That is a narrow, principled rule.
[P1.5] The mechanism of harm is well understood. In tight rental markets, voucher holders — disproportionately Black mothers, people with disabilities, and families with children — apply to dozens of units and get screened out before any individualized assessment occurs. The voucher then expires unused, not because the family failed to qualify, but because the market refused lawful money. Public assistance that can't be spent isn't assistance.
[P1.6] We've already seen this play out with food assistance and employment discrimination: courts and legislatures have long recognized that targeting the *source* of lawful funds is a form of discrimination against the people who hold them.
[P1.7] And the countervailing interest is thin. Landlords' real concerns — delayed inspections, bureaucratic paperwork, payment gaps — are about program administration, and they can be negotiated or regulated directly. What they cannot justify is a categorical refusal of a tenant who meets every other criterion. We don't let landlords refuse paychecks from certain employers; lawful income is lawful income.
[P1.8] The line is simple: judge tenants by whether they can pay, not by how they pay. That is all this rule requires.

[C1.1] A voucher can make rent more reliable without making every tenancy equally acceptable. PRO treats a years-long housing arrangement like a shop refusing a banknote. Yet voucher tenancies can involve separate agency contracts, inspections before move-in, program-specific lease terms, and approval delays outside the landlord’s control. A paycheck from an unpopular employer imposes none of those obligations.
[C1.2] Payment source and legal relationship cannot always be separated. Reliable rent matters; it is not the whole economics of a lease.
[C1.3] That creates a dilemma PRO’s “otherwise qualified” slogan hides. If landlords may refuse because of those administrative burdens, the promised protection is easily evaded: object to the program, not the tenant. If they may not, the motion compels participation in that program even when a small landlord cannot reasonably absorb an empty unit during approval delays while mortgage, tax, and repair bills continue. Calling the tenant “qualified” simply defines away the landlord’s objection.
[C1.4] Nor is every refusal a proxy for race, disability, or family status. Hostility toward voucher holders is wrong, but unwillingness to assume additional contractual burdens is not the same thing. Treating them as interchangeable replaces individualized business judgment with a mandate whose costs fall heavily on small owners.
[C1.5] The predictable response is not automatically more housing access. Landlords can tighten the credit and history screening PRO expressly preserves, choose applicants before voucher status is known, or leave the rental market. The rule itself creates no inspectors, shortens no approval timelines, and fills no payment gaps. Its burdens can therefore reduce supply while its protection is avoided through lawful proxies.
[C1.6] If vouchers expire unused, direct reforms—prompt inspections, clear timelines, dependable payments, and participation incentives—attack the actual bottleneck. This proposition instead either permits the administrative refusals PRO condemns or forbids legitimate ones. On either reading, it is too blunt to justify.
