[P1.1] A teenager in crisis does not need a black-market dealer. He needs an unlocked nightstand. That is the ordinary path to youth firearm suicide, to the accidental shot that kills a younger sibling, and to guns taken from home for later violence. Safe-storage laws target that path.
[P1.2] They do not confiscate or ban carry. They require that a lawful owner's firearm not sit unsecured where a child can reach it.
[P1.3] Impulse is the mechanism. Adolescent suicide is often a brief, reversible crisis. A gun compresses it into seconds. Locked storage inserts a delay a child cannot beat and an adult can.
[P1.4] Unintentional injuries follow curiosity, not crime. Both fall when access falls. Child-access-prevention rules have been associated with fewer youth firearm suicides and accidental deaths while adult ownership continues. The law does not have to be perfect.
[P1.5] It has to change the default in homes where children live.
[P1.6] The burden is thinner than opponents need. Owners remain armed. Quick-access safes exist. A cable lock costs less than a box of ammunition.
[P1.7] The home invasion in which two extra seconds decide everything is vanishingly rare next to children living in gun-owning homes every day. Most defensive use does not require an unlocked loaded pistol within a child's reach. We already require locks on cars, lids on medicine, fences around pools. Storage is that same duty for a more lethal object.
[P1.8] Criminals will ignore this statute as they ignore others. The law is not for them. It is for the parent who meant to lock the gun and did not, and the neighbor whose child visits. Youth deaths and injuries are a concentrated, irreversible harm.
[P1.9] The cost to lawful ownership is a change in habit, not a loss of the right. On that comparison, safe-storage laws reduce the harm more than they burden the owners.
