[C1.2] First, this is not preservation in the ordinary sense. Film archives do not demand that studios keep projection booths running; libraries preserve books without forcing authors to maintain reading rooms. If online games are culture, fund public archives and create legal exceptions for abandoned software. Do not conscript every publisher into permanent custodianship of every dead title.
[C1.3] The proposition collapses “society should remember” into “the company must enable access,” and those are different obligations.
[C1.4] Second, private servers are not harmless time capsules. They require distributing server code or reverse-engineered substitutes, often exposing anti-cheat, account systems, and user data. [C1.5] Archival modes and mod support carry the same tail: compatibility work, customer support, IP licensing, and endless patches for hardware that no longer exists. “Costs little” is true only if someone else absorbs the risk.
[C1.6] Third, the paid-ownership argument proves too much. A concert ticket or a season pass does not entitle buyers to run the venue. Publishers should be transparent about sunset terms; they should not be required to hand over operational control. Voluntary preservation is welcome—Blizzard’s own WoW Classic shows an owner preserving on its own terms, with quality and legal control.
[C1.7] A universal “should” is different.
[C1.8] The real choice is not culture versus deletion. It is whether preservation is a public responsibility or a private mandate. The motion chooses the latter, and that is why it fails.
