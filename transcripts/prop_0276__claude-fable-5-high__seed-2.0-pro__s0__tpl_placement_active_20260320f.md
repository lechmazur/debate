[P1.1] When an airline cancels your flight because it failed to staff a crew, it knows three things instantly: that the delay was its fault, what you paid, and what you're owed. The current system pretends otherwise. It forces the passenger—stranded, exhausted, often mid-trip—to find the right form, document the cause, dispute the airline's self-serving classification, and wait weeks for an answer the carrier always had. That friction isn't an accident.
[P1.2] It's a business model.
[P1.3] The core argument is simple: complaint-heavy processes convert legal obligations into optional ones. Airlines know that most passengers never file, and that many who do give up after the first denial. Every abandoned claim is pure savings, which means the airline is financially rewarded for making the process painful. Automatic compensation removes that perverse incentive.
[P1.4] When payouts happen by default, the only way to avoid them is to actually run the airline well—staff adequately, maintain aircraft, build realistic schedules. Compensation stops being a customer-service afterthought and becomes a price signal on operational failure.
[P1.5] This isn't speculative. Europe has operated a compensation regime for controllable disruptions for nearly two decades, and increasingly carriers there pay through streamlined or automatic channels. Aviation did not collapse; budget carriers thrived. What changed is that delay became expensive for the party that controls it, which is exactly where the cost belongs.
[P1.6] Today, a missed connection costs the passenger a hotel, a lost workday, a missed wedding—while the airline that caused it often pays nothing unless petitioned.
[P1.7] Note the proposal's careful limits: it covers controllable delays—crew shortages, maintenance lapses, scheduling failures—not weather or air traffic control. Airlines already make this distinction internally for their own accounting. They can make it for passengers too.
[P1.8] The question this debate turns on is who should bear the burden of an airline's own failure: the company with the data, the lawyers, and the fault—or the traveler sleeping on the terminal floor. Automaticity simply puts accountability where the control is.
