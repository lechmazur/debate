[P1.1] When a school pays an athlete directly out of its broadcast and ticket revenue, the employment relationship already exists. The only question left is whether its terms are negotiated across a table or imposed from an office down the hall.
[P1.2] The NIL-and-compliance patchwork was built for a different world: third-party endorsements, autograph signings, the local car dealer. It was never designed to administer a salary system, yet that is what it has become. Major programs are now distributing on the order of twenty million dollars a year to athletes through a framework with no uniform national rules, conflicting state statutes, and a compliance bureaucracy that answers to the athletic department itself. The result is chaos by design: tampering dressed as recruiting, contracts nobody can reliably enforce, and a fresh antitrust lawsuit every season.
[P1.3] Collective bargaining fixes the actual defect: the total absence of an athlete voice. Every rule shaping a player's life — transfer windows, roster limits, practice loads, medical coverage after injury, how the revenue split is divided among sports — is decided unilaterally by the people whose financial interest runs the other way. A negotiated agreement converts one-sided power into enforceable terms: guaranteed health benefits, injury protection, multi-year security, transparent pay. That is not radical; it is how every other labor market treats the people who generate the revenue.
[P1.4] Note the resolution is narrow. It applies only where schools already share revenue directly — programs already behaving like employers. Formalizing that relationship changes everything on paper and nothing in reality; it concedes what is already true.
[P1.5] Schools win too. A bargained cap with standardized contracts, shielded from antitrust attack the way negotiated labor terms are, finally gets college sports off the litigation treadmill that has destroyed every other attempt at control. Professional leagues learned this decades ago.
[P1.6] You cannot pay someone a salary on Monday and deny them a voice on Tuesday.
