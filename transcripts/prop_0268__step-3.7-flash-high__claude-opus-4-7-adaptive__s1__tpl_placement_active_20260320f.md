[P1.1] Shrinkflation is a silent, regressive tax on shoppers, and the current rule letting supermarkets and food apps hide size reductions behind unchanged headline prices and fine-print unit pricing is a failure of basic consumer protection. Right now, a busy shopper grabbing their usual 12-ounce cereal box will likely not notice it now holds only 10.5 ounces, that the shelf tag’s tiny, low-contrast unit price line is nearly unreadable, or that their go-to food app defaults to sorting by nominal shelf price, not per-ounce value.
[P1.2] This information asymmetry is no accident: retailers rely on shoppers’ habit and inattention to slip through size cuts that would spark backlash if paired with a visible headline price hike.
[P1.3] The harm falls hardest on the people least able to absorb it: low-income households stretching food budgets, elderly shoppers with visual impairments who cannot parse fine print, and families who cannot afford to waste money on worse-value products. A parent buying four boxes of what they assume is the same cereal they purchased a month prior is effectively paying 15% more per ounce with no clear, upfront warning that the product changed.
[P1.4] Our proposed requirement is minimal, low-cost, and aligned with existing consumer rights norms: retailers and app operators must display size reductions and corresponding unit price increases prominently, alongside the headline price, both in-store and on digital platforms. They already track this data for supply chain and pricing purposes; the only added burden is surfacing it where shoppers can actually see it. This rule does not ban shrinkflation—it simply gives shoppers the information they need to make informed choices, rather than being tricked into paying more for less.

[C1.1] The motion sounds like a free lunch: just "surface" information retailers already have. It isn't. [C1.2] Start with the tool that already exists: unit pricing. Per-ounce, per-100g, per-sheet labels are mandated or standard across most major markets precisely so that a size cut shows up as a higher unit price. If the complaint is that those labels are too small or too faint, the honest fix is enforcing legibility on the existing rule — not bolting on a second, parallel "shrinkflation flag" regime. PRO is solving a known problem twice and calling the duplication consumer protection.
[C1.3] Second, the motion targets the wrong actor. Supermarkets and apps don't shrink packages — manufacturers do, often quietly, across tens of thousands of SKUs, with rolling transitions where old and new sizes sit side by side for months. Forcing the retailer to detect, verify, and prominently flag every supplier-side size change is a serious compliance burden, and the cost lands on shelf prices. A rule sold as protecting low-income shoppers ends up taxing their grocery bill.
[C1.4] Third, define "prominent" and "recent." For how long does a product wear a shrinkflation badge — a month, a year, forever? What about quality downgrades, recipe changes, or stealth size *increases* that mask price hikes elsewhere? Any honest version of this rule either explodes in scope or becomes an arbitrary sticker that shoppers learn to tune out, drowning the unit price that actually matters.
[C1.5] Finally, the market already punishes shrinkflation loudly — viral posts, news segments, store-brand competition. Adding a clunky mandate on top buys little extra vigilance and risks information fatigue.
[C1.6] The clash is simple: enforce the unit price we already have, or layer a vague new mandate on top that costs more and informs less. CON picks the first.
