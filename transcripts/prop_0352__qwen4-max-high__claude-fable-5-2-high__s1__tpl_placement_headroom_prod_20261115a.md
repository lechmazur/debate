[P1.1] Concentration is not gravity. It is the accumulated result of choices: where we routed highways, sited universities, aimed research funding, and wrote housing and mortgage rules. When governments spend decades building metropolitan advantage and then call the resulting map inevitable, they are laundering their own decisions as physics. The question is whether we keep doing that, or start counting the costs.
[P1.2] Decline is self-reinforcing, which is why markets will not fix it. A rural county loses its hospital; young families who might have stayed now face an hour's drive for a delivery room; the school consolidates; the tax base shrinks; the next employer looks elsewhere. No private actor captures the value of being the last clinic standing, so the efficient-looking outcome is a spiral, not an equilibrium. Economists call this cumulative causation.
[P1.3] Calling it destiny is just refusing to intervene in a process that intervention could reverse.
[P1.4] And the metro premium is smaller than the headline. A wage that looks far higher can become a loss once rent, commuting, and childcare are subtracted; for workers without degrees, the richest cities often deliver the least. Meanwhile the pipes, roads, school buildings, and housing stock of small towns are already built and half-idle. The marginal public dollar buys more usable capacity there than in a city where every addition requires bidding against scarcity.
[P1.5] "Do more" does not mean freezing every village in amber or subsidizing a factory that no one needs. It means broadband, clinics, community colleges, and permitting that lets a small town actually add housing and businesses.
[P1.6] So CON must defend one of two claims: that this spiral is genuinely efficient once costs are counted, or that nothing governments try can bend it. Both are hard. The map we have is the map we paid for — and we can pay for a different one.
