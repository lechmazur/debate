[C1.2] Start with opportunity cost. Every billion spent boring a tunnel is a billion not spent on surface transit, grid modernization, water mains, or housing. A single urban tunnel project can consume the capital budget of an entire mid-sized city for a decade. The question isn't tunnel versus nothing—it's tunnel versus dozens of surface interventions that collectively move more people, serve more neighborhoods, and come online in years rather than decades.
[C1.3] PRO calls the boring cost a "sticker price." But the opportunity cost of tying up capital for fifteen years before a single passenger rides is real money too.
[C1.4] Second, tunneling carries hidden costs PRO omits entirely. Geotechnical risk is the most notorious budget-killer in infrastructure. You don't know what's underground until you're there—buried utilities, fault zones, water table surprises. The Big Dig, which PRO cites approvingly, is the most famous cost overrun in American history—not a model.
[C1.5] Boston didn't prove tunnels are cheap; it proved they're unpredictable.
[C1.6] Third, the "learning by doing" argument is circular. Madrid's cost performance reflects specific institutional conditions—continuous programs, standardized designs, experienced crews—that most jurisdictions cannot replicate by simply spending more. Telling a city with no tunneling industry to "invest heavily" and expect Madrid-level costs is like telling a non-swimmer to jump in deeper water to learn faster. The costs stay high because institutional capacity doesn't exist, and heavy upfront spending doesn't create it overnight.
[C1.7] Finally, the necessity claim is overstated. Surface disruption is a planning failure, not a physical law. Dedicated bus lanes, road pricing, distributed utility networks, and smart grid management address the same constraints at a fraction of the cost. The proposition conflates "dense cities need solutions" with "dense cities need tunnels."
[C1.8] Those aren't the same claim.
