[P1.1] When you buy a physical book, concert shirt, or paper ticket, a basic legal principle applies: once you pay for it, you can give it to a friend or sell it if your plans change. The shift from paper tickets to rotating digital barcodes should not quietly erase that basic consumer freedom. Today, dominant ticketing platforms use technological locks to strip buyers of ownership, turning a purchased ticket into a revocable license trapped inside a single company’s app. If a fan gets sick, faces a family emergency, or simply buys four tickets for a group arriving separately, they should have a legal right to transfer those tickets peer-to-peer without begging permission from a corporate gatekeeper.
[P1.2] Ticketing giants claim transfer restrictions protect fans from industrial scalpers, but their actual business model tells a different story. Frequently, platforms forbid open peer-to-peer transfers or sales on competing marketplaces while encouraging resale inside their own proprietary exchanges, where they collect steep fees a second time on the exact same seat. Blanket transfer locks are rarely about stopping price gouging; they are about walling off rival platforms, forcing every recipient to surrender personal data, and extracting duplicate fees from captive consumers.
[P1.3] Crucially, a right paired with narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits already protects genuine efforts to keep live events affordable. When organizers truly want to stop commercial profiteering, they can cap resale prices at face value, block bulk bot purchases, or enforce strict quantity limits. Because those targeted safeguards remain completely legal, the threat of scalping cannot justify blanket digital lock-in. Opposing this right means defending sweeping, untailored restrictions whose only real function is to trap ordinary fans, suppress competing marketplaces, and block harmless transfers between friends.
[P1.4] Consumers who pay for a ticket deserve the legal right to transfer it.
