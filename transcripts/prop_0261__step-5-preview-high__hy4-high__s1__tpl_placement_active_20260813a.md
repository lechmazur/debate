[P1.1] When someone spends a lifetime buying books, albums, software, courses, or family photo archives, the payment is supposed to create a durable estate, not a revocable relationship. Yet today the word “purchase” often hides a non-transferable license. If the buyer dies, next of kin can lose thousands of items that functioned, economically and emotionally, like property for years. That outcome is not meaningful consumer choice; it is a private term of service quietly overriding the ordinary law of succession.
[P1.2] The principle is simple: once a consumer has paid for a digital file or access right, estate law should guarantee that the item can pass to next of kin. This does not mean heirs receive passwords, accounts, or the right to rummage through private messages. It means the executor can identify the purchased library after proof of death and deliver it to beneficiaries. Personal communications, browsing history, and unrelated cloud data remain protected.
[P1.3] Nor does this require stripping providers of copyright. They can retain ownership of the underlying work, enforce licensing limits, prevent simultaneous sharing, and administer transfer through their own systems. The entitlement is economic and practical: next of kin may read, listen, play, study, or use what the deceased bought. A license can be non-transferable as a contract term; it should not make a paid cultural estate vanish at death.
[P1.4] The stakes are concrete. A widow loses a family photo archive trapped in a vendor cloud. Children cannot continue their father’s paid language courses. A researcher’s purchased reference library disappears in the middle of estate administration.
[P1.5] A musician’s paid sample library cannot help the family finish the final record. The physical equivalents survive death and pass through the estate. There is no principled difference between a shelf and a server—only a contract clause.

[C1.1] Estate law is for passing what the decedent owned, not enlarging a licence after death. A non-transferable ebook, game library, course, or software seat is not a hidden crate of books behind a mean clause; it is a personal permission to use a file or service under ongoing technical and copyright constraints. The buyer paid for one person's access, not a perpetual family licence. Forcing the heir to receive it is not succession, but a compulsory new bargain with the provider and creators.
[C1.2] The shelf/server analogy [P1.5] misses the distinction. A physical book passes because the estate owns a durable copy and needs nothing more from the seller. A digital library may need authentication, DRM, migration, storage, updates, servers, and rights clearances that may expire. If a film licence ends or a course includes live support, an inheritance statute cannot guarantee use without ordering fresh performance.
[C1.3] Where the estate owns a durable local copy or device, property law already reaches it; the proposal only erases “non-transferable.”
[C1.4] That erasure has a bill. Guaranteed inheritability converts one-user licences into household assets. Student discounts, personal subscriptions, and cheap lifetime libraries would be priced and policed as cross-life rights; providers would recover through higher prices, tighter DRM, fewer downloads, or thinner catalogues. PRO frames non-transferability as “quietly overriding the ordinary law of succession” [P1.1], but it also lets consumers buy narrower, cheaper access instead of an estate licence they never wanted.
[C1.5] And the guaranteed beneficiary is wrong: next of kin, not necessarily someone the decedent trusted. A reading list, course history, game saves, annotations, or photo archive can expose illness, politics, sexuality, and other people's privacy. Wills, export tools, clear disclosure, and voluntary legacy designations allow chosen transfer; a guarantee instead turns silence, privacy, and contract limits into defeatable obstacles. The law should pass what people owned and chose to give, not turn permissions into property for next of kin.
