[P1.1] Cities face a simple reality: curb space is scarce, demand is surging, and giving it away for free guarantees it will be wasted. Dynamic pricing for delivery and ride-hail vehicles is not some radical experiment—it is the rational response to a broken status quo that already imposes enormous costs on urban life.
[P1.2] Consider what free curb space actually produces. Delivery drivers double-park because there is no open spot, blocking traffic lanes and bike infrastructure. Ride-hail vehicles circle the block or idle in travel lanes waiting for passengers. The result is cascading congestion, longer commute times, dangerous conditions for cyclists and pedestrians, and meaningful increases in local air pollution.
[P1.3] A recent federal analysis estimated that cruising and double-parking account for a significant share of downtown traffic in major cities—traffic that falls on every road user, not just the person who chose to park illegally.
[P1.4] Dynamic pricing solves this by making the cost of occupying scarce curb space visible in real time. When prices rise during peak hours, drivers make quicker stops, logistics companies shift deliveries to off-peak windows, and the total vehicle-minutes spent at the curb drops sharply. San Francisco's SFpark program, though focused on passenger vehicles, demonstrated this mechanism clearly: dynamic rates reduced cruising for parking, shortened average parking duration, and improved traffic flow. The same principle applies with even greater force to commercial vehicles, which are the primary source of curb congestion in dense urban cores.
[P1.5] Critics will object that pricing burdens small businesses or shifts costs onto consumers. But the status quo already imposes costs—on bus riders stuck behind a double-parked van, on cyclists forced into traffic, on residents breathing exhaust from idling vehicles. Those costs are invisible, unpriced, and distributed regressively. Dynamic pricing makes them transparent and lets market forces do what they do best: allocate scarce resources efficiently.
[P1.6] The question is not whether curb space is rationed—it always is. The question is whether we ration it through chaos and double-parking, or through a system that moves traffic, generates revenue, and serves the public interest.
