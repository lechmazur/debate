[P1.1] Peer review is the quality-control system for roughly three million papers a year, and we run it on volunteer labor and hidden reports. Imagine any other safety-critical industry—drug trials, structural engineering—where the inspectors work unpaid, on evenings, with no accountability for their judgments. We wouldn't accept it there, and we shouldn't accept it in science.
[P1.2] Payment first. Reviewers currently absorb real costs: hours of skilled labor, often from junior researchers who can least afford it. When something is free, it gets treated as worthless. Unpaid review invites the pathologies everyone complains about—cursory reports, months-long delays, reviews delegated to unqualified trainees, and refusals to review at all.
[P1.3] Journals charge libraries and authors billions collectively; the money exists. It simply stops before it reaches the people doing the actual gatekeeping. Paying reviewers says: this is professional work, do it professionally, and we can demand professional standards—deadlines, thoroughness, responsiveness—in exchange.
[P1.4] Anonymity is the second half of the problem. Reviewer reports decide which findings enter the scientific record, yet they are themselves unaudited. The public never sees whether a paper was rejected for genuine methodological flaws or because a rival wanted to delay a competitor's work. Published reports change that calculus completely.
[P1.5] A reviewer who knows their report will appear next to the paper writes for the record, not for the editor's eyes alone. Sloppy one-paragraph dismissals become reputationally costly. Reports also become citable scholarly contributions in their own right—readers learn why a paper's claims were softened, what alternatives were considered, what limitations were flagged.
[P1.6] The standard defense is that anonymity enables candor. But candor without accountability is just unaccountable power. Junior reviewers might fear retaliation—but a published report documents the reviewer's reasoning, and reputations are built by demonstrating good judgment publicly, just as they are in every other scholarly function. Open peer review has already been adopted by journals like those in the Frontiers family and f1000Research, and the sky has not fallen.
[P1.7] Paid, published, accountable review is professional review. Anything less is a system that asks for trust while refusing to earn [clipped]

[C1.2] Start with payment. A fee per review does not buy quality; it buys volume. Once reviewing becomes piecework, the rational move is to accept more assignments and turn them around fast—exactly the shallow, delegated, checkbox reviewing PRO condemns. We already see the shape of this: paper mills and fake review rings thrive precisely because someone's money is attached to an outcome.
[C1.3] Paying reviewers creates a constituency with an interest in more reviewing, more papers, more review rounds. And notice where the money goes: well-paid senior scholars will hoard the easy assignments while junior researchers—PRO's supposed beneficiaries—still do the grind, now at gig-work rates that insult the skill involved. What actually motivates careful review is service to a scholarly community and the quiet reciprocity of one's own papers being read carefully. Cash doesn't top that up; it replaces it.
[C1.4] When people stop volunteering for the local food bank and get hired at minimum wage, they don't become more devoted.
[C1.5] Now the named report. Anonymity is not a shield for the lazy; it is what lets a postdoc tell a professor their ANOVA is wrong. Fields are small, careers are long, and retaliation is quiet—a declined collaboration, an unfunded grant panel, a cold shoulder at conferences. PRO waves this away by saying published reports "document reasoning," but retaliation does not need a pretext, and a documented complaint is a named target.
[C1.6] The predictable effect: reviewers decline the hardest papers and soften verdicts on powerful authors. Refusal rates rise, and the work concentrates on the famous and the untouchable.
[C1.7] Worse, the two reforms compound. A paid, named reviewer is building a brand for a fee. Reports become CV items—confident, audience-facing, performatively tough on weak targets and diplomatically useless on strong ones. Editors lose the private candor they rely [clipped]

[P2.2] First, the piecework objection [C1.2]: pay per review allegedly buys speed and volume. But this describes a badly designed contract, not payment itself. Editors already control assignment; journals can and do tie compensation to accepted, completed reviews, just as courts pay expert reviewers for thorough work rather than per word. Compare the status quo honestly: today the "compensation" is a line on a service record that almost nobody checks.
[P2.3] That is the system currently producing three-week reports and ghost-written trainee reviews. Unpaid work is not more virtuous—it is simply unauditable, and it's the incentives CON says they fear, minus any lever to discipline them. A journal that pays can withhold payment for shoddy work. A journal that doesn't pay can withhold nothing.
[P2.4] Second, the junior-reviewer-fears-retaliation claim [C1.5]. Notice what anonymity actually protects in practice: the tenured professor who submits a lazy, dismissive review, and the rival who quietly sinks a competitor's paper. CON's postdoc scenario is real but asymmetric—the postdoc's protection is the *editor*, who knows who wrote what. Publishing the report does not eliminate that channel; it simply adds sunshine afterward.
[P2.5] And the evidence cuts against the fear: journals that publish reviewer names and reports have not seen review collapse. Senior people sign reviews there routinely. The catastrophized refusal rates never materialized.
[P2.6] Third, the compounding claim [C1.7]: paid, named review becomes branding. But contrast this with every other named, sometimes compensated scholarly function—commentaries, editorials, serving as discussant. Scholars manage to be useful in public constantly. The idea that only hidden, unpaid judgment is honest judgment is an insult dressed up as a defense of candor.
[P2.7] The deeper issue: CON offers no mechanism for accountability anywhere in their system. Their answer to every abuse is "trust the editor." That is precisely the arrangement that failed us—paper mills, fake reviews, decade-long delays. Detachment without oversight isn't disinterest; it's impunity.
