[P1.1] A food-delivery app does not merely offer options. It decides which one feels normal. Too often, checkout opens already on priority delivery, a stacked service fee, and a tip sized for the expensive path. Hungry and late, most people do not compare.
[P1.2] They hit the button attached to the fulfillment method that pays the platform most. That preset should more often be pickup or a lower-fee option. Premium delivery should be an explicit upgrade, not the path of least resistance.
[P1.3] Defaults stick because attention is scarce. Presets capture a large share of choices even when something better is one tap away. Food apps exploit that stickiness when comparison is least likely. A preselected window and a buried fee line do not reveal a preference.
[P1.4] They manufacture one. Anyone who needs the meal at the door can still choose it. They should not have to undo a design that assumes the costly path.
[P1.5] The costs are ordinary and repeated. A weeknight order can stack delivery, service, and small-cart fees on a marked-up menu price for food collectible nearby. Restaurants lose margin to commissions on orders that never needed a courier. Couriers absorb speed promises sold before anyone weighed the price.
[P1.6] Extra trips and packaging follow from the default, not from revealed need. Shifting the preset does not ban convenience. It stops charging inattention for it.
[P1.7] A default already decides whose interest governs the moment of least attention. An upgrade path is the more respectful design: speed must be affirmed, while money and proximity are not taxed for a missed opt-out. Express delivery and a labeled premium can stay. The silent assumption that the costliest fulfillment path is the normal one should not.
[P1.8] Steer the distracted buyer toward the cheaper, often sufficient option, and leave the costliest path one deliberate tap away. Default to the lower fee. Let the expensive fulfillment be chosen, not assumed.
