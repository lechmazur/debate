[P1.1] Fast fashion looks affordable only because the price tag lies.
[P1.2] A five-dollar shirt does not include poisoned rivers from dye runoff, microplastics shedding into water and food, mountains of barely worn clothes dumped in landfills here and in poorer countries, or the carbon of growing, sewing, and flying garments across the planet to be worn three times. Those costs are real, they are paid in health, cleanup, and climate damage, and they fall hardest on lower-income communities. A heavy production tax simply forces polluters to pay their own bill instead of dumping it on the public.
[P1.3] That matters because fast fashion is not designed to clothe people efficiently. It is designed to make clothes disposable, pushing dozens of micro-seasons to trigger overbuying and rapid disposal. Cheap stitching that warps after three washes is not a bargain; it is a treadmill that forces families to buy again and again. Taxing that model while subsidizing local, durable, lower-impact production reverses the incentive: make longevity cheap and waste expensive.
[P1.4] The objection about poor consumers gets the economics backwards. Low-income shoppers are already punished by this system — stuck with garments that cost more per wear, while breathing dirtier air and facing higher climate and waste costs. A tax-and-subsidy shift does not abandon them. It uses polluter revenue to scale up sturdier alternatives, bring their prices down, support repair and secondhand markets, and allows targeted help like rebates or clothing allowances — far fairer than keeping everyone's shirts artificially cheap by subsidizing pollution.
[P1.5] Finally, rebuilding local textile capacity means stable jobs under enforceable labor and environmental standards, instead of a race to the bottom chasing the lowest wages abroad, plus supply chains less vulnerable to shipping shocks.
[P1.6] Vote for honest prices: tax waste, build clothes that last.

[C1.2] Low-income households spend a larger share of their income on essentials and cannot easily substitute toward expensive ethical brands. So the policy works as a regressive sales tax on being poor.
[C1.3] PRO's "polluter pays" framing [P1.2] sounds fair, but this tax is not a pollution meter. It hits all fast fashion—including cheap basics made with relatively low impact—while the revenue subsidizes local manufacturers, not the communities harmed by dye runoff or landfills. If the goal is to price damage, regulate dyes, microplastics, and waste directly, or impose extended producer responsibility so costs track actual harm. A blanket production tax is a blunt instrument that invites lobbying over what counts as "sustainable" or "local."
[C1.4] Nor is local manufacturing a guaranteed cure. "Local" can mean a highly automated plant with few jobs; "sustainable" can mean small-batch clothing that is still thrown away. Higher labor, energy, and compliance costs are structural. A subsidy may narrow the price gap temporarily, but if it cannot make durable clothes genuinely cheap, government must either keep taxing poorer shoppers or abandon the program.
[C1.5] PRO's own answer—rebates and allowances [P1.4]—concedes the tax is regressive and adds bureaucracy that often misses informal, transient, or undocumented workers.
[C1.6] Climate and labor abuse are real. But the motion's remedy is worse for the people it claims to protect. Direct standards, repair and secondhand support, and targeted income help can cut waste without making necessity a luxury. The proposition sacrifices the present needs of the poor for speculative industrial benefits, and that is the wrong trade.
[C1.7] Vote CON.
