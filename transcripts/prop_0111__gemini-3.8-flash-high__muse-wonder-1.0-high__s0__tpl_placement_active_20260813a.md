[P1.1] Healthcare policy fails when it lets ideological perfection become the enemy of human survival. The fundamental duty of the state is not to purchase every elective convenience on the market; it is to ensure that no citizen suffers or dies from a manageable condition simply because they could not afford to see a doctor.
[P1.2] Primary care is the highest-leverage investment a society can make. Routine screenings, early detection, vaccinations, and chronic disease management prevent common ailments—such as hypertension and diabetes—from deteriorating into strokes, renal failure, and bankrupting emergency room visits. When basic care is universally guaranteed, societies save both lives and immense fiscal resources by intervening when treatment is simple and cheap, rather than late and catastrophic.
[P1.3] The proposition strikes the vital balance required for durable public health: universalize the foundation, without bankrupting the state on elective consumer preferences. Basic triage, preventative screenings, and essential medicines possess enormous positive externalities and highly inelastic demand; they are quintessential public goods. By contrast, boutique amenities, cosmetic enhancements, and purely elective procedures operate comfortably within private markets. Insisting that the state must cover everything or nothing creates political gridlock and fiscal exhaustion, leaving millions completely unprotected while debating luxuries.
[P1.4] Guaranteeing universal primary care establishes an unshakeable floor of human dignity. It ends the tragic, inefficient practice of emergency rooms serving as primary clinics of last resort for the uninsured. Low-income families gain permanent access to continuous, trusted medical relationships, dramatically reducing health disparities at the root.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, allowing private markets to handle elective and supplementary services provides flexibility, retains clinical capacity, and prevents the rationing of discretionary care.
[P1.6] We do not need an all-encompassing state monopoly to achieve health equity. We need an unconditional universal foundation that protects every citizen’s life from the ground up.

[C1.1] Guaranteeing universal primary care is not a modest floor. It is an open-ended entitlement to the vaguest, most expandable part of medicine — and that is why it fails on cost, on access, and on equity.
[C1.2] Primary care is not just a blood-pressure check. In modern practice it bleeds into chronic disease management, mental health, diagnostics, referrals, preventive screening, and long-term prescribing for an ageing population. Guarantee all of that, free at the point of use, for everyone, and demand does not stay 'simple and cheap.' It expands to fill the entitlement.
[C1.3] Every new test, guideline, and expectation becomes a political right that cannot be withdrawn without a scandal.
[C1.4] That guarantee also inverts sensible public spending. Routine, predictable care is precisely what households and targeted subsidies can handle; the state's scarce role is protection against catastrophic, unpredictable costs. Universal primary care instead spends heavily on the healthy and wealthy who could pay for a check-up, while consuming the budget, clinicians, and appointments needed by the genuinely sick. Prevention is valuable for individuals, but universal screening and early intervention often find more to treat, follow up, and medicate — they improve some outcomes at added cost, they do not reliably pay for themselves.
[C1.5] With a fixed budget and a legal promise, rationing does not disappear. It moves to queues, seven-minute appointments, closed GP lists, and gatekeeping that delays referrals. The poor, who cannot exit, wait. The better-off buy the supplementary private tier the motion explicitly preserves, seeing the same doctors faster.
[C1.6] So the compromise is unstable: private elective and top-up work pays more, pulls scarce clinicians out of the guaranteed sector, and leaves the public floor formally universal but practically hollow.
[C1.7] A targeted safety net, catastrophic coverage, and supply-side reform direct help where need is greatest without promising everything routine to everyone. A universal guarantee promises equality and delivers waiting, creep, and a two-tier drain.

Universal primary care is not an indulgent luxury for the wealthy; it is the essential clinical firewall that prevents predictable ailments from deteriorating into bankrupting emergencies.
[P2.2] CON warns that primary care is an "open-ended entitlement" that produces rationing, queues, and runaway costs [C1.1, C1.2, C1.5]. But rationing already occurs under CON’s model through ability to pay: low-income patients ration insulin or skip checkups entirely until they collapse into emergency wards. Universal primary care replaces arbitrary financial exclusion with orderly clinical triage. Moreover, primary care serves as a gatekeeper, not a blank check.
[P2.3] Standard public packages define clear boundaries—routine consultations, basic diagnostics, essential medicines, and immunizations. Catching disease early does not manufacture phantom costs; managing hypertension or pre-diabetes early is exponentially cheaper than treating end-stage strokes and kidney failure.
[P2.4] CON also argues that permitting private elective markets will pull clinicians away and hollow out the public floor [C1.6], proposing a means-tested safety net instead [C1.7]. But targeted safety nets are notoriously fragile. Bureaucratic eligibility hurdles, income cliffs, and coverage churn consistently leave vulnerable working families uninsured until acute disaster strikes. Furthermore, private elective and supplementary markets—such as cosmetic surgery, elective orthopedics, or luxury amenities—do not siphon away family physicians and community clinic nurses; they absorb discretionary consumer demand without burdening the public ledger.
[P2.5] Guaranteeing universal primary care establishes an unconditional baseline of health equity. It controls systemic costs at the source while allowing private elective care to operate freely where public necessity ends. CON’s alternative abandons prevention, forces the poor into emergency rooms, and guarantees the very fiscal catastrophe it claims to avoid.
