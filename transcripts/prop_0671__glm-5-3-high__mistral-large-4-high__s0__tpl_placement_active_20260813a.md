[P1.1] A seven-year-old learning to form letters is doing something physical: grip, pressure, eye-tracking across a page, the slow coupling of hand and mind. That coupling is what early literacy is built on, and it is exactly what a screen dilutes. A device-light early-grade policy is not nostalgia; it is matching the tool to the developmental task, while keeping tablets available for the grades where they actually pay off.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.3] First, literacy foundations are motor and tactile. Handwriting practice builds letter recognition far better than tapping or tracing on glass, because the movement is slower, more varied, and more deeply encoded. Reading comprehension itself tracks with the physical act of tracking text on paper. Multiple comparisons of reading on paper versus screens have found a consistent paper advantage, and the gap is widest for younger and poorer readers, precisely the students early grades serve.
[P1.4] Second, attention is the scarcest resource in a young classroom, and devices are engineered to fragment it. Notifications, animations, and the mere presence of a screen tax working memory that six-year-olds have very little of to spare. Teachers spend real instructional time managing devices rather than teaching. Paper cannot buzz, badge, or autoplay.
[P1.5] The default medium of a classroom should be the one that cannot interrupt.
[P1.6] Third, the proposition costs us almost nothing. This is not a ban. The policy keeps one-to-one tablets available later, when students can type, research, and self-regulate. It simply says: don't spend scarce early-grade budget and teacher capacity on hardware whose measurable benefits in kindergarten through third grade are thin, while its distractions and developmental tradeoffs are well documented.
[P1.7] The burden on the other side is heavy: they must show that tablets specifically, in early grades specifically, outperform paper for young learners, not that technology is good in general. Generic enthusiasm about "digital skills" won't meet that burden, because kindergartners acquire those skills quickly later; they cannot retroactively acquire handwriting fluency or sustained attention.
[P1.8] Paper first, then pixels. That sequencing respects how children actually develop.

[C1.1] The proposition converts one genuine finding—handwriting practice supports early literacy—into a sweeping rule that would make paper the default and screens the exception in kindergarten through third grade. That is the wrong policy, because early grades are not only about forming letters; they are about building access, confidence, and flexible skill. A device-light, paper-first classroom rations the very tools that can equalize learning.
[C1.2] Consider the students for whom paper is the barrier. A child with dysgraphia can produce a paragraph by speaking or typing long before a pencil gives them one. A multilingual learner can hear a text read aloud while following highlighted words, then tap to hear a word again. A student with motor difficulties can zoom, use switch access, or adjust contrast.
[C1.3] Those are not enrichment extras; they are routes into the same curriculum. If early grades are paper-first, the students who most need adaptable tools are the ones asked to wait, while classmates move ahead on paper. Wealthier families will supply tablets at home, so the policy would not remove screens from early childhood; it would make schools the place where access is most unequal.
[C1.4] Nor is the age cutoff coherent. Digital fluency is not a high-school subject that can be switched on later. Young children learn tool habits, self-monitoring, and how to move between media when adults model them. A system that keeps devices scarce early and then hands one-to-one tablets to the same students later creates a cliff, not a sequence.
[C1.5] Teachers must retrain routines, and students must unlearn the message that technology is for later, higher-status work.
[C1.6] The stronger policy is media-rich and intentional: paper for handwriting, sketching, and sustained reading; devices for access, practice, feedback, and creation—guided by teachers. The proposition mistakes a preference for one good tool into a hierarchy that early learners can least afford.
