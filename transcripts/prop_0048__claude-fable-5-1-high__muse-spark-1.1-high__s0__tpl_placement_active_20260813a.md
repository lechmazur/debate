[P1.1] Picture a night shift on a medical ward: one nurse, eight patients. A post-operative patient begins bleeding quietly under the dressing while the nurse is two rooms away managing a confused elderly man trying to climb out of bed. Nobody decided that patient should die. Someone decided the ward could "manage" with one fewer nurse tonight.
[P1.2] That decision was made in a budget meeting, by someone who will never see the dressing.
[P1.3] This is the heart of the case. Hospital administrators are not villains, but they face a structural incentive problem. Nursing is the largest controllable cost on a hospital's books. The savings from cutting a nurse show up immediately and precisely on a spreadsheet.
[P1.4] The costs—a missed deterioration, a fall, a medication error, a readmission—show up later, diffusely, and are almost never traced back to the staffing decision that caused them. When one side of a trade-off is visible and the other is invisible, decision-makers systematically underinvest. Ratios by law correct that bias the only way that works: by taking the floor off the table.
[P1.5] The evidence points one direction. Studies across many countries consistently find that each additional patient assigned to a nurse is associated with a meaningful rise in surgical mortality, and that nurses in overloaded units report more care left undone. California has had mandated ratios for roughly two decades; Australian states have followed. Predictions of hospital closures and collapsed access did not materialize.
[P1.6] What did happen: nurses stayed in the profession longer, and turnover—which itself costs hospitals enormously—fell.
[P1.7] Notice what a ratio law is not. It is a minimum, not a maximum. Administrators keep every bit of discretion above the floor. The law simply forbids the one choice that reliably harms patients.
[P1.8] CON will say "flexibility." Ask what flexibility has actually produced: understaffing so chronic that nurses strike over it worldwide. We regulate the number of pilots in a cockpit and lifeguards at a pool. Patients in a ward deserve the same protection.
