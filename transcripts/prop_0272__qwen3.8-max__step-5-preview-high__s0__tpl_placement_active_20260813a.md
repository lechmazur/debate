[P1.1] The proposition does not say schools should ban laptops or reject digital learning. It says when scarce after-school time, space, and money are allocated, embodied human development should come first. That ordering is right because sports, arts, and unstructured play teach what screens struggle to teach: cooperation under pressure, emotional regulation, physical confidence, and the ability to invent a game, resolve a conflict, or recover from a mistake in real time.
[P1.2] The developmental case is strongest. A student on a soccer team or in a band does not merely consume content. They commit to shared goals, accept roles, practice repeatedly, and experience visible failure and improvement. Unstructured play is especially valuable because it forces children to set rules, negotiate, and self-organize without adult scripts.
[P1.3] These capacities support classroom learning: better attention, discipline, attendance, and social stability. Purely digital enrichment can reinforce skills, but it often remains individual, sedentary, and dependent on interface design. For many children, after-school already includes abundant screens; what they lack is supervised movement, live collaboration, and creative space.
[P1.4] The equity case is even sharper. Affluent families can buy club sports, music lessons, and safe play spaces privately. When public schools retreat to digital-first enrichment, they turn after-school opportunity into a home resource contest: children with reliable devices, quiet rooms, and tech-savvy adults benefit most. Schools should counteract that gap by providing the physical, social, and artistic opportunities that money otherwise rations.
[P1.5] A school gym, art room, or supervised courtyard can serve many students without requiring every household to supply hardware, bandwidth, and technical support.
[P1.6] Finally, schools are not just test-score factories. They are responsible for developing healthy, socially competent citizens. If the choice is between another hour of adaptive software and a basketball practice, mural project, or free play session, the latter more reliably builds well-being, belonging, and motivation to stay in school. Expand digital tools later; first give children the human-rich experiences that make enrichment worth having.

[C1.3] The physical option is also not the cheap universal alternative. Sports fields need maintenance, coaches, insurance, and safe transport. Arts rooms need supplies and specialists. Unstructured play needs supervision and space that many urban schools simply lack.

[C1.6] The equity argument cuts the other way. Affluent families already buy club sports and music lessons. They also buy laptops, broadband, and tech support. A school that offers only physical enrichment ignores that the next homework assignment is already on a screen.
[C1.7] If schools wait to expand digital, they cement a home-resource gap that physical programs alone cannot close. Expanding digital now levels the playing field that money otherwise rations.
[C1.8] The choice is not embodied humans versus screens. It is whether schools build both tracks in parallel. They can. Delay is the only strategy guaranteed to lose.
