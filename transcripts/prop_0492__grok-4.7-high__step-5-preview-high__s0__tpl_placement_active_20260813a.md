[P1.1] When a firm replaces a payroll with a robot line, it does not merely rearrange its own costs. It sheds the obligations that human employment carries—income withholding, payroll contributions, health and unemployment financing—and shifts the shock onto workers, local demand, and the public budget. The gains are private and immediate; the losses are social and lasting. An automation tax reconnects those two sides of the same decision.
[P1.2] This is not a case for stopping robotics. It is a case for making the activity that creates the adjustment burden pay for the adjustment. Displaced workers do not retrain themselves costlessly. Mid-career machinists, warehouse staff, and clerical workers face credit constraints, thin local markets, and skills that do not transfer on a weekend course.
[P1.3] If support is left to general revenue, it competes with every other claim on the budget precisely when employment-based revenue is falling. Tying the tax to replacement, and dedicating the proceeds to retraining and adjustment, makes the funding automatic, visible, and proportionate to the disruption.
[P1.4] There is also a distortion to correct, not a new penalty to invent. Labor is taxed; a machine doing the same work is not. That gap already tilts the choice toward substitution even when a human-plus-tool arrangement would be better once social costs are counted. A targeted tax on replacement narrows the gap.
[P1.5] Firms that automate because the technology is genuinely superior will still automate; they will simply help fund the retraining their choice requires. Firms automating mainly to escape labor-related taxes will think twice. That is efficient, not Luddite.
[P1.6] Unshared automation gains breed backlash that can freeze adoption—local resistance, crude bans, or broad hostility to technology itself. A bargain that lets productivity rise while funding the people displaced to move with it protects innovation rather than threatening it. Impose the tax. Spend it on the adjustment.
[P1.7] The alternative is private profit, public scramble, and a slower, angrier transition.

[C1.1] An automation tax does not tax a windfall; it taxes a choice to raise output per worker. The motion’s first mistake is treating every robot as a displaced employee and every displaced employee as a fiscal free lunch. Firms already pay wages, payroll charges, corporate taxes, and capital costs, and automation often replaces hours lost to quits, retirement, and weak demand, not living workers. A warehouse operator that buys a robot arm after years of unfilled shifts has replaced no one, yet the proposal needs a taxable “replacement” it cannot define.
[C1.2] The claim that labor is taxed while machines are not [P1.4] confuses a narrow payroll comparison with tax exemption. The relevant question is whether automation creates an uncompensated externality or merely shifts production to a method with fewer human inputs. Most adjustment costs are already mediated by unemployment insurance, severance, wage insurance, portable benefits, and training systems. If those are inadequate, the direct remedy is to make them more generous and portable, not to impose a second levy on the investment that will fund the broader tax base.
[C1.3] An earmarked automation tax also discourages the adaptation that pays for the public sector. Productivity growth sustains real wages and receipts; a significant added cost on domestic automation pushes firms to delay upgrades, outsource to less automated suppliers, keep unproductive roles, or invest abroad. A narrow earmark is then self-starving: it collects less as avoidance grows, while future employment-based receipts shrink.
[C1.4] Tax capital only where the externality and measurement are clear. Replacement is neither: software and robotics blur task boundaries, firms differ in baseline staffing, and displacement often follows demand or organizational change. The motion responds to a genuine anxiety with a crude trigger.
[C1.5] Oppose the tax. Fix adjustment policy directly, keep adoption productive, and fund support through institutions designed for displaced workers rather than a penalty on machines.

[C2.1] PRO’s central promise is a “workable trigger” [P2.2]. It is not. Quit versus layoff is a fact determined after the event; replacement is a counterfactual payroll that never existed. A firm losing demand sheds hours without buying a single robot, while a growing firm can automate and still hire.
[C2.2] A “net hours” baseline therefore taxes decline as if it were automation and exempts substitution inside expansion—exactly backwards. The trigger is not narrow; it is a second corporate tax with random incidence, inviting litigation over every line item.
[C2.3] The funding promise collapses into a dilemma. If the charge is too small to deter genuinely superior automation [P2.5], it cannot finance meaningful retraining. If it is large enough to finance robust programs, it taxes the marginal upgrade—the sorting arm, the CNC cell, the scheduling software that keeps a factory viable. That marginal adoption is not a windfall; it is the productivity growth that expands the tax base which pays for unemployment insurance, pensions, and adjustment itself.
[C2.4] PRO would fund the safety net by slowing the engine that pays for it [C1.3].
[C2.5] The “fiscal wedge” [P2.3] overstates the loss. Machines are not untaxed. Corporate profits, property, sales, and the higher real wages of remaining workers all remain taxable, and consumers share gains through lower prices. The wedge is partial and temporary, not an open subsidy.

[P4.1] A1: The test is paid hours, not a ghost payroll. Compare compensated hours in the function where the robot is installed, before and after, against output in that function. Filling a vacancy or a quits backlog does not cut paid hours; those hours were already gone, so the event is exempt. If hours were already falling before the capital arrived, attrition is not charged to the machine.
[P4.2] What is charged is the joint pattern: new robotics, paid hours down, output holding or rising. It catches substitution inside a growing firm and exempts pure demand decline, where hours and output fall together. The comparison sits on payroll records and production logs.
[P4.3] A2: The charge need not finance unemployment insurance, pensions, and the whole safety net. It finances retraining for workers whose paid hours the taxable event removed. That cost is a fraction of the payroll just shed, including contributions no longer remitted. A levy on that shed obligation can fund the transition and still leave a genuine unit-cost collapse intact.
[P4.4] The sorting arm that keeps the crew is not in the base, because it does not cut paid hours. The dilemma in [C2.3] bites only if every robot is taxed. It is not.
[P4.5] Answered this way, neither objection blocks the tax. The trigger is a before-and-after on compensated hours, not a counterfactual payroll that never existed. The revenue is a partial clawback of charges the firm stopped paying, spent on the people those charges used to cover — not a second corporate tax on every upgrade. The alternative still offers instruments that thin out as displacement grows, financed from a general fund the displaced must outbid.
[P4.6] Payroll receipts fall when the injury hits. The enlarged base, if it arrives, arrives later. Waiting on a future tax base does not retrain anyone this year. Charge observed replacement.
