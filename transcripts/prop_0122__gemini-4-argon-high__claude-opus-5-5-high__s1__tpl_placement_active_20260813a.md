[P1.1] Criminal law rests on a foundational premise: punishment is only just when an offender possesses mature moral culpability—the capacity to foresee consequences, resist manipulation, and regulate impulses. Before age fourteen, the brain’s prefrontal cortex is still under basic construction. Society recognizes this everywhere else: ten- and twelve-year-olds cannot vote, sign contracts, or live independently because they cannot reliably govern themselves. Yet countries with very low ages of criminal responsibility—sometimes seven or ten—suddenly pretend this same child possesses adult-like criminal intent the moment they break a law.
[P1.2] That is a developmental fiction.
[P1.3] Worse, treating young children as punishable offenders manufactures the very crime the state wants to prevent. Prosecuting and detaining an eleven-year-old takes a child during a critical window of neuroplasticity and immerses them in trauma, stigma, and peer contagion. Pull an erratic pre-teen out of school, lock them alongside delinquent peers, and brand them an offender, and the result is predictable: they internalize that identity. Criminal punishment cannot deter impulsive children who barely grasp next week, let alone sentencing statutes; it simply severs their normal development and drives higher adult recidivism.
[P1.4] Crucially, raising the threshold to fourteen is not a license for impunity. Decriminalizing children under fourteen does not mean state inaction when a twelve-year-old commits a violent act. Instead, it replaces adversarial courts and prisons with compulsory child welfare, psychiatric, and educational interventions. Severe childhood offending is almost always the symptom of profound abuse, neurological crisis, or adult exploitation—such as gangs recruiting eleven-year-olds who lack the maturity to resist them.
[P1.5] Welfare frameworks can still mandate intensive supervision, family restructuring, and, when public safety requires it, secure therapeutic care. By drawing the criminal line at fourteen, we stop prosecuting children for their immaturity and deploy interventions that actually leave both the child and the public safer.

The honest response to gradual development is graduated responsibility: separate youth courts, a presumption that young children lack criminal intent unless the prosecution proves otherwise, and sentences built around rehabilitation. [C1.6] Third, blanket immunity hands exploiters a tool. PRO worries about gangs recruiting eleven-year-olds [P1.4]. A legal guarantee that under-fourteens cannot be prosecuted makes them more valuable couriers, not less.
