[P1.1] True crime does not merely report suffering; it packages it. A murdered woman becomes a thumbnail, a surviving mother becomes a reaction shot, and a family’s worst day becomes a bingeable season. That is the core of this case: the people whose lives are torn open are the raw material of a commercial product, yet they are routinely the last to know, the least consulted, and the least protected.
[P1.2] The exploitation is not incidental. Dramatizations invent dialogue for the dead, linger over violence, and amplify half-tested theories. Families discover their relative’s death has been turned into entertainment through a trailer, a podcast ad, or a neighbor’s message. Sometimes real names, photographs, and locations are reused, dragging private people back into public scrutiny and sometimes into danger.
[P1.3] Journalism can justify naming victims when it serves the public interest; entertainment that sells suspense and merchandise is a different enterprise.
[P1.4] The voluntary market cannot fix this. Families have no bargaining power, no advance notice, and no meaningful ability to correct false or invasive portrayals. “Nobody forced them to watch” is no answer when the culture pushes the product into their feed and their children’s schools. Existing defamation law protects reputation, not dignity; privacy law is patchy; platform guidelines are written by the same companies profiting from the genre.
[P1.5] Stronger rules need not ban true crime. They can require notification and consultation with victims’ families, independent review before graphic reenactments, clear corrections, and civil remedies for reckless fabrication or invasive use of private people’s lives. Those are modest floors, not censorship.
[P1.6] The question is not whether terrible stories may be told. It is whether an industry may treat human beings as disposable content and call the damage “entertainment.” It should not.
