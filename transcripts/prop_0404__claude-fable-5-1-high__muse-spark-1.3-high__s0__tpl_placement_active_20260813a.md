[P1.1] An identity document has one job: to tell the truth about the person holding it. When the state hands a nonbinary person a card stamped "M" or "F," it hands them a document that is inaccurate by the state's own design. That is the core of this case. The question is not whether governments should endorse a theory of gender.
[P1.2] It is whether governments should knowingly print false information on the papers people must show to fly, drive, vote, open a bank account, and pick up a prescription.
[P1.3] Consider what a forced binary marker actually does in daily life. A person whose appearance, name, and presentation do not match the letter on their license faces the same friction at a security checkpoint, a pharmacy counter, or a traffic stop that binary transgender people already know well: the double take, the extra questions, the manager called over. A third option does not create scrutiny; it removes the mismatch that invites it. The document finally matches the human.
[P1.4] The practical objections collapse on inspection. International passport standards have long allowed an "X" marker for unspecified sex, so the machine-readable infrastructure already exists. Governments that have adopted it, from Canada and Australia to Germany and multiple US states, did not see border systems fail or fraud spike. Identity verification runs on photographs, biometrics, and document numbers, not on a gender field.
[P1.5] Databases already accommodate "unknown" values. The marginal cost is a checkbox.
[P1.6] Nor does the marker carry any medical meaning that a third option would corrupt. Most jurisdictions already permit changing the sex marker, so it has never been a fixed biological record; it is an administrative descriptor. Adding a third value simply makes the descriptor honest for people it currently misdescribes.
[P1.7] The state has no legitimate interest in compelling a citizen to carry a self-description they reject, for life, on every official interaction. Weigh a trivial administrative change against dignity, safety, and accuracy. That is the whole debate.

[C1.1] An identity document is not an autobiography. It exists so a stranger — a border officer, an emergency doctor, a police officer — can quickly and reliably place who is standing in front of them. Its virtue is not self-expression but objective, stable, externally checkable information.
[C1.2] That is why a sex marker is there at all. It helps narrow a search for a suspect, match a patient to sex-linked medical risks, assign detention and searches, and compile coherent statistics on crime, health, and sex discrimination. For all those uses, what matters is observable biological sex, not inner sense of self. Redefining the field as gender identity corrupts the data at the source.
[C1.3] A third, self-declared category breaks that function while fixing nothing. Nonbinary is not a single observable trait but an umbrella for many different subjective stances, with no stable criteria for the clerk to verify and no way for the officer in the field to use. Once one state lets anyone tick X on demand, its records cease to line up with other states that keep a binary, with birth records, and with laws that still distinguish male and female for prisons, shelters, conscription, and health screening.
[C1.4] The “trivial checkbox” becomes years of rewriting forms, databases, and safeguarding rules — or leaving them contradictory.
[C1.5] Nor does it buy the safety promised. Stamping X on a license shown at every traffic stop, job check, and hotel desk does not let a gender-nonconforming person blend in; it announces nonconformity to every stranger, including in places where that invites hostility. A mismatched M or F can be painful, but a universal out-marker follows you everywhere.
[C1.6] The state has no duty to certify an identity it cannot verify, at the cost of records everyone relies on. Accuracy means telling the truth about the body, not affirming a feeling about it.
