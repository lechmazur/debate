[P1.1] Official forecasts fail for a structural reason: the people who produce them are not paid to be right. Career incentives reward confidence, institutional loyalty, and politically convenient numbers. Recessions are missed, project costs explode, and “base cases” quietly assume success. Expert panels concentrate the same problem—shared training, shared blind spots, and no personal cost for being wrong.
[P1.2] Subsidized prediction markets reverse that logic. A government posts a clear question—will unemployment fall below the target by year three, will hospital capacity hold through the next surge, will the program’s stated emissions cut materialize—and pays a liquidity subsidy so serious traders can take the other side. The resulting price is a probability. It updates as new information appears.
[P1.3] It draws on whoever actually knows something and is willing to risk money, not merely whoever holds a title. Dispersed knowledge that never reaches a closed briefing room can move the price.
[P1.4] This is not a replacement for expertise. Analysts still design the questions, interpret mechanisms, and decide. What changes is the main input. A published market probability sits beside the internal memo as a formal constraint.
[P1.5] Officials who ignore a large gap must explain why their model deserves more weight than people staking capital on the opposite view. That single requirement disciplines optimism that currently survives because no one is forced to confront it in public, in numbers, and in real time.
[P1.6] Governments already spend heavily on forecasting staff and outside panels. A fraction of that budget, used as subsidy rather than salary, buys a signal that cannot be revised in a private edit and that punishes persistent error. Election markets and internal corporate markets have repeatedly shown the same pattern: when accuracy is compensated and error is costly, the aggregate beats the committee. Policy stakes are higher, not lower.
[P1.7] Forecasts that shape law, budgets, and war should be bought the same way—by paying for truth and refusing to treat unpriced opinion as the default.

[C1.1] Prediction markets work when spectators bet on events their wagers cannot change. Major policy is the opposite: governments forecast in order to intervene. Take [P1.2]’s test of whether hospital capacity will hold through the next surge. If traders price a collapse and officials respond by funding surge beds, capacity holds and the traders who sounded the alarm lose money.
[C1.2] Rational traders stop pricing dangers that trigger their own defeat. Conditional markets, pricing outcomes if a policy passes versus if it fails, create a worse trap. Because the rejected option never happens, trades on that branch must be voided, letting interest groups crash a rival policy’s forecast knowing their bets are refunded once officials walk away.
[C1.3] [P1.6] claims higher policy stakes favor markets. Higher stakes are precisely why formal policy markets break. Once a market probability becomes a “formal constraint” officials must justify overriding [P1.4], moving that number buys state power. A public subsidy may put millions into liquidity, while a tariff, bailout, or emissions rule [P1.2] shifts billions.
[C1.4] For a corporate lobby or foreign adversary, absorbing trading losses to skew the benchmark is not costly error; it is cheap policy capture.
[C1.5] Even unmanipulated prices are the wrong main input for governing because they deliver a naked number without a mechanism. Relying mainly on internal forecasts and expert panels lets policymakers audit assumptions, weigh distributional impacts, and ask why a projection moved; no minister can question an anonymous order book. Markets also demand rigid settlement rules, flattening complex goals into narrow targets that invite gaming, while in financial rescues or war [P1.7], public contracts leak state secrets or let insiders profit from failure.
[C1.6] To fix [P1.1]’s incentive problem, governments can score forecasters and pit rival expert teams against each other without handing leverage to anonymous capital. Elevating subsidized markets over expert panels trades auditable reasoning for a signal that corrupts the moment power depends on it.
