[P1.1] Relevance is not a building; it is a habit. The core problem facing public broadcasters is not that they have too few channels. It is that younger audiences do not encounter them. A teenager’s media day is vertical, algorithmic, and creator-led.
[P1.2] If public media is absent there, it becomes inherited furniture—respected, funded, and unused. Prioritizing short-form video and creator partnerships is the strategy that directly attacks that problem.
[P1.3] Traditional channel expansion assumes reach comes from more linear real estate. [P1.4] Short-form is not trivial. It is discovery infrastructure. A sharp clip on how local government spends money, a creator-led science myth-busting series, or a young poet’s response to a public archive can do what a new channel rarely does: earn attention first, then route people toward deeper coverage. Creator partnerships add the trust and fluency institutions lack.
[P1.5] Audiences follow people, not logos. Public broadcasters can bring editorial standards, archives, and funding; creators bring reach and idiom. That is a credible public-service bargain, not a sellout.
[P1.6] Prioritization is not abandonment. Core services can remain. But new money for relevance should go where the next generation’s attention actually is. The test is simple: in five years, is public media a habit for young citizens, or a museum of good intentions?

[C1.2] PRO’s discovery argument rests on an unproven chain: a young person sees a clip, trusts the broadcaster, follows its link, and becomes a lasting citizen-user. Each step can fail. A swipe is cheap; a view can reflect the creator’s existing fame, an amusing format, or an algorithmic accident rather than demand for public media. If the audience follows people rather than logos, as PRO says, then creator-led reach may build the creator’s habit while leaving the broadcaster as disposable infrastructure behind it.
[C1.3] The market already supplies short video and influencers at enormous scale. Public broadcasters add most value where provision is thin: sustained local reporting, educational programming, minority-language services, accessible coverage, and dependable information during emergencies. Diverting priority toward the most crowded content market risks duplicating what is abundant while weakening what is distinctive. Traditional channel expansion is not automatically wise, but targeted expansion can serve identifiable public gaps with owned distribution, predictable scheduling, and universal access.
[C1.4] The motion would subordinate those needs to a speculative youth-attention funnel.
[C1.5] Creator partnerships also import costs PRO ignores. Editorial standards must be enforced across personalities whose incentives reward immediacy, intimacy, and engagement. Vettoo little, and one controversy damages institutional trust; control heavily, and the supposed authenticity disappears. [C1.6] Reach rented from algorithms and personalities is not a durable public asset.

[C2.6] Nor are platform dependence and linear carriage equivalent merely because both involve gatekeepers. [C2.7] Finally, emergencies reveal the weighing error. Discovery may help before a crisis, but relevance is ultimately tested when people need reliable information immediately.
