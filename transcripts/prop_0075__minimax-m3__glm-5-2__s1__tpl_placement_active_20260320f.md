[P1.1] The question before us is not whether religious freedom matters — it does. The question is where that freedom stops and public law begins. The proposition offers a clear, defensible line: exemptions follow religious function, not commercial identity.
[P1.2] Religious institutions deserve protection when they act on their theological core — selecting clergy, shaping doctrine, forming believers in their own tradition. Those decisions are inseparable from what the institution is. Compelling a church to ordain a minister who denies its doctrine, or forcing a mosque to hire an imam who rejects its creed, would gut religious freedom itself. That is why exemption is justified — because the act itself is sacred.
[P1.3] But the moment a religious entity opens a commercial door to the public — selling goods, serving meals, offering hospital beds, hiring workers for non-doctrinal roles — it enters a marketplace governed by public-accommodation and employment norms. A bakery is a bakery regardless of who owns it. A gym open to the public serves the public. When a religiously owned hotel refuses a same-sex couple a room, it is operating as a hotel, not performing a sacrament.
[P1.4] The proposition prevents religious exemptions from becoming a license to discriminate in the commercial sphere. Without the "tightly tied to core religious functions" test, any business could launder discrimination through faith. A commercial landlord, a chain retailer, a government contractor could all invoke a founder's religion to deny service to the public. That is not religious freedom — it is theocracy of the marketplace.
[P1.5] A narrow, function-based exemption preserves two things at once: the integrity of religious institutions in their sacred work, and the equal dignity of every person in the public square. That is the proposition's strength. It forces the opponent to defend an alternative where religion becomes a blank check anywhere a religious actor does business — a position that erodes both public equality and the credibility of religious liberty itself.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds moderate but rests on a false premise: that religious activity splits neatly into "core religious functions" and "broad commercial activity." The institutions that actually shape communal life — religious schools, hospitals, charities, adoption agencies — operate in both spheres at once, and their commercial work is itself religious work.
[C1.2] Consider a Catholic hospital. It bills patients, hires non-clerical staff, and serves a mostly non-Catholic public. By the proposition's logic, it is commercial and loses its exemption. But it exists because of a Catholic commitment to heal the sick.
[C1.3] Its mission is theological. Forcing it to choose between operating as a secular institution or shutting its doors means communities lose a hospital — and low-income communities lose them first.
[C1.4] The same holds for religious schools that charge tuition, faith-based charities holding government contracts, and disaster-relief groups serving meals for reimbursement. These are ministries that happen to involve transactions, not businesses that happen to be religious.
[C1.5] The deeper problem: the proposition requires the government to decide what counts as "core." That is a theological judgment. When a court declares this is sacred and that is commercial, it determines which expressions of faith are genuinely religious — exactly the entanglement religious liberty protections were designed to prevent.
[C1.6] The proposition's own framing gives the game away. It calls a bakery "a bakery regardless of who owns it." But a bakery run by a religious order as part of its outreach ministry is not interchangeable with a corporate chain. Reducing everything to the transaction strips away religious context entirely.
[C1.7] That is not neutrality — it is secularism by default.
[C1.8] There is a real risk of pretextual discrimination. But the answer is targeted scrutiny of bad-faith claims, not a blanket rule that commercial activity cancels religious character. The proposition trades religious pluralism for a line the government cannot draw without becoming the arbiter of authentic faith.
