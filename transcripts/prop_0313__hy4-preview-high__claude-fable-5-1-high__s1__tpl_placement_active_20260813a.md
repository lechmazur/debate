[P1.1] International sport is not a content studio, and athletes are not inventory spent for a rights fee. When heat and travel become material threats to health, federations owe competitors safety—even if that means fewer marquee matches and less broadcast revenue.
[P1.2] Heat and travel are not mere discomfort. Extreme heat plus repeated maximal effort can cause heat stroke, kidney and cardiac stress, and lasting damage. Long-haul travel across time zones, stacked on a compressed schedule, degrades sleep, reaction time, immune function and recovery, and raises injury risk. Expansion magnifies all of it: more teams mean more matches, longer trips, shorter turnarounds, and hotter afternoon kickoffs chosen for television.
[P1.3] At that point the calendar is not competition; it is physiological debt.
[P1.4] The principle is already accepted. The 2022 men’s World Cup was moved from summer to winter because Gulf heat was unmanageable, at huge commercial cost. If an entire tournament can be relocated for heat, a federation can trim expansion or soften scheduling when medical risk rises—for example, an enlarged 48-team World Cup across North American summer cities, or the bigger Club World Cup adding intercontinental travel to a congested calendar. Player unions warn about workload for a reason.
[P1.5] Broadcast money is instrumental, not sovereign. It funds development and reach, but does not buy ownership over athletes’ bodies. Governing bodies already accept lost revenue for safety and integrity: postponements, relocations, empty stadiums, bubbles, doping bans. If they can upend months of sport to fight a virus, they can spare players a few reckless fixtures.
[P1.6] A rights deal cannot require play in conditions that materially threaten welfare. The question is not whether expansion sells, but whether a tournament knowingly imposing that harm still deserves authority. It does not. Cut expansion or intensity when risks are material; revenue is a lesser interest that sport exists to serve, not override.

[C1.2] Both are fixed by evening kickoffs, cooling breaks, climate-controlled venues, regional grouping to minimise flights, longer rest windows, expanded squads, and more substitutions. The motion demands the bluntest instrument on the shelf and insists we pay for it with the very money that buys the precise instruments.
[C1.3] Notice what the 2022 example actually shows. FIFA did not shrink the World Cup or strip out matches. It moved the dates, compressed the footprint into one small area with almost no travel, and cooled the stadiums. Attendance, participation and broadcast income all survived.
[C1.4] That is the opposite of the motion: welfare protected through mitigation and scheduling design, with revenue intact. If the principle "is already accepted," it is our principle.
[C1.5] Then follow the money. Broadcast rights are not a bonus for executives; they are the sport's redistribution engine. World Cup income funds development grants for well over two hundred federations, women's competitions that barely break even, referee training, medical staff, charter flights and cooling infrastructure. Cut expansion and you cut the debut of teams from smaller confederations, the players whose careers depend on that single window of exposure, and the budgets of the poorest federations first.
[C1.6] The athletes hurt by the motion are less visible than the stars in a heat map, but they are athletes too.
[C1.7] The trigger is also unworkable. Every elite sport "materially" stresses the body; the phrase has no floor. Under it, a lawyer for any federation can justify perpetual contraction, and the incentive it creates is perverse: bodies will avoid honest heat assessments because acknowledging risk now mandates losing money rather than solving it.
[C1.8] Protect players by redesigning the calendar. Do not protect them by shrinking the sport that pays for their protection.
