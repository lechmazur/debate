[P1.1] Home charging is what makes an EV workable. Public chargers are scarcer, slower, and can cost several times the overnight home rate. So what decides whether a renter can go electric is not the sticker price or the rebate — it is whether the landlord feels like saying yes. Today, in most places, the landlord can refuse for any reason or none: no explanation, no deadline, no appeal.
[P1.2] The law should flip that default.
[P1.3] First, the asymmetry is indefensible. A homeowner who buys an EV plugs in that week. A renter with the same car and the same willingness to pay for installation can be blocked indefinitely by silence. Nor does this ask landlords to fund anything: the right-to-charge model used in several states has the tenant pay for the charger, a licensed contractor, insurance, the electricity, and removal at lease end.
[P1.4] When the tenant bears every dollar, the landlord's remaining interest is process — approving plans, ensuring safety. Process justifies oversight, not an unreasoned veto.
[P1.5] Second, the veto imposes compounding costs. A renter denied home charging either abandons the EV or pays the public-charging premium forever — a private tax on exactly the urban households where EVs cut the most local pollution. With roughly a third of households renting, landlord apathy becomes a real bottleneck on climate goals governments are spending heavily to reach. Policy cannot subsidize the car and then let one person's inertia veto the fuel.
[P1.6] Third, the stronger right is proportionate. It is a right to request, paired with a duty to grant absent concrete, documented hardship: insufficient electrical capacity, real safety constraints, structural impossibility. Denial must come with reasons, within a deadline. That mirrors reasonable-accommodation law for disability modifications — the owner keeps genuine protections but loses the power of arbitrary refusal.
[P1.7] The central clash is simple: CON must defend a default where one party's inaction overrides another's basic need even when the renter pays every cost. That is not property rights. It is apathy with legal force.

[C1.1] A renter can already ask for a charger. What this adds is leverage: a deadline, a presumed yes, and a landlord who must document hardship or face a dispute. In an apartment building, that transfers control over shared electrical systems, parking, and liability from the owner responsible for all of them to the tenant who asks first.
[C1.2] The homeowner comparison fails because an apartment is not a detached house with one panel and one driveway. The charger may be the cheapest part. Behind it sit load studies, panel upgrades, trenching through common areas, permits, inspections, insurance changes, and parking assigned, lost, or policed. If capacity is limited, one installation can consume headroom needed for heat pumps, safety systems, or the next ten tenants.
[C1.3] Those are collective costs and choices, not expenses contained by one lease.
[C1.4] Nor does “tenant pays” eliminate the owner’s risk. If the contractor cuts corners, the tenant moves, or the unit turns over, the landlord still answers to the insurer, fire inspector, other residents, and the next occupant. Removal at lease end does not restore electrical capacity or undo concrete work. A temporary appliance becomes an alteration to common infrastructure, made under legal pressure.
[C1.5] That pressure lands unfairly. Engineering reviews, legal deadlines, and administrative fights become operating costs spread into everyone’s rent, including tenants who do not drive. Small owners of older buildings face the hardest choice: approve quickly, hire expertise to prove hardship, or risk a complaint. Some will raise rents, defer maintenance, or exit rather than manage that exposure for a relatively affluent car owner’s convenience.
[C1.6] Charging should expand, but at building scale: incentives for planned retrofits, requirements for new construction, shared chargers sited with the utility, and voluntary leases allocating costs. That solves capacity once instead of forcing piecemeal, first-come installations. A landlord’s ability to pause, sequence, or refuse an ill-fitting plan is not apathy with legal force. It is responsibility for the system everyone shares.
