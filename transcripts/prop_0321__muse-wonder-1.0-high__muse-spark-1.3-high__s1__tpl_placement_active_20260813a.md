[P1.1] Posting a photograph, illustration, or essay in public is an invitation to view it, share it, and talk about it. It is not consent to have a platform siphon it into a commercial model that can then produce endless substitutes for the creator's own labor. A stronger default right to stop that use simply restores that distinction: public means accessible, not appropriated.
[P1.2] That word "default" is decisive. Today the burden runs the wrong way: work posted years ago is presumed available for training unless the creator finds a buried setting or opts out platform by platform, often after training has already happened. A protective default flips the burden to the party with the lawyers, engineers, and profit motive. If a platform wants to build a paid image generator or music tool partly from creators' work, it must ask first and honor refusal without punishing reach or access.
[P1.3] This is not an abstract claim. An illustrator builds an audience by posting a distinctive portfolio; a commercial system trained on that portfolio can then be sold to clients to mimic her style at scale, with no license, credit, or payment. A writer's articles likewise become fuel for a tool marketed to publications that once commissioned writers.
[P1.4] A stronger right does not ban generative AI or research. It targets commercial training by the platform hosting the work, and it creates a functioning market: licensed datasets, collective permissions, and paid or consented material. Developers can still train; they just cannot treat the creative internet as free raw material because taking it is convenient.
[P1.5] Without that protection, rational creators will share less, post degraded or watermarked work, hide behind paywalls, or stop posting altogether. The public web becomes poorer for everyone, including future model builders. Protecting creators by default preserves both incentives: creators keep making public work, and commercial AI is built on permission rather than extraction.

[C1.2] Calling learning appropriation confuses inspiration with reproduction.
[C1.3] A protective default sounds like fairness, but it functions as an opt-in toll on knowledge. Training requires millions of works with messy authorship — reposts, collaborations, memes built on memes, accounts long abandoned. Demanding advance permission from each creator does not create a market; it creates gridlock where only the richest firms can afford clearance. Startups, researchers, and open models die first, entrenching the very giants creators fear.
[C1.4] Nor would this right even protect. A photo blocked from training by its host platform remains trainable the moment it is reposted, screenshotted, or crawled elsewhere. The internet has no single gatekeeper. A platform-specific veto offers symbolic control while pushing development to secretive scrapers and offshore actors who ignore refusal entirely.
[C1.5] The losers would be ordinary creators themselves. They depend on open platforms for discovery and increasingly on generative tools for drafting, editing, effects, and audience growth. If platforms face liability by default, they will lock down sharing, limit reach for non-consenting accounts, and charge for distribution. The web becomes more gated, models become narrower and more biased, and established stars with legal teams gain leverage while newcomers lose the commons that made them visible.
[C1.6] PRO warns creators will hide work without a veto, but enclosure is precisely what an opt-in default accelerates. Permission walls, watermarked mush, paywalled portfolios — those are the rational response to a system where every public post triggers legal negotiation. Keep learning open by default with a simple opt-out, and the incentive stays: post widely, be discovered, shape the tools that shape culture.
