[P1.1] The criminal justice system is not an abstract machine for balancing a cosmic ledger; it is a real-world institution tasked with protecting human beings. Almost everyone who enters prison eventually walks back out into our neighborhoods. When that day comes, what matters most is who walks out that gate. Retribution demands that punishment inflict suffering to pay back past wrongdoing.
[P1.2] In practice, designing prisons around deliberate hardship strips offenders of social ties, economic prospects, and stability, ensuring many return to society more dangerous than when they left. Rehabilitation targets the actual drivers of crime—such as addiction, untreated mental illness, and lack of vocational skills. Preventing the next innocent victim is a far higher moral and civic priority than inflicting pain on the last offender.
[P1.3] Even on its own philosophical terms, retribution is hollow. It rests on the fiction of "just deserts," yet no objective formula exists to translate a burglary or an assault into a precise number of years in a cage. Because nobody can measure how much state-inflicted misery "equals" a crime, retributive systems inevitably default to political posturing and escalating harshness. More fundamentally, retribution treats suffering as an intrinsic good—insisting that the world is better simply because a guilty person is in pain, even if that pain produces zero real-world benefit.
[P1.4] A modern state is justified in using coercion to protect its citizens and maintain order, not to indulge the primal impulse for vengeance.
[P1.5] Prioritizing rehabilitation does not mean excusing crime. Dangerous individuals are still incapacitated until they are safe to release. The difference lies in what punishment tries to accomplish. Retributive warehousing lets offenders rot in resentment, whereas rehabilitation demands active accountability: confronting the harm done, breaking destructive habits, and preparing to contribute to society.
[P1.6] Justice is better served by creating fewer future victims than by multiplying human misery.

[C1.1] The proposition gets the hierarchy backwards. Retribution is not a demand that the state maximize suffering; it is the demand that punishment be deserved, proportionate, and publicly tied to the wrong done. That is what distinguishes criminal punishment from coercive treatment or raw preventive detention. Rehabilitation is a valuable aim, but it is a secondary aim—something we pursue within a just sentence, not the thing that makes the sentence just.
[C1.2] Consider what happens when rehabilitation is treated as paramount. The offender who is already employable and drug-free may receive little punishment, while the disadvantaged addict may be held longer because he “needs” more treatment. Sentences then track offenders’ needs and administrators’ judgments rather than the gravity of what they did. That is not equality before the law; it is a lottery of disadvantage.
[C1.3] Retribution supplies the anchor: the burglar and the violent attacker are answered in proportion to their conduct, not their prospects or their therapist’s optimism.
[C1.4] Retribution also respects persons as responsible agents. It says to the offender: you did this, and you owe a debt. It says to the victim: the wrong against you was real, and the community condemns it. A rehabilitation-first system can easily push the victim aside and speak only of the offender’s needs.
[C1.5] And rehabilitation cannot justify punishment by itself; if someone is not dangerous and needs no reform, why punish at all? Only desert answers that question.
[C1.6] None of this denies that prisons should treat addiction, teach skills, and prepare people for release. But those are methods of serving a sentence, not the ultimate reason for it. Rehabilitation may make punishment more useful; retribution makes it punishment. A system that forgets that is not more humane—it is arbitrary power with a therapeutic vocabulary.
