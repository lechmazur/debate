[P1.1] The American housing crisis is fundamentally a crisis of manufactured scarcity. Over the past twenty years, dynamic cities added millions of jobs but legally capped the number of homes through rigid single-family zoning and extreme density restrictions. If cities had legalized denser housing—duplexes, townhomes, and mid-rise apartments—two decades ago, they would be profoundly more affordable today.
[P1.2] The core mechanism is supply and demand, but the secret ingredient is time. Housing affordability relies heavily on "filtering," the process by which older housing becomes naturally affordable as it ages. Today’s middle-income housing is yesterday’s new construction. By outlawing dense development in high-demand areas over the last twenty years, cities blocked the creation of what would now be a mature, naturally affordable housing stock.
[P1.3] A twenty-year head start on building would have prevented the massive, multi-million-home deficit that currently forces high-income renters and buyers to bid up the prices of older units, displacing middle- and low-income residents.
[P1.4] Furthermore, density directly alters the underlying math of urban real estate by dividing land costs. Urban land is inherently expensive. When zoning laws mandate single-family homes, one household is forced to absorb the entire cost of that lot. Legalizing density allows the land cost to be distributed across three, four, or ten households.
[P1.5] This fundamentally lowers the price floor of a neighborhood.
[P1.6] Opponents often point to the high cost of new luxury apartments today as evidence that density fails to produce affordability. This ignores the prompt's timeline. The astronomical prices we see today are the symptom of an accumulated deficit. When you starve a city of housing for decades, any new unit becomes a luxury commodity due to pent-up demand.
[P1.7] Had we opened the release valve two decades earlier, allowing supply to scale organically alongside job and population growth, the baseline competition for every single unit would be drastically lower. We would not be playing a desperate game of musical chairs with housing, and American cities would be dramatically cheaper today.
