[P1.1] Mandatory retirement is the workforce equivalent of amputating a limb because you cannot be bothered to diagnose the injury. It replaces evidence about a person's competence with a birthdate. That is the central reason to abolish it in most occupations: age is a spectacularly noisy proxy for capability, and routine management should not be built on a noisy proxy when direct measures exist.
[P1.2] A 67-year-old surgeon, lawyer, teacher, or accountant may be slower in some ways and sharper in others; the only honest question is whether that individual can still do the job safely and well. Employers already assess that through licensure, performance reviews, physical requirements, and professional standards. If someone is failing, use those tools. If someone is thriving, mandatory retirement fires them for the one characteristic they cannot change and cannot argue against.
[P1.3] That is not management; it is a blunt rule that throws away institutional memory and pushes willing workers into dependency.
[P1.4] The predictable CON case is that retirement ages create turnover, open promotion paths, and protect dignity by making exit normal. But those are conveniences for institutions, not proof that the individual worker should be legally expelled. Turnover can be managed with phased retirement, contract renewal, succession planning, and performance-based exit—without treating age itself as a disqualifier. Safety-critical roles can keep targeted fitness tests; the motion says most occupations, not every last one.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is simple: a mandatory age guarantees that some capable people are removed and some incapable younger people remain. An individualized system can actually identify who should stay and who should go. Abolition does not promise that every older worker is competent; it insists that competence, not chronology, be the test. That is the principle the rest of this debate should turn on.
