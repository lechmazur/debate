[P1.1] Access to education is not a luxury for students who happen to have the right sensory profile. It is a baseline expectation. The proposition before us simply asks that institutions treat accessibility as the default rather than something disabled students must individually beg for, prove they deserve, and wait weeks to receive.
[P1.2] Consider how accommodations typically work today. A deaf student enrolls in a course, discovers there are no captions on lecture videos, initiates a formal accommodation request through the disability services office, submits documentation, waits for processing, and only then—sometimes weeks into the semester—begins receiving materials that hearing peers had from day one. That student has already fallen behind. The proposition eliminates this gatekeeping by making the default inclusive and requiring justification for departures from it.
[P1.3] The "presumptively" language is doing critical work here. We are not mandating that every possible accommodation be provided in every context. We are saying the burden of justification should fall on the institution that wants to withhold accessible materials, not on the student who needs them. This is a modest procedural shift with enormous practical consequences.
[P1.4] And the benefits extend far beyond disability. Lecture recordings help students who are ill, who work jobs to afford tuition, or who are non-native speakers struggling to follow rapid speech. Captions reinforce comprehension for everyone—a finding consistent with how multimedia learning actually works. When institutions record and caption lectures by default, they are not making a narrow accommodation; they are improving the educational product.
