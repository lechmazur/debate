[C1.1] The proposition frames theatrical-first as "an old industry habit" that locks people out. That framing conceals what the theatrical window actually does: it is the revenue engine that makes ambitious, big-budget filmmaking possible at all.
[C1.2] When a studio spends $150–200 million on a film, theatrical exclusivity is what makes that investment recoverable. The window creates urgency—people go to theaters because that is the only way to see the film, and that urgency generates the opening-weekend cultural momentum that drives everything downstream. Collapse the window and you do not get "the same film, also at home." You get a different film.
[C1.3] The economics shift toward smaller budgets, safer formulas, and content designed to populate streaming menus rather than command a screen. The pandemic did not prove simultaneous release "works"—it showed that when forced, studios absorbed heavy losses, theaters went dark by the thousands, and major distributors walked back the model the moment they could.
[C1.4] The accessibility concern is real. But the proposition misdiagnoses the solution. The answer to theater inaccessibility is making theaters accessible—not abandoning theaters as a default. Shorter exclusive windows, captioned screenings, sensory-friendly showings, subsidized ticket programs, and improved physical access all address the exclusion PRO describes without dismantling the funding model that produces the films everyone wants to see.
[C1.5] Simultaneous release is a blunt instrument that solves one problem by creating a larger one: a permanently diminished theatrical sector that can no longer sustain the pipeline of ambitious titles.
[C1.6] PRO calls this "choice." But when the choice erodes the economic foundation that makes big films viable, it is not really a choice—it is a slow exit from the theatrical art form itself.
