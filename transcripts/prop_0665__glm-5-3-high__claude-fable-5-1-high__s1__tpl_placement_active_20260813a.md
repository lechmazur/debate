[P1.1] Severe mitochondrial disease is among the cruelest genetic lottery outcomes a child can draw. Mutations in mitochondrial DNA can leave a child's cells unable to generate energy properly, producing progressive muscle wasting, blindness, seizures, heart failure, and often death in infancy or early childhood. There is no cure, and often no meaningful treatment — only management of decline. Mosaic tissue loads mean even "mild" parental predictions can produce catastrophic outcomes in the child.
[P1.2] Against that backdrop, mitochondrial replacement therapy is a genuine medical breakthrough: reconstitute an embryo using nuclear DNA from the intended parents and healthy mitochondria from a donor, and the disease-causing mutation largely disappears from that lineage. This is not enhancement; it is prevention of a specific, identifiable, lethal pathology — functionally the same moral category as preventing neural tube defects with folate, or preventing HIV transmission from mother to child with antiretroviral therapy.
[P1.3] The question is not whether MRT works or whether it is ethical. Regulators in the UK, after years of expert review, licensed it, and children have been born. The question before us is narrower and, I'd argue, easier: should public health systems fund it?
[P1.4] Yes — for three reasons.
[P1.5] First, public health systems exist to prevent severe disease where prevention is cheaper, safer, and more humane than treatment. That is the founding logic of vaccination, newborn screening, and prenatal care. MRT is precisely this category: a one-time intervention that eliminates a lifetime of catastrophic care costs — ventilators, surgeries, specialist management, early death — and spares families repeated tragedy.
[P1.6] Second, market allocation fails here. These diseases are rare. Affected families are small in number, frequently already financially strained by caring for a sick child, and cannot sustain a private market. Absent public funding, MRT exists only for the wealthy — a two-tier system where access to escaping a lethal disease depends on income.
[P1.7] Third, public funding is what makes oversight possible. Licensed, funded programs generate registries, long-term follow-up, and standardized practice. Defunding doesn't stop desperation; it drives it abroad or underground.

[C1.1] Strip away the framing and the motion asks something quite specific: should scarce public money pay for an experimental germline procedure whose distinctive benefit is not preventing disease, but preserving genetic relatedness. Because the disease itself can already be prevented, cheaply and safely, without touching a single embryo's mitochondria.
[C1.2] A woman carrying a pathogenic mitochondrial mutation who wants a child free of that disease has options today. Egg donation eliminates transmission completely, using decades-old, well-understood technique. Preimplantation genetic diagnosis lets most heteroplasmic carriers select embryos with low mutant load. Adoption exists.
[C1.3] What none of these deliver is a child carrying the mother's nuclear DNA. That is the sole thing mitochondrial replacement adds. It is a real human desire, but it is not a public health outcome, and public health systems do not exist to purchase genetic lineage.
[C1.4] Now weigh what that lineage costs. MRT has produced only a small number of children worldwide. Long-term follow-up is essentially nonexistent, because the oldest are children. The technique does not remove all mutant mitochondria; some carry over, and reports from actual births show mutant loads rising well above the carryover level in some children, a phenomenon called reversion.
[C1.5] Nuclear-mitochondrial mismatch between donor and mother remains poorly understood. And because mitochondria pass down the maternal line, whatever goes wrong is inherited by granddaughters who never consented. This is not folate or antiretrovirals, which protect a child who will exist regardless. This is constructing a genetically novel person under uncertainty, with no way to recall the product.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, mitochondrial disease patients who already exist face thin specialist services, patchy diagnosis, and almost no funded research into treatment. Every pound spent on IVF-based germline engineering for a handful of families is a pound not spent on the living.
[C1.7] Licensing MRT is one thing. Funding it publicly is a declaration that the state judges this ready, routine, and worth prioritising over alternatives that already work. None of those three things is true.
