[P1.1] Being visible in public is not consent to being identified, catalogued, and followed wherever you go. A passerby seeing your face is fundamentally different from a system making thousands of faces searchable. The legal default should protect that distinction: biometric mass surveillance should be unlawful unless a narrowly defined emergency justifies an exceptional, temporary authorization.
[P1.2] This is not a ban on cameras or ordinary investigation. It addresses systems that identify or track people by bodily characteristics across public spaces without individualized grounds. Police can still interview witnesses, examine conventional footage, and pursue genuinely targeted investigations. What they cannot routinely do is turn everybody’s presence into an identity check simply because someone dangerous might be nearby.
[P1.3] The central harm persists even if the technology becomes perfectly accurate. Linking identities to visits to a protest, clinic, religious service, or political meeting can reveal intimate associations. People need not be arrested to feel pressure to stay away. Routine biometric surveillance gives governments—and private operators—the capacity to reconstruct lawful lives, while those monitored cannot meaningfully opt out of streets, stations, or public squares.
[P1.4] Errors make that power more dangerous. Scanning enormous numbers of innocent people for comparatively few suspects can generate false alerts, exposing bystanders to stops and suspicion. But better accuracy cannot solve the underlying authorization problem: an efficient system for tracking innocent people is still a system for tracking innocent people.
[P1.5] An emergency exception preserves the strongest public-safety uses without normalizing the infrastructure of suspicion. Authorization should require a concrete, imminent threat to life, demonstrated necessity, and no adequate less intrusive alternative. It must specify location, duration, and purpose, with rapid independent approval, deletion of unrelated data, and enforceable review.
[P1.6] CON must explain why ordinary usefulness warrants extraordinary power over everyone. Safeguards governing how a system operates do not establish when society should permit it. Presumptive unlawfulness puts the burden where it belongs: on whoever seeks to identify the public, not on the public to justify remaining untracked.
