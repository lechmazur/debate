[P1.1] A $25 entree is never actually $25. It carries an unwritten surcharge collected through social pressure. That is not a service model; it is a hidden labor cost. Restaurants should do what virtually every functional business does: set an honest price, pay employees from revenue, and tell customers the real cost before they order.
[P1.2] Tipping is a tax on civility with no receipt. A server’s income depends less on competence than on a customer’s mood, bias, or attraction. Identical service can produce different pay based on appearance, race, age, or which section a manager assigns. And the system splits the workforce: front-of-house staff collect volatile tips while cooks and dishwashers do not, even though the kitchen often determines the dining experience.
[P1.3] A clearly included service charge paired with higher base pay lets the restaurant distribute compensation equitably across the whole staff.
[P1.4] Tipping also transfers business risk onto the lowest-paid workers. On a slow Tuesday, the owner still owes rent and food costs, but the server’s wage shrinks. That is backwards. Higher base pay moves demand risk onto the business, which can manage it through pricing, scheduling, and operations.
[P1.5] It gives workers stable income, makes the job less precarious, and reduces pressure to tolerate harassment because “the customer pays the rent.”
[P1.6] The usual objection is that tips incentivize service. But workers can be trained, evaluated, and held to standards under base pay and service charges, like employees in countless other customer-facing jobs. Tips do not reliably reward good service; they reward flirting, speed, or lucky sections as much as competence.
[P1.7] The right test is transparent pricing plus stable compensation. Customers should see one honest number. Workers should know what they will earn. The hidden guilt payment fails both.
