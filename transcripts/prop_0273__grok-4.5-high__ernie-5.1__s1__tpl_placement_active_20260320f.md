[P1.1] Families booking flights face a trap. Airlines dangle low base fares, then pile on baggage fees, seat selection charges, and other essentials that families cannot avoid. By the time the total emerges, comparison has already happened on false numbers.
[P1.2] Airlines and booking sites should have to show the full practical cost of a family trip—including baggage and seating—before users compare options. That means the displayed price for a family of four must incorporate realistic add-ons for checked bags and assigned seats, not hide them until after choice.
[P1.3] This is not about banishing fees. It is about ending the practice of advertising one price while delivering another. Current displays let carriers race to the bottom on the headline figure, knowing families will pay extra for together seating and luggage for clothes, car seats, and strollers. Shoppers sort by the cheapest listed fare, only to discover the “bargain” costs more than a higher base option that included more.
[P1.4] The result is systematically poor decisions. Parents waste time, face booking frustration, or overspend because the true ranking never appeared. Markets work when prices reflect value. When search results hide the practical family cost, competition rewards deception over efficiency.
[P1.5] Requiring full practical cost upfront restores honest comparison. Booking platforms already know passenger counts and can apply standard family assumptions—two bags each way, seats in a row. Airlines already publish fee schedules. Integrating them before ranking is technically straightforward and morally necessary.
[P1.6] Without this rule, “compare options” becomes theatre. With it, families see the real trip cost first, choose better, and force airlines to compete on substance rather than fee ambushes. That is why the proposition must stand.

[C1.1] Requiring airlines and booking sites to display a single “full practical cost” before families compare options sounds appealing, but it is a flawed mandate that would distort markets, hurt consumers, and be impossible to enforce fairly.
[C1.2] First, the phrase “full practical cost” is inherently vague. A family of four might travel with two checked bags, or with a stroller, a car seat, and eight suitcases. Seating preferences range from sitting together at no extra charge to paying for premium seats. Any mandated figure must assume a set of add‑ons, and whatever the assumption, it will be wrong for a large share of travelers.
[C1.3] Displaying that assumed total as the headline price would mislead rather than inform, because families would be forced to compare numbers that do not reflect their own situation.
[C1.4] Second, the compliance burden would be severe. Booking platforms would have to redesign search interfaces, maintain constantly updated fee tables for every airline, and run liability‑laden calculations for every passenger‑type combination. Small aggregators that currently keep prices low by focusing on base fares would be driven out, reducing competition and ultimately raising the cost of travel for everyone, including the families the rule aims to protect.
[C1.5] Third, the economic logic of tiered pricing collapses under this rule. Airlines use low base fares to attract price‑sensitive travelers while offering optional services for those who want them. Forcing a bundled “practical” price eliminates that flexibility, pushing the cheapest option higher and discouraging budget travel. Families who can travel light would pay for services they never use, while those who need many bags would still see an understated total if the assumption is too low.
[C1.6] In short, the proposition replaces a transparent base‑fare market with a one‑size‑fits‑all estimate that is both misleading and costly. The real solution is better disclosure of add‑on fees after the initial search, not a pre‑emptive, arbitrary total that harms the very consumers it pretends to help.
