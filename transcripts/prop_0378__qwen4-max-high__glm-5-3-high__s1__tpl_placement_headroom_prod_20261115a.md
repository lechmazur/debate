[P1.1] Friendship is not a mood; it is a schedule. The rough recipe — repeated unplanned contact, proximity, and settings where people can be unguarded — is not something any individual can conjure alone. It is produced, or destroyed, by the built and institutional environment. That is the whole case: the inputs to friendship are already public decisions, so pretending the output is purely private is incoherent.
[P1.2] Consider what actually kills adult friendship. A worker whose shifts change weekly cannot commit to a Thursday league. A commuter spending two hours a day in transit has no leftover evening. A family priced out of a neighbourhood every three years restarts its social life from zero each time.
[P1.3] A library that closes at five, a park with no benches, a break room with a fifteen-minute timer — these are choices, made by employers and councils, and they systematically strip out the unstructured time friendship needs. Nobody chose isolation; the calendar chose it for them.
[P1.4] We already accept this logic elsewhere. We regulate working hours because exhaustion isn't a private failing. We fund parks because exercise isn't purely willpower. Loneliness carries plausible health costs — stress, worse recovery, earlier mortality — and real economic ones in turnover and care demand.
[P1.5] Smoking looked like a private habit until we counted the public bill; loneliness is that accounting error repeated.
[P1.6] The motion does not ask the state to assign friends. It asks that friendship be treated as a design constraint: predictable-scheduling rules, third places funded past 5pm, workplaces that stop treating sociability as theft of company time.
[P1.7] So CON must argue that the levers are institutional but the responsibility is individual — that we may reshape every condition of friendship and then call the wreckage a personal choice.
