[P1.1] Tech platforms that host and distribute user-prompted AI-generated content profit handsomely from that content—through ads, subscriptions, and increased user engagement—yet they currently face zero accountability when that content infringes existing copyrights. That is a broken system that dumps all the costs of infringement onto the creators whose work is stolen, while platforms reap all the rewards. These platforms are not neutral, passive intermediaries, the core premise of the safe harbor rules that currently shield them from liability. They actively curate, recommend, and surface AI-generated content to users, often prioritizing viral or high-engagement posts regardless of their origin.
[P1.2] When a user prompts an AI to output a new chapter of a bestselling novel using the original author’s exact prose, or generate a song that copies the melody and lyrics of a hit track, the platform hosts that content, runs ads on its page, and profits from the traffic it drives—while the original creator is left to foot the bill for legal fees, lost revenue, and the dilution of their work, with no recourse beyond a slow, often ignored takedown notice.
[P1.3] This rule does not ban AI content, nor does it punish users who generate non-infringing work. It simply aligns liability with profit: if a platform benefits from hosting infringing content, it should share in the cost of remedying that harm. Right now, platforms have no incentive to build even basic filters to catch obvious copyright violations in AI outputs, because they face no consequences for inaction. Shared liability would push them to invest in practical, scalable tools to catch infringement before it spreads, protecting creators without stifling legitimate innovation.
[P1.4] The current status quo lets platforms externalize all the risks of their business model onto the artists, writers, and musicians who make the content that AI models are trained on and copy. It is long past time to make them share the liability they helped create.

Under existing notice-and-takedown regimes, a platform that knows about infringing material and leaves it up loses its protection. So does one that ignores repeat infringers, or that induces infringement.
[C1.2] The status quo is conditional liability: act once you know, or pay. The motion replaces that with liability whenever content turns out to infringe, whether or not the platform knew or could have known.
[C1.3] That shift is the entire debate, and it fails for three reasons.
[C1.4] First, infringement is not visible on the face of content. PRO picks the easy case of verbatim prose from a bestseller. Most real disputes are about whether a work is substantially similar, whether it is parody or fair use, or whether the uploader holds a licence. Courts spend years on these questions.
[C1.5] A host cannot answer them at upload, across millions of posts, for works it has never seen. A filter can match copies. It cannot adjudicate law.
[C1.6] Second, when a platform is liable for every mistake, the rational response is to delete anything risky. Fan art, pastiche, commentary, and lawful AI-assisted work all go, because wrongly removing them costs nothing and wrongly hosting them costs damages. The people censored will be the legitimate creators PRO says it protects, and they will have no appeal.
[C1.7] Third, the motion targets the wrong party. The user chose the prompt. The model developer built the machine that produced the output. The host is the furthest from the infringing act, and often did neither.
[C1.8] PRO's "liability follows profit" principle would equally implicate the payment processor and the broadband provider. Liability should follow control and knowledge instead.
[C1.9] There is also the question of who survives. The largest incumbents can afford nine-figure filtering systems and legal departments. Small forums, startups, and open communities cannot. Shared liability would not discipline Big Tech.
[C1.10] It would hand them the market.
[C1.11] Hold platforms liable when they know and fail to act, not for what they cannot possibly know.
