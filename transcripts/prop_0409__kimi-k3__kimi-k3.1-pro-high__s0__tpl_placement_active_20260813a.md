[P1.1] A park is the rare place where your wallet doesn't determine your welcome. The motion asks whether parks, beaches, and waterfronts remain broadly free, or whether the drift toward ticketed lawns, reservation-only beaches, and sponsored "experiences" on public land continues. The case for free access rests on three pillars.
[P1.2] First, equality. Cities are already sorted by income—housing, schools, restaurants all filter by price. Public space is the last layer of shared life where a nurse and a banker occupy the same ground on equal terms. Convert that ground into paid experiences and you haven't just added a ticket booth; you've told lower-income residents that the best land in their own city—the waterfront their taxes may have cleaned up—is no longer really theirs.
[P1.3] Second, the economics cut against enclosure. These are classic high-externality goods: a free waterfront generates health, tourism, and small-business value that no ticket operator captures, so charging shrinks total value even when it raises revenue. And note the double charge: much of this land was acquired, remediated, and maintained with public money. Enclosure means taxpayers fund the asset and then pay again to visit it—a transfer of common wealth to private margin.
[P1.4] Third, enclosure changes what public space is, not just what it costs. Privately managed plazas come with rules—no loitering, no vendors, no protest, security moving along teenagers and the homeless. Even at zero ticket price, the civic function thins. A beach behind a wristband system stops being a commons and becomes a venue.
[P1.5] The likely reply is that private money pays for upkeep. But funding is a choice: taxes, concessions on the margins, and philanthropy maintain parks worldwide without fencing the lawn. What cannot be undone is the precedent—once access becomes a product, the best views migrate to the highest bidder.
[P1.6] Free public space is not nostalgia; it is infrastructure for an equal society. Defend the default: open gates, open shorelines, open waterfronts.

[C1.1] The motion treats every ticket booth as a fence around the commons. That is its central mistake. A city can keep a waterfront overwhelmingly open while charging for a premium deck, a concert enclosure, or a limited-capacity conservation area. Rejecting those tools on principle does not defend access; it makes access depend entirely on general taxation and whatever condition austerity leaves behind.
[C1.2] Zero price is not equal access. A free but polluted beach, an unsafe park, or a waterfront usable only after hours of queuing privileges those with time, transport, and nearby housing. Charges at scarce or costly amenities can fund cleaning, toilets, lifeguards, lighting, and accessibility, while exemptions and free hours protect low-income users. PRO compares an immaculate free park with a degraded paid one; the real comparison is often a maintained mixed-funding space versus a nominally open space people cannot safely use.
[C1.3] PRO’s externality argument also proves too much. Health and tourism benefits justify public support, not a universal zero price. Use creates costs too: erosion, litter, crowding, ecological damage. When demand exceeds capacity, “free” does not abolish rationing; it rations by queues, informal exclusion, or first-come apps.
[C1.4] Timed tickets or fees for high-impact use can ration transparently and finance restoration. Nor is taxpayer support plus a charge automatically a “double charge”: the question is whether the payment buys additional public value, not whether money changes hands twice.
[C1.5] Enclosure is a regulatory problem, not an inevitable consequence of payment. Governments can require continuous public paths, free shoreline access, protest rights, capped concessions, and reinvestment on site. A public authority can exclude through hostile rules just as a private operator can be bound to inclusive ones. PRO’s wristband dystopia assumes the contractual failure good policy prevents.
[C1.6] The standard should be maximum usable public access, not maximum privatization. Where a charge excludes without compensating benefit, reject it. Where a bounded paid experience preserves the free commons, funds upkeep, or prevents overuse, PRO’s blanket rule destroys value in the name of defending it. [P2.2] A paid experience is not static; it is a revenue asset with a duty to grow. Against it they offer [C1.5]'s contract—written once, enforced forever, by the same underfunded authority that needed the concession in the first place.

[P2.5] On rationing [C1.3], they conflate managing demand with monetizing it. Capacity can be rationed by free timed entry, as museums do daily. A queue allocates by time, which a nurse and a banker hold in roughly equal measure; a price allocates by income, which they do not. "Free rations too" is true—and irrelevant.

[C2.1] The “ratchet” in [P2.2] is a story, not a necessity. A paid deck can be confined to hardstanding, a concert enclosure dismantled, and a conservation charge limited to a fragile zone. More paid experiences do not logically mean less free access; they can draw pressure and revenue away from the commons. PRO must defend the claim that growth must cannibalize the free realm, not merely warn that it could.
[C2.2] Nor does “regulation erodes; incentives don’t” [P2.3] help them. Public budgets, booking systems, and informal rules also erode or exclude. If an authority is too weak to enforce shoreline paths and concession limits, it is also too weak to guarantee the immaculate free park PRO assumes. The serious comparison is not contract versus incorruptible government; it is diversified funding under enforceable public obligations versus access hostage to annual appropriations.
[C2.3] [P2.4]’s dilemma—either fees exclude or lifeguards go unfunded—is false. Fees can supplement taxation, apply to discretionary premium services, and vary by time or user. A family that never buys the cabana can still gain a cleaner beach and working toilets funded partly by those who do. Means-testing is one design, not the price of admission to every funding model.
[C2.4] Marginal exclusion must be weighed against decay, closure, and unsafe conditions.
[C2.5] On rationing, [P2.5]’s claim that a nurse and banker have roughly equal time is especially unconvincing. Shift work, caring responsibilities, transport, and flexible schedules make queues and instantly released free reservations deeply unequal too. Free timed entry can be useful; it does not follow that every scarce, damaging, or premium use should be tax-funded.
[C2.6] Finally, [P2.7] manufactures a concession. Protecting a substantial free commons is compatible with rejecting PRO’s opposition to expanding paid experiences. The motion presents a false choice: broadly free access can coexist with commercial offers. Judge whether people gain usable space and whether public assets improve—not whether every square metre remains outside every transaction.
