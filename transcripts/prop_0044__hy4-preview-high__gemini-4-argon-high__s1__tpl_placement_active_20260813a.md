[P1.1] Buying a smart device today is buying a promise nobody has to keep. The hardware sits in the box, but the product’s real life—security patches, app compatibility, cloud services—is controlled entirely by the manufacturer, buried in roadmaps and terms of service. Requiring a guaranteed support lifespan on the physical packaging fixes that asymmetry in one glance.
[P1.2] This is not a mandate to support devices forever. It is a mandate to stop hiding the number. A company chooses its own guaranteed window—two years, five, seven—and then prices, markets, and competes on it. Even a short window is valuable: buyers learn they are purchasing disposable electronics and should not pay durable-goods prices.
[P1.3] The stakes are concrete. Unsupported smart devices do not just frustrate owners; they become vulnerable nodes on home and corporate networks. A cheap camera or router that stops receiving patches can be recruited into attacks that harm people who never bought it. A visible lifespan lets security-conscious buyers and institutions avoid that externality, and rewards makers who maintain their products.
[P1.4] Consider two similarly priced smart cameras. One will get updates through 2027; the other through 2030. Today the boxes are silent, the spec sheets are mute, and the buyer cannot tell. A box label—“Guaranteed software and security support: 5 years”—turns longevity into a feature that can be compared, just like battery life or storage.
[P1.5] The burden is trivial. If a box can carry a barcode, a Wi-Fi logo, and regulatory marks, it can carry a support period. Manufacturers already decide these windows internally; the rule simply forces the number into the light at the moment of purchase, before the sale is final.
[P1.6] The box should tell the truth about how long the device will live, there on the shelf before you pay. If you can print “Wi-Fi certified,” you can print “supported until 2029.”

Smart devices depend on third-party chipmakers, upstream operating systems, and evolving security protocols. If companies are legally bound by the number printed on day one, corporate lawyers will demand the shortest, safest minimum to avoid massive liability if a component supplier drops support.
[C1.3] Worse, that defensive floor becomes an official ceiling: once a "2-year" label sets the contract, companies that previously supported popular devices for five years gain cover to abandon working hardware right on schedule to force upgrades.
[C1.4] Second, physical packaging cannot handle dynamic timelines. Notice the tension between [P1.4], which suggests printing "5 years," and [P1.6], which suggests "until 2029." Engineering support runs from product launch, not the random day a buyer pulls old stock off a retail shelf. If a box prints "5 years" and sits in a warehouse for eighteen months, the buyer is cheated out of a third of that promise.
[C1.5] Conversely, if it prints "until 2029," every box on the shelf visibly loses value each month, and if the manufacturer later extends support for a successful product, every box in retail channels displays outdated, artificially discouraging information unless physically recalled and relabeled.
[C1.6] Finally, a box label sells false security. "Support" is not a static hardware spec like storage [P1.4]; a single number obscures the crucial difference between feature updates, security patches, and third-party app compatibility. Meanwhile, the cheap, vulnerable cameras cited in [P1.3] are frequently sold by disposable shell brands that will gladly print "7 years" on cardboard, capture the sale, and vanish in eighteen months. Ink on a box cannot keep servers running; it only forces honest manufacturers to promise less.
