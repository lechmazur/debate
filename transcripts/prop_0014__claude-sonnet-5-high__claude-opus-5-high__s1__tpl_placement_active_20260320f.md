[P1.1] The federal government now spends more on interest payments than on national defense. That single fact should reframe this entire debate. Every dollar going to bondholders is a dollar that cannot build a school, fund a research grant, or expand a program — no matter how worthy that program is. Debt service has become a silent tax on every future budget, and it is compounding.
[P1.2] Here is the mechanism that should worry everyone, regardless of ideology: when debt grows faster than the economy, interest costs eventually crowd out discretionary priorities on both the left and right. This isn't a hypothetical — it's already happening. Rising interest costs are one of the fastest-growing lines in the federal budget, outpacing most other categories. This isn't abstract accounting; it is a mortgage against future taxpayers who never got a vote on today's spending.
[P1.3] My case rests on three pillars. First, sequencing matters: a household drowning in credit card debt shouldn't take out a new car loan before stabilizing its finances, even if the car would be nice to have. Government is no different — new commitments layered onto an already unsustainable trajectory make the eventual reckoning worse, not better.
[P1.4] Second, credibility matters: bond markets price in fiscal trust. If investors start doubting America's ability to manage its debt, borrowing costs rise for everyone, which then makes it harder to fund even the priorities we care about most, including defense and safety-net programs. Third, intergenerational fairness matters: continuing to spend today while pushing the bill to future taxpayers is not a neutral policy choice — it's a transfer of burden to people who have no say in it.
[P1.5] None of this requires austerity for its own sake. It requires priority-setting: before we add new permanent spending, we should demonstrate we can manage what we've already promised. That is not extremism — it's basic fiscal prudence, the same standard we'd apply to any institution carrying a debt load that keeps outgrowing its income.
