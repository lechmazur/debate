[P1.1] Adolescence exists for one reason: it is the training period for adult autonomy. A fourteen-year-old who has never sent a message a parent hasn't read, never walked to a friend's house untracked, never kept a confidence, arrives at eighteen with no practiced judgment—only a switch that flips. The motion asks for a *growing* right, not an absolute one, and that word does all the work. Rights that expand with demonstrated capacity are how every other domain of adolescence already works: driving, working, voting, consent to medical decisions in many jurisdictions.
[P1.2] Privacy should follow the same logic.
[P1.3] Three arguments carry the case.
[P1.4] First, monitoring at adolescent scale doesn't suppress risk; it relocates and deepens it. Teenagers under total surveillance don't become honest—they become skilled at evasion: burner accounts, deleted apps, coded language. The parent believes they have visibility; they actually have a false map. Meanwhile, the teenager learns that safety comes from concealment rather than disclosure.
[P1.5] A parent who discovers a real problem at sixteen through an unlocked phone has already lost the channel that mattered: the teen's willingness to come to them voluntarily.
[P1.6] Second, identity development requires an unobserved laboratory. Psychologists have long understood that adolescents try on beliefs, friendships, and even failures partly *away* from parental evaluation. A teen who knows every message may be read writes for the reader, not for herself. That's not protection; that's performance, and it delays the self-knowledge adulthood demands.
[P1.7] Third, the practical stakes are highest for the teens surveillance is meant to protect. The closeted gay teen, the kid with a crush, the one confiding a friend's self-harm to a peer—these disclosures happen only in private channels. Indefinite monitoring doesn't just inconvenience them; it cuts the lifeline.
[P1.8] None of this forbids parents from intervening when they have genuine cause. The motion targets *indefinite, child-style* monitoring as the default. Defaults shape behavior. The right default at fifteen is trust with a ladder, not a cage with a clock.

[C1.7] It is supervision proportioned to a brain, and a world, not yet safe for privacy as a right.
