[P1.1] The single largest barrier to cheaper, more reliable, cleaner electric power across the country is not generation cost or technology—it is a broken permitting system that gives small, unrepresentative groups of local holdouts effective veto power over transmission lines that deliver benefits to entire regions. Right now, major cross-border transmission projects take more than a decade on average to navigate siting approvals, even as identical infrastructure can be built in two to three years in most other developed economies.
[P1.2] That delay imposes enormous, widely shared harms for the benefit of a tiny few. A single county board, or even a handful of well-organized local opponents, can kill a high-voltage line that connects low-cost Midwest wind to major population centers, delivering substantial savings to residential ratepayers, or moves surplus power between regions during extreme heat or cold events to prevent the kind of cascading blackouts that left millions without power for days during the 2021 Texas freeze. The status quo does not protect meaningful local input—it creates hold-up power, where narrow groups can extract costly concessions or kill projects outright, even when most communities along a route support the line, even when the project serves urgent regional and national needs.
[P1.3] Prioritizing faster buildout by limiting that unilateral local veto does not erase local voice: communities still get fair compensation, input on routing to minimize local harm, and basic environmental safeguards enforced through clear, predictable timelines. What it ends is the ability of a tiny minority to impose higher power costs, worse fossil fuel pollution, and greater blackout risk on millions of people who have no seat at the local veto table. This is not a choice between local control and top-down fiat; it is a choice between rule by concentrated, unaccountable holdouts and a system that balances legitimate local concerns against the overwhelming public interest in affordable, reliable power for everyone.

Time-limited, balanced review that resolves local concerns through routing adjustments and fair compensation, rather than giving any single locality a final veto, does not steamroll communities—it makes good-faith consultation meaningful, because developers cannot be shaken down indefinitely and local concerns are weighed against the broader public interest, not held for ransom.

The Texas freeze was not worsened by too little local input, as CON claims [C1.7]; it was amplified by a fragmented grid that cannot move surplus power across regions precisely because cross-jurisdictional transmission cannot get past local vetoes.
