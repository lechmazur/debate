[P1.2] Outright voter fraud — impersonation, double voting, ballot stuffing — is in practice vanishingly rare, isolated, and prosecutable. The injury is real but bounded, and it actually invites a remedy that can restore confidence.
[P1.3] Legal corruption has no such remedy because nothing technically illegal occurred. A pharmaceutical lobbyist helps draft the rule that keeps drug prices high. A bank executive rotates into the regulator that then softens bank rules, then rotates back for a larger salary. [P1.4] The election can be perfectly counted and still produce a government that feels pre-decided.
[P1.5] That is the deeper wound to trust. Fraud tells citizens: someone cheated the count. Capture tells citizens: the count does not matter. [P1.6] They conclude that voting is theater, parties are brands for the same buyers, and honest officials are naive.
[P1.7] This damage is also broader and more durable. Fraud panics flare around a single contest and fade after certification. Capture is the daily background of politics: another revolving-door appointment, another loophole written for one industry, another regulator declining to enforce its own rules. It corrodes trust not just in elections but in courts, agencies, media, and expertise — everything citizens must rely on between elections.
[P1.8] A democracy can survive prosecuting a few fraudsters. It cannot maintain legitimacy when its citizens believe legality itself has been captured.
