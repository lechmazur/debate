[P1.1] The motion is about whether a national climate and energy system can be vetoed one neighbourhood at a time. It should not be. The EU and UK face binding decarbonisation duties, rising electricity demand, and a need to stop importing expensive gas. Onshore wind is among the cheapest new sources of power, and new grid capacity is the missing artery that lets windy regions power cities.
[P1.2] When projects stall, the bill lands on everyone: higher prices, more fossil generation, and turbines paid for but switched off because the network cannot carry their output.
[P1.3] Local opposition deserves protection: people should not lose homes, farms, views, or habitats without notice, compensation, and enforceable environmental limits. But the proposition asks whether that opposition should function as an absolute veto. It should not. A village can voice a powerful, legitimate objection; it cannot rationally claim the right to impose blackout risk, fuel poverty, and stranded clean power on millions elsewhere.
[P1.4] Relaxing restrictions means setting national strategic routes and permitting timelines, consulting on details, protecting sensitive areas, and binding developers to community benefits. A route from windy northern Scotland to southern demand, or a wind-rich rural county in Spain, should be judged by national standards—not by whichever local campaign lasts longest. It does not mean bulldozing parks or ignoring wildlife. It means treating some places as necessary for a shared network even when nearby residents object.
[P1.5] The alternative is incoherent: decarbonise, but only where nobody objects; expand the grid, but only if every mile finds unanimous local consent. That gives every local minority the power to cancel a majority-backed national plan. Onshore wind and grid expansion should face high environmental and social standards, not an impossible consent test. The intensity of local feeling is a reason for better process and compensation, not for surrendering the national decision.
[P1.6] Strong opposition should shape how projects are built, not whether they are built.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes local opposition for a nuisance to be managed. In a democracy it is a source of information, legitimacy, and consent—precisely the qualities that make infrastructure durable rather than merely permitted.
[C1.2] Start with what "relaxing restrictions" actually means once a community says no. It means the state overriding a decision made by the people who live with the noise, the altered landscape, the falling property values, and the construction traffic—often for decades. Payments in lieu of consent do not fix this; they price out what cannot be priced. A village told "you will receive a community fund" has been bought off, not heard, and the resentment that follows poisons every project that comes after.
[C1.3] Second, opposition is not noise; it is signal. Residents know things national planners do not: flood patterns on a proposed pylon route, a protected hedgerow missed in surveys, an access road that cannot take heavy transport, a peatland whose carbon release would offset the turbines built on it. Local objections have repeatedly caught genuine failures in environmental assessment. A system that treats strong opposition as the trigger for faster override discards that knowledge at exactly the moment it matters most.
[C1.4] The burden of proof should sit with those who want to build over people's objections, not with the people objecting.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's framing—that a village holding out causes blackout risk and fuel poverty—is a false binary. There are real alternatives: siting offshore where the wind resource is stronger and the land conflicts are not, investing in distributed generation and storage that host communities can share in, demand-side measures, and routing through genuinely consenting areas. These cost more or take time? Then that is the price of legitimate decarbonisation, and the case for cheap renewables is strong enough to absorb it.
[C1.6] Finally, democratic consent is not an "impossible consent test." It is the test we apply to compulsory purchase, to motorways, to nuclear waste sites—because these impose concentrated burdens on specific people for diffuse gains. Weakening it where opposition is strongest is precisely backwards.
