[P1.1] A workplace AI system is a governance decision disguised as a software upgrade. It assigns tasks, measures seconds, records keystrokes, flags “attrition risk,” sets performance targets, and can recommend discipline. When large employers deploy these systems unilaterally, workers lose control over the pace, privacy, and continuity of their own employment. Elected worker councils are the only body with standing, accountability, and collective knowledge to negotiate these changes.
[P1.2] The core case is power asymmetry. An individual employee cannot read a vendor’s model, challenge a productivity algorithm, or ask whether a shift-scheduling system punishes caregiving schedules. A council can demand disclosure, hire technical experts, compare workplaces, and negotiate safeguards before harm becomes systemic. A legal duty to negotiate converts AI deployment from a silent experiment on workers into a structured decision.
[P1.3] Critically, the motion requires negotiation, not a veto. Employers would still decide eventually, but only after confronting evidence, alternatives, and affected voices. That is not a threat to innovation; it is a design review. AI systems built without operator input fail: warehouse quotas become injury engines, call-center scripts drive customers away, hiring filters reproduce past bias, and monitoring tools destroy trust.
[P1.4] Negotiated safeguards—human review, audit rights, retraining, workload limits—prevent costly failures and improve adoption.
[P1.5] Large employers already manage complex legal duties on safety, discrimination, and data protection. A duty to negotiate with elected councils is proportionate: the larger the workforce affected, the stronger the democratic and practical case for collective voice. Workplace AI shifts risk onto workers while concentrating power in the employer. Legally requiring negotiation rebalances that risk before the algorithm makes the mistake permanent.

[C1.1] The proposal is not targeted at harmful AI; it is a blanket statutory gatekeeper requiring one mandatory bargaining channel with an elected worker council whenever a large employer deploys workplace AI. That instrument is overbroad, slow for iterative software, and likely to turn procedure into a veto by delay.
[C1.2] The definition is the first defect. “Workplace AI” includes far more than the alarming systems in [P1.1]: spam filters, captioning, fraud alerts, translation, maintenance prediction, and model updates. A duty tied to that label either sweeps in security patches and productivity software, or creates vague exemptions lawyers litigate for years. Law draws bright lines; AI deployment moves through pilots, versions, and parameter changes. The mandate would produce uncertainty where employers need clarity.
[C1.3] [P1.3] says negotiation is “not a veto” but “a design review.” That hides the dilemma. If the duty has teeth—disclosure, experts, deadlines, enforceable remedies—councils can block or delay by contesting adequacy. If it has no teeth, it is a notice requirement dressed as democracy and does not answer [P1.2]. A meaningful mandate is a procedural veto; a harmless mandate is symbolic.
[C1.4] Nor are costs only managerial. A hospital flagging deteriorating patients, a warehouse detecting unsafe lifting, or an employer adding captions for disabled staff would wait in the same compulsory queue as intrusive monitoring. Workers lose beneficial tools while process is negotiated. Existing law already targets actual injuries: discrimination, privacy, safety, wages, contract.
[C1.5] It regulates harm, not the adjective “AI.”
[C1.6] Elected councils fit badly. They represent current insiders, not applicants, customers, patients, contractors, or future workers, though hiring and service algorithms affect those groups. Councils may defend routines and headcount against tools that reduce drudgery; statute gives that defensive interest leverage over everyone else.
[C1.7] Use consultation, pilots, audits, and enforceable rights against specific harms instead. Do not make one elected gatekeeper the price of deploying software: that regularizes delay, privileges incumbents, and regulates by label rather than risk.

[P5.1] The debate closes on one question: can risk-based safeguards work before harm if the affected workers are not at the table? They cannot. A direct statutory duty still lets the employer draft the quota, choose the metric, and certify the impact check. Workers, applicants, and regulators see the result after the design is fixed; enforcement tends to begin once someone is already scored, disciplined, or denied a shift.
[P5.2] That is correction after the fact, not protection before the algorithm is imposed.
[P5.3] Mandatory negotiation changes the sequence. It forces the employer to justify the system to people who know the work: why this handling rate, what rest is built in, what gets flagged, how a scheduler treats caregiving shifts. That is not “mere conversation.” It is the point where disclosure, audit rights, and contest procedures acquire their actual content. Even if the employer later deploys unchanged, the mechanism has been exposed, alternatives have been argued, and the rejection is on record.
[P5.4] The first warning is no longer the injury.
[P5.5] So C4.7’s warning point cuts toward the proposition, not against it. Council negotiation supplies the ex ante accountability that makes every other safeguard usable before damage occurs. Without it, “safeguards” are retrospective tools. That is the reason PRO wins.
