[P1.1] American borrowing has stopped being background noise and started making the decisions. Total debt now sits roughly in the mid-30 trillions, with annual deficits running near two trillion even without a recession or major war. Interest alone costs on the order of a trillion dollars a year, rivaling what the nation spends on defense. That is payment for past consumption that builds no roads, deters no adversary, and cures no patient.
[P1.2] That burden compounds. Every dollar borrowed at today's higher rates locks in larger interest bills tomorrow, which then must be borrowed again. The result is a shrinking zone of freedom: less room to cut taxes, less room to respond to recession, pandemic, or war, and greater exposure to bond markets that can demand higher yields at exactly the worst moment. A country that must refinance enormous sums every year is not fully sovereign over its own budget.
[P1.3] That is why deficit reduction should now outrank most new spending promises. The word most matters. It allows true emergencies and narrow, temporary measures. But it sets the presumption correctly: no new permanent program should be created while the underlying trajectory is unsustainable, because each new commitment becomes another permanent claimant on a budget already choking on interest.
[P1.4] The alternative is the fantasy that borrowing is still free, that growth will magically outrun debt, or that good intentions erase arithmetic. Decades of that thinking produced the present trap. Deficits in good times were supposed to buy booms; instead debt grew far faster than the economy. Interest rates normalized, and the free lunch ended.
[P1.5] Prioritizing deficit reduction is not cruelty to the future. Endless borrowing is. A government that cannot control its deficits cannot keep its promises for long. Restoring fiscal room is what protects the ability to spend generously when it truly counts.

What binds it is real inflation and idle capacity, not an arbitrary trillions figure.
[C1.2] Deficit reduction should be judged by whether it makes the economy stronger, not by whether it shrinks a balance sheet before we act.
[C1.3] The strongest objection is opportunity cost. Public investment is not consumption forgone; it is capacity created. Public investment in highways, rural electrification, and basic research did not weaken the country; it enlarged the tax base that services debt. After World War II, debt exceeded 100% of GDP, and the country grew out of it through expansion, not austerity.
[C1.4] The risk today is the mirror image: letting bridges decay, moving energy too slowly, and leaving care unaffordable will impose larger future costs and weaker growth. Those future bills are also liabilities, just not ones labeled “debt” on a spreadsheet.
[C1.5] That is why “serious deficit reduction” should not outrank most new commitments. If a program raises productivity or prevents a costlier emergency, it improves the debt trajectory even if it adds to deficits on paper. And where arithmetic truly bites, the answer is not to pre-emptively abandon new spending; it is to make the tax system raise needed revenue from those who can pay. A blanket presumption against new permanent programs [P1.3] would lock in decay to protect a fiscal fetish.
[C1.6] The best rainy-day fund is not a smaller bond stock but an economy with modern infrastructure, healthy people, and secure families.
[C1.7] Prioritizing the debt number over the real economy is not prudence. It is austerity by another name: refusing to plant because we dislike the size of the mortgage.
