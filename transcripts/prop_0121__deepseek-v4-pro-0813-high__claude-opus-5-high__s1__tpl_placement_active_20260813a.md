[P1.1] A 19-year-old with panic attacks knows that seeking help may trigger a call, letter, or insurance notice to her parents. So she doesn’t seek help. That is the core harm this motion prevents. It is not about forbidding family involvement; it is about removing automatic disclosure that turns parents into unwilling gatekeepers.
[P1.2] Confidentiality is not an administrative luxury. It is a clinical precondition for honest treatment. Young adults are more likely to disclose suicidal thoughts, substance use, sexual trauma, or severe anxiety when they trust that disclosure will not travel beyond the treatment room. If the first consequence of asking for help is exposure, many patients will edit their symptoms, delay care, or drop out.
[P1.3] That produces worse outcomes and more expensive crises later.
[P1.4] The insurance-billing loophole makes this worse. Even when a clinician protects records, a routine explanation of benefits sent to the family plan can list “psychotherapy visit.” That means the young adult has privacy in law but not in practice. A college student on a parent’s plan faces a terrible choice: tolerate untreated depression or involuntarily announce the treatment to her family. For many, the safer short-term choice is silence.
[P1.5] Confidentiality also improves safety rather than undermining it. Young adults should be encouraged to involve trusted family when they choose, but voluntary disclosure is very different from automatic notification. Routine disclosure can punish help-seeking in abusive, rejecting, or highly controlling families. Safety exceptions for imminent danger can remain; no one is arguing against emergency contact in an acute crisis.
[P1.6] The motion concerns ordinary outpatient mental-health care, where privacy is often the reason a young person finally makes the call.
[P1.7] Parents paying premiums may feel entitled to know about every service, but paying for insurance does not purchase the contents of an adult child’s therapy session. The cost of deterred care is far greater than the cost of privacy-protective billing alternatives. We should not conscript the insurance system as a surveillance tool. Protecting confidential access for young adults means more of them get early, effective help.
