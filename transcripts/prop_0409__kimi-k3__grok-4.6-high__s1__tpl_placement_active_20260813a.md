[P1.1] The question here is not whether a city may ever charge for a concert on the lawn. It is whether the default condition of shared space — parks, beaches, waterfronts — is openness or enclosure. Everything rides on that default, because the trend runs one way: a lawn fenced once for a ticketed event is easy to fence again, and a beach lost to a private club almost never comes back.
[P1.2] Free access is what makes these spaces public at all. A park is not scenery; it is the one place where a nurse, a banker, and a street vendor stand on the same ground as equals. Price the entry and that equality ends, and the city sorts itself by income in its most visible spaces. These places were overwhelmingly built, cleaned, and defended with public money — charging people to enter what their taxes already created is double billing dressed up as management.
[P1.3] Enclosure also destroys the very value it tries to capture. A park's worth is not the sum of admissions; it is the daily spillovers — children's play, exercise that lightens public health costs, the casual encounters that turn strangers into neighbors, the watchful presence that keeps streets safe. These benefits exist only because access is unconditional; a half-empty ticketed garden produces none of them. Cities that fence the commons to monetize it sell the asset and forfeit the return.
[P1.4] The promised alternative — fees fund upkeep — confuses an exception with a model. Occasional charges for specific events coexist with a free commons; a business model that needs steady paying crowds cannot, because it must exclude the non-paying every single day. A city that cannot maintain parks from general revenue has a budget problem, not a case for privatizing belonging.
[P1.5] The motion asks which direction we normalize. Enclosure compounds; so does openness. Every free beach teaches people the waterfront is theirs; every gate teaches the opposite. Defend free access as the default, and everything else follows.
