[P1.1] If Brexit had never happened, Britain today would be a larger, more productive, more investable economy. The central mechanism is simple: membership in the single market and customs union made trade with our largest, closest, richest market almost frictionless. Brexit replaced that with paperwork, customs checks, regulatory duplication, and years of uncertainty. Those costs are not abstractions; they show up as lower trade intensity, weaker business investment, and slower productivity.
[P1.2] The decisive comparison is not “Brexit versus perfect government.” It is the same UK, facing the same pandemic, energy shock, and global slowdown, but without self-imposed barriers to its main market. Common-sense economics and mainstream forecasts point the same way: leaving the EU reduced GDP relative to staying. When your biggest customer is next door and you deliberately make selling to them harder, you end up poorer than you otherwise would be.
[P1.3] Investment is the clearest casualty. Firms invest when rules are stable and market access is assured. Brexit created prolonged uncertainty over tariffs, standards, data, finance, and supply chains. Some multinationals shifted projects and staff to EU hubs.
[P1.4] Smaller exporters often abandoned EU markets because customs costs are fixed per shipment and crush thin margins. Fewer customers means less scale, and less scale means lower productivity.
[P1.5] The labour market compounds the damage. Ending free movement worsened shortages in agriculture, hospitality, logistics, construction, health, and care. Those shortages raise costs and cut output. A points-based system can recruit some skilled workers, but it cannot replicate the single market’s flexibility for seasonal, local, and service roles.
[P1.6] Could new trade deals and deregulation offset this? Only marginally. No new deal replaces the EU’s proximity and scale, while regulatory divergence imposes fresh costs on firms still selling into Europe. The stronger pro-growth path was to stay inside and reform from within.
[P1.7] Brexit did not cause every British problem, but it made the economy smaller, less dynamic, and less attractive than it would otherwise have been. On output, investment, trade, and living standards, no-Brexit wins.
