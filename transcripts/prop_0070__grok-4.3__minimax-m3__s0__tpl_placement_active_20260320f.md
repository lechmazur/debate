[C1.1] The motion asks us to accept two large things at once: that we can confidently identify today which cities are doomed, and that a government mandate is the right tool to act on that confidence. Neither holds up.
[C1.2] Climate projections come in wide bands. Even recent IPCC trajectories for sea-level rise span tens of centimeters by mid-century, and storm intensification forecasts vary sharply by region. Handing that uncertainty to a "mandatory long-term plan" means locking cities into infrastructure and zoning decisions built on numbers we will revise. A coastal town told today that it must relocate on a 30-year schedule may turn out to need ten more years of adaptation, or five fewer.
[C1.3] Either error is paid in wasted public money or stranded residents.
[C1.4] Then there is the word "mandatory." Compulsory buyouts trigger eminent-domain fights, compensation disputes, and political paralysis that can swallow decades. The Louisiana Isle de Jean Charles resettlement took roughly fifteen years from first designation to first family moving, and that was a small, mostly willing community. Multiply that friction across an entire climate-vulnerable city and the "orderly" plan the motion promises turns into its own form of chaos - just slower.
[C1.5] Local officials facing organized resident resistance tend to do what P1.3 worries about: study, defer, repeat.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, less coercive tools are already moving. FEMA and state managed-retreat programs are buying the highest-risk parcels, not whole cities. Adaptation infrastructure - elevated roads, storm surge barriers, updated building codes, reformed insurance pricing - is bending the risk curve without uprooting households. Voluntary, targeted action lets governments concentrate scarce dollars where damage is documented, not where models say it might arrive.
[C1.7] The motion frames relocation as inevitable, then treats compulsion as the cure. But premature, mandated plans in the face of deep uncertainty mostly produce legal gridlock and misallocated resources. Better to adapt in place, buy out the worst-hit blocks, and reserve comprehensive relocation for the cases where retreat has already become unavoidable.
