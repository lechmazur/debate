[P1.1] When an aggressor shatters a neighbor's cities, someone will pay to rebuild them. The only question is who: the state that chose the war, or the people who suffered it — plus taxpayers in uninvolved countries. Justice answers the same way it always has: the aggressor pays.
[P1.2] Start with principle. Reparation for wrongful injury is the oldest rule in international law: a state that commits an internationally wrongful act must make full reparation, and a war of aggression is the gravest such act. Ukraine's reconstruction is priced in the hundreds of billions; roughly $300 billion in Russian central bank assets sits frozen in Western jurisdictions. The debt exists.
[P1.3] The money exists. The motion connects them.
[P1.4] Then enforcement — the heart of this debate. Aggressors do not pay voluntarily; Moscow will not write a check. The Security Council is paralyzed by veto. Without using frozen assets, "the aggressor must pay" always loses to "the victims and their allies will cover it."
[P1.5] The rare exception shows what reachability achieves: after Iraq invaded Kuwait, Iraqi oil revenues funded a compensation commission that paid out tens of billions. Frozen reserves are reachable money; refusing to use them is all that stands between a legal debt and its collection.
[P1.6] On legality: sovereign immunity is a procedural shield built for ordinary commerce, not a substantive right to wage aggressive war while keeping state wealth parked abroad. International law already accepts countermeasures — otherwise-unlawful acts answering a grave breach — and this motion is narrowly conditioned on aggression, the most fundamental norm the system has. No state that refrains from invading its neighbors has anything to fear.
[P1.7] Expect warnings about stability and precedent. Hold them to a standard: who actually abandons a reserve currency because an aggressor's assets are transferred under court judgment? The euro survived freezing the assets; it will survive transferring them. A precedent only aggressors can trigger is not a loophole — it is deterrence with a price tag.
