[P1.1] Tickets to large events are not ordinary consumer goods. A stadium, festival, or arena imposes real costs on the place that hosts it: traffic, noise, emergency services, disrupted businesses, and months of local political negotiation. Local residents and committed members are therefore stakeholders before they are customers. Reserving a meaningful share for them before the dynamic general sale is not a subsidy; it is a correction for a market that otherwise rewards speed, bots, and outside purchasing power while ignoring the people who make the event possible.
[P1.2] The central failure of an unrestricted dynamic sale is that it treats urgency as if it were devotion. A resident who has lived beside the venue for years can be outcompeted in minutes by a remote buyer with faster connections and no stake in the community. A member who has supported the organization through quiet seasons can be priced out at the moment of peak demand. That is not a well-functioning market; it is a short-term extraction strategy that converts loyalty and local goodwill into revenue and then wonders why permits, volunteers, and public support become harder to secure.
[P1.3] The proposition is deliberately moderate. A “meaningful share” is not a total lockout, and “before” the general sale is not a permanent exclusion. Organizers can verify residency, limit transfers, use waiting lists, and then open the remaining tickets to dynamic pricing. The market still functions; it simply stops treating the host community as an afterthought.
[P1.4] The decisive question is legitimacy. An event that sells its entire inventory to the fastest remote buyers may maximize one weekend’s revenue, but it damages the social license every large organizer depends on. Reserving tickets for locals and members protects the long-term product, not just the short-term price.

[C1.1] The harm my opponent names is real, but postcode is the wrong instrument for it. Bots, speed, and surge pricing hurt a devoted fan inside the city limits exactly as much as a devoted fan twenty minutes down the road. A residency check does not slow a bot; a bot with a local address sails through it. The claim that unrestricted sales confuse urgency with devotion ends in a rule that measures neither — it measures where someone sleeps.
[C1.2] Worse, the reserved block becomes the best arbitrage in the building. Hand a below-market claim to everyone inside an arbitrary line and you have created a lottery for people who never wanted to attend. Enforcement means ID matching at the gate, no gifting a ticket to your sister, no handing one off when you fall ill — machinery that mostly punishes honest fans while determined flippers rent addresses.
[C1.3] Then follow the money. Shrinking the general pool makes the dynamic remainder hotter, so the fan outside the line pays more to fund the discount inside it. That is a transfer from a working-class supporter in the next town to a comfortable homeowner near the venue. Membership priority is no better: dues turn loyalty into something you pre-purchase, which is precisely the complaint about wealth beating devotion.
[C1.4] Social license is real, and it is bought with noise curfews, traffic plans, community funds, and free allocations to local schools — commitments that reach every resident, not just the ones quick enough to click. And notice the escape hatch: "meaningful share" has no number and "local" has no boundary. Until those are named, the motion is a mood, not a policy. Protect the community from the event's costs; don't ration the event by address.
