[P1.1] The case for a 2040 phaseout of unabated coal rests on one simple observation: every voluntary climate framework we have tried has produced pledges, and pledges have not closed coal plants. Enforcement is the missing variable, and this motion supplies it.
[P1.2] Start with why coal specifically deserves a dated, enforced commitment. Coal is concentrated and legible — roughly a few thousand large power plants generate a disproportionately large share of global electricity-sector emissions. That makes it uniquely suitable for enforceable obligations.
[P1.3] And coal is uniquely dirty: it drives both the largest share of power-sector CO2 and severe local air pollution that kills people in the communities surrounding the plants. Nothing else on the emissions ledger offers this ratio of tractability to payoff.

[C1.1] The proposition fails not because coal is good, but because the instrument is bad. A dated deadline backed by trade and finance penalties is the climate policy equivalent of breaking a lock with a hammer: forceful, satisfying, and destructive of everything around the lock.
[C1.2] First, the enforcement mechanism punishes exactly the wrong countries. Coal-dependent developing economies — think India, Indonesia, Vietnam, South Africa — need capital to build replacement capacity *before* they can retire plants. Finance penalties cut off the very funding that makes a transition possible, forcing a choice between blackouts and sanctions. That is not a pathway to decarbonization; it is a pathway to defiance.
[C1.3] A country facing penalties for missing the deadline has every incentive to opt out entirely, and several large emitters would.
[C1.4] Second, 2040 is not a technical consensus; it is a wealthy-country timeline. Several European grids are exiting coal on schedule, as my opponent notes — because they industrialized on coal a century ago, built alternatives for decades, and have dense grids to interconnect. A country whose coal fleet is young — much of Asia's is — is being told to strand assets it just paid for, on someone else's clock, with the bill enforced at the border.
[C1.5] Third, trade penalties will be read as protectionism, because they partly are. Border measures priced by compliance status function as tariffs, inviting retaliation and litigation rather than cooperation. The climate regime has progressed through finance, technology diffusion, and patient negotiation — not coercive deadlines. CFCs, the comparison everyone reaches for, worked through incremental phaseout schedules with funding attached and flexible grace, not trade war by default.
[C1.6] The tragedy is that the good version of this policy — financed, staged, differentiated coal retirement — exists and is working where tried. The motion replaces it with a blunt mandate and a punishment mechanism that will fracture the coalition it needs. We should say no to that trade.
