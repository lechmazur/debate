[P1.1] Fiscal policy fails because the political market rewards borrowing. Legislatures face a commons problem: spending and tax cuts deliver concentrated, immediate benefits, while costs are spread across future taxpayers and easy to defer. Incumbents win by delivering goods now and leaving the bill to the next session and the next generation. So discretion has a structural deficit bias—not only in recessions, but in booms, when restraint should be easiest.
[P1.2] That is the true fiscal gap: the one we choose, not the one the cycle imposes. Legislative discretion ratchets debt upward, shrinks future room to maneuver, and exports risk to people who cannot vote today.
[P1.3] The remedy is a constitutional balanced-budget rule, but a cyclical one. It should bind the structural balance: over the cycle, receipts must match outlays, except for automatic stabilizers. When unemployment rises, benefits rise and tax revenues fall; the deficit widens automatically. When the economy overheats, revenues rise and the gap closes.
[P1.4] Recessions still get their cushion and booms still force savings. What the rule forbids is the discretionary structural deficit legislatures open even at full employment—the permanent gap that never closes.
[P1.5] Constitutional status is essential because a statutory rule is a promise to oneself, repealed by the same simple majority tempted to borrow. A constitutional provision, amendable but harder to change, is how a democracy ties its own hands for the reasons we accept independent central banks and bills of rights: it protects future citizens from present temptation while leaving elected government full authority over priorities, taxes, and spending composition.
[P1.6] Germany’s constitutional debt brake, with cyclical and emergency allowances, and Switzerland’s debt brake show such rules are operative, not utopian; they cut structural deficits and rebuilt fiscal space without banning countercyclical response. The lesson is simple: discretion produces deficit bias; a constitutionally anchored, cyclically adjusted balanced-budget rule with automatic-stabilizer exceptions turns fiscal discipline into a standing rule rather than a recurring hope.

In reality, voters punish runaway debt, and many democracies have swung from deficits to surpluses within a few election cycles precisely because discretion allowed correction without constitutional crisis. Tying policy into a numeric straitjacket does not eliminate politics; it moves decisions to courts and technocrats who cannot be voted out.
[C1.2] Worse, the "structural balance" the rule would constitutionalize is a contested estimate, not a fact—output gaps and potential GDP are revised for years, so the rule would invite litigation over econometrics rather than accountability at the ballot box.
[C1.3] The promised automatic-stabilizer exception is a thin cushion. Automatic stabilizers are passive and notoriously inadequate when a shock is deep. If a severe recession hits, legislatures under this rule would be forbidden from discretionary spending to rebuild infrastructure or protect households, because that would break the structural balance. In recent major downturns, automatic benefits alone proved insufficient; the discretionary fiscal response is what prevented deeper collapse.
[C1.4] The proposition would outlaw that response by default.
[C1.5] Constitutionalizing the rule also destroys flexibility for genuine emergencies. Pandemics, floods, and wars do not wait for supermajority amendments. PRO cites Germany and Switzerland, but those systems have repeatedly relied on escape clauses, off-budget funds, or suppressed public investment to comply on paper. A constitution that must be circumvented to save lives is theater, not discipline.
[C1.6] Fiscal responsibility is a matter of democratic accountability, not algorithmic constraint. Leave the tools in the hands of legislators who face voters, and reject a constitutional fetter that empowers judges and half-measures over the public will. The deficit bias PRO fears is better cured by transparent budgets and electoral stakes than by a rigid amendment that future generations will resent.
