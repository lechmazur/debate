[P1.1] Every dollar has a destination. The question is not whether people will leave the most exposed coasts — sea level rise and intensifying storms guarantee that eventually they will — but whether that departure is planned and dignified or panicked and improvised.
[P1.2] Consider the pattern we already know: a barrier island neighborhood floods, federal flood insurance and disaster aid pay to rebuild roughly what was there, and the same houses flood again in a decade. There are properties in the United States that have filed repeat claims many times over on structures worth less than what has been paid out. That is not resilience; it is a subscription service for destruction. And it is deeply regressive — the money reliably restores homeowners' assets while renters, mobile-home residents, and the low-wage workers who staff the coastal economy are displaced with no equity to recover.
[P1.3] So the first pillar of the case is fiscal honesty. Continuous rebuilding is not cheaper than relocation; it is only cheaper per episode, because we pay it in installments and never amortize it. Roads, sewers, seawalls, bridges, and electrical substations all have to be replaced too, and each replacement must be engineered for worse conditions than the last. At some point the state is paying new-city prices for old-city risk, and getting a place that will flood again.
[P1.4] The second pillar is that migration is happening anyway, and unplanned migration is expensive and cruel. When people flee, they land where housing is scarce, in cities that never planned for them, driving rents up and locals out. Deliberate inland receiving cities — sited above floodplains, outside serious wildfire interface, with water rights secured, transit and housing built at scale, and jobs anchored by relocated public institutions — convert a demographic shock into an economic development program for declining inland regions that already have infrastructure and shrinking tax bases.
[P1.5] The third pillar is agency. Voluntary buyouts at fair pre-disaster value, offered before the water arrives rather than in the trauma of the week after, let families choose. Rebuild-forever [clipped]
