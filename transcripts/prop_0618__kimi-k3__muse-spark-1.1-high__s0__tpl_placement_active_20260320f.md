[P1.1] A university seat is not an education. Yet under enrollment-based funding, the seat is exactly what taxpayers buy. Count heads on census day, cash the check, and whether those students ever graduate, or ever earn more than they would have without the debt, is someone else's problem. That is the system CON defends.
[P1.2] Money shapes behavior, and right now it shapes the wrong behavior. Enrollment-only funding rewards recruitment, not completion. The rational institution under that model spends on marketing and admissions pipelines, admits broadly, collects tuition, and then watches quietly as students drift out in year two. Roughly four in ten American students who start a bachelor's degree do not finish within six years, and the ones who leave often carry the debt without the credential—the single worst financial outcome in higher education.
[P1.3] Enrollment funding pays for that failure at exactly the same rate as success. Outcomes funding makes it expensive.
[P1.4] Earnings complete the picture because a credential is only worth what it commands. Some programs leave graduates earning no more than high school graduates, sometimes less, with five figures of debt for the privilege. When funding ignores earnings, it treats a degree that transforms a life as identical to one that merely transfers wealth from student to institution. Taxpayers are not buying diplomas on a wall; they are buying economic mobility.
[P1.5] The metric should match the purchase.
[P1.6] Anticipate the objection: this punishes open-access institutions serving poorer, less-prepared students while elite schools coast on wealthy admits. Read the motion. It rewards *demonstrated improvements*—progress, not raw levels. A community college that lifts completion and earnings for first-generation students wins; a selective school that merely inherits privilege and adds nothing does not.
[P1.7] Improvement-based, value-added measurement is precisely the design that protects the access mission while ending the free pass.
[P1.8] The question is simple: should public money purchase attendance, or results? We already know institutions respond to incentives—give them the right ones.

[C1.2] Pay for graduation rates and you will get graduation rates - not learning. [C1.3] Pay for earnings and you punish the very work public universities exist to do. [C1.4] The word "improvements" in [P1.6] does not save the plan. An improvement system rewards a previously failing school and penalizes one already doing well with little headroom left. [C1.5] Enrollment-based funding is not payment for failure. It funds capacity - the ability to keep the door open. Turn that into a bounty for private wages and you turn a public good into a placement agency.
