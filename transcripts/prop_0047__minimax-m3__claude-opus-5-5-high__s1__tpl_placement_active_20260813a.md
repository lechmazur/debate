[P1.1] The motion asks whether governments should tax fast fashion production to fund sustainable, local textile manufacturing—even at the cost of higher clothing prices for low-income consumers. The answer is clearly yes, because the supposed cost of acting is far smaller than the real cost of not acting.
[P1.2] Fast fashion is a textbook market failure. Its true cost is hidden in environmental damage, exploited labor, and waste—paid not by the consumer at checkout, but by communities downstream of dye-polluted rivers, by garment workers in unsafe factories, and by future generations facing climate collapse. A heavy production tax internalizes what the market refuses to price in: the carbon footprint of disposable clothing, the microplastics washing into oceans, the dead weight of mountains of textile waste. These externalities are not abstractions.
[P1.3] They land disproportionately on lower-income people, both as residents of polluted neighborhoods and as the workforce at the bottom of the supply chain.
[P1.4] The case against this motion will try to frame it as a choice between environmental virtue and poor people's wallets. That frame is false. The revenue from this tax does not vanish into a treasury—it subsidizes exactly the alternative that makes sustainable clothing accessible. Local manufacturing revived through public investment shortens supply chains, creates domestic jobs, and through targeted subsidies can hold retail prices on sustainable goods below what the unsubsidized market would charge.
[P1.5] The same policy that nudges consumers away from the worst option also expands the better one.
[P1.6] Consider the alternative honestly: doing nothing means cheaper t-shirts today and a textile-waste crisis, hollowed-out domestic industries, and compounding climate damage tomorrow. The poor will pay that bill eventually—through disaster relief, healthcare costs from pollution-related illness, and lost jobs in collapsed manufacturing regions. A modest, predictable price adjustment now is far gentler than the catastrophe it prevents.
[P1.7] The motion isn't asking consumers to sacrifice. It's asking markets to stop pretending pollution is free.
