[P1.1] A commodity windfall is not income. It is a country selling off a finite asset at prices it does not control. The real question is which institution handles that reality better, and the sovereign wealth fund wins on three grounds.
[P1.2] First, volatility. Oil, copper and gas prices can halve within a year. A cash dividend turns that volatility directly into household budgets and politics. When prices crash, governments face a brutal choice: cut a payment citizens now treat as a right, or borrow to keep it going.
[P1.3] Mongolia showed the danger: promises of mineral cash for every citizen collided with falling revenues and helped push the state into fiscal stress. A fund with a spending rule breaks that link. Chile saved copper revenue during booms and drew on it when the economy collapsed in 2009, spending countercyclically exactly when its citizens needed support most.
[P1.4] Second, the money belongs to the future too. The oil under the ground belongs to children not yet born as much as to today's voters. Distributing it as cash consumes the national inheritance in one generation. A fund converts a depleting asset into a permanent financial one.
[P1.5] Norway's fund now produces returns that will outlast its oil, and Botswana's diamond savings helped turn one of Africa's poorest countries at independence into a stable middle-income state.
[P1.6] Third, absorptive capacity. Pouring billions of dollars into a small economy all at once inflates prices, strengthens the currency and hollows out farming and manufacturing. That is Dutch disease. Investing abroad sterilizes the inflow.
[P1.7] Cash handouts accelerate it, and they cannot build the roads, grids and clinics that markets will not provide.
[P1.8] CON will raise corruption. But an unsound fund is a design failure that transparency rules and fiscal rules can fix. A cash payment is uniquely easy to hijack in the run-up to an election, and nearly impossible to reverse.

[C1.1] PRO’s central move is to equate “not distributing the windfall” with “managing it well.” A sovereign wealth fund is a vehicle for saving; cash distribution is a use of revenue. They are not mutually exclusive: a government can save part of a windfall, distribute part under a transparent rule, and invest the rest. Unless PRO proves the cash must be withheld altogether, its superiority is unproven.
[C1.2] More importantly, successful funds combine a fund with institutions the motion assumes away: withdrawal rules, independent boards, transparent audits, and governments able to resist raids. Norway cannot simply be exported to a rentier developing state. Where safeguards are weak, a fund can become an opaque patronage reservoir, financing politically connected managers, loss-making investments, and withdrawals disguised as “stabilization.” Direct transfers can also be hijacked, but a universal payment based on a public formula leaves less discretion than allocating billions among favored state firms and intermediaries.
[C1.3] The future-generations argument also cuts both ways. Foreign assets preserve value, but they do not automatically diversify a commodity-dependent economy or finance the education, health, and infrastructure that expand future earning capacity. Spending revenue on those public goods can leave a richer inheritance than parking money abroad while domestic businesses remain underdeveloped.
[C1.4] A fund’s benefits are often delayed and diffuse, while direct cash can provide immediate relief where jobs are scarce and public services are weak. A transparent, rule-based transfer can even be countercyclical if the rule specifies a sustainable amount from a windfall, rather than tying every payment to current commodity revenue.
[C1.5] Thus, the relevant comparison is which instrument delivers sustainable benefits under real institutional conditions. A sovereign wealth fund needs exceptional state capacity and need not benefit current citizens; well-designed cash can stabilize households and support development. The proposition’s universal claim therefore fails.
