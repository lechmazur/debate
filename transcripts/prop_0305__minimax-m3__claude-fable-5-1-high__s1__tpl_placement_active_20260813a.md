[P1.1] The moral objection is real, but it does not answer the only question that matters: what actually happens to the animals and the people if trophy hunting is banned? In places that have tried both paths, the answer is uncomfortable for our intuitions. Regulated, transparent trophy hunting has funded the protection of large tracts of habitat and given rural communities a direct financial reason to tolerate wildlife that would otherwise be poached or pushed out.
[P1.2] Consider CAMPFIRE in Zimbabwe, Namibia's communal conservancies, and the private-ranch model that dominates South African conservation. In each, hunting revenue pays rangers, anti-poaching units, and community dividends. Lions, elephants, and rhinos on these lands survive partly because a foreign hunter pays tens of thousands of dollars for a single permit. Remove that money, and the same land is typically converted to cattle or crops.
[P1.3] The animals do not relocate to a zoo; they disappear.
[P1.4] Photo-tourism sounds cleaner, but it cannot fund every landscape. It is geographically patchy, requires airports and infrastructure, and produces far less revenue per square kilometer than a hunting permit on remote land. Where the two compete on equal terrain, hunting often wins on raw conservation dollars — and on the ground, that means more rangers employed and more poaching stopped.
[P1.5] The motion is also conditional. It permits trophy hunting only where revenue is transparently used for conservation and local livelihoods. That is a high and deliberately narrow bar. It does not bless corruption, secrecy, or benefits captured by elites.
[P1.6] It accepts the moral distaste argument and builds structural safeguards around it.
[P1.7] So the opposition must answer two things, not one. First, on their preferred metric — animal welfare and human welfare — does banning trophy hunting actually improve outcomes? Second, what concrete institutional alternative replaces the revenue now flowing to rangers and communities? Aesthetic discomfort in wealthy capitals is a genuine feeling.
[P1.8] It is not, by itself, a conservation policy.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to keep killing legal on a promise. Strip away the word "transparently" and the sentence collapses, so notice how much weight that single adverb carries. In the real world, the condition almost never obtains. Revenue routes through outfitters, national treasuries, district councils and chiefs before a cent reaches a village, and at every stage it leaks.
[C1.2] Zimbabwe's CAMPFIRE, PRO's own showcase, saw the community share erode over the years as councils and central government took larger cuts. If the best-case example struggles to satisfy the condition, the motion is describing a hypothetical, not a policy.
[C1.3] Second, even where money is honestly spent, the product being sold corrupts the conservation it supposedly funds. Hunters do not pay top dollar for the sick and the old; they pay for the biggest tusks, the darkest mane. Killing prime males removes exactly the animals evolution and pride structure depend on. Shoot a pride male and the incoming male often kills the cubs.
[C1.4] Quotas get set by what the budget needs, not what the population can spare, because once rangers' salaries depend on permits, the hunting must continue whether or not the herd can afford it. Legal trophy channels have also been abused as cover, as when rhino horn traffickers posed as sport hunters in South Africa.
[C1.5] Third, the "no alternative" claim is false. Kenya has run a large wildlife economy for decades without trophy hunting. Trophy revenue is a small fraction of most range states' tourism income, and per-household community payments are typically trivial. Conservation trusts, biodiversity and carbon payments, and direct compensation for living alongside wildlife are all real institutional alternatives, and none require a foreign millionaire's desire to shoot a lion.
[C1.6] Finally, the moral objection is not "aesthetic discomfort in wealthy capitals." It is the same principle that stops us auctioning other things we consider wrong, however well the proceeds might be spent. A conservation model that only survives if wealthy people keep wanting to kill endangered animals is fragile, distorting, and unworthy of the label.
