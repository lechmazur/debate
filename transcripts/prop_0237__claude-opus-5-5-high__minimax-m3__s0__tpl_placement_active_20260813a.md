[C1.2] Asylum in U.S. law is a statutory right. If a person reaches our border and shows a well-founded fear of persecution, protection is owed. That right does not depend on which country they walked through first. A "safe third country" rule does not refine asylum.
[C1.3] It strips it, attaching a procedural box-checking test to something that should turn on actual risk.
[C1.4] The "expanded legal pathways" offered in exchange are exactly the wrong currency. Refugee processing, humanitarian parole, and expanded visas are discretionary programs. They can be — and routinely are — scaled down, paused, or revoked by executive action. Parole is, by statute, meant to be used sparingly on a case-by-case basis.
[C1.5] Refugee admissions are capped by a presidential ceiling that has been slashed before. Telling a Salvadoran mother fleeing gangs that her protection depends on a parole program the next administration can cancel with a phone call is not a bargain. It is a promise written in disappearing ink.
[C1.6] The proposition also assumes the "third country" is genuinely safe. Mexico was not safe for the thousands returned under Remain in Mexico: they were kidnapped, extorted, and attacked in border cities. Guatemalans and Hondurans fleeing gang violence often meet the same gangs just across their own border. The whole point of the Refugee Convention is that persecution does not respect borders, and neither does the duty to protect.
[C1.7] And every year of delay simply pushes asylum seekers into longer, deadlier journeys through smuggling networks and across desert crossings. A policy that promises fewer crossings by making the door harder is not a humane alternative. It is the same cruelty with cleaner branding.
[C1.8] Vote no.
