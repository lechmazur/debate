[P1.1] A somatic therapy treats one patient, with her consent, and its effects end with her. A germline edit is different in kind: it writes a permanent change into every cell of a future person who cannot consent, and into their descendants, indefinitely. That asymmetry is the heart of this debate, and it is why the line between legal somatic therapy and banned heritable editing is principled, not arbitrary.
[P1.2] First, consent is structurally impossible. We do not permit parents to authorize irreversible, experimental interventions on children except to treat existing disease in that child. Germline editing creates the child as the experiment. Every off-target effect, every disrupted regulatory sequence, becomes an unrequested inheritance passed down the generations.
[P1.3] Consent by proxy cannot cover risks that outlive the proxy by centuries.
[P1.4] Second, the harms are irreversible and compounding. Somatic gene therapy failures harm one patient; germline failures propagate. Basic biology tells us genes are pleiotropic—altering one locus can shift disease risks, development, and traits we do not yet understand. Mosaicism and off-target edits documented in early editing research are exactly the failure modes we would be hard-wiring into family lines.
[P1.5] Once released, an error cannot be recalled.
[P1.6] Third, the slippery slope is not a fallacy here; it is a business plan. Begin with tragic single-gene cases—Huntington's, Tay-Sachs—and the logic immediately extends to polygenic risk scores for IQ, height, appearance. Markets, not medicine, will drive demand. A prohibition on heritable editing for reproduction is the one firewall that keeps us from Gattaca-style selection markets, while leaving every legitimate therapy path open: somatic treatment for existing patients, and preimplantation genetic diagnosis for families avoiding known diseases.
[P1.7] That is the crucial point: nothing compassionate is lost. Children with genetic disease can still be treated—indeed, somatic therapy investment accelerates when capital cannot chase heritable enhancement. The ban costs us only the one thing we should never sell: the ability to design our descendants.
