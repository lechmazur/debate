[P1.1] Basic medical coverage is not a workplace perk. It is the difference between treating an infection early and waiting until it becomes an emergency. When that coverage is bolted onto a paycheck, layoff, caregiving, gig work, or illness itself can strip it away at the moment it is most needed. Single-payer financing severs that knot: a person is covered because they live here, not because a human-resources office still lists them as active.
[P1.2] Employer insurance fragments the pool by design. Firms with younger, healthier staff get cheaper group rates; people between jobs, in small firms, or too sick to work fall out or pay more. Single-payer puts the whole population in one pool for basic benefits, so the healthy share risk with the sick as a community rather than as a coincidence of who shares a payroll.
[P1.3] It also stops coverage from acting as a cage. Workers stay in mismatched jobs, delay starting firms, or accept worse wages because dropping a plan is too dangerous. That is not a labor market; it is a hostage arrangement. Financing basic care through taxes instead of fringe benefits lets people move, rest, and retrain without gambling their health.
[P1.4] The same structure breeds waste. Duplicate billing, eligibility checks, and network rules exist because coverage is a patchwork of employment contracts. A single payer for basic services does not make medicine simple, but it removes a layer of job-status bureaucracy that treats no patient.
[P1.5] The question is not whether some workers like their current plan. It is whether tying basic access to work status is a sound way to finance medicine. It is not. Jobs come and go.
[P1.6] Disease does not wait for the next enrollment period.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on one move: because employer coverage has gaps, we should hand all basic care to a single government payer. That is like answering a leaky roof by demolishing the house. The gaps are real, but they are patchable. Single-payer's core defect is structural and cannot be patched.
[C1.2] First, single-payer does not end rationing. It changes who rations and how. When one payer controls the whole budget, health care becomes a line item competing each year with pensions, schools, and debt service. When budgets tighten, and they always do, the payer doesn't cancel coverage.
[C1.3] It lets queues grow. Canada and Britain are well known for long waits for specialists, scans, and elective surgery. A patient whose hip replacement slips from months to a year is covered on paper and denied in practice. PRO says "disease does not wait" [P1.6].
[C1.4] Under single-payer, patients do.
[C1.5] Second, multiple payers give protection that one payer cannot. When employers and insurers compete for workers, a bad plan loses people and a good benefit spreads. Employers are large, informed buyers who push insurers on price and cover things like preventive programs that keep their workforce healthy. A single payer has no competitor to lose patients to and no exit for dissatisfied citizens.
[C1.6] When it chooses badly on drug coverage or hospital funding, everyone absorbs that mistake at once.
[C1.7] Third, PRO's harms have targeted fixes. Job-lock and coverage loss [P1.1, P1.3] are solved by portability, continuation subsidies, and a public backstop for people between jobs, not by abolishing what works. Germany and Japan finance coverage largely through employment-linked contributions, yet cover nearly everyone because the state pays in for the unemployed and retired. Work-based financing with a safety net keeps coverage universal while avoiding a budget monopoly.
[C1.8] Finally, PRO's waste point [P1.4] ignores the price of transition. Replacing premiums with taxes means large tax increases and forcing many people off plans they chose and value.
[C1.9] Fix the gaps. Don't swap a patchable system for a single point of failure.
