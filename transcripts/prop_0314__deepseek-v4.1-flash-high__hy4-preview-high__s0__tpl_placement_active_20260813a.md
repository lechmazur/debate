[P1.1] Public money is not free money. Every dollar a city puts into a new stadium is a dollar not spent on transit, schools, libraries, or tax relief. Professional teams are private businesses owned by billionaires, and stadiums are their workplaces. The question is not whether sports can enrich a city’s life.
[P1.2] It is who bears the risk and who captures the reward.
[P1.3] Right now, cities almost always bear the risk. Teams ask for land, infrastructure, tax abatements, bonds, and operating subsidies. Then they keep naming rights, media money, concessions, parking, and the appreciation of a franchise that may double in value. If the team later moves—as franchises have repeatedly done—the city is left with debt and a specialized building.
[P1.4] That is not a partnership; it is a one-way transfer.
[P1.5] Promised benefits are often the weakest part of the deal. Officials talk about jobs, tourism, and revitalization, but much stadium spending is substituted from other local entertainment, many jobs are part-time and seasonal, and the promised neighborhood revival may never reach the blocks that need it. Without enforceable terms, those promises are campaign rhetoric with a ribbon-cutting.
[P1.6] That is exactly why the proposition is modest. It does not ban subsidies. It says: if a team wants public capital, it must accept enforceable revenue sharing—a real cut of the upside, with audits and clawbacks—or enforceable neighborhood benefits, such as local hiring, affordable housing, small-business protections, and community funds. Enforceable means contracts, penalties, and public verification, not handshakes.
[P1.7] The alternative is what we have seen too often: cities bid against each other, teams play them off, and taxpayers finance the downside while owners pocket the upside. If a team will not share the gain or guarantee the neighborhood benefit, it should build with its own money. A city can love its team without being its venture capital fund.

[C1.2] The conditions are also self-defeating. A team with upside to write real checks can often finance more itself; forcing revenue sharing in weak markets removes the city’s leverage. If the team rejects the menu, the proposition bars subsidy and produces the [P1.3] nightmare: relocation to a friendlier suburb. Neighborhood clauses are no better.
[C1.3] Codified “affordable” levels, “local” hiring, and set-asides mainly invite litigation. The benchmark becomes a box to sue over, while the project stalls until surrounding private investment goes elsewhere.
[C1.4] The right test is net local benefit, judged case by case. Picture a mid-sized city with a failing riverfront arena and an owner willing to invest if the city prepares the site and extends utilities. There may be little local stadium cash yet, because the building does not exist; attaching revenue sharing is often impossible, and housing quotas can price the deal out. If adjacent apartments, restaurants, and tax base later materialize, the subsidy pays for itself even with no clawback check.

[C1.6] Fix those if weak. Cities should judge a stadium by its net local return, not be barred from acting unless they first win a redistribution fight. A city can reject bad deals, but it should not be forced to extract a lawyer’s menu from a private owner.

The public recovers its money, keeps the land, and captures new property and sales tax next door.
[C2.2] Yet that deal is barred unless the team also surrenders revenue sharing or accepts housing quotas. A ground lease is not a share of ticket revenue, and with little surrounding residential fabric, a neighborhood-benefit quota is a poor fit, so a perfectly accountable project becomes illegal. A retained land lease, non-relocation covenant, and bond repayment already align interests; the motion still demands more.
[C2.3] [P2.7] says such spillover can be captured through tax-increment terms or community payments. Sometimes yes, but increment only exists if the project is built. In a moderate market, forcing a revenue skim or vague neighborhood mandates on a thin-margin team can kill the anchor; the city then gets zero. Nobody demands a profit share from a port tenant or a convention hotel operator before building roads and sewers; we fund public works and are repaid by adjacent taxable growth.
[C2.4] Calling a stadium “exclusionary” does not defeat that; limited exclusion is exactly why the public recaptures spillover through broad taxes.
[C2.5] Contract clauses, [P2.8] notes, already include cure periods. True, but a construction default has a clear trigger and measurable damages. “Affordable” and “local hiring” invite open-ended third-party claims and lawsuits over a compliance standard nobody can certify. Auditing hundreds of seasonal part-time hires against shifting residency rules is not a construction punch list.
[C2.6] The real test is whether the chosen instrument—land ownership, lease-backed debt, staged infrastructure, incremental taxes—repays the public. The proposition refuses that judgment unless private owners first accept the menu. That is not accountability; it is a lawyer’s rigid surcharge on local discretion.
