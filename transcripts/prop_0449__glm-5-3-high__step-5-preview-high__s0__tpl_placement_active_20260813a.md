[P1.1] Coal is the single largest source of global electricity-sector emissions, and it is the fuel we can most credibly replace. That combination—biggest climate damage, cheapest substitutes—is why a 2040 phaseout of unabated coal with real penalties is not utopian; it is the most practical major climate policy on the table.
[P1.2] Start with the science of the deadline. Climate stabilization is a cumulative budget problem. Every year a coal plant runs, it spends budget that can never be reclaimed. A plant built today operates for decades, so a phaseout that merely "encourages" retirement by mid-century locks in emissions we cannot afford.
[P1.3] 2040 is roughly the point where an orderly, planned exit is still compatible with holding warming near internationally agreed limits; delay past that converts an engineering challenge into an impossibility theorem.
[P1.4] Second, "unabated" is the right scope. It targets the harm, not the fuel. Countries with genuine capture technology or coal-dependent grids get a lawful runway; what ends is business-as-usual combustion with the costs dumped into the atmosphere.
[P1.5] Third—enforceability. Every prior coal pledge of consequence has been voluntary, and voluntarism has failed precisely where it matters most. Power-sector decarbonization works when it has teeth: consider how border carbon adjustments suddenly concentrated minds in exporting economies. The proposition's penalties—trade measures and finance restrictions—are the instruments states already use against dumped goods and money-laundering.
[P1.6] Nobody calls those unworkable. The objection is never that enforcement is impossible; it is that it would work.
[P1.7] And the fairness concern cuts my way, not the opposition's. Penalties on noncompliance are precisely what protects early movers. Without them, a country that bears the cost of retiring coal watches a neighbor burn it cheaply and undercut its industry. Enforcement is what makes a just transition politically survivable.
[P1.8] The question is not whether coal eventually dies—economics is already killing it. The question is whether it dies in time, and whether the countries moving first get protection or punishment. Only a binding, penalized phaseout answers both.

[C1.2] A uniform date that treats a wealthy European grid and a lower-income Asian or African grid identically is not climate policy; it is a prescription for energy poverty and resentment that will harden opposition rather than build the broad coalition needed for durable decarbonization.

[C1.4] Enforcement is the deeper failure. That is exactly how every successful environmental treaty has worked.
[P2.2] The Montreal Protocol banned trade in CFCs with developing countries given longer runways and a fund, and it succeeded precisely because enforcement and assistance traveled together. "Equity cannot be retrofitted after coercion"—but nobody is asking for that. We're asking for equity built into the same instrument as the deadline.
[P2.3] The blackout scenario only works if retirement is overnight. It isn't. A 2040 deadline set now gives even a fast-growing grid fifteen years—longer than the planning horizon of most national grid operators. And the substitutes aren't hypothetical: solar and wind are the cheapest new generation across most of the developing world, and the gap is baseload firmness, which is a storage and transmission problem that fifteen years and redirected finance can address.
[P2.4] Diesel filling the gap is an argument against chaotic exit, not against a planned, penalized one.
[P2.5] On the CCS "loophole": it only loopholes if capture works. If carbon capture is as costly and unproven as my opponent says, wealthy incumbents won't use it, and their unabated plants face the same penalty. If they do use it, emissions are actually abated. The carve-out fails only in a world where capture is simultaneously too expensive to deploy and cheap enough to game.
[P2.6] Finally, the abuse objection proves too much. By that logic we should have no trade remedies, no sanctions regimes, no border adjustments—because any tool can be misused. We accept imperfect enforcement in every domain of international cooperation because the alternative is no enforcement. Voluntarism has had three decades with coal.
[P2.7] The plants kept getting built.

[C2.1] PRO's Montreal analogy collapses on contact with electricity. CFCs were a narrow set of industrial chemicals with drop-in substitutes and a compact industry; coal is baseload capacity, grid stability, industrial employment, and sovereign development. The same treaty form cannot enforce a fuel ban across economies at different income levels without becoming a permanent fight over who is compliant. [P2.2] quotes the protocol's fund as if finance automatically accompanies enforcement; the proposition, however, promises penalties first and leaves assistance to be negotiated after the coercive instrument exists.
[C2.2] Nor is the blackout answer settled by calling retirement "planned." Fifteen years is long for a power purchase contract and short for building transmission, storage, land acquisition, regulatory institutions, and workforce transition in a fast-growing grid. Cheapest new solar is not the same as dispatchable capacity that keeps hospitals and factories running when monsoons or heatwaves arrive. The diesel risk [P2.4] concedes is not chaos; it is the predictable result of forcing retirement before firm replacement exists.
[C2.3] The CCS point is also weaker than [P2.5] suggests. "Unabated" is not a binary that automatically disciplines rich incumbents. It invites disputes over capture rates, boundaries, and lifecycle emissions—disputes wealthy jurisdictions can litigate, subsidize, or lobby into lax thresholds while poorer plants face a hard closure date. The carve-out becomes a rich-world runway with paperwork.
[C2.4] Most importantly, [P2.6] overreads the abuse objection. We do not reject all enforcement because tools can be misused; we reject this enforcement design because it is open-ended, extraterritorial, and administered through trade and finance chokepoints that powerful states already control. Border adjustments are bounded to carbon content; a blanket coal phaseout penalty turns climate compliance into a standing veto over development finance and exports.
[C2.5] Voluntarism's failure justifies urgent, funded, differentiated support—not a deadline whose enforcement will be captured by geopolitics and whose fairness is promised only after coercion. Plants were built for power and growth, not because a voluntary label lacked teeth. Reject the proposition.

Yes, wealthy states will try to litigate thresholds in their favor—that's an argument for designing rigorous verification, not for having no rule at all.
[P4.2] And notice what my opponent's worry concedes: the dispute is over how fast capture counts as abatement, which means the deadline itself—the thing that matters—holds.
[P4.3] A2: The finance guarantee comes from the same signature that carries the penalties. That's not Montreal optimism—it's how the bargain structurally works. The scenario my opponent fears, penalties without support, is one no developing country would sign, which is precisely why it won't be the treaty that emerges.
[P4.4] What no treaty can survive is my opponent's alternative: support promised voluntarily, penalties on nobody, and every country rationally waiting for someone else to move first.
[P4.5] Notice the pattern across both answers. My opponent keeps locating problems in the design of enforcement and presenting them as reasons for no enforcement. [P4.6] The choice is not between a flawed treaty and a pristine one. It is between an imperfect, negotiable, binding instrument and a repeat of the decade we just lost. Only the proposition puts a date on the table that means anything.
