[P1.1] Democratic control of lethal force is not an administrative nicety. It is the line that separates legitimate state power from unaccountable violence. Every other norm of war rests on it: when people die in combat, the public that authorised the fight must be able to judge those responsible, and remove them if they fail.
[P1.2] Private combat contractors break that chain entirely, by design. Regular uniformed troops sit under a clear military chain, answer to civilian ministers, face public courts martial for abuses, and their deployments are subject to parliamentary votes and electoral scrutiny. Contractors answer first to corporate shareholders. When they act, there is no disciplinary chain leading back to an elected official that voters can hold to account.
[P1.3] This is not theoretical. In 2007, armed contractors opened fire on unarmed civilians in a Baghdad public square. Seventeen people died. None of the men who pulled the triggers served meaningful prison time.
[P1.4] No minister resigned. No government lost an election over the incident. The company lost a single contract, then won more work two years later.
[P1.5] This debate will not turn on whether contractors are cheaper, or fill staffing gaps, or operate faster. It turns on one non-negotiable standard: if someone can kill in the name of a democratic nation, that killing must be answerable to the public of that nation. That is impossible for private combat operators. That is why they must be banned from combat roles, completely and without exception.

[C1.1] Democratic accountability is measured at the level of policy, not payroll. When parliament authorises intervention, allocates funds, and sets rules of engagement, the decision to use force is democratically controlled. Whether the rifle is carried by a conscript or contracted specialist does not dissolve sovereign responsibility of the state that hired, equipped, and directed him. The chain of accountability runs from voter to cabinet to commander to mission; the contract is an administrative detail, not a firewall against public scrutiny.
[C1.2] The Baghdad incident PRO invokes does not prove that private combat roles are inherently unaccountable. It proves that legal jurisdiction was poorly designed at the time. States have since extended domestic criminal law to cover contractor conduct abroad, and can embed military lawyers and operational oversight directly into private units. A jurisdictional gap is reason to tighten prosecution and contractual discipline, not to abolish an entire category of force.
[C1.3] If private employment status were the true barrier to justice, we would expect uniformed troops to face automatic accountability. They do not. State soldiers have committed atrocities from My Lai to Abu Ghraib, often receiving negligible sentences or institutional protection. The chain of command did not produce transparency or proportionate punishment.
[C1.4] PRO mistakes a bureaucratic uniform for a functioning accountability mechanism.
[C1.5] Banning contractors would not restore democratic control; it would simply displace combat roles onto less visible, less controllable actors. Democratic nations already outsource violence to allied militias, intelligence assets, and remote air strikes that keep boots off the ground while civilians die in the rubble. When contractors are barred, states turn to proxy forces over whom they exercise even looser oversight, or withdraw entirely, abandoning populations to genocidal militias. Voters can hold no one accountable for atrocities their government pretends not to see.
[C1.6] The proposition confuses employment status with governance. Democratic accountability is strengthened by regulating, integrating, and legally binding contractors to military command structures, not by banning them and driving the same violence into darker channels. Fix the oversight; do not discard the tool.
