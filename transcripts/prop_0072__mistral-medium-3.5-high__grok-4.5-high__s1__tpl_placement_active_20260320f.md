[P1.1] History is not a victory parade. When public schools teach only national achievements and victimhood, they create a sanitized myth that undermines democratic citizenship. Germany’s mandatory Holocaust education, which has made denial socially toxic and ensured widespread historical knowledge, has anchored the country’s moral reconstruction and resistance to extremism. Japan’s textbook controversies, which consistently minimize or omit atrocities like the Nanking Massacre and the comfort women system, have fueled repeated diplomatic protests from China and Korea while enabling domestic historical revisionism to thrive, proving that omission is not neutrality but active distortion that serves those in power.
[P1.2] A curriculum that whitewashes a nation’s own abuses disarms its citizens by depriving them of the moral and analytical frameworks needed to recognize contemporary injustice. This requirement cultivates responsibility, not collective guilt. Without it, history becomes a weapon of the powerful to maintain unearned pride. The United States’ long minimization of slavery and Native American genocide in textbooks has not only sustained systemic racism but also contributed to persistent racial tensions, while South Africa’s truth education demonstrates how confrontation with past atrocities can begin healing societal wounds.
[P1.3] Nations that teach their atrocities honestly foster accountability, critical thinking, and vigilance against repetition. The alternative—curated heroism—produces a brittle patriotism that crumbles under scrutiny and leaves nations vulnerable to repeating their worst actions. True national strength lies in the courage to face hard truths. Governments must require this study to build citizens equipped to uphold human rights and resist future abuses.
[P1.4] Historical amnesia enables repetition; historical honesty enables prevention. This empowers citizens rather than shames nations, and the moral imperative is clear: students need the full truth to participate ethically in democracy.

[C1.1] Requiring public schools to emphasize major atrocities committed by the home country does not forge stronger democrats; it manufactures division and resentment under the banner of honesty. History instruction should equip students with chronological knowledge, causal reasoning, and the ability to weigh competing accounts—not install a government-scripted hierarchy that ranks national crimes above achievements or genuine experiences of suffering.
[C1.2] The mandate immediately founders on selection and motive. “Major atrocities” is never a neutral inventory. Which episodes make the list, at what age, and with whose framing? Conquest, slavery, civil war, and population transfers appear in virtually every national past. Handing the state power to designate the obligatory self-indictments invites curriculum capture by whichever faction currently dominates, converting contested memory into permanent civic liturgy.
[C1.3] Germany’s post-war approach arose from total defeat and the singular industrial character of the Holocaust; it is not an off-the-shelf template for ordinary plural societies.
[C1.4] Focusing the state’s coercive curriculum on ancestral guilt also corrodes the psychological and social preconditions of democratic life. Children who repeatedly absorb that their own community is uniquely stained gain little motivation to defend or improve the institutions they inherit. Shared identification is not “brittle myth”; it is the ordinary glue that allows majorities to accept electoral loss and minorities to claim belonging. Continuous compulsory emphasis on domestic wrongdoing thrives in polarized climates and feeds precisely the cynicism and revisionist backlash the proposition claims to prevent.
[C1.5] Honest history already reaches failures when evidence warrants them. The proposition confuses normal incompleteness with deliberate whitewashing and confuses mandatory national self-accusation with moral seriousness. Critical thinking and vigilance against abuse are better served by comparative study of human patterns of power, by primary sources from multiple sides, and by open debate—not by elevating one nation’s sins as the privileged core of the curriculum. Governments should reject the requirement.
