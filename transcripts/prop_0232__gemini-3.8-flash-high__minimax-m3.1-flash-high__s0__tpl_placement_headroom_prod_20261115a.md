[C1.1] The proposition assumes that cannabis is the essential engine of organized crime and that removing it will weaken criminal organizations. That is too narrow, and the causal chain is uncertain. Cartels are not cannabis monopolies; they also profit from cocaine trafficking, extortion, robbery, counterfeit goods, and control of territory and public officials. A legal market may reduce one source of revenue while those organizations pivot to other activities or become more violent in competition over them.
[C1.2] Legalization also creates a new contest over state licenses, taxes, and access to the legal market. Organized crime can infiltrate licensing boards, bribe inspectors, intimidate compliant businesses, and use distribution networks to supply the regulated sector without displaying overt signs of prohibition enforcement. The state must possess stronger administrative capacity to prevent this capture, yet national regulation is treated as if one uniform law would produce uniform enforcement. In countries with weak courts, corrupt local officials, and uneven infrastructure, the legal market may become another arena for organized influence rather than a decisive transfer of profit to the state.
[C1.3] Moreover, national rules will differ across borders: a licensed commodity in one country can be smuggled into another where prices are higher. Criminal organizations have long profited from such jurisdictional gaps. A national law therefore does not nationalize the supply chain.
[C1.4] The real question is not whether some illegal cannabis sales disappear. It is whether criminal organizations become less capable and less coercive overall. A cartel displaced from cannabis can concentrate on extortion and territorial control, attacks that may intensify if it must replace lost income. Conversely, weak legal-sector oversight can let criminal groups capture a business that appears lawful while retaining threats, fraud, tax evasion, and corruption.
[C1.5] Legalization alone guarantees neither reduced coercive capacity nor stronger state institutions.
