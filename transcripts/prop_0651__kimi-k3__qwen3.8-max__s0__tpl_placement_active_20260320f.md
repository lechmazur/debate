[P1.1] English's defenders will describe a bridge: one shared language letting a Brazilian chemist read a Korean physicist. Bridges are useful. But the motion concerns dominance, and a bridge is only a bridge when you can also get home. When one language becomes the price of admission to science, business, and the internet, what we have is not a bridge but a toll gate, and the toll is paid almost entirely by non-anglophone cultures.
[P1.2] First, epistemic narrowing. Researchers everywhere are evaluated by English-language publication, so the world's research agenda bends toward what anglophone journals reward. The plant disease threatening a specific Andean crop, the fisheries knowledge held in a Senegalese dialect, the soil practices a Tamil farming community refined over centuries — these go unstudied, unpublished, or translated into frameworks that flatten them. Knowledge that does not enter English increasingly does not count as knowledge at all, and much of it simply dies.
[P1.3] Second, domain loss and cultural erosion. A language that cannot do science, law, and commerce gets quietly demoted to the kitchen and the grandparents — and then it dies, taking its library of ecological and medical knowledge with it. The internet accelerates this: when search results, software, and technical documentation exist overwhelmingly in English, a teenager in Dakar or Jakarta absorbs the lesson that her language is for yesterday.
[P1.4] Third, the talent filter. Two equally capable engineers, one in London, one in Hanoi: one spends years and money acquiring academic English, pays editing fees, risks rejection over phrasing, and is read as less rigorous because of an accent. Prestige and funding pool in anglophone institutions even when the problems being studied lie elsewhere.
[P1.5] Against this, the other side will offer connectivity. But connectivity needs a shared second language, not a master language — and machine translation now supplies much of that cheaply. The goods of dominance are increasingly substitutable; its harms — dead languages, abandoned questions, homogenized thought — are permanent. A lost convenience can be re-engineered.
[P1.6] A lost knowledge system cannot. That asymmetry decides the motion.

[C1.1] English is best understood not as a trophy awarded to native speakers, but as the closest thing the world has to a public interface. The question is not whether English creates costs—it does—but whether non-anglophone cultures and knowledge systems are better off with a common working language than without one.
[C1.2] The proposition treats English as a toll gate. In reality, it replaces many toll gates. A Vietnamese epidemiologist, a Chilean climatologist, and a Ghanaian public-health officer do not need separate publishers, conferences, and standards in dozens of languages to coordinate during an outbreak. They need one shared grammar of technical exchange.
[C1.3] Machine translation helps, but it routes through English and depends on English-anchored corpora. English supplies that layer, and the result is not just convenience: faster error correction, larger replication pools, and access to funding, markets, and peers that would otherwise be trapped inside national borders.
[C1.4] The strongest harm claim is epistemic narrowing. But the alternative is often not a protected pluralism; it is isolation. Local knowledge becomes globally legible when it can be explained, compared, and defended across borders. English has carried Ayurvedic pharmacology, Polynesian navigation, Indigenous fire management, and African agricultural experimentation into wider scrutiny.
[C1.5] That translation can flatten, yes; invisibility kills faster. The practical route is better multilingual publication and translation, not abandoning the channel through which most of the world can hear you.
[C1.6] Cultural survival also depends on economic agency. English gives nurses, programmers, sailors, accountants, and researchers mobility and remittance power. Those resources often fund the very schools, media, and digitization projects that keep minority languages alive. A teenager in Jakarta can learn coding in English while speaking Javanese at home; these are not mutually exclusive.
[C1.7] The real enemy is gatekeeping: unfair editing costs, biased reviewers, underfunded archives. English dominance can create friction without being net destructive. The remedy is to widen access to the bridge, not to burn the bridge and declare isolation authentic.
