[P1.1] The decisive question is not which policy sounds thriftier, but which one keeps water flowing when reservoirs fail. Conservation stretches an existing supply; desalination creates a new one. Rationing manages shortage; desalination reduces it. That difference should decide the motion.
[P1.2] First, conservation has a floor, while water scarcity can deepen without one. Fixing leaks, pricing waste, and improving irrigation are valuable, and a sensible desalination strategy should include them. But once avoidable waste is removed, further “savings” increasingly mean less water for sanitation, households, farms, and industry. Rationing then protects the system by transferring the damage to people: poorer households suffer most, businesses cut output, and farmers lose productive assets.
[P1.3] A region cannot responsibly make its long-term security depend chiefly on repeatedly demanding that citizens use less of something essential.
[P1.4] Second, large-scale desalination provides the dependable backbone that scarcity planning requires. Rainfall, snowpack, and river flows are becoming less reliable, while populations and economies still need a baseline supply that does not disappear during a multi-year drought. Seawater desalination draws on a source largely independent of local rainfall. Its capacity can be planned, expanded, and contracted around, giving hospitals, cities, and industry confidence that emergency restrictions will not become a permanent way of life.
[P1.5] Israel’s coastal plants and the Gulf’s large systems illustrate the basic strategic point: where conventional freshwater is inadequate, manufactured freshwater can turn supply security from a weather forecast into an infrastructure choice.
[P1.6] Third, cost, energy use, and brine are serious design problems—not reasons to prefer rationing. Reverse osmosis, energy recovery, cleaner electricity, careful intake design, and well-sited outfalls can reduce these burdens. They must be weighed against the costs CON often leaves off the ledger: emergency water deliveries, lost production, damaged ecosystems from overdrawn rivers and aquifers, and the human consequences of unreliable taps.
[P1.7] “Primarily” does not mean exclusively, wastefully, or without conservation. It means putting investment behind the option that expands supply rather than institutionalizing scarcity. Desalination should be the backbone; conservation should make that backbone cheaper, not excuse failing to build it.

[C1.1] Investing primarily in desalination in water‑scarce regions is a strategic misallocation that magnifies scarcity rather than solves it. The real lever is demand: conservation and smart rationing deliver far more water per dollar than any plant can manufacture.
[C1.2] Consider cost. A modern reverse‑osmosis plant costs billions and consumes enormous electricity; every cubic metre of desalinated water carries a price tag many times higher than the same volume saved by fixing leaks, upgrading irrigation, or shifting to drought‑tolerant crops. Las Vegas, a city that once seemed destined for depletion, cut per‑capita use by nearly a third through tiered pricing, aggressive leak repair, and xeriscaping—achieving a savings that would have required multiple desalination facilities at a fraction of the expense.
[C1.3] The same principle holds in Israel, where water‑reuse and drip irrigation have supplied the bulk of agricultural needs, leaving desalination as a supplemental source, not the mainstay.
[C1.4] Energy and environment seal the case. Desalination’s power demand drives carbon emissions, feeding the climate change that intensifies droughts. Brine discharge harms coastal ecosystems, turning a supply solution into an ecological liability. A region that pours capital into plants is simultaneously deepening the very problem it seeks to escape.
[C1.5] Geography further undercuts the claim. The most acute scarcity often sits inland—think the American Southwest, the Sahel, parts of Australia—where seawater is unreachable without prohibitive conveyance costs. Building massive coastal infrastructure locks planners into a single technology and delays the systemic reforms that inland communities desperately need.
[C1.6] Finally, equity matters. Desalination raises water tariffs, burdening low‑income households, while well‑designed rationing—tiered allocations, subsidised basic blocks—protects the vulnerable. Cape Town’s “Day Zero” aversion hinged on strict rationing and public‑behavior change, not on any desalination plant.
[C1.7] The proposition asks regions to place desalination at the centre of their water strategy. The evidence shows that the centre must be conservation and rationing; desalination, if used at all, belongs on the periphery.
