[P1.1] A pandemic ends only when vaccination outruns transmission everywhere, not when wealthy countries are protected. Emergency authorization currently starts a race that manufacturers and rich governments have already shown they will not run fairly. A legal duty to transfer full manufacturing technology—processes, know-how, quality systems, and technical assistance, not merely patent text—to qualified producers in developing nations within a fixed window is the minimum rule that turns a scientific breakthrough into global control.
[P1.2] COVID made the alternative concrete. High-income markets were supplied within months. Large parts of Africa, South Asia, and Latin America waited far longer, while donation schemes and voluntary hubs arrived late and thin. Unchecked spread did not stay local.
[P1.3] It generated variants that reinfected vaccinated populations, reopened borders in reverse, and extended the economic shock. Patents were never the bottleneck. Firms withheld the practical package needed to manufacture at scale safely. Trusting the same voluntary promises next time is a decision to repeat that delay.
[P1.4] The fixed window preserves the incentive that matters. Companies can still capture early returns where demand and prices are highest in the first phase after authorization. Only then does the transfer obligation bite. That is not confiscation.
[P1.5] Pandemic vaccines are already built on public research, advance-purchase commitments, and liability protection. Public money should not buy a temporary monopoly over survival and then leave scale-up to charity.
[P1.6] Opponents will call this a chill on innovation. Ordinary drug markets can rely on exclusivity. Pandemic countermeasures cannot, because the social return depends on speed outside the first purchasing bloc, and because the next variant does not wait for licensing negotiations. A clear legal clock after emergency authorization is narrower, and more predictable, than open-ended waiver fights or ad hoc diplomacy.
[P1.7] It tells developers the rules before the crisis, and it tells the world that authorization is the start of coverage rather than the start of another queue.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that mandating technology transfer will make vaccines arrive faster in developing nations. It does the opposite, because it attacks the one thing that made ultra-fast vaccines possible in the first place: the expectation of controlled, near-term returns on a staggering, high-risk investment.
[C1.2] mRNA vaccine development cost billions and moved in under a year only because firms believed they would control the commercial window that followed authorization. A legal clock that starts ticking the moment authorization is granted converts that window into a countdown to forced disclosure. Rational firms respond to that incentive before the crisis, not after it—by slow-walking authorization filings, by launching first in jurisdictions without the mandate, or by underinvesting in the next platform technology precisely because pandemic countermeasures would carry a shorter guaranteed return than ordinary drugs.
[C1.3] The proposition claims speed is what saves lives; then it enacts a rule that makes firms less willing to be fast.
[C1.4] It also solves the wrong bottleneck. What actually stalled global supply was not a locked drawer of patents or specifications—it was tacit expertise, specialized inputs like lipid nanoparticles and single-use bioreactor components, and quality-control capacity that a legal transfer requirement cannot conjure into existence. Handing a facility a manufacturing dossier without the supply chain and trained workforce to execute it does not produce vaccines; it produces failed batches, contamination risks, and the very safety incidents that erode public trust in vaccination campaigns.
[C1.5] A law can compel disclosure. It cannot compel competence, and rushing unqualified production under statutory deadline pressure is dangerous, not humanitarian.
[C1.6] Voluntary and negotiated mechanisms already outperform this blunt instrument. The Serum Institute deal, regional mRNA hub partnerships, and licensing agreements tailored to a specific producer's actual capacity moved real doses faster than any rigid universal clock would have, because they matched transfer to readiness instead of forcing transfer to a calendar. A fixed legal window sounds decisive. In practice it trades away the incentive structure that produces vaccines quickly, for a paper guarantee that developing-world manufacturers may not yet be equipped to use.
