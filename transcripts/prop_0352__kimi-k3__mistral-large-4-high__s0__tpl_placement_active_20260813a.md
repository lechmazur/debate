[P1.1] Concentration in major metros is not a force of nature; it is a policy outcome. Governments already decide where the airports go, which universities get funded, where military bases sit, and how tax codes reward headquartering. Every one of those choices has pushed talent toward a handful of cities for decades. So the real question tonight is not whether government shapes geography—it plainly does—but whether it keeps tilting the board toward congestion or finally invests in the places it has been emptying.
[P1.2] Start with the costs "inevitability" dumps on the metros themselves. When opportunity concentrates, housing prices explode, commutes lengthen, and infrastructure strains past capacity. Accepting concentration doesn't avoid public spending; it commits governments to endlessly expensive retrofitting of overcrowded cities while perfectly good schools, roads, and housing sit underused two hours away. That is the worst of both worlds.
[P1.3] Second, decline is self-reinforcing and largely irreversible. Once the regional hospital closes, the bank branch leaves, and the school consolidates, the young nurse and the young electrician leave too—and they do not come back. A government that waits for the market to reverse this will wait forever, because no private actor recaptures the value of a functioning town. Keeping regions viable now preserves an option that, once lost, cannot be bought back at any price.
[P1.4] Third, national resilience. Food production, energy generation, water catchments, and supply chains physically live in these regions. A country that lets its productive hinterland hollow out becomes fragile—dependent, brittle, and strategically exposed. The pandemic also proved remote work can decouple good jobs from dense cores, making decentralization cheaper than at any point in history: broadband and anchor public institutions can hold a regional economy together for a fraction of what metro congestion costs.
[P1.5] Finally, legitimacy. A state that writes off whole regions tells their citizens their communities are acceptable losses. Equal citizenship cannot mean equal rights on paper and abandonment in practice.
[P1.6] The bar is not preserving every village. It is doing more—deliberately—so that viable regional life remains a real choice.

[C1.1] The proposition asks governments to fight economic gravity. For two centuries, every advanced economy has seen labor and capital flow toward density—not primarily because of airport placement or tax codes, but because cities multiply productivity. Engineers, financiers, and researchers cluster where knowledge spillovers are thickest and labor markets deepest. These agglomeration effects are global, historical, and stubbornly resistant to subsidy.
[C1.2] When governments try to override them, they do not reverse decline; they merely finance it.
[C1.3] The PRO case treats rural towns as assets that policy has abandoned. In reality, many are economic liabilities the market has already priced. When a factory closes because global competition moved production overseas, no amount of broadband or "anchor institution" funding recreates the wage premium that once drew workers there. Pumping money into non-viable regions does not preserve communities; it prolongs hospice care.
[C1.4] It keeps pensioners in towns with no doctors, children in distant consolidated schools, and taxpayers maintaining infrastructure for shrinking populations.
[C1.5] The opportunity cost is brutal. Every dollar spent propping up a declining town is a dollar not invested in the growing metros where most citizens—including most low-income households—actually live. If the goal is helping people, we should fund housing, transit, and opportunity where demand is expanding, not where it is evaporating. Mobility, not stasis, is the humane policy: help people move to opportunity rather than chaining them to geography.
[C1.6] Finally, "viability" is doing too much work in the motion. Governments cannot make every town economically self-sustaining without destroying value elsewhere. Accepting concentration is not abandonment; it is honest accounting. The alternative to "doing more" for rural areas is not cruelty—it is refusing to waste resources on geography the market has already judged.
