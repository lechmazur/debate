[P1.1] The kafala system has one defining feature: it hands a private employer the power of the state. Whether a worker can stay in the country, change jobs, or in some jurisdictions even leave, depends on their sponsor's signature. That is not a labor market. It is a leverage machine, and everything wrong with Gulf migration flows from it.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. When workers cannot quit, employers face no penalty for withholding wages, confiscating passports, or housing people twelve to a room. The abusive firm keeps its workforce anyway. Portable, worker-held visas restore the single most basic market discipline that exists everywhere else: exit.
[P1.3] An employer who wants to keep workers must pay them on time and treat them decently. No inspectorate, however well-funded, substitutes for millions of workers able to walk away.
[P1.4] Second, portability serves Gulf states' own economic ambitions. Every diversification plan — Saudi Vision 2030, Qatar's post-World Cup agenda — depends on attracting skilled, productive labor. But talented workers avoid systems where a bad boss becomes a jailer, and workers trapped in mismatched jobs are unproductive by definition. Labor mobility reallocates people to where they create the most value.
[P1.5] Kafala freezes them where they happen to have landed.
[P1.6] Third, this is proven feasible, not utopian. Qatar has already abolished the no-objection certificate requirement and exit permits for most workers; Saudi Arabia loosened transfer rules in 2021. The sky did not fall. Wages ticked up modestly, absconding cases fell, and businesses adapted.
[P1.7] The question is no longer whether reform is possible, but why it should stop halfway — with loopholes that let employers file "absconding" charges to sabotage transfers.
[P1.8] Be clear about what portability is not: it is not open borders. The state still issues visas, sets quotas, screens entrants. The only thing that changes is who holds the leash — a government accountable for policy, or a private boss accountable to no one. That is the choice, and it decides this debate.

[C1.3] Exit is only discipline when the worker has somewhere to go, and the two-thirds of Gulf migrants in low-skill construction, cleaning and domestic work have almost no bargaining power to exit toward. Portability liberates the accountant and the engineer. It hands the laborer a piece of paper.

[C1.7] The real choice is not who holds the leash. It is whether reform targets wage theft and recruitment debt directly, or dresses up unemployment as freedom.
