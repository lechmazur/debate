[P1.1] Consider what a new neighborhood built in the last forty years actually permits. A house, a lawn, a garage. Not a corner tavern, not a diner, not a barbershop with a bench outside, not a bookstore with a café. Those uses are illegal on most residential land in North America, and the commercial strip where they are allowed sits behind a mandatory parking lot, reachable only by car.
[P1.2] The third place did not lose a fair fight for people's affection. It was zoned out of existence, then blamed for its own absence.
[P1.3] The decline is not in dispute among anyone who has looked. Bowling leagues, union halls, fraternal lodges, neighborhood churches, local taverns, and independent shops have thinned dramatically over decades, and time-use surveys consistently show people spending more hours alone and fewer with anyone outside their household. Public health authorities in several countries now treat loneliness as a mortality risk on par with smoking. Whatever one calls the threshold for "declined enough," a society where the average adult's only shared space is a parking lot has crossed it.
[P1.4] Two mechanisms drive this, and both are governmental. First, single-use zoning and parking minimums make small, low-margin gathering spaces physically impossible to site. Second, land economics: a place that earns its keep by letting people linger over one coffee cannot outbid a bank branch for rent. The benefits it produces, trust between neighbors, informal childcare, elderly people seen daily rather than found weeks later, accrue to everyone and to no one's balance sheet.
[P1.5] That is a textbook case for public support, and the public library already proves the model works: government-funded, free to enter, no purchase required, and beloved.
[P1.6] So the choice is not government intervention versus free choice. The zoning code is already choosing, every day, on our behalf. The only question is whether it should keep choosing isolation, or whether we should fund and permit the places where a community can actually meet itself.

[C1.1] The case for public revival skips why people left. Older lodges, leagues, and taverns often declined because work schedules changed, households dispersed, and cheaper alternatives won voluntary time, not only because zoning made them illegal. Loneliness is serious, but it does not prove that subsidized physical venues cure isolation; it means we should measure the social outcome, not count new funded spaces.
[C1.2] Governments are bad at manufacturing informal community. A third place is valuable because it is voluntary and self-organizing. When a city funds selected cafes, bookstores, or halls, it must pick winners, write rules, chase usage numbers, and absorb losses. That is social service delivery.
[C1.3] Libraries prove public goods can work; they do not prove governments can fund commercial-style third places or engineer the trust that grows between regulars. A subsidized lounge can become another underused municipal amenity while the actual community remains elsewhere.
[C1.4] The funding is also regressive. Subsidies flow to developers and nearby property owners, to residents with leisure time, and to favored businesses. Tax costs fall broadly. A city choosing between a subsidized lounge and repaving bus stops has no obvious reason to put the lounge first.
[C1.5] The benefits are diffuse and uncertain; the bill is immediate and certain.
[C1.6] The zoning half is strongest only as deregulation. Removing parking minimums and allowing corner shops is permission, not active revival, and needs no public funds. Affirmative zoning for community space repeats the planning mistake: it presumes the desired gathering type, then yields empty amenity rooms, underused plazas, or developer concessions. Current restrictions are real, but they argue for removing mandates, not adding new ones.
[C1.7] A government that caused scarcity through zoning should not then demand credit and a budget to correct it. Better to lower barriers and let demand reveal where people actually gather, rather than funding a preferred image of neighborliness.
