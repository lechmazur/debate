[P1.1] Modernizing the electric grid faces a fatal structural flaw: regional lifelines are held hostage by fragmented local vetoes. High-voltage transmission lines must cross hundreds of miles and dozens of jurisdictions to connect where power is cheapest to generate with where people actually live and work. A pass-through county in the middle of that corridor experiences construction disruption and steel towers while the electricity flows to distant towns. Under today's patchwork rules, a single local board has every political incentive to say no, killing an entire multi-state project.
[P1.2] Granting every jurisdiction along a linear network an absolute veto does not create democratic balance; it creates a tragedy of the anticommons that guarantees paralysis.
[P1.3] The cost of that paralysis is severe and mounting. First, it undermines basic reliability. When extreme winter freezes or summer heatwaves slam one region, neighboring grids often have spare electricity nearby, yet homes go dark because interregional lines have been tied up in permitting for years.
[P1.4] Second, it inflates household energy bills and chokes off cleaner supply. Abundant, low-cost wind and solar projects sit stranded in interconnection queues across rural plains and deserts, unable to reach cities, factories, and surging industrial and digital demand. Without faster transmission buildout, ratepayers remain trapped paying congestion surcharges for dirtier, costlier local generation.
[P1.5] Crucially, reducing local veto power does not erase local voice or fairness. Permitting reform shifts final siting authority to state or federal regulators who weigh regional necessity while enforcing environmental guardrails, adjusting routes around sensitive land, and mandating direct landowner payouts and host-community tax revenues. No nation could have built interstate highways or natural gas pipelines if every town along the route held an unreviewable kill switch, and electricity is no less vital. Communities deserve real consultation and generous compensation, not the unilateral right to strand millions of neighbors on a fragile, overpriced grid.
