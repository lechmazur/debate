[P1.1] Solar geoengineering field tests do not stay local. When someone releases reflective aerosols even at small test scale, those particles drift across borders within days. There is no way to run an outdoor experiment that only affects the country running it. Right now, there are no agreed rules for who bears responsibility if a test shifts monsoon patterns, damages crop yields, or exacerbates drought on the other side of the planet.
[P1.2] No liability framework. No requirement to notify neighbouring states beforehand. No independent audit rights. No agreed threshold for what level of risk is acceptable to impose on others without their consent.
[P1.3] Opponents will say we need to test urgently to prepare for climate catastrophe. That urgency is exactly why governance needs to come first. Running experiments before we have rules rewards first actors who move fast, cuts everyone else out of decision making, and creates fait accompli technologies that no one will ever agree to regulate afterwards. This moratorium does not ban research.
[P1.4] It does not ban lab work, modelling, or indoor testing. It only pauses outdoor field deployment until we have binding global rules. That is not slowing down climate action. That is ensuring that when we do test these powerful technologies, we do it in a way that does not trigger new international conflicts, dump harms onto vulnerable populations, and destroy public trust before this work has even properly begun.
[P1.5] Waiting for binding governance is not permanent delay. It is the only responsible path that actually lets this field proceed without blowing itself up.

[C1.1] The moratorium's own wording defeats it. "Temporary" is conditioned on a "binding international governance framework," and binding frameworks are the slowest instruments in international law. The Paris Agreement, after decades of negotiation, is largely non-binding. The Montreal Protocol addressed a single industrial chemical class and still took years.
[C1.2] So this motion does not pause research; it suspends it indefinitely behind a trigger that may never fire. A moratorium with an unreachable sunset clause is a ban wearing a temporary costume.
[C1.3] Second, the transboundary argument proves far too much. Proposed field tests involve releases measured in kilograms — trivial next to the aerosols that aviation, shipping, and coal already drift across every border on Earth every day. If particles crossing a border justify a moratorium, then every smokestack and jet engine qualifies. The principle only works if it applies to detectable, consequential harm at meaningful scale, and at that threshold existing small-scale experiments do not come close.
[C1.4] The proposal treats a weather balloon like a weapons test.
[C1.5] Third, a moratorium binds exactly the wrong people. Transparent, university-led, peer-reviewed programs halt; militaries and private actors do not. A startup has already released sulfur balloons unilaterally, precisely because no legitimate pathway exists. Prohibiting accountable research does not eliminate experimentation — it strips it of oversight and public data.
[C1.6] Fourth, the sequencing is backwards. You cannot write sensible binding rules about risks you have never measured. Historically, governance follows emerging science — recombinant DNA research continued under scientist-led guidelines at Asilomar and regulation matured alongside the knowledge. Field experiments generate the evidence a framework needs; demanding the framework first guarantees it is written blind.
[C1.7] Finally, the true nightmare scenario is not a small test — it is a climate-stressed nation deploying at scale, uninformed, because we banned the research that would have told anyone what actually happens. The moratorium does not prevent that future. It manufactures it.
