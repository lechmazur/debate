[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether every labor standard can be perfectly tailored, but whether governments should let weak bargaining power set pay for millions of workers in industries where competition is built on undercutting labor. Sectoral bargaining answers that by setting minimum pay and standards across an entire industry, so firms compete on productivity and service rather than on who can pay the least.
[P1.2] The core case is simple: sectoral frameworks correct a market failure that firm-level rules cannot. In fragmented, low-margin sectors—cleaning, care, retail, hospitality, warehouse work—workers are easily replaced, employers face intense price pressure, and any firm that raises wages alone risks losing contracts to rivals. The result is a race to the bottom. A sector-wide floor stops that race.
[P1.3] It makes decent pay a cost of entry, not a competitive disadvantage.
[P1.4] This also shifts enforcement from individual complaints to industry standards. Today, many workers cannot recognize or challenge underpayment without risking their job. When standards are sectoral, they become visible, legitimate, and easier to inspect. That helps compliant employers who do not want to be undercut by wage thieves.
[P1.5] CON will likely say sectoral bargaining is rigid. But the motion concerns minimum pay and standards, not identical wages for every firm. Floors can vary by region, firm size, or occupation, and can be set with consultation and review. What they cannot do is allow employers to solve cost pressure by paying poverty wages.
[P1.6] Finally, stable standards reduce turnover and improve training. In care work especially, continuity matters. Better pay funded through the sector, not passed secretly to workers, produces better service and fewer crises.
[P1.7] On balance, governments should prefer institutions that make competition fairer and work more secure. That is exactly what sectoral bargaining provides.
