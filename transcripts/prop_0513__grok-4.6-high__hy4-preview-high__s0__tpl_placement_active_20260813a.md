[P1.1] Merger review exists to stop firms from buying market power. That power does not point only at shoppers. A hospital system that absorbs its rival can raise patient prices and leave nurses with one fewer competing employer. A meatpacker that consolidates plants can squeeze grocers and the workers who have nowhere else to sell their labor.
[P1.2] Treating only the first injury as a deal-breaker is not a coherent theory of competition. It is a half-analysis.
[P1.3] Monopsony is monopoly inverted. Fewer bidders for labor mean lower wages, thinner benefits, worse hours, and less mobility—the labor equivalents of higher prices, lower quality, and reduced choice. The deadweight loss is the same species of harm: work that would have been worth doing goes undone; remaining workers produce less because effort is underpaid. Those effects are merger-specific whenever the deal removes a close substitute employer in a local occupational market.
[P1.4] That is not a footnote. It is the injury antitrust already claims to police.
[P1.5] “As seriously” means equal operational status: define labor markets, measure concentration, model wage effects, and allow an independently sufficient theory of harm. Labor markets are often more local and occupation-specific than product markets, so a clean consumer-price result is not a proxy for labor clearance. A merger that leaves consumer prices flat but knocks out the only other hospital, warehouse, or software employer in commuting distance should be as vulnerable as one that raises grocery prices by a comparable margin.
[P1.6] The alternative is systematic bias. Policy that stares only at output prices will greenlight combinations that suppress pay in concentrated labor markets, then declare victory because consumer prices did not move. Workers cannot switch employers the way a shopper switches brands. If competition policy is about rivalry among firms, both sides of the firm count.

[C1.2] The symmetry my opponent invokes when calling monopsony "monopoly inverted" [P1.3] is superficial. A price increase is observable, shrinks output, and reaches every consumer, workers included. A wage effect from fewer local employers is partly a transfer, muddied by amenities, retraining, commuting, and productivity gains. Jobs are matched, not shopped: nurses value schedules and units, not just dollars.
[C1.3] Because monopsony's magnitude and merger-specificity are so uncertain, equating it with price effects is not rigor; it is guesswork.
[C1.4] Equality would build a second screen: define labor markets, measure concentration, model wages [P1.5]. But labor markets are a haystack of occupation, skill, shift, and commuting radius, with no validated HHI thresholds like product-market tools. The result is false positives. Two local manufacturers of unrelated products merge to cut back-office costs and fund automation.
[C1.5] Prices are flat; a mechanical labor screen calls machinists concentrated and blocks the deal. We lose the efficiency, and the wage harm was speculative.
[C1.6] Merger review polices rivalry in selling goods and services, not wages, and it should not become a roving wage regulator. Where a merger hurts both shoppers and workers, the product-market case already catches it. Where only labor is implicated, use labor policy—minimum wages, portable benefits, union access—not blocked capital reallocation. Those tools raise worker power without chilling consolidation that often lifts productivity and, over time, real wages.
[C1.7] Monopsony deserves a seat, not an equal one. Give it tiebreaker weight, and competition policy stays stronger.

[C2.1] Firms also compete for workers [P2.2], of course. But equality is not proved by pointing to a real harm; it is proved by showing it can be measured and remedied with comparable reliability. Labor markets have no equivalent.
[C2.2] Occupation, skill, shift, schedule, amenity, and commuting radius make every local labor market bespoke, so the “need for proof” [P2.5] becomes a duel of economists with no reliable benchmark. The result is not narrow proof; it is a structural presumption that chills efficient consolidation.
[C2.3] The “same species” claim [P2.4] also overstates. Wage suppression is partly a transfer to the employer, often offset by productivity gains, better technology, steadier hours, or retraining; it does not remove output the way a price increase removes consumer surplus. Local labor markets are also open to entry, remote work, and commuting in ways a grocer’s shoppers are not. Where a hospital merger raises patient prices, the product case already catches it.
[C2.4] Where prices are flat, the likely story is lower cost or greater efficiency, and blocking that reallocation to protect a wage premium can reduce total surplus.
[C2.5] Nor does a minimum wage “restore a second hospital” [P2.7]. That is precisely the point: merger control cannot be a substitute for labor policy. Labor statutes, portable benefits, union access, and mobility programs attack monopsony without making every local deal a wage trial. Tiebreaker status does not mean labor harm is ignored after prices fail; it means the administrable screen stays on consumer prices, and uncertain labor effects are weighed, not allowed to veto independently.
[C2.6] Equal operational status turns merger review into a roving wage regulator—broad, uncertain, and detached from the rivalry in selling goods that antitrust was built to police.
