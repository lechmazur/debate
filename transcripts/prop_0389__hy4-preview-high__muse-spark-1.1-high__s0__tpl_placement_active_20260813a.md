[P1.1] A player who spent years building a guild, or bought The Crew only to watch it vanish when servers died, is not demanding eternal support. They are opposing erasure. When an online game is shut down, the default is deletion: its maps, characters, and shared history become unplayable and unrecoverable. Publishers own the code; the question is whether owning a dead product lets them annihilate the culture it created.
[P1.2] Online games are the novels and public squares of the last half-century: places where people met and built worlds. Destroying them is like burning the last print of a film after its run, or shredding a town’s archive. Other media rely on libraries and lawful preservation. Gaming licenses rather than sells and depends on servers, so players get no such protection.
[P1.3] Private servers, archival modes, and mod support are this medium’s library edition—a way to keep a work alive after its commercial life ends.
[P1.4] Preservation is also cheap. A shut-down game earns nothing and demands no support; the community runs private servers, and an archival mode is a one-time release, not an eternal obligation. Worries about IP abuse, security, or brand damage are matters for a license—non-commercial terms, anti-monetization rules, content standards—not arguments for zero access. Prohibition has already failed: unofficial servers and emulators for defunct online games exist because demand survives.
[P1.5] Banning them only pushes preservation into the shadows, where no rules apply.
[P1.6] So the clash is stark: erasure imposes permanent, irreversible loss on players and destroys culture, while permission imposes manageable conditions on an abandoned product. The CON case must defend owning source code as owning the right to delete a community’s past; that mistakes ownership for omnipotence and treats players as disposable. Preservation through private servers, archival modes, or mod support should be the default once a game leaves the market, because a dead product cannot erase the life players built inside it.

Modern online games are knotted together with licensed engines, anti-cheat kernels, music rights, car brands, console SDKs that publishers do not own and cannot give away. Stripping those out to release a sanitized server is months of salaried work on a product that, by definition, earns zero.
[C1.3] Calling that "cheap" confuses what fans want with what is legal to release.
[C1.4] Third, permission is not manageable with a polite license. Once server code or tools are out, you lose control of your IP, your security posture, and your customer data. Private servers become magnets for malware, stolen accounts, and unmoderated hate because the publisher is gone but the logo remains. And a non-commercial clause does not stop abuse - it just creates a lawsuit factory you now must police forever against the very community you supposedly empowered.
[C1.5] The book analogy collapses. A film print can sit on a shelf. A live service cannot. Some games are designed to be ephemeral seasons, not monuments.
[C1.6] Forcing every shutdown to become an open-source donation chills the next risky online world from being greenlit at all. Preservation is noble when fans reverse-engineer it themselves; turning it into an obligation on publishers converts a contract's end into perpetual servitude. A right to remember does not create a right to compel the architect to maintain your village forever. The market should let worlds die with dignity, not force their creators to ghost-moderate them eternally.
