[C1.1] PRO’s treaty bans the visible act, not the capability. States will still possess, deploy, and in a crisis use anti-satellite weapons; the motion simply strips away the testing that makes those weapons predictable. A ban therefore does not prevent debris—it prevents knowledge. If a state needs an ASAT, it will build one through advanced simulations, component trials, and covert launches.
[C1.2] We will have traded transparency for a false sense of security while the underlying hardware remains untouched.
[C1.3] Monitoring “all kinetic” tests is also a verification nightmare. The same boosters, interceptors, and tracking systems serve missile defense and ASAT programs. Without intrusive inspection regimes that no major power would accept, every state will accuse others of cheating while shielding its own dual-use work. The treaty becomes a diplomatic fig leaf, not a safety mechanism, and compliance becomes impossible to prove, turning every peaceful launch into a diplomatic incident.
[C1.4] Worse, the ban removes a crucial safety valve. Open tests reveal the limits of a system, letting rivals calibrate rather than guess. Untested weapons in a crisis invite dangerous miscalculation: a leader may believe an ASAT will work when it will not, or may launch preemptively because the enemy’s capability is a terrifying unknown. The treaty increases crisis instability by making ASAT arsenals more opaque, not less.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO overstates the environmental payoff. Debris also comes from accidental collisions, upper-stage explosions, and non-kinetic attacks that a test ban does not touch. Nor does it remove the thousands of fragments already in orbit. It is a narrow, symbolic constraint on responsible states while aggressive actors simply ignore it, offering the worst of both worlds: no environmental gain and no security benefit.
[C1.6] The real goal should be verifiable rules of behavior, robust debris mitigation standards, and funded active-removal programs—not a test ban that leaves the weapons intact but shrouded in secrecy. A treaty that bans tests but not use is not disarmament; it is strategic blindness. CON opposes it.

[P2.1] The opposition’s central mistake is to demand that a debris-prevention treaty also abolish ASAT possession, then condemn it for failing at that different task. The motion targets the conduct that can turn a satellite into persistent orbital fragmentation. Simulations and component trials do not create that debris field; prohibiting kinetic tests therefore delivers a real environmental gain even if hardware, expertise, and other debris sources remain.
[P2.2] Nor is testing simply “knowledge” or transparency. Testing establishes confidence that a weapon will work, allowing it to be integrated into operational plans. CON’s crisis argument consequently cuts both ways: uncertainty about an untested weapon can discourage reliance on it, while demonstrated reliability can make attacks seem more usable. Testing may reassure one military planner, but it cannot recall the fragments imposed on every other space user.
[P2.3] Dual-use technology also does not make verification meaningless. Developing a booster is not the same act as conducting an ASAT interception. Launches, orbital trajectories, intercepts, and resulting breakups generate observable evidence. A treaty can define prohibited conduct without treating every missile-defense experiment or peaceful launch as a violation.
[P2.4] Cheating and disputes are possible under any security agreement; the relevant question is whether detection and reciprocal obligations reduce testing compared with voluntary restraint, not whether inspectors can achieve metaphysical certainty.
[P2.5] Finally, accidental collisions, upper-stage explosions, legacy debris, and non-kinetic weapons are reasons for complementary mitigation and remediation, not reasons to preserve an additional avoidable source of fragments. A flood-prevention policy is not worthless because storms have other causes. Likewise, active removal cannot justify creating debris faster than we can manage it.
[P2.6] CON offers speculative opacity dangers as a trump card while discounting persistent debris risk as symbolic. The weighing is reversed: military reassurance is contingent and temporary; orbital damage can outlast the governments that ordered it. A binding test ban addresses precisely that irreversible externality.
