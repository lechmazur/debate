AI companions are entirely different. They are single-user, closed-loop software.
[C1.2] Applying a regulatory framework designed to police public, multi-user networks to a private, generative interface is a mismatch that introduces profound unintended harms.
[C1.3] First, the "high-risk social" regulatory toolkit relies heavily on intrusive compliance, such as mandatory identity verification and parental notification. While logical for open social networks where minors interact with strangers, applying this to AI companions destroys the precise feature that makes them valuable: confidential, low-stakes interaction. For neurodivergent youth, those struggling with social anxiety, or minors in abusive homes, a private AI is a safe, non-judgmental sandbox to practice social skills. Forcing these apps into a high-risk surveillance regime strips away the anonymity that protects these vulnerable minors, driving them instead toward truly dangerous, unregulated online spaces.
[C1.4] Second, the "ordinary chatbot" framework is far from toothless; it is the superior tool for safety. Chatbot regulation focuses on developer-side input/output guardrails, strict data privacy, and deterministic safety filters. We do not need a massive, platform-style regulatory apparatus to manage a single-user interface. We simply need to enforce strict product safety and data-security standards.
[C1.5] Treating these apps as chatbots means we can legally mandate safety guardrails—such as hardcoded crisis redirects—without converting private, therapeutic user queries into a target for corporate and parental surveillance.
[C1.6] By treating AI companions as high-risk social platforms, we misdiagnose the risk, compromise youth privacy, and lock out the vulnerable minors who rely on these safe conversational spaces the most.
