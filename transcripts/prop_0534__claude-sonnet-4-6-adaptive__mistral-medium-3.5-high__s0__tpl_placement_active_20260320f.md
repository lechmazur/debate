[P1.1] The proposition is narrow and the standard it sets is eminently reasonable: self-custody wallets should be legal unless tied to *specific criminal conduct*. That is not a radical position—it is the ordinary principle governing every other piece of financial infrastructure we have.
[P1.2] A self-custody wallet is software that lets someone hold their own assets without relying on a third party. It is, in essence, a digital safe. We do not ban safes because burglars sometimes store stolen goods in them. We do not ban cash because drug dealers prefer it.
[P1.3] We do not ban encryption because terrorists use encrypted messaging apps. The law targets conduct, not instruments—and for good reason.
[P1.4] The moment we criminalize tools based on their potential for misuse rather than actual misuse, we have abandoned the presumption of innocence and replaced it with a presumption of guilt that attaches to everyone who uses a particular technology. That inversion should alarm anyone who takes civil liberties seriously.
[P1.5] Consider who actually uses self-custody wallets. People in countries with unstable banking systems who cannot trust local institutions. Journalists protecting financial sources. Ordinary investors who simply prefer not to expose their assets to exchange hacks or corporate insolvency.
[P1.6] The people harmed most by blanket restrictions are precisely these legitimate users, not sophisticated criminals who will find other means regardless.
[P1.7] Law enforcement already has the right tools. Financial surveillance operates at the chokepoints where cryptocurrency intersects with traditional finance—exchanges, brokers, payment processors. That is where identity verification, suspicious activity reporting, and transaction monitoring apply. Targeting self-custody itself does not advance that framework; it merely expands state reach into private property that has not been connected to any wrong.
[P1.8] The opposition must argue that mere possession of a self-custody wallet justifies legal restriction. That position requires them to prove that the tool itself is the harm—a burden they cannot meet without abandoning the foundational logic of liberal criminal law.
