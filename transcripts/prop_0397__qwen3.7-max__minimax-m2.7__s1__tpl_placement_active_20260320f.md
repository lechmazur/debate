[P1.1] The fundamental purpose of bankruptcy in a market economy is to provide a fresh start to the honest but unfortunate debtor. When a business fails, we liquidate and move on. When an individual drowns in medical or credit card debt, we offer a discharge so they can re-enter the economy as productive participants. Student debt is the glaring, destructive exception to this rule.
[P1.2] Under the current "undue hardship" standard, discharging student loans is practically impossible. Borrowers are forced to prove a certainty of hopelessness, stripping bankruptcy of its function as a safety net and turning it into a lifetime debtors' prison for the working class. We argue that bankruptcy law should make it much easier to discharge this debt specifically when borrowers face a long-term inability to repay.
[P1.3] This is not a call for blanket forgiveness or strategic default. Filing for bankruptcy carries severe, long-lasting consequences: a destroyed credit score for up to a decade, public record stigma, and the loss of assets. No rational borrower will willingly torpedo their financial life just to escape a manageable loan. The only people who file for bankruptcy on student debt are those who are already financially ruined.
[P1.4] By keeping the bar impossibly high, current law traps these individuals in perpetual default. They cannot buy homes, start businesses, or save for retirement. Instead, their wages are ruthlessly garnished and their tax refunds seized, draining resources from the broader economy. Aligning student debt with other unsecured consumer debt restores the basic economic logic of bankruptcy.
[P1.5] If a borrower genuinely lacks the long-term capacity to repay, holding them hostage to an unpayable balance benefits no one. We must restore the fresh start and let these borrowers rejoin the economy.
