[P1.2] Pay gaps are not only about overt discrimination. They grow from information asymmetry and anchoring. When compensation is hidden, employers can slot candidates differently based on prior pay, confidence, negotiation style, or stereotypes. Candidates from lower-information networks—often women and workers of color—are less able to identify a fair market number and more likely to be penalized for negotiating.
[P1.3] A posted range changes the anchor. If a posting says $75,000 to $85,000, the employer cannot as easily open with $70,000 for one candidate and $80,000 for another. The band narrows discretionary room, makes deviations easier to detect, and gives applicants a credible reference point.
[P1.4] This also helps racial gaps because much pay inequality is compounded by network and insider knowledge. A first-generation applicant may not know what similar workers earn; a posted range replaces whisper networks with public information. [P1.5] The efficiency cost is smaller than it sounds. A salary range is not a single wage. [P1.6] Meanwhile, ranges improve matching: applicants self-select out of roles that are not viable, recruiters spend less time on dead-end negotiations, and interviews focus on actual qualifications rather than salary bluffing.
