[P1.1] Childhood has undergone a massive, involuntary migration onto screens. During the regular school day, students already spend hours tethered to laptops, portals, and automated curricula. When the final bell rings, public schools face a decisive choice: double down on screen time, or protect the embodied world children are rapidly losing.
[P1.2] First, public schools must provide what cannot be downloaded. Digital enrichment scales cheaply, but what cannot be replicated on a screen is a soccer pitch, an art studio, an ensemble rehearsal, or a playground where kids negotiate social conflict face-to-face. Affluent families readily purchase private sports leagues, music lessons, and summer camps. When schools substitute digital programs for physical ones, they deepen a two-tiered system: wealthy children receive embodied mentorship and real-world agency, while lower-income students are handed another screen.
[P1.3] Second, this is a matter of developmental triage. Youth face compounding crises of isolation, anxiety, and chronic physical inactivity. Children do not suffer from an under-exposure to software; they suffer from an acute deficit of movement, tactile creativity, and unstructured peer socialization. Team sports foster somatic resilience and real-time collaboration.
[P1.4] The arts cultivate emotional regulation and sensory expression. Unstructured play builds organic problem-solving and independence. These are biological prerequisites for healthy development that no digital platform can simulate.
[P1.5] This motion does not reject technology; it demands proper sequencing. After-school hours and school budgets are finite. Before districts pour scarce resources into expanding purely digital modules that keep children isolated behind glass, they must guarantee that every student has access to the foundational physical, creative, and social experiences that make them whole. That priority belongs on the field, in the studio, and on the playground.

[C1.1] The motion’s load-bearing word is “before.” It does not merely praise sports, arts, and play; it would make digital enrichment wait until those are “more available.” That is a false bottleneck. Schools can expand both in parallel, and digital programs often have low marginal cost: an online animation lab, a virtual music-theory course, or a collaborative coding club can start while the gym schedule is being fixed. A student who needs advanced art instruction unavailable locally should not be told to wait for a better playground.
[C1.2] PRO calls the screen a “massive, involuntary migration” and demands “developmental triage.” But real triage treats the most urgent case first; it does not force every child into one queue. After-school access is unequal for concrete reasons: buses leave, parents work, disabled students face transportation barriers, rural districts lack specialized teachers, and unsafe streets limit outdoor play. Digital enrichment is sometimes the only enrichment a student can reach at 7 p.m. The motion would ration that flexible option to protect a romantic image of the soccer pitch.
[C1.3] The categories are also muddled. Digital arts, online collaborative game design, virtual robotics, and recorded dance instruction are creative and often social. Unstructured play does not require a new budget line. The real problem is unequal access to enrichment, not an excess of pixels.
[C1.4] Ranking media lets districts satisfy the slogan while leaving gaps: keep the existing team, postpone the online lab; or fund the lab, neglect the field.
[C1.5] The defensible rule is student need, not medium. Expand what works, combine physical and digital, and let a school run an art studio and an animation club, a soccer team and a sports-analytics club. The proposition turns complements into a hierarchy and harms the students with the least access. That is not caution; it is delay dressed as principle.
