[P2.2] When a park falls into disrepair, the proper response is to fix the funding, not to fence it off. [P2.3] The claim that only paid experiences can generate maintenance money ignores the reality of concession agreements. A city can contract a vendor to run a food kiosk or rent kayaks, collecting a fee that funds lifeguards and trail work, without surrendering the shoreline to a private club. In fact, the revenue from beach‑front condos and ticketed festivals often disappears into private hands, leaving the public area exactly as neglected as before.
[P2.4] Look at the pattern: wherever selective paid zones are introduced, the best view, the cleanest sand, the safest swimming area migrate behind the paywall. The “free” portion becomes the leftover. Families who cannot pay are shuffled to the crowded, less‑maintained edge. That is not expanded access; it is a sorting mechanism that reinforces the very inequality the proposition seeks to prevent.
[P2.5] Consider the example of many state parks that charge a modest parking fee but keep the beach and trail network free. That revenue covers restrooms, accessibility ramps, and ranger patrols, and the shoreline stays open to anyone willing to walk or bike in. No one is locked out by a turnstile.
[P2.6] The burden must fall on those who want to enclose common space. They must show that the enclosure raises the quality of the whole, not just the privileged slice.
