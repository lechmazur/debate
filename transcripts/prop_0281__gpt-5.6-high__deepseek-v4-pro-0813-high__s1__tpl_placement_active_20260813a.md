[C1.1] The motion treats any post-enrollment friction as a dark pattern, but sign-up and cancellation are not symmetrical events. Sign-up grants immediate access and begins an ongoing obligation; cancellation ends a billing relationship and may require proration, refunds, device deauthorization, and account security. A confirmation screen or a retention call is not automatically coercion. The relevant legal line should be deception or unauthorized billing, not the number of clicks.
[C1.2] The serious-welfare claim is overstated. Subscriptions provide enormous value, and automatic renewal is a convenience many households choose to avoid lapses and obtain lower prices. Fatigue and clutter are manageable through card controls, app-store subscription hubs, reminders, and competition. Where firms actually mislead consumers or bill without consent, existing consumer-protection rules already apply.
[C1.3] A blanket parity mandate is not needed to reach genuine fraud.
[C1.4] A parity rule would also backfire. If one-click sign-up must mean one-click cancellation, firms will not simply accept higher churn; they will slow enrollment, trim free trials and discounts, or raise prices. And “as easy as sign-up” is unworkable: is one save offer allowed?

[C1.6] Consumers already reward easy cancellation when it matters: services advertise “cancel anytime,” platforms centralize subscription management, and payment tools allow future charges to be blocked. The better policy is accurate pre-enrollment disclosure, clear renewal notices, and enforcement against genuine misrepresentation, not forced identical exit flows.
