[P1.1] A buyer scrolling through a resale listing has minutes to decide. The seller has had the item in hand for weeks. Add a counterfeiter who has studied stitching, holograms, and serial codes for months, and the asymmetry is brutal. That asymmetry is exactly why authentication should sit with the platform, not the buyer.
[P1.2] Three reasons. First, comparative advantage. Platforms aggregate volume. A single authenticator handling thousands of pieces a month learns patterns no individual buyer can.
[P1.3] The marginal cost of authenticating one more item at scale is far lower than forcing every buyer to become an amateur forensic examiner. The platform can hire specialists, build reference libraries, and update those libraries as fakes evolve. A buyer can do none of that between swiping and tapping "Buy."
[P1.4] Second, the externality. A fake that slips through does not only harm the buyer who lost money. It poisons the listing pool, depresses prices for legitimate sellers, and shrinks the market for everyone. The platform owns the marketplace; the marketplace's reputation is the platform's asset.
[P1.5] If the platform will not defend it, no one will.
[P1.6] Third, the alternative already fails. We see what happens when buyers bear the risk: chargebacks, returns, panicked forum posts, and a market retreating to the wealthy and the suspicious. Sneaker resale moved past this once authentication became the seller's hurdle rather than the buyer's gamble. The model exists and works.
[P1.7] Pretending it is too costly ignores the price of a market collapsing under fraud.
[P1.8] Counterfeiters industrialize. Buyer vigilance does not. Platforms should authenticate more and absorb more of the risk, because they are the only party positioned to do it cheaply, the only party whose incentive runs toward a trustworthy market, and the only party that already proves the model works where it has actually been tried.

[C1.1] The sneaker analogy sounds tempting until you look at what people actually buy. Resale is not just four-figure Jordans; it is millions of twenty-dollar dresses, vintage jackets, and children’s coats traded on Vinted, Depop, and eBay. Demanding that platforms authenticate more items and absorb counterfeit risk ignores a basic economic reality: it destroys the circular economy for everyday clothing.
[C1.2] First, the comparative advantage argument collapses on ordinary apparel. Sneaker authentication works because sneakers are standardized products with uniform molds, factory codes, and high margins. Clothing is completely heterogeneous—spanning decades of inconsistent tags, fabric blends, and wear. Physical inspection requires skilled, labor-intensive handling.
[C1.3] Routing garments through central depots doubles transit emissions, adds weeks of delay, and incurs processing fees that easily dwarf the price of an ordinary garment. High platform fees price out casual sellers, driving cost-conscious shoppers straight back to cheap, disposable fast fashion.
[C1.4] Second, absorbing risk breeds systemic moral hazard. When platforms guarantee every purchase and absorb all counterfeit liability, they become prime targets for swap fraud. Bad actors buy authentic garments, swap them with cheap replicas, and demand platform-funded refunds under the counterfeit guarantee. Absorbing risk does not eliminate fraud; it simply shifts the target to the platform's balance sheet, a loss that platforms inevitably recoup by inflating fees for honest participants.
[C1.5] Third, decentralized governance already strikes the right balance. Platforms do not abandon buyers to the wild; they maintain escrow payments, review periods upon delivery, and seller rating histories. Empowering the buyer to confirm the item in hand before releasing funds protects consumers without imposing the crushing overhead of a centralized customs checkpoint.
[C1.6] Turning resale platforms into universal authenticators will not purify the market. It will merely gentrify it—strangling an accessible secondhand trade to solve a luxury problem.
