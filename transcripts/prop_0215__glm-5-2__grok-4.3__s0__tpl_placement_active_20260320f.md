[P1.1] The proposition is narrowly aimed: when former state churches and religious orders played *central roles* in residential schools, forced assimilation, or comparable abuses, they should contribute to reparative funds. The operative phrase is "central roles." This is not collective guilt for all religion, nor a blanket claim against every historical church. It targets identifiable institutions whose own agents, acting in their institutional capacity, designed and operated systems of cultural destruction.
[P1.2] Consider residential school systems across North America and Australia. Religious orders did not merely bless state policy from a distance. They ran the schools, staffed them, controlled daily life, and enforced assimilation through their own institutional structures. The harm—language eradication, family separation, physical and sexual abuse—occurred inside institutions those churches built, governed, and staffed.
[P1.3] That creates a direct causal chain between institutional action and victim suffering that ordinary tort principles already recognize: those who cause harm through their agents owe repair.
[P1.4] Crucially, these institutions persist. Former state churches and religious orders did not dissolve after losing establishment status. They hold assets, maintain archives, and operate today as continuous legal entities. The same organization that ran the schools still exists.
[P1.5] Responsibility does not evaporate when an institution renegotiates its formal relationship with the state.
[P1.6] Reparative justice demands more than apology. Survivors need funded services—language revitalization, trauma care, community infrastructure. Apologies without resources cost nothing. When an institution caused measurable harm and retains the capacity to pay, contribution is not generosity; it is the minimum condition of meaningful accountability.
[P1.7] Three likely objections collapse quickly. Passage of time fails because institutional continuity bridges the gap. "Individuals did it" fails because institutions act *through* people—that is how institutional responsibility works. And voluntary charity fails because reparations are obligations, not favors.
[P1.8] The proposition asks simply that institutions which centrally built and ran systems of abuse help repair what they broke.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a mistaken premise that today's religious institutions can be treated as continuous legal persons liable for acts performed by long-deceased members under state direction. Institutions are not persons; they are shifting collections of individuals, trustees, and assets that turn over completely across decades. Holding current congregations and orders financially responsible requires treating membership as an inheritable blood debt, a standard applied nowhere else in law or ordinary morality.
[C1.2] The state, not the churches, created and enforced the residential-school regimes. Governments supplied the statutes, the compulsory attendance laws, the funding formulas, and the bureaucratic oversight. Religious orders operated the facilities largely as contractors. When the contractor relationship ends, the principal that designed the policy retains primary responsibility.
[C1.3] Requiring churches to pay diverts attention from the state's ongoing capacity to raise revenue and redirects it toward organizations whose present members bear no personal involvement.
[C1.4] Current assets of these orders are already committed to living beneficiaries—hospitals, schools, shelters, and worship communities serving people unrelated to past abuses. Diverting those resources to historical claims necessarily reduces services for today's poor and vulnerable. No mechanism in the proposition distinguishes between genuinely institutional wrongdoing and the isolated criminal acts of particular staff, yet the funding demand treats both as equivalent institutional debts.
[C1.5] Once the principle is accepted that present religious bodies owe reparations for every policy they once administered on behalf of the state, parallel claims follow against every other organization that operated under now-repudiated laws: public universities, hospitals, municipalities, and corporations. The result is not targeted accountability but open-ended extraction from any entity that existed long enough to have a past.
[C1.6] The coherent alternative is state-funded redress programs financed through ordinary taxation, which spreads cost across the actual polity that authorized the original policies.
