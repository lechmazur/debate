[P1.1] The central fact about pandemic vaccines is that the bottleneck is not science but permission and production geography. Wealthy countries can sequence a virus and authorize a vaccine in months, then insist that the know-how to make it must stay in a handful of plants. That is not natural scarcity; it is a policy choice—and it costs lives while giving the virus more time to mutate.
[P1.2] A legal requirement with a fixed window after emergency authorization changes the default. It says: once a vaccine is deemed safe and effective enough for emergency use, the manufacturing process—not merely the patent, but the cell lines, protocols, training, quality systems, and licensing—must be transferred to qualified producers in developing nations within a defined period. Voluntary pledges have already shown their limits: during COVID, technology hubs and voluntary licenses arrived late and covered too few producers, while rich countries queued doses and poorer ones waited.
[P1.3] Charity is discretionary; a deadline is not.
[P1.4] This is pro-innovation, not anti-innovation. The motion does not abolish patents or force inventors to work for free; it requires negotiated sharing with compensation and oversight. The innovation risk is exaggerated because pandemic vaccines are already underwritten by enormous public funding and guaranteed demand. A predictable legal duty can even strengthen preparedness by building distributed capacity before the next crisis.
[P1.5] And the self-interest is brutal: a vaccine that is scarce in the Global South is a reservoir for variants that come back to the Global North. Wealthy countries cannot keep the technology bottled up and claim to be protecting their own citizens. If emergency authorization is enough to roll out a product, it is enough to trigger the duty to let the world make it. The question is not whether sharing is generous.
[P1.6] It is whether we legally require it before the next pandemic makes the cost of delay undeniable.
