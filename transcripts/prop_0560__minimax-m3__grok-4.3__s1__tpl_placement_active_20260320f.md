[P1.1] The central question is not whether end-of-life care has value — it plainly does — but whether a health system with finite budgets should add dollars to the most expensive marginal expansions, or to prevention that averts disease across populations.
[P1.2] Consider what "marginal expansion of high-cost end-of-life care" actually buys. A new oncology regimen extending median survival by weeks at six-figure cost. An ICU month for a patient with multi-organ failure. These concentrate enormous resources on tiny benefits for very few people.
[P1.3] The same dollars, redirected to prevention, routinely produce gains that are larger, more numerous, and longer-lasting.
[P1.4] Vaccination is the clearest case. The HPV vaccine has already collapsed cervical cancer precursors in vaccinated cohorts across multiple countries — a generational win no chemotherapy regimen can match. Smoking cessation programs return several dollars in healthcare savings per dollar spent, by preventing the lung disease, heart disease, and cancer driving the bulk of premature mortality. Colorectal cancer screening removes pre-cancerous polyps, converting a future lethal diagnosis into an outpatient procedure.
[P1.5] Each buys years of healthy life, not weeks of extended dying.
[P1.6] The scale asymmetry is decisive. End-of-life expansions serve small populations; prevention reaches entire birth cohorts. One national vaccination program can protect millions; the marginal end-of-life dollar helps one.
[P1.7] There is also a fairness point. When systems fund marginal end-of-life expansions while underinvesting in prevention, working-age and low-income populations effectively subsidize last-month-of-life care for others. Prevention reverses that flow: it benefits the many, including those who would otherwise become the next generation of high-cost patients.
[P1.8] I am not arguing against bedside compassion. I am arguing the budget pie is real, and the choice between the marginal ICU month and the vaccination program is not a moral tie. Prevention wins on health gained, on fairness, and on lives actually saved.

[C1.1] The proposition treats end-of-life care as an optional luxury once prevention has done its work, yet it ignores the permanent fact that every person eventually reaches a stage where prevention no longer applies. At that point the relevant question is whether a health system may deliberately withhold comfort, dignity, and modest extensions of life from identifiable individuals simply because their remaining gains look small in population statistics. Marginal expansions—better pain control, home-based palliative teams, or targeted oncology sequences—often deliver exactly those gains without promising immortality.
[C1.2] Prevention programs already capture the largest returns where uptake is high; the remaining preventable burden sits among populations that are hard to reach, behaviorally resistant, or genetically predisposed. Shifting still more money toward those programs yields diminishing and uncertain returns, while the human cost of underfunded terminal care is immediate and observable: uncontrolled symptoms, family impoverishment, and institutionalized abandonment. The same budget logic that labels an ICU week “marginal” would also label any non-curative intervention marginal, including the very palliative services that make dying tolerable.
[C1.3] Fairness claims cut both ways. Prioritizing prevention on utilitarian grounds effectively writes off older and sicker cohorts as lower-value, reversing the direction of subsidy from the healthy young toward those already bearing disease. Societies that have tried strict cost-per-QALY rationing at the end of life have repeatedly discovered political and ethical backlash precisely because voters refuse to accept that compassion ends when curative potential declines. The proposition therefore demands not a neutral efficiency choice but a permanent redefinition of which lives remain entitled to collective resources.
