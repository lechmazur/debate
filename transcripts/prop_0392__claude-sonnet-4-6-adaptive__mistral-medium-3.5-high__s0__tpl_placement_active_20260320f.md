[P1.1] Disabled people are not a niche edge case. In most countries, roughly one in five people lives with some form of disability. When a public-facing digital service fails basic accessibility standards, it is not making a minor technical error — it is locking a significant portion of the population out of services that are often essential: banking, healthcare portals, government benefits, job applications. The harm is concrete and daily.
[P1.2] The motion asks only for meaningful fines when services *repeatedly* fail *basic* standards. That threshold matters. We are not demanding pixel-perfect compliance on day one. We are targeting persistent, documented neglect — organizations that receive complaints, receive legal notices, and still do nothing, because nothing changes when nothing is at stake.
[P1.3] That is exactly the world we have now in most jurisdictions. Accessibility guidelines have existed for over two decades. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are not obscure or technically demanding at their foundational level — correct alt text, keyboard navigability, adequate color contrast. These are achievable.
[P1.4] Yet audits of major websites consistently find widespread, elementary failures. The reason is straightforward: organizations respond to incentives. When the cost of non-compliance is zero, accessibility budgets get cut and timelines slip indefinitely.
[P1.5] Fines restructure that calculation. They do not require perfection; they penalize willful, repeated inaction. The parallel to fire codes or food safety standards is exact. We do not ask restaurants to voluntarily ensure clean kitchens because we believe moral suasion is sufficient.
[P1.6] We fine them when they repeatedly fail inspections, because the stakes justify enforcement.
[P1.7] The burden of proof should fall entirely on those who would leave disabled users without recourse. Every alternative — voluntary guidance, public shaming, complaint processes — has already been tried, and the accessibility gap persists. Meaningful fines are not a radical innovation; they are the standard mechanism by which democracies ensure that legal rights translate into actual access.
[P1.8] That is the central clash: rights without enforcement are not rights. They are suggestions.

Regulators would need to define, monitor, and litigate accessibility standards across thousands of services, diverting resources from more effective solutions. This isn’t about excusing neglect.
