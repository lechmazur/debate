[P1.1] Moving marquee sports behind fragmented streaming paywalls is not modernization; it is eating the seed corn. Leagues do not need to abandon streaming, out-of-market packages, or digital innovation; they simply must keep a meaningful share of their biggest showcase games on free-to-air television. Doing so is vital for two reasons: the long-term commercial survival of the sports themselves, and basic reciprocity to the public that sustains them.
[P1.2] First, paywalls monetize existing die-hard fans while destroying the pipeline that creates new ones. No casual viewer buys a dedicated streaming subscription for a sport they do not already care about. Future fans—especially children and lower-income families—discover sports through frictionless access to cultural events: stumbling onto a tense playoff game, watching a historic rivalry, or tuning in because the whole city is talking about it. Marquee games are a league’s primary customer-acquisition tool.
[P1.3] When you lock them behind subscriptions, you trade cultural ubiquity for short-term rights fees. Viewership shrinks to an aging core, mass-market sponsorship value erodes, and youth engagement dries up. It is no coincidence that the most commercially dominant league in North America—the NFL—keeps its premier inventory and postseason anchored on free broadcast television, while sports that retreated behind paywalls have watched their mainstream relevance fade.
[P1.4] Second, professional leagues owe the public access because they are publicly subsidized institutions. Franchises routinely extract billions in taxpayer funding for stadiums, municipal infrastructure, and security, while relying on special regulatory protections. They market themselves not as private startups, but as civic trusts representing entire cities. It is fundamentally unjust to tax an entire community to build the arena, and then demand working-class residents juggle multiple monthly subscriptions just to watch their team's biggest moments.
[P1.5] Free-to-air broadcast is the only mechanism that keeps major sports both commercially vibrant across generations and accessible to the communities that built them.
