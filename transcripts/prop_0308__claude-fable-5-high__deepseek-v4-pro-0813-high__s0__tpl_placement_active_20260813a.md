[P1.1] The motion turns on a comparison: when populist movements appear, what do they usually tell us, and what do they usually do? The answer to both cuts the same way. Their appearance signals that institutional guardrails are already weakening, and their behavior almost always weakens them further.
[P1.2] Start with the diagnosis. Populism is not just anger at elites; it is a specific claim — that a single leader or movement embodies "the real people," and everyone else, including courts, the press, and the political opposition, is illegitimate. That anti-pluralist logic is the definitional core, and it explains why populism flourishes precisely where trust, party systems, and accountability mechanisms have already corroded. A fever tells you infection is present; it does not cure it.
[P1.3] Then look at the track record once populists gain power. Chávez's Venezuela, Orbán's Hungary, Erdoğan's Turkey, PiS-era Poland: in case after case, populists packed courts, captured media regulators, rewrote electoral rules, and hollowed out the civil service. The pattern is remarkably consistent because it follows from the ideology — if you alone speak for the people, independent referees are obstacles, not safeguards. Scholars who track democratic quality have repeatedly found that populist governance correlates with measurable declines in press freedom, judicial independence, and electoral fairness.
[P1.4] Contrast this with genuine correctives to elite failure. Real correction channels grievance into institutional reform: anti-corruption prosecutions, campaign finance rules, new parties competing within democratic norms. Populism does the opposite — it personalizes power and delegitimizes the machinery of accountability itself. Even populists who lose leave lasting damage: normalized election denial, degraded trust, and opponents tempted to imitate their tactics.
[P1.5] Note what the motion asks: "usually" and "more than." Populist movements occasionally highlight real elite failures — that much is conceded in the word "usually." But highlighting a problem while attacking every institution capable of fixing it is not a corrective. It is a warning siren that too often becomes the fire.

[C1.2] Populism is a symptom of representation failure: established parties, courts, or regulators stop serving large groups, so outsiders mobilize. [C1.3] The clearest example is the Populist and Progressive movements of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. They were anti-elite, attacked party machines and corporate capture, and were routinely called populist. Their pressure produced direct election of senators, primary elections, antitrust law, labor protections, and wider voting rights. Those changes strengthened democratic accountability rather than destroying it.
[C1.4] Similar incorporation happened across democracies: labor and suffrage movements often used populist rhetoric against a corrupt establishment and widened the franchise without packing courts or cancelling elections.
[C1.5] That exposes the flaw in the PRO case. It treats institutional decay as something populism causes, when the usual sequence is reversed: decayed, captured institutions generate populist backlash. Hungary and Venezuela already had weak party systems, concentrated economic power, and elites who evaded constraints before populists became dominant. Blaming the backlash for the pre-existing disease gets the causal order wrong.
[C1.6] The proposition also relabels every populist movement that succeeds through ordinary democratic means as “reform,” leaving only the authoritarian cases in its category. That is definitional gerrymandering, not a comparison.
[C1.7] The motion says “usually.” Look at the ordinary pattern, not the famous catastrophes. Most populist movements never take power; they shift the agenda, push new entrants into politics, and force established parties to respond. That is exactly what a healthy corrective looks like.
