[P1.1] Robotaxi fleets should stay geofenced and numerically capped in dense city centers because uncontrolled growth immediately collides with streets that have fixed curb space, labor markets that depend on driving jobs, and safety standards still written for human drivers. Expanding without those rules turns a useful technology into a source of persistent street-level friction.
[P1.2] Curb management illustrates the issue most directly. Dense blocks already see rideshare vehicles circling or stopping short of legal zones, cutting bus lane capacity and forcing other cars into unsafe merges. Robotaxi fleets multiply that pressure by removing any per-vehicle limit on attempts per hour; every idle minute at the curb now competes with deliveries, accessible parking, and emergency access. Without assigned virtual zones and time windows first, the result is measurable drops in overall throughput rather than the efficiency gains promoters claim.
[P1.3] Labor displacement follows the same pattern. Cities relying on thousands of professional drivers for taxis and rideshare shifts lack transition programs or tax-base adjustments when software replaces the wheel. Sudden concentration of job loss in specific neighborhoods creates concentrated political opposition, as seen when earlier platform growth destabilized local taxi markets without replacement employment. A numerical cap slows that shock, giving time to design pathways for affected workers instead of reacting to protests after the fleets are already entrenched.
[P1.4] Safety rules present the steepest gap. Urban environments contain construction zones, erratic pedestrians, and weather events that current systems still encounter unevenly. Large-scale deployment before updated liability rules and performance benchmarks exist risks early incidents that reduce public acceptance across the whole category. Geofencing keeps vehicles inside simpler corridors where data collection is reliable, while caps limit the total exposure until regulators can set verifiable standards based on observed performance rather than vendor projections.
[P1.5] Capping and geofencing therefore function as practical governors. They let cities measure real usage patterns, negotiate curb allocations, and draft enforceable safety metrics without first creating the very congestion and backlash that could delay wider adoption for years.
