[P1.1] Climate policy is judged by tonnes avoided this decade, not ribbon-cuttings next decade. Latin America’s urban transport reality is bus-based: in most major cities, buses and minibuses carry the overwhelming share of transit trips, often on corridors that already exist. Electrifying those fleets converts the workhorse of the network immediately, while rail projects spend years in planning, land acquisition, tunneling, and litigation before carrying a single passenger.
[P1.2] The near-term arithmetic favors buses on speed, scale, and cost per tonne. An electric bus deployment can begin with depot charging, predictable procurement, and existing routes; cities can phase in hundreds of vehicles as grids and maintenance capacity grow. Urban rail, by contrast, is lumpy and slow: one line can consume a city’s entire transport budget for a decade, face cost overruns, and still serve a narrow corridor. Latin America already has deep operational experience with high-capacity bus corridors and bus rapid transit, so the institutional learning curve is shorter and the rollout can be distributed across neighborhoods rather than concentrated on one showcase route.
[P1.3] This is also an equity argument. Bus riders are disproportionately lower-income and exposed to diesel pollution at street level. Electrifying buses delivers cleaner air and quieter streets across the whole network, not just along a new rail line that may accelerate displacement near stations.
[P1.4] None of this says rail is worthless. Rail can move huge volumes and shape long-run urban form. But “main near-term decarbonization strategy” means the tool that can be deployed fastest, broadly, and affordably. That is bus electrification.
[P1.5] Rail can follow, and electrified buses can feed it. The near-term priority is clear.

In cities where motorcycle and car ownership is climbing fastest, the marginal tonne lives in the private vehicle, not in the bus tailpipe.
[C1.2] A diesel bus carrying fifty people is not the emissions problem. The traffic jam of single-occupant cars beside it is — and an electric bus sits in that same jam, at the same speed, attracting the same riders.
[C1.3] Second, "expanding urban rail" is not synonymous with boring a new tunnel. Latin America already runs extensive metro and suburban rail — Buenos Aires, São Paulo, Mexico City, Santiago, Medellín. Signalling upgrades, longer trains, more rolling stock, reopened branches, and extensions of existing lines add capacity in a couple of years, at a fraction of new-line cost, on grids that in Brazil, Colombia and Paraguay are already largely hydro. Those are near-term tonnes PRO's framing simply erases by assuming every rail project is a decade-long megaprogram.
[C1.4] Third, "rapid" is doing unearned work. Much of the region's bus fleet is informal, fragmented, and credit-constrained. Electrifying it means re-tendering routes, buying depot land in dense cities, and reinforcing feeders — Santiago's transition took years of restructuring, and it was the easy case.
[C1.5] Finally, the lock-in. Fleets need replacing roughly every decade; rail infrastructure serves for a century. Spending the decade's capital on vehicles buys a cleaner version of the same congested geometry, and builds a constituency against the capacity the region actually needs.
[C1.6] Judge tonnes avoided this decade, by PRO's own standard: capacity that pulls drivers out of cars beats cleaner buses stuck behind them.
