[P1.1] A seventh grader misses the advanced-math cutoff by a couple of points. Nothing about her reasoning changed that week; she had a bad test day, or attended a feeder school that didn't offer the same pre-algebra. In a tracked system, that single score becomes a destination: next year she is in the regular section, then the low honors section, and rigor, peers, and expectations thin out. Moving up is rare, and gaps compound.
[P1.2] By high school, calculus is unreachable for her, not from lack of capacity, but because the system locked the door early. Most advanced-tracking is not precise tailoring; it is a one-time sorting machine that mistakes pace for rigor and advantage for aptitude.
[P1.3] Heterogeneous classes with intensive tutoring keep the bar high while making support real. Instruction can still be differentiated: compacting for students who already know a unit, open-ended problems for those ready to go deeper, and daily checks that move a child the same week, not the same year. Intensive tutoring is the engine: targeted, frequent, tied to classwork, not a once-a-week help desk. Tutoring should serve everyone: a struggling learner gets reteaching on gaps, while an advanced learner gets coaching on proof or modeling.
[P1.4] Separating students permanently does not create excellence; it narrows the pipeline. High attainers deepen mastery by explaining ideas, seeing multiple strategies, and tackling messy problems with varied peers. The real metric is not how fast a small, already-advantaged group reaches calculus, but how many students reach genuine mathematical power and whether that group reflects the whole school. The advanced track is not the only road to advanced math; it is a shortcut that too often bypasses the students who need the road most.
[P1.5] Replacing most tracking with heterogeneous classes plus tutoring does not lower standards—it stops wasting talent that today is sorted away.

[C1.2] The strongest students coast, while students with gaps do not get the sustained explicit teaching they need.
[C1.3] The tutoring condition is the motion’s escape hatch, but it is also its weakest point. “Intensive” tutoring is one of the most expensive interventions schools can run. It requires trained adults, schedules, curricular alignment, and stable funding. Most districts cannot staff enough qualified math tutors at scale. If the tutoring is underfunded, the policy does not stop at heterogeneous classes; it stops at detracked classes with no added support.
[C1.4] That is not high standards; that is wishful thinking substituting for an operational plan.
[C1.5] The cutoff story in [P1.1] is a placement problem, not a reason to dismantle advanced sections. The fix is better identification: multiple measures, universal screening, clear retesting, bridge courses, and summer acceleration. A system can be more permeable without being abolished. Tearing down most advanced-track math because some students are misclassified punishes the students who are correctly placed and benefit from faster pacing and deeper proof work.
[C1.6] Finally, “heterogeneous classes plus tutoring” is not neutral for excellence. Parents with resources will still accelerate their children through outside programs, leaving pace and peer effects uneven. The students most harmed are the ones the motion claims to help: those who need a coherent, well-sequenced math class, not a split teacher’s attention. Public schools should keep advanced tracks and add tutoring and multiple entry points within that tracked system, not replace the structure that actually teaches advanced math.
