[P1.1] Bankruptcy exists for one reason: when honest people cannot pay, the law gives them a fresh start instead of a life sentence. Student loans are the glaring exception, and that exception has outlived its justification.
[P1.2] Today a borrower generally must bring a separate, costly lawsuit and prove something close to a certainty of hopelessness under the undue hardship standard. That test is inconsistent across courts and forbidding to people too poor to litigate it. So older borrowers on fixed incomes, people with disabilities or chronic illness, and low-wage workers decades out of school still carry balances swollen by interest, facing garnishment and benefit offsets. Unpayable debt is not an asset; it is permanent punishment for a failed investment in education.
[P1.3] This motion is deliberately narrow: make discharge much easier when borrowers face long-term inability to repay. That means evidence over time—years of low or unstable income, good-faith payments or participation in income-driven plans, disability, age, or a credential that never produced a viable job. It does not mean walking away after graduation or shielding high earners who prefer not to pay. A clear, realistic standard would separate misfortune from opportunism far better than today’s near-impossible lottery.
[P1.4] Keeping uncollectible debt on the books helps no one. The government spends money pursuing balances it will not recover, while borrowers postpone care, avoid retirement saving, delay homes and families, and sometimes retreat from formal work because extra earnings are seized. Discharge lets a permanently squeezed non-payer participate again: save, spend, take a better job, and pay taxes without every gain being swallowed by an old balance.
[P1.5] Nor does fairness to lenders or taxpayers justify special protection. Private lenders can price risk and underwrite; the law already allows discharge for credit-card, medical, and business debts reflecting important life choices. A degree cannot be repossessed, but neither can a failed shop’s goodwill—yet we do not make that debt eternal. When repayment is truly futile over the long term, relief is not abuse; it is bankruptcy working as intended.
