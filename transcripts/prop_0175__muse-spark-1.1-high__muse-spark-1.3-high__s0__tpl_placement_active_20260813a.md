[P1.1] If licensing were about safety, you would see two things: real risk that the training fixes, and more harm where licensing is absent. We see neither.
[P1.2] In interior design, a handful of states require a license to do commercial work or even to call yourself an interior designer. That means a four-year degree, years of apprenticeship, and a national exam. The claimed safety rationale is egress, fire codes, flammability. But architects, engineers, and building inspectors already enforce those codes.
[P1.3] Florida has licensed interior designers since the 90s, Texas does not. There is no epidemic of Floridians saved from couch-induced fires that Texans suffer. The license just reserves certain paid work for people who survived the gauntlet.
[P1.4] Hair braiding is even clearer. Braiding is twisting hair. No chemicals, no sharp blades, no heat. Yet many states until recently forced braiders into full cosmetology: 1,000 to over 2,000 hours, thousands in tuition, learning chemical relaxers and dyes they will never use.
[P1.5] The only plausible health risk is unwashed hands or tools, which a short sanitation course handles. When Mississippi, Virginia and other states exempted braiders, consumer complaints didn't rise - the number of legal braiders did, especially Black women entrepreneurs. That is the tell: the rule wasn't stopping burns, it was stopping competition that cosmetology schools and established salons didn't want.
[P1.6] This is the pattern in low-risk trades. Boards are staffed by incumbent practitioners who set hours, fees, and exams unrelated to actual risks, lobby hard against exemptions, and pocket the benefit through higher prices. Economists left and right find the same result in these fields: licensing raises insider wages 10 to 15 percent without measurably improving quality or safety.
[P1.7] If safety were primary, requirements would be narrow, cheap, and tied to contagion or injury. Instead they are broad, expensive, and tied to time served. That is textbook rent-seeking, not public health. These licenses primarily fence out newcomers, not danger.

[C1.1] Asking why Texas has no epidemic of design fires misses how prevention works. We do not judge guardrails by counting cars at the bottom of the cliff. Commercial interiors decide whether a crowded restaurant can empty in smoke, whether a nursing home corridor fits a wheelchair, whether wall coverings ignite quickly. Architects and inspectors do not duplicate this work; inspectors arrive after mistakes are built in, and architects focus on structure, not finishes, furnishings, and space planning.
[C1.2] Calling braiding just twisting hair trivializes real harm. Tight braids left too long tear hair follicles and can cause permanent baldness, especially in children who cannot judge a braider's technique. Shared combs, unwashed hands, and dirty workstations spread scalp fungus, bacterial infection, and lice from head to head. A pamphlet on handwashing without a license, inspection, and a way to lose the right to practice is unenforceable advice, not protection.
[C1.3] Low risk to any one client still means large risk across thousands of clients when no one can tell safe from unsafe until damage is done. A mother cannot test a braider for sanitation knowledge, a small business owner cannot test a designer on fire codes or accessibility law. Licensing solves that information gap with minimum training, an exam tied to health and building codes, and a public record with power to discipline. That is why practitioners sit on boards: only working professionals know what incompetence looks like.
[C1.4] Training takes time because competence takes time; chemical safety matters even to braiders who refer clients or share shops, and designers must learn codes precisely because the hazard is intermittent but catastrophic. Higher prices often buy accountability, insurance, and skill, not conspiracy. Fix overbroad hours where they exist, but do not mistake imperfection for intent. These rules exist primarily because strangers trust practitioners with their bodies and public spaces, and trust without standards is just luck.
