[P1.1] Fertility clinics are selling a product that fundamentally alters how women plan their lives. Yet, the marketing of egg freezing frequently relies on an illusion of certainty, portraying the procedure as a fail-safe "insurance policy" against biological aging. This misrepresentation must end. We must mandate the prominent display of age-based success rates and inherent uncertainty in advertising.
[P1.2] First, the success of egg freezing is inextricably tied to age at extraction. The probability of a live birth using eggs frozen at age 38 is drastically lower than at 28. Yet, clinic advertising routinely obscures this reality by highlighting aggregated success rates, showcasing best-case scenarios, or burying age-specific drop-offs in fine print. Women pay tens of thousands of dollars and undergo invasive treatments based on inflated expectations.
[P1.3] Requiring prominent, age-differentiated data ensures transparency matches the gravity of this medical and financial investment.
[P1.4] Second, downplaying uncertainty creates irreversible harm by inducing false hope. When ads obscure the real risk of failure, a woman may delay family planning longer than she otherwise would, placing absolute trust in her frozen eggs. If those eggs later fail to result in a viable pregnancy, the window for natural conception has often permanently closed. Inadequate disclosure directly contributes to involuntary childlessness.
[P1.5] Finally, a severe asymmetry of information exists in this market. Clinics have a clear profit motive to downplay failure rates, while consumers—acting under the intense pressure of a closing biological window—are uniquely vulnerable to optimistic marketing. Mandating unmissable disclosures of uncertainty protects these vulnerable consumers and levels the playing field.
[P1.6] It is unacceptable to market a high-stakes, highly variable medical procedure with the glossy optimism of a lifestyle brand. Prominent, realistic success rates are not just a necessary consumer protection measure; they are a fundamental prerequisite for genuine informed consent.

[C1.4] The mandate also compels clinics to display numbers that misrepresent. [C1.5] That is a government-scripted statistic, not informed consent.
[C1.6] Nor is advertising the right venue. [P1.4] and [P1.5] assume women are passive victims of ads. The answer to any information gap is the physician consultation, mandatory informed-consent documents, and case-by-case action against deceptive claims—not turning a complex medical choice into a warning label. A consult already requires discussing individualized odds and uncertainty; an ad cannot.
[C1.7] The debate is not whether clinics may lie. They may not. It is whether the remedy is prescribed ad copy. It is not.
[C1.8] The better answer keeps medical truth in the exam room, not in the billboard.
