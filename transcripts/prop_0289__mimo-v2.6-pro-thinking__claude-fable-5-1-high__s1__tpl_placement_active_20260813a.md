[P1.1] Automation gave robotaxis permission to use our streets; it did not give operators permission to redraw the map of who gets mobility. The proposition sets a modest floor: low-income and low-demand neighborhoods should get reasonably similar reliability, not identical fleets or the same ride counts. That floor is worth defending, and the case turns on three points.
[P1.2] First, robotaxi service is not a boutique product. It runs on public roads, under public permits, with public safety enforcement and public patience for blocked lanes and test miles. That is a bargain, and the price of the bargain is coverage. Transit agencies have always imposed service obligations on the networks they license — the last train, the unprofitable bus route, the wheelchair lift.
[P1.3] We should not lower our standards of access simply because the vehicle now has no driver.
[P1.4] Second, the alternative is a slow-motion mobility redline. If fleets cluster where fares are rich, rides are frequent, and streets are mapped clean, then low-income neighborhoods get what they already have: thin service, long waits, and unreliable pickups. But the stakes are different now. For a night-shift worker without a car, a forty-minute wait or a canceled ride isn't an inconvenience — it's a lost shift or a lost job.
[P1.5] Reliability is the service. Density without reliability is decoration. "Reasonably similar reliability" targets exactly the metric that matters to someone deciding whether they can accept a job across town.
[P1.6] Third, the efficiency objection is self-fulfilling. "Low-demand" areas are low-demand partly because service there is bad — bad service suppresses ridership, which "justifies" bad service. Left alone, fleets will redline, and the neighborhoods least able to absorb that cost will absorb it. If we wait for the market to reach them, we will wait forever, and the equity argument becomes moot by the time we concede it.
[P1.7] The requirement is light: similar reliability, not identical economics.

In a dense district, one idle car covers thousands of potential riders within a few minutes. In a sparse neighborhood, matching that pickup time means parking cars that mostly wait, burning capital and electricity to serve a trickle of trips.
[C1.2] "Reasonably similar reliability" sounds like a modest floor. Geometrically, it is the most expensive promise an operator can make.
[C1.3] So who pays? PRO's transit analogy answers itself. Cities run unprofitable bus routes because taxpayers fund them through the farebox and the general budget. Nobody orders a private bus company to run the empty route for free.
[C1.4] The proposition does exactly that: an unfunded mandate on operators that have not yet turned a profit anywhere. Three outcomes follow, and none helps the night-shift worker PRO describes. Fares rise citywide, pricing out the very low-income riders the rule claims to protect. Or operators shrink or delay launches, because a city that demands full-map reliability from day one is a city you enter last.
[C1.5] Or they leave, and the neighborhood gets zero rides instead of few.
[C1.6] That last point matters most. Every robotaxi deployment so far has grown the way it must: a small validated zone, then a larger one, block by block as the software proves itself. [C1.7] PRO says waiting for the market means waiting forever. The honest comparison is a fleet that reaches those neighborhoods in year three versus a mandate that keeps it from reaching the city at all.
[C1.8] If a community wants coverage, it should buy it the way it buys every other public service: contract for it, subsidize rides, fund the last mile. Redlining is a real harm. Bankrupting the map is not the cure.
