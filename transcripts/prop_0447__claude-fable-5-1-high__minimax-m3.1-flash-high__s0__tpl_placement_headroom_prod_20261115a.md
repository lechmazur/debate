[P1.1] Every time a large company replaces an employee with a platform contractor or a fourth-tier subcontractor, the same work gets done, but something disappears: the employer's contribution to pensions, sickness insurance, and unemployment funds. The work stays in Europe; the funding for the welfare state does not. That gap is not an accident of modern business. It is the business model.
[P1.2] Consider a delivery rider in Madrid or Berlin. The platform pays little or nothing into social insurance for her. When she gets injured, the public health system treats her; when the platform exits the market, unemployment support comes from a fund she and her "employer" barely financed. The company captures the savings; the state, and every directly employing competitor, absorbs the cost.
[P1.3] The same logic runs through construction and meat processing; Germany banned subcontracting in slaughterhouses outright after the 2020 outbreaks exposed what those chains concealed.
[P1.4] The motion fixes the price signal. Outsourcing should be chosen because it is genuinely more efficient, not because it is a cheaper way to shed social obligations. Requiring large users of platforms and chains to pay a higher contribution share makes the two options compete on real merits. It also gives the firm at the top of the chain a reason to police what happens below it, something no inspectorate can do from outside.
[P1.5] Three design points matter. First, it targets large companies, which can afford compliance; the corner bakery is untouched. Second, it is a contribution, not a ban: firms remain free to use platforms and subcontractors, they just stop being subsidized for it. Third, it protects honest employers, who today lose tenders to rivals whose only advantage is contribution avoidance.
[P1.6] A single market that lets its largest firms outsource their social obligations while keeping the profits is hollow. Close the gap.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a payment rule for a protection rule. Making outsourcing more expensive does not establish whether a worker is genuinely self-employed, whether conditions improve, or whether the worker gains status or rights. If platforms already treat many workers as employees and pay contributions, a “higher share” is merely an indiscriminate surcharge.
[C1.2] The causal chain is also backwards. Contribution gaps arise from sham self-employment, bogus subcontracting, unpaid work, and firms evading liability. A large client may not know that a contractor has misclassified staff, while platforms can restructure relationships across borders. A levy on use therefore risks taxing legitimate specialists, franchisees, and genuinely independent providers alongside abusive chains; it cannot reliably identify the party actually responsible.
[C1.3] Its likely effects are perverse. Companies may raise prices, reduce platform orders, or push liability further down the chain. A burden that vanishes is not worker protection. Moreover, a small cleaning contractor and a multinational manufacturer do not have equal bargaining power over subcontractors, so one rate cannot price their role correctly.
[C1.4] A better rule is to enforce contributions against the party controlling the work, require transparent contracting chains, and impose joint liability where a principal has helped conceal misclassification. Those measures target misconduct without penalizing outsourcing itself. They also avoid encouraging companies to conceal platform use rather than disclose it.
[C1.5] Finally, contributions do not automatically equal benefits. If lawfulness fails, a higher charge paid by an innocent large company may compensate public systems while leaving the rider’s status, rights, and remedy unchanged. That makes the invoice larger, not the employment relationship fairer.
[C1.6] The EU should require the correct payer, genuine employment status, and enforceable responsibility—not a surcharge for using labor platforms or subcontracting. Otherwise it taxes the business model while leaving the underlying disease untreated.
