[P1.1] The proposition that governments should rely mainly on consumer choice campaigns assumes a fiction: that shoppers stand in aisles armed with nutritional knowledge, willpower, and time to decode ingredient lists. Reality is different. Ultra-processed foods are engineered for hyper-palatability, with hidden sugars and additives buried in marketing language. When a parent grabs a juice box labeled "100% fruit" or a snack claiming "natural flavors," they are not making an informed choice; they are navigating a deliberately confusing environment designed by industry.
[P1.2] Consumer choice campaigns—brochures, websites, school posters—fail because they treat obesity and diet-related disease as individual moral failings rather than structural outcomes. They ask people to resist billion-dollar advertising budgets with a pamphlet, and they disproportionately reach the already-health-literate while missing the time-poor and the young.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, diabetes and obesity rates climb globally, and the costs land on public health systems.
[P1.4] Warning labels change the choice architecture at the exact moment of decision. Chile's front-of-pack octagon warnings on sugary drinks and ultra-processed snacks measurably reduced purchases of those products. Mexico's similar law cut sugary drink consumption. Unlike campaigns that require literacy and motivation to seek out, these are stop-signs that interrupt automatic purchasing and work across language barriers.
[P1.5] Tobacco control offers the template: we do not rely on "consumer choice" to reduce smoking. We mandate graphic warnings because we recognize that addiction and information asymmetry defeat individual willpower. Sugary drinks and ultra-processed foods create similar dependency loops, particularly in children who cannot evaluate long-term risk.
[P1.6] The alternative is not freedom; it is letting industry write the rules of the game. Governments have a duty to ensure that "choice" is actually informed. Prominent warning labels are the minimum intervention that respects autonomy while correcting a market failure. The question is not whether we can afford labels, but whether we can afford another decade of campaigns that change attitudes while waistlines expand.
