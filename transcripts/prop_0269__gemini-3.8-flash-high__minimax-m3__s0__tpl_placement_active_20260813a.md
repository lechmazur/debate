[P1.1] Secondary fashion platforms like Poshmark, Depop, Vestiaire Collective, and eBay take healthy cuts of every transaction—often fifteen to twenty percent—yet systematically externalize the single greatest risk of their business model: the counterfeit. Today, platforms routinely restrict physical inspection to luxury goods above steep price thresholds, leaving mid-tier fashion entirely unprotected. When a buyer receives a convincing fake, the system forces them to prove the fraud within an absurdly narrow window, pay for outside appraisals, or eat the loss. This status quo is economically indefensible.
[P1.2] First, platforms hold a massive structural advantage in detection. A casual shopper buying a designer jacket or rare sneakers cannot reliably spot modern "superfakes" engineered with cloned tags and near-identical materials. Platforms, by contrast, possess aggregated transaction data, seller history, digital verification tools, and the scale to employ trained authenticators. Expecting an ordinary consumer to act as a forensic customs inspector while the marketplace skims a commission is deeply perverse.
[P1.3] Second, liability must align with incentives. Under buyer-beware regimes, platforms face a dangerous moral hazard: they pocket their take-rate regardless of authenticity, losing money only when an unusually persistent buyer successfully navigates a hostile claims process. Forcing platforms to expand authentication and absorb financial risk changes that equation entirely. The moment counterfeits directly hit platform balance sheets, marketplaces will aggressively purge organized counterfeit rings and invest in proactive screening.
[P1.4] Finally, absorbing risk protects the resale ecosystem. When buyers shoulder counterfeit risk, consumer trust erodes, creating a classic market for lemons that depresses prices and punishes honest sellers. Resale platforms exist precisely because they claim to offer a secure, curated alternative to sketchy classifieds. If they want to reap the lucrative rewards of acting as trusted intermediaries, they must shoulder the core commercial responsibility of that role: actively authenticating what they facilitate and guaranteeing transactions with their own balance sheets.
