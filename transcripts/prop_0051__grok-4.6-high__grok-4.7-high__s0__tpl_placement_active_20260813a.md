[P1.1] Ultra-cheap fashion is cheap because the costs are hidden: in factories nobody can name, in chemicals nobody tests, and in parcels that outrun inspection. Fast-fashion marketplaces should face stronger product-safety and labor-transparency obligations even if that slows the flood of five-dollar dresses. The slowdown is not the tragedy. The tragedy is treating skin-contact goods like untraceable junk mail.
[P1.2] These platforms are not corkboards. They recruit sellers, rank them, take payment, and route goods through customs as personal packages. A buyer clicks once and receives kids’ pajamas, jewelry, or workout wear from factories that never faced a real audit. Product safety fails first.
[P1.3] Clothes sit against infants, near heat, in washers. Restricted dyes, lead in charms, and flammable sleepwear follow from racing the lowest bidder with no batch testing. A conventional importer faces paperwork. A marketplace parcel often faces a barcode.
[P1.4] The platform that chose volume can require tests, substance lists, and recalls. If fewer mystery boxes arrive, that is the point.
[P1.5] Labor opacity is the same mechanism. Prices that cannot cover a living wage are not consumer surplus; they are extraction. Without mill-level transparency, unpaid subcontracting and high-risk inputs stay off the receipt. Platforms already map shipping; they can map mills.
[P1.6] Refusing keeps the subsidy invisible.
[P1.7] We do not accept this bargain for car seats or baby formula. Clothing that covers the body all day should not be exempt because it is trendy and disposable. Shoppers who need low prices are also least able to absorb rashes, burns, or the fact that a bargain was paid in someone else’s body. Consumers can still buy inexpensive clothes.
[P1.8] They should not have to buy untested ones from nameless plants. Honest markets are slower than unaccountable ones. That is why the marketplace, not the firehose, should change.

[C1.1] The motion does not ask whether unsafe clothes and hidden labor are bad. It asks us to impose stronger marketplace obligations even when ultra-cheap cross-border shopping slows. That is the wrong trade. Delay and higher prices are not proof of safety, and a mill name on a form is not a wage.
[C1.2] These platforms connect millions of small sellers to people buying cheap clothes because they must. Prices rise.
[C1.3] The household that needs a five-dollar shirt does not upgrade to an audited brand. It buys less or leaves the app for a channel with thinner visibility. Pushing risk into group chats does not protect skin.
[C1.4] Safety and labor are not one lever, and apparel is not a car seat. Children's sleepwear and metal jewelry deserve targeted tests, recalls, and customs attention. Most garments, when they fail, fail as irritation or weak durability—not as a reason to throttle an entire trade lane. Formula and restraints can kill.
[C1.5] A cotton tee does not earn the same friction. The motion erases that difference and then calls the slowdown the point.
[C1.6] Labor transparency fails at the factory door. A platform can demand a mill name. It cannot see the subcontract, the hours, or the pay from a parcel label. The mandate's reliable effect is fewer orders, and fewer orders are not a raise.
[C1.7] Workers do not get safer because checkout slows; many lose the export job for something informal and less visible. Unaudited disclosure is a sticker, not a wage.
[C1.8] Low prices are not the villain. Seller bans, sampling, and recalls can hit hazardous goods without consecrating delay. If the obligations buy costlier access instead of safer products and better jobs, the motion loses.
