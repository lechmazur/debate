[C1.1] A categorical phaseout is a blunt answer to a material problem. The proposition asks governments to identify the “most wasteful” packaging and remove it, but that is an unstable category. Waste depends on material, distance, cleaning, recovery capacity, and what replaces it. A rule that looks progressive in one city may perform differently in another, while bans prevent local tailoring.
[C1.2] That uncertainty matters because packaging prevents leaks, preserves temperature, protects hygiene, and enables delivery. Replacing a soupy meal with a washable container is not costless: reusable systems require collection, washing, energy, water, and reverse transport; paper alternatives can be heavier and use coatings that defeat recycling. If businesses respond to a ban with double bags, bulkier boxes, or more accessories, the policy may relocate waste rather than reduce it.
[C1.3] The burden is also uneven. Chains can negotiate bulk packaging and spread compliance costs. A small café ordering one hundred lids at a time cannot; it may lose margins, raise prices, or abandon delivery, harming employees and independent operators. Calling these “transition costs” does not establish that benefits exceed harms, especially when governments could address litter through disposal fees, producer responsibility, and reuse requirements for genuinely poor formats.
[C1.4] PRO’s claim—that convenience has been privatized while cleanup is socialized—presumes the cleanup benefit will materialize after a ban. It does not compare that benefit with the substitute’s footprint, or whether consumers will use reusable packaging voluntarily, return it reliably, and wash it at home. A phaseout can impose certain, concentrated losses for uncertain, diffuse gains. Nor does inconvenience justify the policy.
[C1.5] Target externality with targeted rules, not packaging by appearance. Governments should measure outcomes, standardize producer responsibility, and require reuse or safe disposal only where evidence shows net benefit. A blanket phaseout could reduce trash; it cannot be justified as written.

[C2.1] PRO shifts the burden of proof onto critics while asking governments to supply the very comparison it claims is unnecessary. [P2.4] says a rule producing double bags or bulkier boxes would have “missed its own standard,” but which standard? No objective threshold defines “most wasteful,” and no proposed process compares the full consequences of each substitute. A rule cannot avoid judgment by calling any disappointing result failure after the fact.
[C2.2] [P2.1] claims that cities already apply a test combining high volume, poor recovery, and easy substitution. In practice, those criteria pull in different directions. A lightweight container may be difficult to recover but require less material to produce; a durable container may be reusable but impose substantial washing and reverse-transport burdens. Local waste systems, delivery distances, and consumer behavior determine which option performs better.
[C2.3] Publishing a line does not resolve that calculation; it transfers the political choice to officials while pretending the technical answer already exists.
[C2.4] The proposed alternatives are also wrongly dismissed. [P2.6] asserts that disposal fees and producer schemes hit the same counter “less predictably,” yet EPR can impose clear per-unit obligations and reward reductions without banning a package or forcing a specific substitute. Such schemes make polluters pay for shared costs, whereas a phaseout can push costs onto businesses and consumers instead. The question is not whether cleanup has a cost, but why mandate one supply choice rather than internalize that cost and let improvements compete.
[C2.5] [P2.8] rightly says litter costs are incurred now, but that does not establish that a ban produces the largest net reduction. Current harm can be reduced through targeted collection, fees, standards, and reuse systems. A categorical phaseout may reduce one category while increasing packaging volume elsewhere.
[C2.6] Finally, “one dated switch” is not modest for a small café with thin margins. Chains can spread compliance costs; independents cannot. Without lifecycle evidence and workable alternatives for each locality, governments should target waste rather than prescribe packaging by appearance.
