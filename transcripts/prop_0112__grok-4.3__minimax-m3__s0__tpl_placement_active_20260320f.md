[P1.1] Tying basic medical coverage to employment creates immediate vulnerability because job loss or even a shift to part-time work can cut off access overnight. A laid-off worker with an ongoing condition must then navigate expensive temporary options or go without, turning routine employment turnover into preventable health setbacks. Single-payer financing severs this link so coverage continues uninterrupted.
[P1.2] The employer model also locks workers into roles they would otherwise leave. People stay in unsuitable jobs solely to keep insurance for themselves or dependents, reducing labor mobility and bargaining power in ways that compound over careers. Single-payer removes that constraint, letting individuals accept better positions, start businesses, or change fields without medical risk.
[P1.3] Administrative overhead grows under multiple employer plans. Each insurer duplicates eligibility checks, billing systems, and prior-authorization processes, diverting billions from treatment itself. A single payer handles claims uniformly, lowering per-person costs and freeing resources for direct care.

Employer-based systems leave these groups exposed by design, while single-payer extends the same protections continuously. This structural difference determines whether coverage serves health needs or employment status.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to choose single-payer financing over employer-based coverage, but that framing conceals a critical trade-off. Employer-based systems are not a flaw to escape — they are a working structure that already delivers substantial value, especially for basic coverage.
[C1.2] First, employer contributions subsidize premiums in ways no single-payer system can match. A typical employer pays the majority of an employee's premium — often the largest share of individual coverage. Replace that arrangement with tax-funded single-payer, and the subsidy does not disappear; it shifts onto workers in the form of higher taxes, frequently exceeding what they previously paid in premiums and lost wage growth combined. The proposition's promise of "free" coverage is paid for by the same people it claims to help.
[C1.3] Second, employer plans harness market competition and collective bargaining power. Multiple carriers compete for employer groups, producing plan diversity, innovation in care management, and price negotiation leverage a single monolithic payer cannot replicate. Where single-payer systems exist, patients routinely face longer waits for specialty care, restricted drug formularies, and slower adoption of new treatments. We do not improve basic coverage by removing the very mechanism that currently negotiates bulk rates and pushes innovation.
[C1.4] Third, the problems the proposition highlights — job lock and coverage gaps during transitions — are real but addressable without dismantling the employer system. Portable benefits accounts, expanded bridge coverage, and public backstops for the unemployed fix the structural weakness without sacrificing the strengths. Single-payer offers a sledgehammer where a scalpel suffices.
[C1.5] Finally, employer-based coverage creates built-in accountability. Employers, employees, and unions all have stakes in plan performance, producing feedback loops a government monopsony lacks. Basic medical coverage works best when those closest to the workforce have a voice, not when every decision is centralized in a bureaucracy answering to political rather than patient pressure.
[C1.6] The proposition asks us to throw out what works to solve what can be fixed. That is a bargain the evidence does not support.

[C4.3] A2: Universal cuts under single-payer are the problem, not the safety net. When a single payer's budget tightens, it restricts formularies, lengthens diagnostic waits, and starves capital investment—every patient feels it simultaneously, with no alternative plan to switch to. Real basic coverage is not just whether a card exists in your wallet; it is whether you can see a specialist promptly, access the drug your doctor prescribes, and choose among competing plans that negotiate on your behalf. Employer-based coverage delivers all three for the overwhelming majority of working Americans while creating temporary, addressable gaps at transitions.
[C4.5] Single-payer delivers continuous enrollment while trading depth, choice, and responsiveness for it. The proposition's bargain is continuity purchased through permanent degradation. That is the deal voters are being asked to accept, and it is the wrong one.
