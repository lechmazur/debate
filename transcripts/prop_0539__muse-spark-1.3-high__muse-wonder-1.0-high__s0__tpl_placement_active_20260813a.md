[P1.1] Outlawing cross-border commercial surrogacy does not end it. It pushes it underground, where exploitation thrives. Women still carry pregnancies for foreign clients, but without contracts they can enforce, without independent lawyers, without guaranteed medical care. Babies are still born, but risk limbo or statelessness when home states refuse to recognize parentage.
[P1.2] The choice is not between surrogacy and no surrogacy. It is between a visible trade the law can govern and an invisible one it cannot. Conditional legality chooses governance.
[P1.3] That governance respects two forms of agency prohibition erases. Intended parents facing infertility, medical risk, or same-sex partnership have a profound interest in having a child. Surrogates, often poor but not passive, have an interest in being paid fairly for difficult, valuable work — money that can pay debts, buy a house, educate children. Denying all compensation does not liberate these women; it infantilizes them while leaving unpaid or coerced arrangements untouched.
[P1.4] Regulation draws the moral line in the right place: coercion and deception are banned, informed consent with fair pay is permitted.
[P1.5] And the three required standards are enforceable, not aspirational. License only agencies that use escrowed payments, independent legal counsel and medical screening for surrogates, cooling-off periods, caps on embryo transfer and repeat cycles, and comprehensive health insurance with postpartum care. Recognize parentage only with a pre-birth court order, informed consent on record, and coordination on citizenship so no child is left stateless. Punish violators where it hurts: no enforceable contract, no parentage order, no visa facilitation, loss of license.
[P1.6] Home states already control those chokepoints.
[P1.7] The alternative is failure on both sides. A total ban abandons surrogates to black markets and children to legal limbo. Unregulated permission abandons them to baby farms. Conditional legality alone keeps the path to parenthood open while making protection the price of admission.

[C1.1] Conditional legality sounds humane until you ask who enforces the conditions, where, and against whom. An intended parents’ home state can grant or withhold a parentage order, but it cannot inspect a foreign clinic’s screening room, confirm that a surrogate’s “independent” lawyer was not selected by the agency, or prevent her pay from being withheld after a miscarriage or medically advised abortion. Agencies can recruit in one country, incorporate in a second, implant in a third, and arrange birth in a fourth.
[C1.2] When harm occurs, every jurisdiction can point elsewhere. That is not a chokepoint; it is a shell game with paperwork.
[C1.3] Even perfect paperwork would not cure the underlying bargain. Cross-border commercial surrogacy works because of inequality: affluent buyers purchase gestational risk that poorer women abroad accept because they need money for debt, housing, or their own children’s schooling. Calling that choice informed does not make it free from economic compulsion. Insurance can pay for complications, screening can identify them, and escrow can guarantee payment, but none removes the risks of pregnancy, surgery, infertility, or death — it only prices them.
[C1.4] Standards do not prevent exploitation; they define how much exploitation the state is willing to certify.
[C1.5] The child is also turned into the object of performance. A commercial contract is ultimately enforceable only if a baby is produced and handed over, which pressures clinics to transfer embryos, pressures women to continue pregnancies, and makes the child’s legal identity depend on adults’ compliance. Giving that transaction a license does not restrain the market; it reassures buyers, expands demand, and recruits more women into it.
[C1.6] The answer to underground abuse is not to legalize the buyers’ market, but to target agencies and purchasers while protecting children already born through citizenship rules that do not validate the contract prospectively. A ban can protect the child without selling permission to create the next one. Cross-border commercial surrogacy should be prohibited, not given a compliance logo for exploitation.
