[P1.1] Historic cities are living communities, not amusement parks. When the influx of mass tourism via cruise ships and large tour buses destroys the livability of these cities, local governments have a fundamental duty to intervene. Capping these arrivals is essential when their social and environmental costs outweigh their economic benefits.
[P1.2] Cruise ships and mega-buses do not just bring tourists; they deliver sudden, concentrated shocks of thousands of day-trippers to fragile infrastructure. This specific type of travel maximizes physical footprint while minimizing local economic contribution. Because cruise passengers typically sleep and dine on their ships, they leave historic centers with diesel pollution, excess waste, and pedestrian gridlock, while providing only a fraction of the revenue generated by overnight guests.
[P1.3] Furthermore, unchecked mass arrivals drive the "theme-parkification" of historic centers. When daily surges of tour groups overwhelm neighborhoods, essential local services are pushed out by low-value souvenir shops, and housing is aggressively converted into short-term rentals to capture the surrounding demand. In places like Venice and Dubrovnik, unrestrained daily surges historically forced locals to flee, hollowing out the community. A historic city without its residents is just a stage set.
[P1.4] Capping massive arrival vectors protects the authentic cultural fabric that makes the destination valuable in the first place.
[P1.5] Ultimately, the resolution sets a perfectly rational threshold: caps apply precisely *when* the harms of crowding, pollution, and housing displacement demonstrably outweigh the revenue gains. To oppose this is to argue for civic self-destruction—demanding that cities allow an extractive industry to strip-mine their heritage at a net loss to the people who actually live there. Capping mass transit arrivals is not a ban on tourism. It is a necessary regulatory valve used to match visitor volume to a fragile city's carrying capacity, ensuring that tourism serves the community rather than consuming it.
