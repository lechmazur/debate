[P1.1] A hectare of Bornean peat forest, once drained and burned, does not grow back on any human timescale. The income a smallholder loses to a land-use rule can be recovered, subsidized, or replaced by better yields on land already cleared. That asymmetry — permanent ecological loss versus temporary economic loss — is the whole logic of this motion, and CON must argue against it, not just cite GDP.
[P1.2] First: palm oil's price tag is a lie. Unregulated expansion dumps its real costs on people who never see the profits. The 2015 fire season, driven by peatland clearing, blanketed Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore in haze — schools closed, flights grounded, respiratory illness spiking, billions in losses. None of that appears in the export figures CON will quote.
[P1.3] When growth depends on leaving its waste in other people's lungs, restricting it is not anti-growth; it is correcting a rigged market.
[P1.4] Second: the damage is not linear; it is terminal. Peat soils store centuries of carbon that escape once canals are cut. Orangutan habitat in Sumatra and Borneo, once converted, is gone — no policy reversal restores an extinct population. A government that waits for the right time to regulate is making decisions it can never unmake, while the costs CON fears — slower export growth, tighter margins — can always be revisited.
[P1.5] Third: the trade-off is overstated and increasingly backward. Smallholder plots typically yield a fraction of what well-managed land can, so the path that actually raises rural income is intensification on existing land, not clearing new forest. The market is moving too: the EU's deforestation rules and corporate zero-deforestation pledges make weak domestic regulation the bigger export risk. And regulation done well — land titles, replanting support, certification premiums — shields smallholders from land grabs, not just constrains them.
[P1.6] The question is not growth versus trees. It is whether governments act while action still matters.
