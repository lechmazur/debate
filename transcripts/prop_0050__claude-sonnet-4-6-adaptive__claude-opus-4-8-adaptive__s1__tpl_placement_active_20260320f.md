[P1.1] Aviation is one of the hardest sectors to decarbonize, and the central problem isn't the occasional holiday traveler—it's the small fraction of the population that boards planes dozens of times a year. A progressive frequent-flyer levy targets exactly that group, and the case for it rests on three interlocking arguments that the opposition cannot escape.
[P1.2] First, the polluter-pays principle demands it. Aviation's climate damage is radically concentrated. Research consistently finds that a small minority of people account for the vast majority of flights. Someone who takes fifteen flights a year imposes enormously more environmental harm than someone who takes one.
[P1.3] Yet under flat per-flight or per-ticket taxes, the marginal cost of each additional flight stays constant. That design lets the heaviest users absorb their environmental bill as a trivial overhead. A levy that rises steeply with each additional flight makes the externality visible at precisely the moment when it matters most—when consumption is already high and discretionary.
[P1.4] Second, it is fairer in its incidence. Frequent flying correlates strongly with wealth. Business travelers, senior executives, and the affluent professional class take the lion's share of flights. A flat tax falls proportionally harder on the budget traveler taking a single trip to see family abroad than on the corporate consultant who barely notices another ticket charge.
[P1.5] Progressivity corrects this inversion. The first flight in a year might carry a low or zero surcharge; the tenth carries a serious one. That structure protects the occasional traveler while charging the repeat emitter appropriately.
[P1.6] Third, it actually changes behavior. Economists broadly agree that price signals matter at the margin. When each successive flight becomes materially more expensive, businesses reconsider whether every meeting needs a flight, and frequent travelers genuinely weigh alternatives. A flat tax that becomes background noise for high-volume travelers cannot do this work.
[P1.7] The question before us isn't whether aviation should be taxed—it already is. The question is whether that taxation should be designed to match where the harm actually comes from. A progressive levy does that. Existing regimes do not.
