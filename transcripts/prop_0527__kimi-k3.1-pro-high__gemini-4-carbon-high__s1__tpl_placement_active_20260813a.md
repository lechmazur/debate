[P1.1] An antibiotic that must be used sparingly cannot succeed by being sold aggressively. That contradiction is why advance market commitments are necessary: governments guarantee a defined return or purchase stream for a qualifying new antibiotic, rather than expecting developers to recover costs through high sales volumes. Countries should permit them even when unit prices remain high, because the alternative is not automatically cheap medicine; it can be no medicine when resistance strikes.
[P1.2] First, these commitments repair a broken innovation incentive. Conventional blockbuster economics reward chronic, widely used drugs. Antibiotics face short treatment courses, stewardship restrictions, and the possibility that a new drug will be reserved as a last resort. A hospital may desperately need that drug only occasionally, while still needing it to exist, remain manufactured, and remain supported by resistance monitoring.
[P1.3] An advance commitment pays for that readiness and development risk. Removing it simply because each dose is expensive leaves society relying on a sales model that deliberately rewards what good medicine seeks to prevent: excessive antibiotic use.
[P1.4] Second, high unit prices do not establish bad value. The relevant comparison is the full contract cost against avoided deaths, prolonged hospitalization, isolation, toxic fallback treatments, and outbreaks that become harder to contain. A costly drug used for a small number of patients can consume less public money than routine services yet deliver exceptional strategic value. Opposition that points only to the price per vial ignores both the tiny treatment volumes stewardship requires and the enormous cost of an empty last-resort arsenal.
[P1.5] Third, “permit” means preserve a tool, not write developers a blank cheque. Contracts can require demonstrated novelty, clinical value, reliable supply, access conditions, transparent costs, and fixed public payments independent of prescribing volume. Countries can reject poor offers and negotiate terms; prohibition would remove that discretion altogether. Existing subscription-style approaches show the central insight: revenue can reward availability and value rather than consumption.

The entire logic of an advance market commitment is to pay for development risk upfront so actual doses can be supplied near manufacturing cost. Permitting commitments where unit prices remain high betrays that bargain: it forces the public to pay twice, rations care at the bedside, and accelerates resistance worldwide.
[C1.2] First, [P1.1] and [P1.6] pose a false choice between high-price commitments and high-volume commercial sales. Countries can fund antibiotic pipelines through public grants and market commitments that strictly cap unit prices near production cost. Notice the contradiction between [P1.3] and [P1.5]: if a guaranteed commitment already pays a country’s share of readiness and development risk, why would the price per vial stay high? Either taxpayers fund a fixed guarantee while hospitals still face monopoly prices per dose, paying twice for one drug, or the commitment itself pays out through steep per-vial charges, re-tying developer revenue to sales volume and undoing the delinkage [P1.1] promised.
[C1.3] Second, contrary to [P1.4], high unit prices inflict lethal harm even when patient volumes are tiny. Hospitals generally treat inpatients under fixed per-stay budgets, so expensive drugs come straight out of local pharmacy funds. Administrators impose approval hurdles that push doctors to try cheap, failing, or toxic drugs first, delaying the new antibiotic until sepsis is irreversible.
[C1.4] Stewardship should follow microbiology, not hospital solvency.
[C1.5] Third, [P1.5] confuses legal permission with bargaining power. Against a patent monopolist, permitting high-unit-price deals destroys a government’s leverage to refuse them. Abroad, exorbitant vial prices leave resistant outbreaks uncontained to cross borders and push patients into incomplete, rationed courses, breeding resistance against the very drug countries paid to protect.

[P2.1] The opposition has shown why some high-price commitments would be bad deals, not why countries should be forbidden to accept any of them. That distinction decides the motion.
[P2.2] [C1.1] simply declares that near-cost doses are essential to an advance commitment. But a commitment can guarantee revenue or purchases conditional on successful development while leaving separate per-dose charges. Calling that “paying twice” is accounting rhetoric, not valuation. Add the guarantee and expected dose payments, then compare the **total public cost** with the health benefits and the realistic alternative.
[P2.3] Two payments can still purchase one drug worth far more than both combined.
[P2.4] Nor do grants plus mandatory price caps eliminate the choice. Grants fund research before anyone knows which candidates will succeed; commitments can pull a qualifying product across the finish line and sustain its manufacture. A country may certainly prefer grants or capped contracts, but their possible usefulness does not prove they will finance every needed drug. prohibition replaces discretion with hope.
[P2.5] The hospital-budget objection in [C1.3] identifies a genuine contracting risk, not an unavoidable consequence of high unit prices. A national agreement can reimburse the hospital separately, supply the drug centrally, and require timely clinical access. Then microbiology, not the pharmacy budget, determines treatment. Without any viable commitment, the hospital saves the vial price while the patient receives an older, failing treatment—or faces an infection for which no last-resort drug exists.
[P2.6] Finally, legal prohibition is not bargaining power. A government permitted to negotiate can reject extortionate terms; a government forbidden to proceed cannot accept even an exceptionally valuable offer. Nor does refusing a commitment in one country automatically create affordable supply abroad. Access clauses, differentiated pricing, and licensed supply can address cross-border availability; destroying the incentive to develop and manufacture the drug addresses nothing.
[P2.7] CON prices the vial and assumes an identical cheap drug will otherwise appear. PRO prices the whole arrangement against delay, toxicity, and absence. High-price offers should sometimes be rejected—but sometimes accepted. Permission preserves exactly that judgment; prohibition eliminates it.
