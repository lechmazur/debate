[P1.1] A toddler points at a dog in a picture book and looks up. What happens next is the engine of human language development: an adult follows the child's gaze, names the thing, expands on it, reads the child's face, adjusts. Developmental researchers call this "serve and return," and decades of work on early language — including the well-known finding that live human interaction teaches infants far better than equivalent audio or video — converge on one point: young brains are built through contingent, responsive, embodied exchange.
[P1.2] A smart speaker cannot follow a pointing finger. It cannot notice that a child said "goggie" while glancing at the window. It delivers content; it does not do relationship. And relationship, not content, is what early reading and conversation actually are.
[P1.3] Note what the motion asks. Not a ban, not zero screens, not techno-panic. It says parents should not *routinely* outsource three specific, developmentally loaded rituals — early reading, conversation, bedtime — during the *very young* years. Occasional use, supplements, accessibility tools all survive.
[P1.4] What must not happen is systematic substitution during the narrow window when attachment styles form and language architecture is laid down.
[P1.5] The stakes are asymmetric. If I'm wrong, families lose a modest convenience. If the other side is wrong, we run an uncontrolled experiment on infant attachment: a child whose bedtime comfort comes from a device learns that soothing is on-demand and frictionless, not relational. Bedtime is precisely when children process fear and separation with a trusted adult.
[P1.6] Outsourcing it isn't like outsourcing laundry; it's outsourcing the parent's core function.
[P1.7] Finally, routines are self-reinforcing. What starts as "just tonight" becomes the default, because AI is always patient and never tired — the temptation compounds exactly when the child needs the human most. A norm against routine outsourcing protects parents from that slow drift.
[P1.8] The question is not whether AI can talk to children. It's whether children learn to be human from things that aren't. They don't.
