[C1.2] They are the point.
[C1.3] Learning effects are mixed, and they track lost minutes more than the number of days. States set minimum instructional time, so districts that lengthen the remaining days are not discarding a fifth of school. A focused morning can beat a drained final period after a long rural bus ride. The off day is not automatically forgetting.
[C1.4] It can be planning, collaboration, and targeted help. Count that as instruction and the lost-week story shrinks.
[C1.5] Retention is not a temporary trick that vanishes when neighbors copy it. In hard-to-staff places, schedule flexibility is one of the few offers that does not require dollars those districts lack. A veteran who stays beats a rotating substitute on relationships, management, and finished curriculum. If nearby districts adopt the same week, that confirms the tool has value.
[C1.6] A shared norm is not the same as a shared shortage. The alternative, holding a five-day calendar while teachers leave, is the arrangement that actually compounds learning loss.
[C1.7] Savings look small only against a budget dominated by salaries, buildings, and debt. The flexible cuts are transportation, utilities, and hourly operations, the costs that otherwise force a teacher layoff or a closed program. Protecting one math position is a learning gain. Childcare burdens are real in some towns and overstated in others, where family care already covers the day and older students can work, recover, or get extra help.
[C1.8] The proposition needs a large, reliable learning loss that outweighs both retention and the budget room that keeps classrooms open. It does not have that loss. On the tradeoff districts actually face, the four-day week helps more than it harms.
