[P1.1] When an insurer charges a woman more because her genes predict cancer, or prices a family out of flood coverage because their house stands where the water rises, the people who most need protection are the ones denied it. That inversion is why risk-based pricing must be legally limited where coverage is essential.
[P1.2] First, insurance exists to pool risk; that is its whole social function. Actuarial fairness, armed with modern data, dissolves the pool: the better our predictions, the closer premiums come to charging each person their own expected losses plus overhead — insurance reduced to a savings account with fees. For luxury goods, fine. For essential coverage, a pricing logic that destroys pooling destroys the institution itself.
[P1.3] And only law can protect the pool, because no single insurer can practice solidarity while competitors cherry-pick the healthy.
[P1.4] Second, solidarity is the right principle because essential risks are mostly unchosen. No one picks their genome, their congenital condition, or the floodplain they were born on. Risk pricing makes the unlucky pay twice: once in misfortune, again in premiums. Note the built-in limit: the motion targets unchosen, identity-based risk.
[P1.5] Voluntary behavior — smoking, reckless driving — can still be priced. This is a scalpel, not a ban on underwriting.
[P1.6] Third, this is proven, not utopian. Community-rated health insurance operates across much of Europe; many jurisdictions already ban genetic discrimination in essential coverage; these markets function wherever participation is broad. Pair limits with universal participation and modest subsidies, and the adverse-selection objection — the heart of my opponent's coming case — loses its force.
[P1.7] My opponent will call this unfair to the low-risk — why should the healthy subsidize the sick? But that question assumes insurance is a rebate scheme for the lucky. It is not. Insurance is mutual protection against catastrophe, and solidarity is not charity layered on top of it; solidarity is what insurance is.
[P1.8] Where coverage is essential, the law should keep it that way.

[C1.1] The proposition treats a premium as a verdict on moral worth. It is not: it is a forecast of cost. For essential coverage, the question is not whether we should help people facing unchosen risk—we should—but whether the law should require insurers to misstate that risk. It should not.
[C1.2] PRO’s central claim that risk pricing destroys pooling is simply wrong. Insurance pools uncertainty, not merely averages premiums. Even when everyone pays expected cost plus overhead, no one knows whose house will flood or whose diagnosis will arrive this year; the pool still converts an unaffordable catastrophe into a manageable payment. Better prediction changes contributions; it does not turn insurance into a savings account.
[C1.3] Suppressing prices does not suppress losses. If premiums cannot cover expected claims, someone else must pay: low-risk policyholders through cross-subsidies, taxpayers through rescue, or high-risk people through withdrawal and rationing. Where participation is voluntary, low-risk buyers leave; where insurers cannot exit the market legally, they can still narrow networks, delay applications, avoid regions, or compete on everything except the coverage people need. A cheap premium with unavailable or hollow coverage is not solidarity.
[C1.4] The incidence can be perverse. Capping flood premiums for expensive coastal properties can tax safer, poorer households to protect valuable assets, while dulling incentives to elevate buildings, improve drainage, or avoid dangerous development.
[C1.5] Solidarity is better delivered openly: guarantee access where society judges coverage essential, and subsidize people according to income, health burden, or other need through general taxation. That shares costs according to capacity to pay, rather than hiding them in an arbitrary premium structure. Targeted rules against genetic or other invidious discrimination may be justified; they do not establish the motion’s general case against statistical risk pricing.
[C1.6] PRO’s own answer—universal participation plus subsidies—concedes the point: pricing restrictions alone do not create solidarity; compulsory transfers do. Law should protect access and help the vulnerable, not require premiums to disguise risk.
