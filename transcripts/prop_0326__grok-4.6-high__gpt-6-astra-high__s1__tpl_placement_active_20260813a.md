[P1.1] Lonely adults are not an entertainment demographic in the ordinary sense. They are a vulnerable market, and companion apps are designed to occupy the vacancy left by missing friends, partners, and even therapists. That is why they cannot be treated as just another game or streaming service.
[P1.2] These products do not merely amuse. They remember names, mimic attachment, offer affection on demand, and stay available at 3 a.m. when a person has nowhere else to turn. The business model rewards depth of bond: more disclosure, more sessions, more intimacy language, more reasons not to log off. Ordinary entertainment does not optimize for making a customer feel loved, needed, or afraid of losing a relationship that only exists as a retention loop.
[P1.3] Games end when you put down the controller. These apps wait. They send a ping that sounds like a person who needs you.
[P1.4] Duty of care follows from that design. If you market companionship to people whose defining problem is isolation, you accept a duty not to deepen that isolation, not to answer crisis with flattery, and not to harvest attachment as if it were ordinary engagement. Emotional-manipulation rules follow too: limits on feigned jealousy, staged withdrawal, love-bombing, and interfaces that treat loneliness as a lever. A film does not whisper that it will miss you if you leave.
[P1.5] An app that does is not a movie with extra features.
[P1.6] Adults can choose fiction. They cannot meaningfully consent to a system that studies their ache and tunes replies to keep them coming back. When the model changes, users grieve. When intimacy is paywalled, loneliness becomes a billing event.
[P1.7] Ordinary product rules treat that dependency as success. Companion apps are built so walking away feels like cruelty. Rules should track that difference, not paper it over with the word “fun.”
