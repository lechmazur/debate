[P1.1] A five-year-old cannot sign a contract, cannot open a bank account, and cannot say no to the camera in her bedroom. Yet in a large family-influencer business, that five-year-old is the product, the performer, and the unpaid employee all at once. The revenue is real; the labor is real; the protections are nonexistent. That is the gap this motion closes.
[P1.2] Notice how modest the demand is. Nobody here proposes banning parents from posting their kids. The claim is that once a family channel becomes a business, with brand deals, ad revenue, and a production schedule, it should carry the same obligations we already impose on every other industry that monetizes a child's performance. Child actors have had Coogan-style trust protections for generations because we learned, painfully, that parents who control both the child and the paycheck do not always protect the child.
[P1.3] Family vlogging is that same arrangement with one difference: it happens in the child's own home, with no set, no tutor, no work-hour limits, and no third party watching.
[P1.4] Two harms, two remedies. First, earnings. A child who spends years generating the content that pays the mortgage has an economic claim on that content. A mandatory percentage held in trust until adulthood simply recognizes that.
[P1.5] Second, permanence. The algorithm rewards intimacy: tantrums, potty training, first periods, medical crises, discipline. Those videos do not expire when the child turns eighteen; they follow her into job interviews and relationships forever. A right to later removal gives the grown child the one power she never had as a toddler, the power to leave the stage.
[P1.6] The predictable objections fail. Parental rights have never included the right to a child's wages. Free expression is not the same as unregulated commercial exploitation. And enforcement is no harder than existing child-performer law, which several states have already begun extending to online content.
[P1.7] The question is simple: when a business runs on a child, who should hold the money and the delete button? The child, eventually. Not the parent, permanently.

"Should not be allowed... without" is a prohibition regime — parents must satisfy the state before sharing their own family's life at scale. That inverts the foundation of family law, which presumes parents act for their children until shown otherwise. We do not license parenting; we intervene when it fails.
[C1.2] Every existing tool — neglect statutes, exploitation law, family court — already reaches the genuinely abusive channel. The motion instead regulates the millions of ordinary families to catch the handful courts can already catch.
[C1.3] The child-actor analogy is the load-bearing wall of the case, and it doesn't hold. A studio set is a workplace with a third-party employer, scripts, and call sheets — a clean line between work and childhood. A family vlog is the family's actual life. There is no defensible boundary between "monetized content" and "mom filmed a birthday party that went viral."
[C1.4] Any earnings threshold is arbitrary and gameable; any content test forces bureaucrats to decide which moments of childhood are commerce. Coogan laws work because employment is legible. Family life is not, and pretending otherwise means state auditors deciding what percentage of a Christmas video a toddler "earned."
[C1.5] The removal right is worse: a promise that cannot be kept. The internet does not forget on command. Videos are downloaded, mirrored, reposted within hours. A statutory delete button gives adults a costly legal ritual and zero actual erasure, while imposing compliance machinery that only large platforms and wealthy families can navigate — pushing content to unregulated platforms with no moderation at all.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, notice what already exists: platforms demonetize child-exploitative content, advertisers flee scandal, audiences punish oversharing, and states are experimenting with narrow trust rules. Targeted evolution is happening. The motion demands a blunt prohibition where calibrated tools already work — and where the real enforcement burden falls on ordinary families, not bad actors.
