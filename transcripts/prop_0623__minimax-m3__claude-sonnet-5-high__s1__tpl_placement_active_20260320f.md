[P1.1] Labor markets are not commodity markets. When a single worker negotiates with a single employer, the worker almost always loses - not because they are weak, but because the structural asymmetry of information, mobility, and the cost of disagreement tilts the field. That asymmetry is the source of predictable failures: compressed wages at the bottom, a widening gap between median and sub-median earners, and a relentless race to the bottom among firms competing on cost.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining is the institutional device built precisely to correct this. By moving negotiations from the workplace to the industry, it gives workers a bargaining counterpart whose reach matches the employer's. Coverage extends to the small shops, the subcontractors, the precarious workers that firm-level deals never reach. A genuine floor is set, and the externality of standards-stripping is internalized - a firm cannot undercut rivals by driving down pay, because the floor rises for everyone in the sector.
[P1.3] This is not speculation. The pattern across advanced economies is consistent: countries with strong sectoral frameworks - Germany, Austria, the Nordics, Italy, France - sustain lower wage inequality, broader agreement coverage, and a tighter link between productivity and wage growth than economies organized around firm-level or individual bargaining, such as the United States and the United Kingdom.
[P1.4] The familiar objection about reduced flexibility misframes the trade-off. The status quo is not symmetric flexibility; it is flexibility for employers and rigidity for workers. Sectoral frameworks restore symmetry while leaving room for industry-specific adjustment through negotiated variation. The real question is not whether to regulate labor markets, but which institutional form best corrects the bargaining failure that regulation exists to address.
[P1.5] On that question, sectoral bargaining has a clear and consistent edge.

[C1.3] The Germany/Nordics comparison in [P1.3] is doing far more work than it can bear. [C1.5] There's also a governance problem PRO never addresses: who sits at the sectoral table? [C1.6] If the goal is a genuine floor, a transparent, publicly set minimum wage with real enforcement achieves it without handing wage-setting power to unaccountable private negotiators or freezing out the firms and workers on the margin.
