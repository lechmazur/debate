[P1.1] Climate change is not a lack of green factories. It is a price that is wrong. When carbon is free, we use too much of it everywhere - in how we drive, heat, eat, build, fly. A rising carbon price corrects that everywhere at once.
[P1.2] Returning the money as an equal per-capita dividend makes that correction both fair and durable.
[P1.3] First, efficiency. A high, predictable carbon fee harnesses every decision-maker. A steel mill, a landlord, a commuter all see the same signal to cut. Subsidies do the opposite: government pretends to know the winning technology and writes huge checks to it.
[P1.4] That is how you get billions for battery plants while coal plants keep running because the electricity to charge is still underpriced. You pay twice - once for the subsidy, once for the climate damage you didn't stop.
[P1.5] Second, equity. Yes, a carbon price alone is regressive. But a dividend flips it. Rich households emit far more - more flights, bigger houses, more stuff.
[P1.6] Poor households emit less. Take all the revenue and divide it equally, and most low and middle-income families come out ahead in cash even as prices shift. Contrast green industrial policy: EV credits flowing to six-figure buyers, land rents for wind speculators, cost-plus contracts for incumbents. That is upward redistribution branded as climate.
[P1.7] Third, politics. Subsidies create clients, not citizens. They invite lobbying, trade disputes, and backlash the moment budgets tighten. A dividend creates a constituency of everyone who gets a monthly check that grows as polluters pay more.
[P1.8] People will defend the price because they benefit from it directly.
[P1.9] Set the price high, let people decide how to adapt, and give the money back equally. That is how you cut emissions without letting the state pick winners.

[C1.1] The PRO case rests on a tempting but dangerous simplification: climate change is just a wrong price. In the real economy, it is also a missing infrastructure problem, an investment problem, and a political-economy problem. A price can discourage emissions, but it does not build the grid, storage, clean steel, heat pumps, transit, and supply chains that make low-carbon life possible.
[C1.2] That is why the motion’s “rather than” is self-defeating. Returning all carbon revenue as equal per-capita dividends consumes the fiscal capacity needed to de-risk the transition. Private capital underinvests in early technologies, long-duration storage, retrofitting, and transmission because returns are uncertain and slow. Public subsidies, loans, procurement, and standards mobilize investment before a price alone can do the job.
[C1.3] We saw this with solar and batteries: costs collapsed when policy combined support, scale, and learning curves, not merely because carbon was priced.
[C1.4] The dividend also misreads equity. A flat check does not solve the barriers poor and rural households actually face: high upfront costs, rented housing, weak transit, no charging access, inefficient homes. A low-income commuter may receive a dividend but still have no practical alternative to an expensive fuel hike. Targeted green subsidies can make clean options cheaper and available, not just tell people to pay more.
[C1.5] Politically, PRO overstates the durability of checks. A universal dividend can be framed as a handout, cut or clawed back when budgets tighten, especially if energy prices spike. Industrial policy, done well, creates factories, workers, local tax bases, and export sectors with real stakes in decarbonization. That coalition is harder to dismantle than a monthly transfer.
[C1.6] Carbon pricing has a role, but the motion asks governments to disarm themselves. Vote CON for a policy that builds the clean economy, not just bills people for the dirty one.

[C2.2] If the price is high enough to force investment, it triggers backlash before infrastructure is built; if it is low, it does not move capital. Subsidies and standards bridge that gap; a price can even slow deployment by raising costs before alternatives exist.
[C2.3] PRO also claims dividends do not consume fiscal capacity [P2.3]. They do: every dollar returned is a dollar not used to leverage private investment. A dividend is consumption support. Industrial finance is capacity building.
[C2.4] In a climate emergency, the question is not only how to make carbon expensive, but how to make clean alternatives available fast. Returning all revenue to households leaves the state unable to solve missing markets.

Cash helps with the bill; it does not break the landlord’s incentive to ignore insulation, build a bus line, or install charging in a rental block. [C2.6] Finally, “Alaska proves” dividend durability [P2.7] proves far too much. Alaska’s fund comes from oil rents, not a punitive climate price, and even it faces political pressure. A carbon dividend may be popular until energy bills spike; then it is attacked as a handout. Factories, unions, and local supply chains create deeper, more durable stakes.
