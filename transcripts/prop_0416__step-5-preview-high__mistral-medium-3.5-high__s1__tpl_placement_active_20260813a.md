[P1.1] A woman in her late thirties pays thousands of dollars to freeze her eggs because a clinic’s website shows happy families and calls it “insurance.” She is not told that live-birth rates at her age may be a small fraction of what the imagery implies. Nor is she told that success depends most heavily on the age eggs are frozen, not when they are thawed. By the time she learns the odds, she has already spent the money and, often, the years.
[P1.2] That is the core of this debate: fertility-clinic advertising sells possibility, but patients need probability. Egg freezing is not a lottery ticket with uniform odds; its efficacy tracks age closely, deteriorating sharply after the mid-thirties. Yet marketing foregrounds potential while relegating age-specific live-birth rates, miscarriage risks, and egg-yield data to fine print—or omitting them entirely.
[P1.3] Requiring realistic age-based success rates and uncertainty to be more prominent is not an attack on reproductive choice. It is the informed-consent logic that governs other medical advertising: do not sell a procedure on hope while hiding the variables that determine whether it works. If a clinic implies freezing at forty-two is roughly as safe as at thirty-two, it distorts the decision. If a patient believes she has “stopped the clock” and can delay indefinitely, she may forgo other paths—partner choice, adoption, earlier conception—that would have remained open.
[P1.4] Uncertainty itself must be displayed, not laundered into reassurance. Clinics know the age curve and have the data. They simply are not required to put it in the headline. The asymmetry is stark: the clinic profits regardless of outcome, while the patient bears the financial cost, the medical risk, and the emotional fallout when thawed eggs do not yield a baby.
[P1.5] Disclosure does not close clinics. It closes the gap between marketing and reality. A patient who sees clear numbers may still freeze her eggs—but with eyes open, not because an ad let her imagine a guarantee.

[C1.1] Fertility-clinic ads are not the place for complex medical counseling. The proposition treats advertising as the primary educational touchpoint, but the decision to freeze eggs depends on personalized factors—ovarian reserve, lifestyle, clinic protocols—that no single age-based statistic can capture. Mandating prominent display of such numbers would force a reductive narrative: overstating risk for younger women, deterring beneficial action, or understating it for older women if averages are used. Worse, it implies the data are uniform and certain, when success rates vary widely by clinic and individual biology.
[C1.2] The current framework already requires disclosure; patients receive detailed, age-specific information during consultation, where context matters most. Pushing these numbers into headlines would not enlighten—it would overwhelm, as patients fixate on isolated probabilities without the expert interpretation that makes them meaningful. Regulation here risks replacing nuanced consent with a misleading facade of transparency, while ignoring that the real issue is patient engagement with existing information. The answer is better counseling, not cluttered ads.
[C1.3] Additionally, a one-size-fits-all standard would penalize high-performing clinics and might deter patients who could benefit from prematurely rejecting an option based on a headline number. Moreover, the requirement could lead to information overload, where patients tune out all numbers, or to clinics gaming the system by highlighting only the most favorable interpretations of the data. Heavy-handed ad regulation is a blunt instrument that could stifle informed choice rather than serve it.
