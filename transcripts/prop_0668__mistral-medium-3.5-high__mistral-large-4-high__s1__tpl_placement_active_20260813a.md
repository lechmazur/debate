[C1.1] The proposed ban rests on a fantasy of control. You cannot embargo an entire category of software and hardware—"advanced surveillance" is not a discrete object like a centrifuge, but a sprawling spectrum from facial recognition to network analytics—without creating an unenforceable, porous regime that punishes compliant firms while leaving determined abusers untouched.
[C1.2] First, the substitution effect is fatal. When Western suppliers refuse, regimes do not abandon surveillance; they pivot to Chinese and Russian vendors whose equipment is cheaper, less scrutinized, and explicitly designed for domestic control. An export ban does not reduce global repression; it cedes the market to Beijing, exporting not just hardware but the technical standards and training that entrench authoritarianism. We would be disarming our own industry while arming our rivals.
[C1.3] Second, the human-rights trigger is a political minefield. Who adjudicates "poor records"? Do we ban sales to Saudi Arabia over Yemen but allow them to Mexico over cartel violence? The criteria will inevitably reflect geopolitical convenience, not principle.
[C1.4] Such selectivity breaches the ban's moral authority and invites charges of hypocrisy that undermine any normative value.
[C1.5] Third, the legitimate-use premise is not a loophole—it is the core of the debate. Fragile democracies and even flawed states face genuine threats: terrorist networks, human trafficking, cybercrime. Denying them tools to protect their citizens does not make those threats disappear; it pushes them toward less accountable suppliers and degrades the security of the democratic world itself, which relies on shared intelligence and interoperable systems.
[C1.6] Finally, innovation requires scale. Surveillance technology improves through deployment and feedback. Cutting off export markets shrinks R&D budgets, slows defensive cybersecurity advances, and ensures that the next generation of privacy-preserving, court-supervised surveillance tools is developed in Shenzhen, not Seattle.
[C1.7] The ban is unilateral disarmament dressed as ethics. It sacrifices our technological leadership, our economic interests, and potentially our own security, while doing nothing to stop the regimes it claims to target.

[C2.1] The proposition treats surveillance technology like a discrete commodity rather than fluid code. You cannot ban "advanced surveillance" any more than you can ban "advanced mathematics." The category spans facial recognition, network analytics, and machine learning—much of it dual-use civilian software. A ban would require policing every software update and algorithmic tweak, creating an unenforceable regime that ensnares compliant firms while determined regimes route around it through shell companies or indigenous development.
[C2.2] PRO claims the substitution effect is manageable because Western tech is superior. This underestimates the market. Chinese firms like Huawei and Hikvision dominate global surveillance infrastructure, offering integrated systems designed for social control at lower cost. When Western suppliers exit, regimes do not settle for inferior tools; they gain capabilities optimized for repression without end-user monitoring or human-rights clauses.
[C2.3] We surrender leverage to impose conditions; Beijing and Moscow gain market share and technical standards.
[C2.4] The legitimacy argument is paternalistic fantasy. Authoritarian regimes do not purchase Western technology for democratic approval; they purchase capability. Denying them our products does not delegitimize their rule; it merely redirects procurement to suppliers who ask no questions.
[C2.5] The nuclear analogy collapses. Nuclear materials are scarce, trackable, and difficult to replicate. Surveillance software is code—easily copied, endlessly updated, and developed by thousands of firms globally. Export controls work for centrifuges; they fail for algorithms emailed across borders.
[C2.6] Finally, PRO dismisses legitimate policing needs as pretext. Yet the ban cannot distinguish between a police force tracking kidnappers and a secret police tracking dissidents; it blocks both. Fragile states facing terrorist networks require these tools. By cutting them off, we push legitimate users toward unaccountable vendors while doing nothing to prevent the regimes PRO cites from obtaining equivalent capabilities elsewhere.
[C2.7] The result is not less surveillance, but worse surveillance—conducted by suppliers with no interest in accountability or democratic security.
