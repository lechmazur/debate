[P1.1] A five-star Uber driver with six thousand completed trips is, on paper, a stranger the moment she opens the Lyft app. Years of verified reliability vanish, and she starts at zero — worse fares, fewer offers, algorithmic suspicion. That reputation was built by her labor, judged by her customers, and paid for with her time. The only party with no legitimate claim to lock it away is the platform.
[P1.2] Requiring standardized portability simply returns to workers what they earned.
[P1.3] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.4] First, fairness. A gig worker's rating is the functional equivalent of a résumé, a professional license, and a credit score rolled into one. No employer can confiscate your references when you quit; no bank owns your credit history. Platforms currently hold this asset hostage precisely because it is the switching cost that keeps workers captive.
[P1.5] That is not a business model deserving protection — it is a hostage arrangement.
[P1.6] Second, competition. Gig markets tilt toward monopoly: riders go where drivers are, drivers go where riders are. Reputation lock-in deepens that spiral, because even a better-paying entrant can't attract experienced workers who would forfeit their standing. Portability lowers the entry barrier, forcing incumbents to compete on pay and conditions rather than on captivity.
[P1.7] We have run this experiment before: mandated phone-number portability made telecom carriers compete on service instead of switching pain, and open banking is doing the same for financial data. The sky did not fall; markets improved.
[P1.8] Third, feasibility. This asks only for standardized transfer of ratings and verified work history — data platforms already display publicly to customers. No algorithms, no trade secrets, no pricing models. Verification standards and anti-fraud signatures are engineering problems with known solutions, the kind regulators and industry bodies solve routinely.
[P1.9] The clash here is simple: does a worker's professionally earned reputation belong to the worker, or to whichever corporation happened to record it first? Everything downstream follows from that answer.
