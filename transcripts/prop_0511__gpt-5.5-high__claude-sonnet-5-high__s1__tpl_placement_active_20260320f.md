[C1.1] Land-value taxation sounds elegant on a whiteboard. It collapses the moment you try to build it. The entire case for LVT depends on assessors cleanly separating "land value" from "improvement value" parcel by parcel, city-wide, every few years, without political interference. In practice, almost no jurisdiction does this well.
[C1.2] The handful of real-world split-rate experiments — small Pennsylvania cities, a few pilot programs — are tiny, contested, and easily confounded with other local factors. PRO is defending a theory of incentives, not a demonstrated record. Transaction taxes and rent caps, whatever their flaws, are real policies funding real budgets and protecting real tenants right now, at scale, in dozens of major cities. Comparing an untested mechanism's best-case theory against existing tools' worst-case critique is not a fair test of "improves more."
[C1.3] Even granting the incentive logic, look at who bears the shock. A pure LVT taxes site value regardless of income, which means the retired widow on a fixed pension sitting on a lot that appreciated because a subway line opened nearby faces a rising annual bill she cannot pay from cash flow. The predictable response isn't more housing — it's forced sales to whoever can absorb carrying costs, often assemblers and speculators, not builders ready to break ground immediately. Land assembly takes years of permitting fights regardless of the tax base.
[C1.4] LVT accelerates transfer of ownership, not units delivered.
[C1.5] On finance, land value is exactly the base most exposed to speculative swings, since land captures anticipated future rents. A city that leans on LVT is leaning on the most bubble-sensitive revenue source available, with assessments that lag the market on the way up and on the way down. That is a fragile foundation for the "stable" public finance PRO promises, not a superior one.
