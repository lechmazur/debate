[P1.1] The current aviation tax regime is blunt where it should be surgical. A flat ticket tax or fuel charge hits the annual holiday flyer and the executive logging forty flights a year at exactly the same rate per trip, despite wildly different contributions to emissions and wildly different capacity to pay. A progressive frequent-flyer levy fixes both problems at once, and it does so without threatening the family visit, the funeral, or the once-in-a-decade holiday.
[P1.2] Start with who actually flies. Flying is not a universal habit. A small minority of the population takes a large share of all flights, and within that minority, a core of very frequent flyers accounts for an outsized slice of aviation emissions. Meanwhile, a substantial portion of the population flies rarely or never.
[P1.3] That distribution matters morally and practically: it means the biggest, cheapest emissions reductions lie with persuading a small group to fly less, not with squeezing everyone a little harder. A levy that starts at zero or near zero for the first flight or two in a year and escalates sharply by the fifth, tenth, or twentieth flight targets exactly that group.
[P1.4] Second, it is progressive in the fiscal sense. Frequent flying correlates strongly with income: business travel, second homes abroad, weekend city breaks. Flat aviation taxes are regressive — they take a larger bite out of a modest income than a wealthy one. Escalating the per-flight charge turns aviation taxation right-side up: ability to pay and responsibility for the externality both rise together.
[P1.5] The occasional flyer on a cheap fare to see relatives pays little or nothing extra; the person treating the skies as a personal taxi network pays for the true cost of that habit.
[P1.6] Third, it changes behavior where behavior needs changing. Cheap short-haul flights proliferate because the price signal is too weak. A levy that bites hardest on marginal flights makes the fifth weekend trip genuinely expensive while leaving the first untouched — precisely the pricing logic that reduces excess demand without punishing basic mobility.

[C1.4] Second, the levy misfires on its primary target: the "executive logging forty flights a year" [P1.1]. [C1.6] If we want greener, fairer skies, tax jet fuel, distance, and luxury cabin classes directly—do not build a state travel registry that punishes geography while giving long-haul luxury a tax holiday.
