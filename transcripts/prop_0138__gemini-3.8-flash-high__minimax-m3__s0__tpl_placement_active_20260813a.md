[P1.1] A human life should never be engineered around a permanent, state-enforced secret. When an adult is denied the legal right to know their genetic parentage, they are denied a foundational element of human identity. This debate is not about compelling a forced relationship or financial obligation; it is about whether the state should actively conceal the identity of the person whose biology brought them into existence.
[P1.2] First, biological knowledge is integral to individual autonomy and dignity. Understanding one’s roots—ethnicity, ancestral history, and the basic reality of where one comes from—shapes how a person navigates the world. To systematically withhold this information is to consign donor-conceived individuals to permanent genealogical bewilderment, treating them as products of a commercial transaction rather than autonomous rights-bearers.
[P1.3] Second, identifying information is a medical necessity. Non-identifying profiles captured at the time of donation are frozen snapshots. They cannot account for late-onset hereditary diseases, emerging cardiac conditions, or cancer diagnoses that develop in donors decades later. Direct identifying access allows for real-time, potentially life-saving family health histories and provides a critical safeguard against accidental consanguinity in an era where single donors can father dozens of half-siblings.
[P1.4] Third, the rights of the conceived person must override market convenience. Even if lifting anonymity reduces donor supply, that reduction cannot justify the ongoing violation of a person's identity rights. In adoption and broader family law, we have long recognized that the welfare and fundamental rights of the individual brought into the world must take precedence over the preferences of the adults involved. Donors and intended parents enter a voluntary arrangement, but the child never consented to having their lineage legally erased to make procurement easier.
[P1.5] A supply contraction is a manageable policy challenge. Severing a person from their genetic truth is an irreversible harm. We must prioritize human dignity over donor convenience.
