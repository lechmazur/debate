[P1.1] The central question is whether a better default can convert widespread approval of organ donation into actual transplants without overriding anyone. Presumed consent does exactly that. Most people support donation but never complete a registry form. Under opt-in, that inertia produces shortages.
[P1.2] Under presumed consent, silence is treated as permission only when there is no recorded refusal and families still have a chance to speak. The result is a measurable shift: jurisdictions that move to opt-out systems tend to see higher donation and transplant rates, not because they coerce, but because the default matches what most people already favor.
[P1.3] This matters for “most patients” because the bottleneck is not moral opposition; it is administrative friction and ambiguous bedside decisions. A presumed-consent framework moves the conversation from “Did they ever sign up?” to “What did they say, and does anyone know an objection?” That reduces the burden on families who otherwise must guess during the worst moments of their lives.
[P1.4] Ethically, the change is conservative. It adds no power to take organs over a clear refusal. It preserves an easy, well-publicized opt-out. It excludes people who cannot decide for themselves and cases where wishes are unknown and no surrogate can confirm.
[P1.5] It keeps families involved as witnesses to the patient’s values, not as obstacles. Nor is there a reason distress must rise: replacing silence with a structured presumption gives relatives a clearer, more compassionate script than asking them to invent consent from nothing.
[P1.6] The real ethical violation is allowing preventable deaths to persist when the public already supports donation but the paperwork default stands in the way. Presumed consent respects choice, increases actual donation where consent is rebuttable, and delivers the outcome patients need: more transplants.

[C1.1] Changing the paperwork does not create more donors. Most organs suitable for transplant come from a narrow, difficult pathway: patients dying in intensive care under specific brain-death conditions, identified in time, maintained physiologically, with timely surgical teams and matched recipients. A registry default does nothing to add ICU beds, coordinators, or operating room capacity. That is why opt-out switches so often disappoint: registration figures rise on paper while actual deceased donations barely move, because families still say no and hospitals still miss potential donors.
[C1.2] Silence is not consent, and pretending that it is creates ethical violations, not removes them. Under opt-in, no means no and yes means yes. Under presumed consent, confusion, illiteracy, language barriers, distrust of medicine, or simply never hearing about the law becomes a yes. The burden falls hardest on the poor, the elderly isolated from care, recent immigrants, and religious minorities whose objections were never recorded.
[C1.3] Taking organs on that basis is not respecting what most people want; it is conscripting the least informed.
[C1.4] It also makes the bedside worse. When staff must tell grieving relatives their loved one is presumed a donor unless they prove otherwise, suspicion replaces generosity. Families asked to veto feel pressured, guilty, and defensive, and many refuse precisely to protect their loved one. That conflict is distress by design, and it invites the very mistrust that reduces donation.
[C1.5] Proponents cannot have it both ways. If families keep a veto, the default changes little; if the state overrides grieving families to make the numbers move, that is by definition an ethical violation and a source of trauma.
[C1.6] Preventable deaths demand real solutions — better end-of-life identification, trained requesters, support for living donation — not a legal fiction that inflates consent while violating autonomy and tormenting families.
