[P1.1] Courts exist to stop preventable harm, not merely to catalog it after the fact. When credible evidence shows a person poses an imminent risk of gun violence to themselves or others, a judge should be able to order temporary firearm removal. Waiting for a criminal conviction waits for a body.
[P1.2] A conviction answers whether a crime already happened. Red-flag orders answer whether a shooting is about to happen. Those are different questions, and treating them as the same question is how families bury people who had already asked for help. A partner who has been threatened, a parent who has watched a child spiral into suicidal crisis with a gun in the house, a neighbor who has seen a specific plan—these people do not need a prosecutor to prove an old felony.
[P1.3] They need a court that can act now, briefly, and under evidence.
[P1.4] We already do this elsewhere. Emergency protective orders can keep an abuser out of a home before any conviction. Short-term mental-health holds can restrict a person's liberty without a trial. Banks can be ordered to freeze accounts.
[P1.5] Temporary gun removal is not a novel tyranny; it is the same civil tool applied to the weapon that turns minutes of rage into irreversible death.
[P1.6] The order is temporary, court-supervised, and tied to credible evidence—not rumor, not politics, not a permanent ban. Due process is the hearing, the burden, the expiration date, and the right to contest. What due process does not require is that society stand still until after the shot is fired.
[P1.7] If courts cannot act until after a conviction, then the only people protected are those lucky enough to survive the first attempt. A wrongful pause can be undone at a hearing. A completed shooting cannot. That is not a rights regime.
[P1.8] It is a delay regime, and delay is not liberty.

[C1.1] Notice what the order actually does. A judge finds that a man is so dangerous he may kill someone within days—and then sends him home. Not to a hospital. Not to a cell.
[C1.2] Home, with his car keys, his kitchen knives, his gasoline can, and his grievance, now sharpened by the knowledge that someone he knows testified against him in a hearing he wasn't told about. If the danger is real, that is malpractice. If the danger is not real, we have just stripped a constitutional right from an innocent person on a prediction. Red-flag laws are built for the gap between those two outcomes, and the gap is where injustice lives.
[C1.3] The mechanism matters. These orders are almost always ex parte: the accused is absent, cannot cross-examine, and often learns of the case when officers arrive at dawn. A Maryland man was shot dead in his own doorway when police came to seize his guns under one of these orders. He had been convicted of nothing.
[C1.4] Then the burden flips—the respondent must prove a negative, that he is *not* dangerous, frequently without appointed counsel, while "temporary" stretches through renewals into a year or more and a permanent record.
[C1.5] PRO's own analogies indict the proposal. Mental-health holds require a clinician's evaluation and restrain the actual person. Protective orders rest on a specific act against a specific victim who faces the accused. Threatening someone is already a crime, arrestable tonight.
[C1.6] Every tool PRO lists either has a professional gatekeeper, a completed act, or removes the dangerous human being. Red-flag laws have none of these. They are the one instrument that deprives a right on a layperson's forecast of future conduct—and forecasting violence is something judges, and even psychiatrists, do badly, because the base rate is tiny and most people who "seem dangerous" never act.
[C1.7] So the real choice is not action versus delay. It is existing tools that confront the person, versus a shortcut that confiscates an object, skips the accused, and calls the leftover risk "solved."
