[P1.1] Legal personhood is a standing rule, not a hymn. When a river or ecosystem can hold rights, guardians can go to court before the last wetland is filled. Ordinary environmental law still treats these systems as property or as an externality on a balance sheet. That is why dams, mines, and highways keep winning until collapse is irreversible.
[P1.2] Granting personhood when Indigenous or local communities request it changes who may say no, and it should.
[P1.3] Those communities drink the water, fish the banks, and bury their dead along the same shores. Their request is a legitimacy filter. The state is not inventing spirits; it is letting the people most bound to a living system appoint guardians and enforce duties of care. New Zealand’s recognition of the Whanganui River as a legal person after Māori insistence did not freeze every project.
[P1.4] It forced the state to share guardianship and treat harm to the river as harm to a rights-holder, not a permit cost.
[P1.5] Complication is treated as a reason to refuse, and it is the reason to accept. Infrastructure is cheap and fast when downstream villages, fisheries, and sacred sites are ignored until the concrete is poured. A legal person cannot be bargained away as inventory. Developers must redesign, reroute, or walk away.
[P1.6] That is not hostility to roads and power. It is a rule that development may not annihilate the conditions of life for people who never consented. Without personhood, mitigation is usually a promise after extraction begins. With it, the river’s interest is a party before the first blast.
[P1.7] Refuse, and distant ministries still decide while locals get consultation after the fact. Personhood on community request reverses that sequence. Flood control, power, and transport remain possible; they simply cannot treat a living system as dead inventory. Delay is reversible.
[P1.8] A dead river is not. Governments should choose the harm that can still be undone.

[C1.1] Rivers deserve strong protection, but making them legal persons on demand is the wrong tool and will hurt the very people it claims to help.
[C1.2] A river cannot speak, hire a lawyer, or compromise. Guardians will. Granting personhood on community request hands anyone certified as the river's voice a veto over water, power, flood control, and transport affecting millions who never chose them. That is not democracy; it is unaccountable rule by self-appointed trustees, immune from elections and able to re-litigate every permit as a violation of the river's rights.
[C1.3] Ordinary environmental law can be tough without this mysticism. Enforceable pollution limits, flow minimums, impact assessment, and co-management give rivers real protection while leaving final trade-offs to accountable governments that must weigh farmers against fishers, cities against villages. Personhood collapses that balance into a courtroom question: what does the river want? Judges have no principled way to answer, so decisions become arbitrary, protracted, and chilling.
[C1.4] Developers, utilities, and municipalities walk away not because projects are dirty, but because risk is unknowable.
[C1.5] The cost falls hardest on the poor. The dams, levees, reservoirs, irrigation canals, and bridges that personhood would complicate are how poor communities get clean water, electricity, flood safety, and access to hospitals and markets. Delay is not reversible when a child dies in a flood season that a stalled levee could have prevented, or when villages stay on diesel because hydro was litigated to death.
[C1.6] Nor is a community request a clean legitimacy filter. Communities divide. Elders disagree, villages upstream compete with villages downstream, and outsiders can fund a faction to claim guardianship. The state must then pick which Indigenous voice counts, inflaming conflict while granting a local minority a national veto.
[C1.7] Protect ecosystems through rights of people to clean water and binding environmental duties, not by inventing a new person to block the infrastructure people need to live.

[C2.1] PRO says personhood is just standing, guardians can lose and be replaced, so no veto [P2.1]. That is legal theory. In infrastructure practice, standing to sue over every permit as a rights violation is a veto by delay. A reservoir or levee that survives judicial review after eight years of injunctions does not survive financing, elections, or flood seasons.
[C2.2] Developers do not need a formal ban to walk away; unknowable risk is enough.
[C2.3] The company analogy misleads. A company has a charter, owners, and a measurable purpose judges can interpret. A trust has settlor intent. A river has none. “What does the river want” — higher flows or flood control, wild banks or safe crossings — has no answer except the guardian’s preference dressed as nature’s will.
[C2.4] Courts would not be applying law; they would be choosing between villages while pretending the river chose.
[C2.5] Nor has ordinary law simply failed [P2.3]. Limits, flow minimums, and co-management fail when unenforced or underfunded. Giving the same state another defendant and another courtroom does not fix enforcement; it moves the trade-off from legislators who must answer to farmers and fishers alike into litigation where only one side claims to speak for life itself.
[C2.6] That is why the flood child still counts. PRO says levees are not outlawed, only barred from treating the river as dead inventory [P2.5]. But levees and reservoirs by nature straighten, store, and fill. If that violates a duty of care to the river-person, permission becomes the exception.
[C2.7] PRO calls delay reversible. A drowned harvest, a cholera season, a decade without power are not reversible for the people who live them.
[C2.8] And a request is no legitimacy filter. PRO admits states already choose which faction to hear [P2.7]. Personhood raises the prize for that choice from a consultation note to permanent legal control, inviting rival claims, outside money, and upstream villages vetoing water downstream cities need to live.
