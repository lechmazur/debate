[C1.1] Solidarity is a vital social goal, but it cannot survive on mathematical denial. Insurance is not a sovereign treasury; it is a mechanism of solvency built on the empirical reality of statistical risk. When the law forcibly suppresses actuarial pricing in essential coverage, it does not eliminate risk—it merely forces low-risk participants to absorb high-risk liabilities through opaque, coercive cross-subsidies.
[C1.2] The fatal consequence of limiting risk pricing is adverse selection. When governments mandate uniform rates or restrict actuarial rating, low-risk individuals—frequently younger, healthier, or lower-income families—are dramatically overcharged relative to their actual expected loss. Faced with unaffordable premiums that subsidize others, healthy participants delay purchasing coverage or exit the market entirely. This triggers the classic insurance death spiral: the risk pool becomes progressively sicker and more costly, premiums escalate across the board, and the entire system collapses.
[C1.3] Far from fostering social solidarity, price suppression destroys the solvency of the very pools needed to sustain essential coverage.
[C1.4] Furthermore, actuarial pricing provides an indispensable informational signal. When property insurance accurately reflects flood or wildfire risk, it deters construction in hazardous areas and forces local governments to invest in physical mitigation, such as levees and firebreaks. Blunting that price signal creates pervasive moral hazard, subsidizes risky development, and leaves communities more vulnerable to catastrophic shocks.
[C1.5] The debate presents a false choice between unassisted vulnerability and distorted markets. If society chooses to protect individuals who face unchosen, catastrophic risks—such as congenital health conditions or inherited environmental hazards—the sustainable instrument is targeted public subsidies financed through progressive general taxation, not the legal distortion of private risk mechanics.
[C1.6] Direct fiscal support protects the vulnerable while preserving actuarial integrity. Limiting actuarial fairness breaks the pricing mechanism that makes insurance viable, ultimately replacing solidarity with systemic market failure.
