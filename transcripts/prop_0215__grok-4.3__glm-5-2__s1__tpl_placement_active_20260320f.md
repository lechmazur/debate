[P1.1] When institutions ran residential schools and assimilation programs as direct agents of the state, their retained assets create a standing obligation to help fund remedies for the harms they administered. Religious orders controlled daily operations, enforced language bans, separated families, and oversaw sites where documented abuse occurred. Requiring financial contributions to reparative funds simply aligns cost with that operational control rather than leaving survivors or the broader public to absorb it.
[P1.2] Exempting these entities preserves the economic gains they secured through state contracts, land grants, and labor arrangements tied to the same policies. Successor organizations continue to hold property and endowments traceable to that period; shielding those resources while victims bear lasting medical, cultural, and economic losses produces a clear transfer of burden away from those most responsible.
[P1.3] Targeted payments into survivor-managed funds also deliver practical redress that governments alone have repeatedly under-delivered. Such contributions can expand counseling access, support language programs, and finance community claims processes without new taxation or cuts elsewhere. This mechanism matches the scale of institutional involvement instead of diffusing accountability across society at large.
[P1.4] Claims that current church structures bear no link to past actions overlook how the organizations themselves endured, retained legal personality, and benefited from continuity. When the same bodies accepted state authority to reshape children’s lives, their later refusal to share in repair severs remedy from the agents who executed the policy on the ground.

[C1.1] The state designed residential schools and forced assimilation as official policy—it legislated their creation, appropriated the funds, and retained supervisory authority. Religious orders served as implementers under state direction. The proposition inverts this relationship by billing the instrument rather than the principal, and that inversion matters: it lets the architect of policy escape full financial responsibility while drawing down assets from organizations that carried out state directives.
[C1.2] This is not exculpation. It is a question of where the binding obligation lies. When a government mandates a system, funds it, and oversees it, that government owns the debt of repair. Redirecting the obligation to religious orders—many of which have already contributed through existing legal settlements—creates a political escape valve.
[C1.3] The state can claim reparations are "funded" while quietly shifting cost away from its own treasury onto smaller institutional actors.
[C1.4] The proposition also assumes institutional continuity transfers moral debt to current members. But today's members of religious orders had no role in historical abuses. Legal personality endured, yes—but so did the state's, and the state actually designed the policy. If continuity generates obligation, the state's share cannot be reduced by distributing it to contractors who executed its instructions.
[C1.5] Finally, many of these institutions still run schools, hospitals, shelters, and social programs in the very communities reparative funds aim to serve. Seizing their endowments and property may satisfy a symbolic demand for accountability, but it risks hollowing out services vulnerable people rely on today. Reparation that impoverishes institutions serving survivors defeats its own purpose.
[C1.6] The state commissioned the harm. The state must fund the repair. Anything less is a transfer of obligation from the powerful to the convenient.

[P2.1] When religious orders accepted state contracts to operate residential schools, they exercised daily control over enrollment, discipline, language policy, and family separation. Requiring targeted contributions into survivor funds does not relieve the state of its larger duty; it prevents the state from becoming the sole payer while the organizations that profited from the same policies keep every asset untouched.
[P2.3] Continuity matters here in the same way it matters for any enduring legal entity. The orders retained corporate personality, property, and internal governance across generations. Shifting the entire burden onto the state because current members were not alive at the time would mean every long-lived corporation could disclaim responsibility for its prior acts, a rule no other domain accepts.
[P2.4] The warning that contributions will harm ongoing social services assumes these institutions have no capacity to adjust without collapse. Many have already reached settlements or redirected portions of endowments without ending their work. Survivors who lost language, family ties, and health bear permanent costs; preserving every institutional dollar to shield current programs simply transfers those costs once more to the harmed rather than the institutions that helped administer the policy.

They were state-commissioned implementers executing legislated policy under public authority. [C2.2] Calling what remained "daily control" overstates autonomy. A facility manager who administers a government program to government specifications isn't the architect of that program—and billing them as if they were lets the designer walk away from most of the cost.
[C2.3] [P2.2] frames retained assets as profit from the same policies. But the land and buildings were typically the infrastructure of the schools themselves—state-contracted facilities, not extracted wealth. And the proposition as written doesn't require the state to pay anything. It attaches obligation to religious orders.
[C2.4] Once institutional assets flow in, political pressure on the state to fund its own share dissolves. The mechanism becomes a substitute for public reparations, not a supplement.
[C2.5] On continuity [P2.3]: corporate liability attaches because corporations act in pursuit of their own objectives. Residential schools were not the orders' independent enterprise—they were state policy they agreed to administer. The analogy breaks precisely because initiative, design, and funding all came from the state.
[C2.6] PRO's answer on services [P2.4] concedes that survivors depend on these institutions' current programs while insisting their assets be redirected anyway. You cannot drain the endowments of organizations running shelters, clinics, and schools in the very communities reparative funds aim to serve and call that justice. The costs survivors carry are undeniable. The remedy is a state that designed and commissioned the harm paying in full—not a scheme that trades services survivors use today for yesterday's accounting.
