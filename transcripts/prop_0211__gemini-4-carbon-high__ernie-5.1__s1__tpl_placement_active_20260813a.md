[P1.1] Modern obesity drugs offer historic clinical promise, yet they pose an unprecedented fiscal math problem. Traditional drug pricing assumes high prices apply to small patient pools, while common conditions get low-cost treatments. Preventive obesity therapy shatters that rule: it pairs specialty-tier annual prices with an eligible population spanning a massive share of the adult public. Reimbursing these drugs broadly with an open checkbook would swallow healthcare budgets whole, crowding out emergency services, cancer care, and mental health clinics years before any downstream savings from avoided diabetes or heart disease ever materialize.
[P1.2] Unconditional reimbursement also forces payers to absorb lopsided clinical risk. Preventive value depends entirely on sustained, multi-year results. Yet in everyday practice, outside tightly coached clinical trials, many patients stop taking these medications due to side effects or treatment fatigue, and when treatment stops, weight and cardiometabolic risk typically rebound. Without outcome-based pricing, health systems pay billions upfront for theoretical future prevention that evaporates whenever persistence falters, while manufacturers collect full revenue regardless of whether lasting health gains actually occur.
[P1.3] Conditioning broad coverage on spending caps or outcome agreements solves both problems without choking off innovation. Once a drug is developed, manufacturing extra doses costs a small fraction of list prices. Under spending caps, drugmakers trade uncapped per-unit windfalls for vast, guaranteed patient volume, securing enormous, predictable rewards for research while giving public budgets and insurers hard fiscal certainty. Under outcome-based deals, manufacturers gain a direct financial stake in real-world tolerability and sustained patient adherence.
[P1.4] Most importantly, the real-world alternative to this condition is not generous, frictionless access. Confronted with ruinous, uncapped budget exposure, payers inevitably protect solvency through harsh rationing, arbitrary eligibility walls, and denying treatment until preventable organ damage has already begun. Requiring outcome-based pricing or spending caps does not block broad preventive care; it builds the only fiscal foundation sturdy enough to deliver it.

Preventive obesity drugs, such as the GLP‑1 receptor agonists now entering formularies, act over years; the clinical endpoints that would trigger payment—major adverse cardiovascular events, dialysis initiation—may never materialize for many individuals, making any “outcome” clause indeterminate and legally fraught.
[C1.2] Spending caps sound fiscally prudent, yet they create a supply‑side paradox. Manufacturers already price these agents at a premium because development costs are high and the eligible population is enormous. Imposing a per‑year ceiling forces firms to either curtail production, withdraw from markets with tight caps, or shift costs to other products, ultimately reducing patient access. [C1.3] Moreover, the PRO’s premise that without such conditions payers will resort to draconian rationing is speculative. Health systems routinely manage expensive therapies through tiered formularies, step therapy, and negotiated discounts without binding outcome contracts. Broad reimbursement paired with robust health‑technology assessment—evaluating cost per quality‑adjusted life‑year—offers a flexible, evidence‑based lever that preserves both access and fiscal responsibility. Conditioning coverage on manufacturer concessions makes the system hostage to industry bargaining power, slowing rollout precisely when rapid, population‑wide treatment is needed to curb the obesity epidemic.
[C1.4] The real fiscal risk lies not in paying for preventive drugs, but in refusing to act while the disease burden climbs.
