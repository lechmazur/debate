[P1.1] Elite universities sell ladders. They operate as vaults.
[P1.2] Admission is not an open race for talent. It is a contest where wealth buys preparation years in advance: stable housing, well-funded schools, tutoring, test coaching, music lessons, travel teams, unpaid internships, essay editors, and early applications that only families indifferent to aid can risk. On top of that come legacy boosts, donor attention, and feeder-school pipelines that turn parental status into points. The predictable result is a student body drawn wildly out of proportion from affluent homes, with low-income students present as exceptions that prove the rule.
[P1.3] A few remarkable ascents cannot outweigh a system that mainly admits people already at the top.
[P1.4] The degree then launders that advantage into merit. Top firms in finance, consulting, law, and technology concentrate recruiting on a handful of famous campuses. Professors recommend familiar students, alumni open doors for juniors who remind them of themselves, and interviewers reward polish, confidence, and cultural fluency as “leadership potential.” The credential does not erase class; it certifies it, giving inherited comfort the appearance of earned desert. Employers can then hire for pedigree while claiming to hire for ability.
[P1.5] That is why reproduction outweighs mobility. Mobility means moving large numbers of disadvantaged people upward. What these institutions mostly do is move advantaged people securely onward, protecting them from downward competition while a small scholarship class provides the photograph for the brochure.
[P1.6] Policy should stop subsidizing the myth. Tax exemptions, deductible donations, public research money, and federally backed loans should not flow on the assumption that elite schools are fairness machines. Condition support on ending legacy preference, expanding enrollment, and transferring resources, and direct the center of funding toward broad-access colleges that actually educate working people at scale.
