[P1.1] States do not exist only to minimize liability. They exist to do justice, and a formal apology is one of the few tools that can name a wrong without pretending it never happened. Legal advisers will always warn that apology may increase compensation claims. That warning is a reason to plan, not a reason to remain silent.
[P1.2] The proposition does not require reckless apologies; it requires more willingness to apologize despite legal risk.
[P1.3] First, apology is not just a precursor to money. It is recognition. For survivors of atrocities, the state's refusal to say "this was wrong" is a second injury. Germany's postwar reckoning, Australia's apology to Indigenous peoples, Canada's apology for residential schools—these did not end history, but they gave public truth a place in law and memory.
[P1.4] When a state withholds that truth because some claimants might sue, it treats victims as liabilities rather than citizens.
[P1.5] Second, the fear of increased claims often reveals that claims were unjustly suppressed. If an apology allows people to seek compensation, that is not necessarily a crisis; it may be the first fair hearing they have had. A government that says, "We are sorry, but we cannot admit it because you might ask for something," is not protecting the public purse. It is using legal technicalities to avoid accountability.
[P1.6] Third, legal advice is not a veto. States routinely accept legal risk for infrastructure, treaties, and war. They can structure apologies: distinguish moral acknowledgment from legal admission, create compensation funds, set limitations periods prospectively, and negotiate settlements. If the warning is that some claims may succeed, the answer is democratic deliberation about what justice costs—not silence.
[P1.7] The CON side will say apology is a blank check. But the real blank check is impunity: a state that can commit atrocities and then refuse to acknowledge them whenever lawyers predict a bill. More willingness to apologize does not mean ignoring law. It means refusing to let law become an excuse for historical amnesia.
