[P1.1] Populism is best understood not by its slogans but by its mechanism, and that mechanism is the tell. Every genuine populist movement, left or right, shares one structural feature: it claims to be the *sole* legitimate voice of "the real people" against a corrupt, illegitimate elite. That is not incidental rhetoric — it is the ideology's core. And an ideology built on the premise that anyone standing in your way (courts, press, civil service, opposition parties) is illegitimate is, by construction, hostile to the pluralist machinery that lets democracies self-correct without a strongman.
[P1.2] This is why the "healthy corrective" story gets the causal arrow backwards. Ordinary party competition already channels elite failure into reform: a scandal-plagued government loses an election, a new coalition governs, institutions absorb the shock. Populism arises specifically where that channel is already clogged — where trust in courts, media, and parties has already eroded enough that "burn it all down" becomes plausible. So populism's rise is evidence institutional decay has *already happened*, not proof that a corrective is now healthily underway.
[P1.3] And the track record shows populism doesn't repair the channel — it widens the hole. Hungary's Fidesz rode real grievances about post-Soviet corruption into power, then rewrote the constitution, packed the courts, and captured the media landscape until elections became formally free but substantively uncompetitive. Venezuela's Chavismo channeled legitimate anger at oligarchic elites into a project that hollowed out the legislature and judiciary until "the people's will" meant one man's will. In the United States, a movement styling itself as anti-elite corrective spent its energy delegitimizing election results and branding independent press "the enemy of the people" — attacking the referees rather than reforming the game.
[P1.4] The question for this debate isn't whether elites fail — they do. It's whether populism's *characteristic response* to that failure is institutional renewal or institutional capture. The evidence points overwhelmingly to capture, which is exactly why populism should be read as the fever, not the medicine.
