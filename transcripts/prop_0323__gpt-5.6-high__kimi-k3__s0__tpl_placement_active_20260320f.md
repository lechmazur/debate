[P1.1] A number beside a post looks like information, but it is also an instruction: this is popular, so notice it, trust it, and help it spread. Platforms should stop making that instruction the default. Public like and repost counts should be hidden unless users choose to display them. That preserves choice while removing a platform-designed popularity contest from everyone’s baseline experience.
[P1.2] Visible metrics distort expression. Users learn to write for immediate applause: sharper outrage, safer conformity, more flattering images, and more provocative claims. A teenager considering whether to share artwork is pushed to ask not “Is this meaningful?” but “Will this perform?” A creator whose thoughtful post receives little early engagement may delete it, while an inflammatory post gains momentum precisely because its count signals momentum. Hidden counts weaken this feedback loop and let people evaluate content before being told how the crowd reacted.
[P1.3] They also corrupt judgment. Popularity is not quality, truth, or social value. Yet a large count provides social proof, causing later users to imitate earlier reactions. That creates cumulative advantage: small accidents in timing or audience can become enormous differences in reach.
[P1.4] Bots, coordinated groups, and engagement bait can exploit the same shortcut. Removing the public scoreboard does not eliminate manipulation, but it takes away one of its most powerful advertisements.
[P1.5] The default is crucial. Platforms know that most people accept default settings, while those most vulnerable to comparison pressure should not bear the burden of finding obscure controls. A reversible, count-hidden default is therefore genuine user empowerment, not paternalism. People who enjoy visible metrics can opt in; creators can still receive private analytics; platforms can still recommend material using relevance, relationships, and user-selected interests.
[P1.6] The choice is not between metrics and chaos. It is between using engagement quietly as one signal and turning engagement into a public status hierarchy that pressures every user. Social platforms already design the environment in which attention is allocated. They should design for considered participation, not make every conversation resemble a leaderboard.
