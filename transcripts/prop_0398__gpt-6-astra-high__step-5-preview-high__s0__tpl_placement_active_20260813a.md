[P1.1] A headquarters salary is not a permanent entitlement to the headquarters premium. When workers voluntarily move somewhere substantially cheaper, a transparent, proportionate location adjustment is generally reasonable. The right comparison is the whole arrangement—pay, purchasing power, and freedom to relocate—not whether the number on a payslip remains untouched.
[P1.2] Compensation reflects more than output. Employers pay for skills and responsibility, but also compete in labor markets where recruitment costs and workers’ alternatives differ. Expensive headquarters locations can require higher salaries to attract staff. A worker who leaves that location retains their skills, but cannot simply assume every geographic premium must follow them forever. “My output is unchanged” supports preserving compensation for the role; it does not establish that the headquarters band is the uniquely fair price.
[P1.3] This is not an invitation to audit anyone’s grocery bills. The defensible policy uses published regional bands for comparable roles, preserves performance rewards, and honors contractual commitments. [P1.4] A worker can accept a moderate reduction while retaining substantial savings on housing and a higher material standard of living.
[P1.5] Regional consistency also avoids an arbitrary hierarchy. Imagine two equally capable colleagues doing the same job in the same lower-cost town. [P1.6] Location-adjusted bands provide a coherent baseline instead of making headquarters access a privileged route to permanently higher pay.
[P1.7] Finally, sharing the economic gains gives employers a reason to offer relocation rather than restrict remote work or reserve positions for headquarters. Savings do not guarantee additional hiring, but they make geographically distributed employment easier to sustain.
