[P1.1] The central question is what buys the largest immediate reduction in the poverty gap per dollar. Child poverty is, first and foremost, a shortage of household income. Expanding a refundable child tax credit with monthly payments attacks that shortage directly: the money reaches the household, month by month, before rent is missed, before the electricity is cut off, before food runs short. Because it is cash, families can spend it on the constraints that actually keep them poor: housing deposits, car repairs, medicine, utilities, clothing, and yes, child care if that is the binding need.
[P1.2] Monthly payment matters. Poverty is not just low annual income; it is volatility. A lump-sum refund after tax season does not stop an August eviction or a winter heating shutoff. Regular payments smooth consumption, reduce arrears, and make it easier for parents to keep jobs and children to stay in stable homes.
[P1.3] A well-designed credit can be fully refundable, so it reaches children in families with little or no earnings, including infants, disabled children, and caregivers outside the formal labor market. That makes it highly progressive per dollar.
[P1.4] Expanded childcare subsidies can help, but they are a slower and leakier anti-poverty tool. They depend on available slots, eligible providers, paperwork, and often work requirements. Their value is mediated by supply and quality; in many communities there simply are not enough affordable slots, especially for infants, evenings, or rural areas. Equivalent spending on subsidies can therefore be absorbed by administrative costs, provider payments, or families who would have paid for care anyway, while doing little for the poorest households.
[P1.5] The comparison is not whether child care is valuable. It is. It is whether the same dollars most cost-effectively reduce child poverty. Direct monthly income does that immediately, with lower administrative burden, and lets the poorest families solve their own binding constraints.

[C1.5] Subsidies are tied to an actual service that frees labor supply.

It leaves the parent home and the poverty rate stuck.
[C1.7] The comparison is not whether cash feels immediate. It is whether the same budget produces deeper, more durable exits from child poverty. Enabling work through childcare does. Passing out monthly credits does not match that return.

The poorest families are exactly the ones least likely to clear those hurdles.

[P2.5] A parent may use it for a deposit, utilities, medicine, or yes, informal child care with a relative. That stabilizes the household immediately and can preserve employment. A subsidy tied to formal care cannot pay the overdue rent that precedes eviction.
[P2.6] Finally, CON treats cash as merely consumed, but reducing child poverty is precisely about consumption: enough food, heat, and housing this month.
