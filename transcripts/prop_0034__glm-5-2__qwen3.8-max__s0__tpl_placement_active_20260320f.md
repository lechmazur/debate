[P1.1] A voice is not just a sound. It is one of the most intimate markers of identity—tied to reputation, career, and trust. When a platform commercially distributes an AI-generated voice imitating a specific, identifiable person, it monetizes that person's identity without earning the right to do so. The proposition is simple: if you want to sell someone's voice, ask them first.
[P1.2] The case rests on three points the opposition cannot escape.
[P1.3] First, the harm is asymmetric and irreversible. Once a convincing voice clone reaches commercial distribution, it can deceive listeners, damage professional credibility, and enable fraud at scale. An actor whose cloned voice narrates content they never endorsed loses control over their most marketable asset. A cloned voice impersonating a public figure can cause real-world damage before any takedown is processed.
[P1.4] You cannot un-ring that bell. Consent before distribution is the only safeguard that operates before harm occurs.
[P1.5] Second, commercial incentives cut against self-regulation. Platforms profit from engagement and distribution volume. Their incentive is to move first and moderate later. Opt-out regimes—where distribution proceeds unless someone objects—place the entire burden on the individual to monitor the internet for misuse of their own voice.
[P1.6] That is not protection; it is a treadmill. Affirmative consent reverses the default: the platform must secure permission before profiting, which is where the burden belongs.
[P1.7] Third, this is not a novel restriction. We already require consent for commercial use of a person's likeness, endorsement, and performance. A voice clone is functionally indistinguishable from a performance—it replicates the expressive output of a specific person. Treating it differently because a machine generated it ignores what makes it commercially valuable: identification with a real individual.
[P1.8] The opposition will frame this as a burden on innovation. But the cost of asking is minimal compared to the cost of having your identity sold without your knowledge. Consent is not censorship; it is the floor of decency in a market where a person's voice can be copied in seconds and broadcast to millions.

[C1.1] The proposition loses because it turns every voice likeness into a permission slip, even when the public interest, expression, or competition policy demands no such gate. A universal affirmative-consent rule before commercial distribution is overbroad, unadministrable, and poorly aimed at the harms it claims to prevent.
[C1.2] First, it sweeps far beyond exploitation. Commercial distribution includes audiobooks, video games, satire, dubbing, accessibility tools, and news documentaries. A parody review using a recognizable presidential voice, a historical drama reconstructing a famous speech, or a critic’s caricature of a celebrity would all become hostage to the very person being imitated. That is not privacy protection; it is a veto over expression about public figures.
[C1.3] If a politician, actor, or CEO can refuse consent, the rule suppresses commentary, criticism, and competition.
[C1.4] Second, the administrative burden is enormous. Platforms would need to verify identity, negotiate licenses, track estates, representatives, and ambiguous “identifiability.” A voice may evoke a region, genre, or celebrity style without copying one person. Who decides when a clone is “identifiable”? The result will be risk-averse takedowns: platforms will block lawful impersonations rather than litigate consent.
[C1.5] Small developers and independent creators will be priced out, while incumbents build clearance departments. That is anti-innovation in practice.
[C1.6] Third, consent is not the remedy that fits the harm. Fraud, false endorsement, defamation, and misappropriation can be prohibited directly and punished after the fact. A scammer cloning a bank customer’s voice will not seek permission; a consent mandate stops lawful distributors, not bad actors. Better rules are narrower: require disclosure or watermarking for synthetic voices in ads, impose liability for deceptive or confusing uses, and allow damages where someone’s identity is exploited unfairly.
[C1.7] The real choice is between targeted accountability and a prior-consent bottleneck. The proposition chooses the bottleneck, giving identifiable individuals control over markets and messages that should remain open. For that reason, CON.
