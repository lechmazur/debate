[P1.1] Populism in a democracy is not a repair mechanism; it is a fever that reveals and deepens institutional infection. Its central move is anti-pluralism: one authentic people, one corrupt elite, and opponents treated as enemies rather than legitimate citizens. That logic cannot correct elite failure—it redirects discontent into an assault on the constraints that make correction possible.
[P1.2] The pattern is consistent across left and right. Hungary's Fidesz entered as a critique of corruption, then rewrote electoral rules, captured courts, took over much of the media, and entrenched itself. Turkey's Erdoğan used mass grievances to centralize authority, purge judges, and criminalize dissent. Venezuela under Chávez showed the same: popular mobilization converted into the dismantling of horizontal accountability.
[P1.3] In the United States, a populist president pressured election officials and allies tried to overturn a defeated election. These are not separate pathologies; they are the natural endpoint of claiming that "the people" speak through a single leader and that any check is elite obstruction.
[P1.4] A healthy corrective punishes bad decisions, expands accountability, and strengthens independent oversight. Populism does the opposite: it weakens the referee. It attacks courts as enemies, smears journalists, pressures prosecutors, and treats separation of powers as a betrayal of the majority. Even where it does not immediately collapse democracy, it degrades norms of fair contestation and leaves institutions easier to capture.
[P1.5] The trade-off is asymmetric. Elite failure can often be punished at the next election or fixed by reform; once populists weaken vote-counting, judicial review, and the free press, the damage is durable and self-reinforcing. The warning is not simply that people are angry—it is that their anger is being converted into a movement against pluralism itself. That is why populist movements in democracies usually signal institutional decay and backsliding, not healthy correction.

[C1.1] The motion asks what populist movements *usually* are. Answer that with the full population of cases, not a handpicked rogues' gallery. For every Chávez there are dozens of populist movements that never captured a state and never touched a court: Syriza governed Greece and left office through a normal election; Italy's Five Star Movement entered coalitions, disappointed voters, and shrank; Denmark's and the Netherlands' populist parties forced mainstream rivals to address immigration and then bled support; America's original Populists of the 1890s gave us the secret ballot, the direct election of senators, and the antitrust agenda before being absorbed into the mainstream.
[C1.2] The typical populist movement doesn't dismantle democracy. It loudly signals that elites have failed, drags ignored grievances onto the agenda, pulls alienated citizens back to the ballot box, and forces cartel parties to compete again. That is the corrective function working.
[C1.3] Notice what the extreme cases actually prove. Turkey and Venezuela were weakly institutionalized democracies before their strongmen arrived; the decay preceded the populist, it wasn't caused by him. And where institutions were genuinely healthy, the story ends differently: Poland's PiS was voted out in 2023, Bolsonaro lost and power transferred, the American system rejected the attempt to overturn 2020. Populism stress-tested those systems and they held—which is precisely the opposite of "usually a warning sign of decay."
[C1.4] Get the causal direction right. Populism is the fire alarm, not the fire. After the financial crisis, bailouts flowed to banks while no elites were held accountable; voters who turned to outsiders were performing democracy's core function—punishing failure that insiders had agreed not to punish. The alternative to populist anger is not calm deliberation; it is apathy, exit, or the street.
[C1.5] So the burden here is clear: to win, the affirmative must show the modal populist movement ends in capture. It cannot. The modal case ends in agenda change, absorption, or electoral defeat—correction, by any honest measure.
