[P1.1] Noise and parking are real problems with known fixes. Loneliness with nowhere to go is a real problem with only one fix: somewhere to go. That asymmetry decides this debate.
[P1.2] Picture the neighborhood without these venues. The teenager has a bedroom and a phone. The widower has a television. The shift worker finishing at ten has a gas station.
[P1.3] The chess club, the knitting circle, the cheap café, the late-hour room where nobody makes you buy a twenty-dollar cocktail — these turn strangers into neighbors, and they are precisely what zoning codes and permit hearings squeeze out. Isolation is broadly linked to worse health and shorter lives, and occupied streets tend to be safer than empty ones. The benefits flow to people with the fewest alternatives.
[P1.4] The motion says "worry," and that word matters. A worry is a prediction, usually voiced before a venue opens, by residents who attend hearings — typically older, settled homeowners. The people who would use the venue aren't in the room. Giving anticipated annoyance an automatic veto lets the most comfortable residents decide what everyone else may have.
[P1.5] And the harms themselves are manageable. Noise can be regulated directly: decibel limits, soundproofing requirements, closing times for outdoor seating, licenses revocable on repeated complaints. Parking is the weaker worry still — neighborhood venues are small and draw people who walk there. A hobby club in a storefront is not a stadium.
[P1.6] So here is the standard: regulate the nuisance, not the venue. Punish the loud operator; don't ban the quiet one in advance. To defeat this, my opponent must show that ordinary rules cannot control noise and parking even at a board-game café. Otherwise they are defending a blanket prohibition to solve a problem a noise ordinance already solves.
[P1.7] Allow more. Enforce well.
