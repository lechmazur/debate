[P1.1] Public universities are paid for by the public, so their seats and budgets are a social investment, not an open-ended subsidy for every preference. When some programs produce chronic underemployment while hospitals, schools, factories, and infrastructure projects cannot fill essential roles, the mismatch is a failure of allocation. Capping enrollment in low-employment programs and redirecting funding toward national skills shortages is how public universities meet that duty.
[P1.2] A cap is not a ban. It limits how many publicly subsidized places a university may fill in fields where graduates persistently struggle to find work that uses their training. The capacity and money move toward shortage areas: nursing and allied health, engineering, shortage-subject teaching, cybersecurity, and advanced manufacturing. Students who want a weaker-prospect major can still pursue it through smaller cohorts, private providers, or self-funded study.
[P1.3] What ends is unlimited public intake where the employment return is poor.
[P1.4] The payoff is concrete. Graduates in high-demand fields are more likely to find relevant work, service their loans, and pay taxes. Employers get people they cannot currently hire. Taxpayers get a clearer return on money already spent.
[P1.5] A capped program can remain excellent; it simply stops expanding while shortages elsewhere go unmet. That is responsible stewardship, not crude vocationalism.
[P1.6] The objection that this sacrifices choice or undervalues culture does not hold. Choice remains. What changes is the public priority. Literature, philosophy, and the arts can be honored without pretending that every additional seat in a low-employment major is as urgent as a missing nurse or engineer.
[P1.7] When skills gaps slow care, delay construction, and weaken competitiveness, directing public university places toward those needs is the fairer use of limited funds. The test is not whether every program has some value. It is whether public universities should keep expanding the ones with weak employment outcomes while national shortages persist. They should not.

[C1.1] A shortage of nurses does not make an additional literature student its cause. PRO identifies a real problem—skills mismatches—but then assumes that capping some programs will smoothly transfer money, students, and graduates into shortage occupations. Every step of that mechanism is unreliable.
[C1.2] First, “low-employment” is a dangerously blunt label. Employment statistics vary with graduation year, local labor markets, students’ backgrounds, postgraduate study, and whether a job is counted as related to a major. A philosophy graduate entering policy, law, or management may be employing exactly the analytical skills a university taught, yet appear as a mismatch in a crude classification. If weak outcomes reflect disadvantage or a regional recession, caps punish access rather than improve education.
[C1.3] And because degrees take years, funding decisions based on today’s national shortage can produce tomorrow’s oversupply while stripping out capacity that becomes valuable later.
[C1.4] Second, cutting seats does not create shortage-field graduates. Nursing expansion requires clinical placements, laboratories, and qualified instructors; merely moving an enrollment allocation cannot conjure them up. More fundamentally, a student denied a publicly supported history place does not automatically become a nurse. That student may choose another institution, accumulate more private debt, or leave higher education altogether.
[C1.5] Meanwhile, shortages often persist because of pay, working conditions, location, and attrition. Training more entrants without addressing retention can simply feed a leaking pipeline.
[C1.6] Third, PRO’s assurance that “choice remains” conceals a sharp inequality. Wealthier students can preserve their choices through private providers or self-funded study; poorer students bear the cap. Public universities would therefore ration opportunity according to both family resources and a government forecast, while treating taxable employment as the principal public return. Research, teaching, civic judgment, cultural production, and adaptable general skills are also public benefits—and they do not fit neatly into a national vacancy list.
[C1.7] Targeted investment in genuine bottleneck fields may be worthwhile, but it does not require this motion’s punitive caps. PRO must show that suppression reliably solves shortages, rather than merely reshuffling inequality and capacity. It has not.
