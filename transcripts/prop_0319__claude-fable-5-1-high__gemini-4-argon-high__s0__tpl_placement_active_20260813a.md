[P1.1] A 200-capacity room above a pub is where almost every act you have ever paid to see learned its trade. Nobody headlines a stadium without first playing to forty people on a Tuesday. That pipeline is not a market accident; it is infrastructure in the plain sense: the base layer everything above it depends on. The motion asks only that cities recognise this when their own decisions put it at risk.
[P1.2] The pattern of closures is depressingly uniform. A venue operates for twenty years. A developer builds flats next door. New residents complain about noise that predates them.
[P1.3] Licensing attaches conditions the venue cannot afford; police treat it as a nuisance address; the room becomes a Tesco Express. London lost something like a third of its grassroots venues in a decade through exactly this mechanism, which is why Britain eventually adopted an "agent of change" rule: whoever arrives second bears the cost of coexistence. That rule is the motion in practice, and it has made the sky fall nowhere.
[P1.4] Treating venues as cultural infrastructure means three concrete things. First, assess before you act: a zoning or licensing decision that would likely close a venue should be weighed like closing a branch library, with the loss counted rather than ignored. Second, agent of change. Third, proportionality in policing, so that a single complaint does not become an existential threat.
[P1.5] Notice what this does not mean. Not immunity from fire codes. Not blank cheques. Cities already extend this courtesy to concert halls and museums; the question is why the opera house gets careful planning treatment while the room that produced the next generation of artists gets none.
[P1.6] The decisive asymmetry is irreversibility. Residents can insulate windows; a venue converted to apartments never returns. When one side of a decision is cheap and reversible and the other is permanent, the burden belongs on the reversible side. That is not special pleading.
[P1.7] It is how competent cities plan.
