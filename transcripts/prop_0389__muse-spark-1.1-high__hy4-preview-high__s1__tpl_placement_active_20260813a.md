[C1.1] The motion asks us to treat a cancelled online service as a public library that the owner must keep open. That framing is emotionally powerful and legally backwards.
[C1.2] Start with ownership. Players did not buy a world; they bought a license to access a hosted service. When the operator closes it, the service ends. The claim in [P1.4] that players had a “reasonable expectation of continued use, not a rental that can be evaporated at will” reads the receipt as a deed.
[C1.3] You can keep a movie disc because you own a copy; you cannot keep broadcasting a film or running a studio lot after the license ends. An MMO is closer to a ticketed theme park than a DVD. When the park closes, you do not get the rides.
[C1.4] Then the costs. [P1.5] insists “There is no legitimate competing interest after shutdown.” That is wrong on four fronts. Contract: games are bundles of licensed engine code, music, middleware, and brand rights; those permissions often expire, so a final patch or server binary can breach third-party deals. Security and privacy: fan servers become malware and phishing vectors and frequently replay old account data, dragging the publisher into privacy exposure.
[C1.5] Reputation: unmoderated private servers can host harassment, cheating, or extremist content under the original brand. Control and legacy: blessing reverse-engineered backends normalizes circumvention, weakens the publisher’s IP, and complicates remasters, reboots, or sequels that depend on clean title and coherent canon. None of this is “almost nothing.”
[C1.6] The fan-rescue stories in [P1.8] concede the point. City of Heroes and Star Wars Galaxies servers survive in gray zones precisely because publishers withheld permission; if the practice were clearly harmless, no cease-and-desist would be needed. Demand proves appetite, not entitlement.
[C1.7] Preservation means licensed archives, museum access, and fan-made wikis, footage, and permitted mods—not commandeering someone else’s copyrighted service. Publishers may choose generosity. The motion would make it an obligation. Reject it.
