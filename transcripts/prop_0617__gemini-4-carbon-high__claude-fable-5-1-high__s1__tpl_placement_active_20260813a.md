[P1.1] Under current merger review, antitrust enforcers must prove a counterfactual future before stopping a dominant digital platform from buying a potential rival. That burden was built for steel mills and supermarkets, where market shares move slowly and harm appears immediately in higher prices. Digital platforms operate by different rules: powerful network effects, data feedback loops, and rapid tipping points. Because gatekeepers enjoy entrenched moats, true competitive threats rarely come from mirror-image rivals; they come from fast-growing startups in adjacent spaces, just as Instagram and WhatsApp once threatened Facebook's social graph.
[P1.2] Yet at the moment of acquisition, a nascent rival always looks too small or uncertain for regulators to prove it would have matured into a full competitor. When uncertainty defaults in favor of the deal, incumbents systematically buy out future competition while enforcers remain paralyzed.
[P1.3] Reversing that presumption corrects a stark asymmetry in the cost of mistakes. When authorities wrongly approve an entrenching acquisition, the damage is permanent: network effects lock users in, an independent challenger vanishes, and courts almost never unscramble merged platforms years later. Conversely, blocking a dominant giant from buying a startup does not kill innovation. Dominant platforms already command the capital and engineering talent to build new features internally, giving consumers two competing products instead of one consolidated gatekeeper.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, startups retain healthy exits through public markets, independent growth, or sales to non-dominant buyers, steering venture funding toward genuine disruption rather than building buyout bait for monopolies.
[P1.5] Crucially, this rule is a rebuttable presumption, not a ban. Dominant platforms hold overwhelming informational advantages over regulators regarding user trajectories, technical synergies, and product roadmaps. Requiring them to prove substantial consumer benefit places the burden of proof on the party possessing the evidence. Deals that deliver breakthroughs neither firm could achieve alone will still proceed; defensive buyouts that merely entrench digital monopolies will finally stop.
