The deployment side loses decisively because delay compounds while capacity lags.
[P1.2] Consider solar. American installers rely on imported cells and modules. Tariffs raise project costs substantially, pushing developers to delay or cancel installations. Meanwhile, the domestic factories supposedly being nurtured require years to permit, build, and ramp.
[P1.3] During that window, we are not deploying clean energy at the pace climate goals demand; we are paying more for hardware while waiting for capacity that may never achieve cost parity with global competitors.
[P1.4] The same logic applies to electric vehicles. Batteries, semiconductors, and specialized components flow through integrated global supply chains. Tariffs on these inputs increase sticker prices for consumers, slowing the adoption curves that justify building domestic battery plants. Protectionism creates a captive but shrinking market: domestic manufacturers produce expensive goods for a deployment market that tariffs have throttled.
[P1.5] Semiconductors reveal the structural flaw. Chip fabrication requires imported equipment, chemicals, and wafers. Tariffs on these raise costs for the very domestic fabs they purport to protect, delaying the clean-tech electronics that wind turbines, EVs, and grids all require.
[P1.6] The opponent will claim tariffs create manufacturing jobs. But capacity without deployment is a monument, not an industry. We face a choice between marginally faster factory construction in 2028 and significantly slower clean-energy deployment from 2024 through 2027. [P1.7] The deployment slowdown outweighs the capacity benefit because time is the scarcest resource in the energy transition.
