[P1.1] When a stock exchange sells the office next door to its own matching engine, it is not selling real estate. It is selling the right to see the market before everyone else. That is the heart of this debate: exchanges are not ordinary businesses, they are quasi-public utilities entrusted with running fair markets — and a fair market cannot auction off the front of the line to whoever pays most.
[P1.2] Three arguments anchor the case.
[P1.3] First, the conflict of interest is structural. Exchanges earn enormous revenue from co-location and proprietary data feeds sold to high-frequency firms. This gives the referee a direct financial stake in preserving a two-tiered market: one speed for paying insiders, another for everyone else. Any institution charged with neutrality should be barred from monetizing privileged physical access to its own core infrastructure.
[P1.4] Second, co-location fuels a socially wasteful arms race. Microseconds confer no benefit to capital formation, price discovery over human-relevant timescales, or ordinary investors. They confer only the ability to pick off stale quotes — latency arbitrage, which economists studying market microstructure have estimated extracts billions annually from pension funds, mutual funds, and retail order flow. That money is a tax paid by savers to fund a zero-sum sprint, and the exchange collects rent for hosting the racetrack.
[P1.5] Third, the harm shows up as fragile, phantom liquidity. Co-located firms can flood the book with quotes and yank them in microseconds when conditions shift — exactly when liquidity is needed most, as flash-crash episodes have illustrated. Markets look deep until the moment they aren't.
[P1.6] Note what prohibition does not do: it does not ban fast trading or technology investment. It simply stops exchanges from selling positional advantage inside their own walls. Speed earned through better algorithms is competition; speed bought through proximity to the referee is privilege.
[P1.7] A market's legitimacy rests on the belief that prices reflect information, not floor plans. Prohibition restores that.
