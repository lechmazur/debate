[P1.1] The function of disclosure rules has never been about the medium—it's about the moment a message stops being honest opinion and becomes paid persuasion. A television host who reads a sponsor's script without saying "advertisement" deceives viewers. An influencer who shows a product in a "morning routine" video without disclosing the brand paid for that placement deceives followers in exactly the same way. The platform changes; the deception, and the harm it does to consumer trust, does not.
[P1.2] That identity of harm is why the identity of rules should follow.
[P1.3] Consider the mechanism of influence itself. Traditional media personalities are trusted because audiences believe they're reporting or reviewing in good faith. Influencers are trusted for the same reason—often more so, because their content is styled as personal, intimate, peer-to-peer advice rather than obvious commercial broadcasting. A teenager watching a skincare "routine" video is less guarded than someone watching a 30-second TV commercial precisely because it doesn't look like an ad.
[P1.4] That lower guard is exactly why disclosure matters more here, not less. If anything, the case for parity is a floor, not a ceiling.
[P1.5] Scale is the second pillar. We don't apply strict disclosure rules to a friend's casual text recommending a restaurant, because the reach and financial incentive are negligible. But once an account has hundreds of thousands or millions of followers, it is functionally a media outlet—it shapes purchasing behavior at a mass scale, sells ad inventory, and often works through the same marketing agencies that place traditional television spots. At that scale, the "just a person sharing their life" framing is a legal fiction CON will lean on, and I'll dismantle it directly: once you're monetizing attention at scale, you've become media, and media rules should apply.
[P1.6] Finally, think about regulatory coherence. If disclosure strictness depends on medium rather than function, advertisers simply migrate their spend to the less-regulated channel to evade accountability—which is already happening. Consistent rules close that loophole and protect consumers regardless of where persuasion happens to live.

[C1.1] Reach is not a role. A television anchor, radio presenter, or magazine host speaks inside an institution: a broadcaster or publisher that licenses the channel, sells distinct advertising slots, vets scripts, and can be fined as an organization. A large influencer, even with millions of followers, remains an independent person publishing from a personal account, without that editorial machine, legal department, or clear separation between programme and commercial break. Rules built for the first cannot simply be copied onto the second without regulating a way of life, not just an advertisement.
[C1.2] That difference also breaks the claim of identical deception. Audiences grant a newsreader or talk-show host trust because the format promises editorial independence, with adverts visibly fenced off. Followers approach a creator expecting monetized taste: hauls, discount codes, brand trips and affiliate links are the familiar grammar of the feed, and repeated viewing teaches people to ask who benefits. Hidden payment should be disclosed, but it does not betray the same institutional promise, so it does not justify the same formal timing, wording and separation requirements designed for broadcast spots.
[C1.3] In practice, strict parity would be both vague and chilling. Traditional advertising has a sponsor, a script and a paid slot. Creator content is messier: a creator's own product, a free sample with no obligation, a product bought then later linked for commission, a long-term ambassadorship mentioned in passing. Forcing every ambiguous mention into broadcaster-grade labelling either compels over-disclosure that trains audiences to ignore labels, or punishes honest reviewers for guessing wrong.
[C1.4] The winners would be the largest agencies and platforms that can absorb compliance lawyers; independent creators would self-censor or move promotion into less visible forms.
[C1.5] Consumers deserve a clear, conspicuous warning when money changes hands for promotion. They do not need, and fairness does not require, treating a bedroom livestream as if it were a licensed television network.
