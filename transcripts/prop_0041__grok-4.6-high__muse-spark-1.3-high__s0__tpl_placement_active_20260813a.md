[P1.1] Personalized algorithmic pricing based on a customer's willingness or ability to pay is not clever retail. It is extraction. Two people buy the same item at the same moment from the same seller. One pays more because software decided they look richer, more desperate, less likely to compare, or less able to leave.
[P1.2] Cost did not change. The person did. That is not matching supply to demand. It is turning a private dossier into a markup.
[P1.3] Markets work when prices communicate scarcity. This practice severs that signal. The listed price becomes a starting bid against device, browsing history, neighborhood, past purchases, and inferred urgency. People with less time, less digital fluency, or less power to mask themselves pay a hidden tax.
[P1.4] Ability-to-pay targeting is especially corrosive: it charges more because you appear able to absorb it, or because you appear unable to shop around. Either way, identity and vulnerability become the surcharge. The shopper least equipped to fight the model is the one most likely to be billed for it.
[P1.5] Disclosure cannot fix an inscrutable model. No shopper can audit, live, why their quote is higher. A consent checkbox is not informed choice. The firm holds the data, the model, and the quote.
[P1.6] The customer holds a take-it-or-leave-it number they cannot test against anyone else's. Competition will not dissolve the practice; it will spread it. Once rivals can profile, refusing to do so leaves money on the table. Equal treatment becomes optional.
[P1.7] Privacy becomes a luxury that decides the price.
[P1.8] Ban the personalization. Sell the good, not a tax on the person. Retailers can still vary prices by time, inventory, location, or publicly posted segments. They cannot tailor the sticker to a secret estimate of what this particular customer will bear.
[P1.9] That restores a basic rule of commerce: the same offer, to anyone who walks through the door.
