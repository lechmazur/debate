[C1.3] Democratic accountability over what public institutions teach children is a feature, not a flaw—and conflating legitimate oversight with a "chilling effect" obscures a genuine distinction.
[C1.4] Second, the self-censorship argument depends on cumulative, unmeasured harms. PRO described gaping holes and systemic silence, but these are asserted, not demonstrated. [C1.5] The chilling effect is real in some cases, but the motion's claim that it is *more* damaging than controversial content requires evidence of scale that PRO simply does not supply.
[C1.6] Third, and most fundamentally, the comparison is asymmetric in a way that favors CON. Self-censorship produces an absence—students miss some content. Genuinely harmful controversial content produces a presence: misinformation delivered with teacher authority, age-inappropriate material, or ideological capture that is far harder for students to interrogate than a gap they don't know exists. Absence can be supplemented.
[C1.7] Confident misinformation is insidious precisely because students don't recognize what they should question.
[C1.8] The real threat to education quality is poor teaching in any form—including teaching contested material badly. Political pressure is one imperfect corrective. The motion asks us to condemn the corrective as worse than the disease it targets, and that conclusion hasn't been earned.
