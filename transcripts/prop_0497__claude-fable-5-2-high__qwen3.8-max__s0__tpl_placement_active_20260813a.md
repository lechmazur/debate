[P1.1] Most silent-era films are gone. Nobody decided to destroy them; they vanished because nobody was obliged to keep them, and the studios saw no profit in storing old reels. We are repeating that mistake at scale with the defining creative medium of our own time.
[P1.2] Legal deposit exists for one reason: markets preserve what sells today, not what matters tomorrow. That logic applies to software with more force than it ever did to books. A book on a shelf survives neglect, but software does not. It dies when a server is switched off, when a storefront delists it, when an operating system update breaks it, or when a licence check has nothing left to phone.
[P1.3] Early mobile games that millions played are already unrecoverable, and so is much of the interactive web art of the Flash years. With software, the default outcome is loss.
[P1.4] So my case rests on three claims.
[P1.5] First, parity. If a forgettable paperback merits a deposit copy, then the games, apps, and digital artworks that shape how a generation plays, communicates, and creates do too. No principled line puts culture on paper inside the national memory and culture in code outside it.
[P1.6] Second, only a mandate works. Voluntary preservation depends on hobbyists and under-resourced archivists working in legal grey zones, often by circumventing copy protection because no publisher handed them a clean copy. Companies go bankrupt, merge, and lose their own source material. A deposit duty captures the work at release, the one moment when a working, unlocked copy and its documentation are certain to exist.
[P1.7] Third, it is practical. Deposit is not publication. Archives already hold unreleased and commercially sensitive material under restricted access, and they can hold software the same way, in secure storage with on-site research access and embargoes. [P1.8] Some countries have already brought games within their deposit regimes, and their industries did not collapse.

[C1.1] The proposition wants to solve real loss with the wrong tool. Legal deposit works for books and films because they are fixed, self-contained objects whose cultural value can be captured in a copy. Software is not that kind of thing. A game, app, or digital artwork is a set of behaviors that depends on operating systems, hardware, drivers, cloud services, licenses, and constantly changing updates.
[C1.2] Deposit a build and you may preserve an icon, not the experience. Deposit a live-service game or social app and what exactly is archived: the client, the servers, the community data, the matchmaking rules? If the mandate stops at a clean copy, it preserves shells. If it expands to environments, updates, and server-side functions, it becomes a limitless regulatory machine.
[C1.3] The phrase “culturally significant” makes this worse, not better. Significance is judged retrospectively. At launch, nobody can reliably say which mod, mobile puzzle game, or meme app will matter. A bureaucracy that picks culturally significant software will either be so broad that every app store submission triggers deposit, or so selective that it preserves what officials notice while losing the rest.
[C1.4] Developers face uncertainty, and archives inherit an impossible triage task.
[C1.5] The costs are not trivial. Small studios and independent artists would face compliance burdens, versioning duties, and potential exposure of trade secrets or licensed middleware. Centralizing executable code, including security-sensitive software, creates targets and obligations archives are not built to manage. PRO’s analogy to a paperback ignores privacy data, network code, encryption, and third-party dependencies.
[C1.6] Preservation matters, but a mandate is not the only or best route. Legal safe harbors for libraries, emulation research, voluntary deposits, platform archiving requirements, and public funding for selective acquisition can protect software without forcing every culturally significant work into a state archive. The decisive question is not whether software deserves preservation, but whether this blunt mandate is workable. It is not.
