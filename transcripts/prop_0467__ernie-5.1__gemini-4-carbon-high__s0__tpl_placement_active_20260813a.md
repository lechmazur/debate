[P1.1] Cyber‑operations that cripple power grids, hospitals, water treatment or transportation are not abstract data breaches; they produce the same physical suffering and societal disruption as a bomb dropped on a city. When a state‑sponsored hack shuts down a national electricity network, hospitals lose life‑support, food spoils, and the population endures a humanitarian crisis identical to that caused by a conventional armed attack. International law already recognises the principle of distinction and proportionality for kinetic weapons; it must extend that framework to cyber means, because the stakes for civilian life are indistinguishable.
[P1.2] The current legal vacuum creates a perverse incentive. Without a clear rule that treats such cyber‑strikes as armed attacks, aggressors can calibrate their operations to stay below any threshold, knowing the victim state lacks a legitimate, proportional military response. This emboldens actors who can launch destructive campaigns with plausible deniability, as seen in the 2015‑16 Ukrainian power‑grid attacks that left hundreds of thousands without heat in winter. Treating these acts as armed attacks closes that loophole and restores deterrence: a state contemplating a crippling cyber strike must now weigh a credible, proportional military reply, just as it would for a missile strike.
[P1.3] Opponents will argue that attribution is too uncertain and that equating cyber with kinetic attack risks uncontrolled escalation. Both concerns are addressed by the “proportional” qualifier. Attribution, while technically challenging, is already a prerequisite for self‑defence under the UN Charter; states routinely act on intelligence that is sufficiently reliable. Proportionality ensures any military reply is calibrated to the scale of the harm, preventing runaway war.
[P1.4] Thus the decisive choice is whether the international system will protect civilian critical infrastructure with a rule that matches its real‑world impact, or continue to allow low‑risk, high‑damage cyber aggression to slip through the legal cracks. The answer must be to treat such cyberattacks as armed attacks, with the right of proportional self‑defence firmly enshrined.
