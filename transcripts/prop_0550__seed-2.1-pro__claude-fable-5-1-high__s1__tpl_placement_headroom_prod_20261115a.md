[P1.1] Paid youth apprenticeships and employer-linked career pathways outperform expanded four-year college subsidies as a driver of long-run earnings because they fix the two structural flaws that deflate college returns for most marginal young people: crippling skill mismatch and backwards risk alignment, while building wealth earlier instead of delaying it. Broad college enrollment subsidies overwhelmingly reach the marginal student—the person not already set to enroll in a selective, high-return four-year program. For that group, expanded subsidies too often funnel students into underfunded institutions, low-return majors, and programs with near-50% dropout rates, where non-completers leave with debt, no credential, and none of the wage premium college is supposed to deliver.
[P1.2] Even many completers end up in roles that do not require a four-year degree, wasting four years of foregone wages on a credential that delivers no measurable earnings lift a decade out. Paid apprentices, by contrast, earn a wage from day one, accumulating zero student debt while building on-the-job tenure, professional references, and exactly the skills local employers have already signaled they will pay a premium to fill. These pathways anchor training to real, open roles in high-wage sectors from skilled trades and health care support to IT and advanced manufacturing, where mid-career earnings routinely match or exceed the median four-year graduate’s pay, with far less downside risk.
[P1.3] Long-run earnings compound: early work experience, zero debt drag, and direct ties to stable employers let apprentices start building home equity, retirement savings, and promotion tracks years earlier than their college-bound peers. Unlike college subsidies, which leave students holding the bag if their program fails to deliver, apprenticeships align incentives so employers only invest in training that leads directly to durable, well-compensated work, producing returns that are not just higher on average, but far more reliable for young people without pre-existing professional advantages.

[C1.1] The motion asks about long-run earnings, so the decisive question is not who earns more at twenty-two but who earns more at forty-five. On that question, the evidence cuts hard against apprenticeship-first policy. A four-year degree still carries a lifetime earnings premium commonly estimated near a million dollars over a high school diploma, and that premium has widened for decades even as enrollment expanded. Typical student debt, roughly the price of a modest car, is a rounding error against that gap.
[C1.2] "Zero debt drag" is a marketing line, not a comparison.
[C1.3] PRO's "marginal student" argument runs backward. Quasi-experimental studies of students who barely clear admission cutoffs, or who gain access through tuition subsidies, consistently find large earnings gains for precisely those marginal enrollees, not losses. Subsidies also attack the dropout problem PRO deplores: students leave largely because they cannot afford to stay, and cheaper tuition raises completion. The remedy for incomplete degrees is more support, not steering the student into a welding bay instead.
[C1.4] Apprenticeships front-load earnings and then plateau. Germany, the model everyone cites, shows apprentices leading university graduates in their twenties and falling well behind by their thirties and forties, because occupation-specific skills depreciate as industries shift while general skills transfer. The electrician who apprenticed in 2005 earns well today; the pathway built around a single employer's current needs is a bet that those needs survive automation, offshoring, and the next recession. Employer-linked slots also collapse exactly when young people need them most, since firms cut training first in downturns, and they sort fifteen-year-olds into tracks before anyone knows their ceiling.
[C1.5] College subsidies scale through institutions that already exist and produce portable credentials. Apprenticeships depend on employer appetite that has never scaled in America. A policy that pays better at twenty and worse for the next forty years does not raise long-run earnings more.
