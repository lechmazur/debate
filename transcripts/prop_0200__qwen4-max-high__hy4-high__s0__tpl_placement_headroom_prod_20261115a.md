[P1.1] Nobody is proposing to own the Moon. The question is whether ice scooped from a polar crater, without claiming one square meter of ground, can be sold. Article II of the Outer Space Treaty bars national appropriation of celestial bodies by sovereignty, use, occupation, or any other means. It does not address material severed from the surface.
[P1.2] Article I protects free use. Apollo, Luna, and Chang'e all brought lunar material home and treated it as theirs; nobody called it contraband. On the high seas, no state owns the water, yet the fish in the net belong to the crew that caught them.
[P1.3] This is recognition, not invention. Several countries — the United States, Luxembourg, Japan, the UAE — have already legislated that extracted space resources may be owned while disclaiming sovereignty. The proposition takes that existing practice and attaches the two obligations that make it safe: publish what you take and where, and control the debris you generate.
[P1.4] Those conditions bite precisely because activity is legal. Article VI already makes states responsible for national operators and requires authorization and continuing supervision. A licensed business has something to lose: its permit, its insurance, its access to courts. Transparency and debris rules become enforceable conditions on a permit.
[P1.5] Keep extraction legally grey and you have no permit to condition — only states with rockets and no reason to explain themselves.
[P1.6] Waiting for a full treaty is not neutral. The Moon Agreement attracted a handful of ratifications and no major launch power; consensus bodies do not converge on demand. "Later" means indefinitely, and the interim goes to whoever moves first in silence. Nothing here is irreversible: a future treaty can supersede these rules.
[P1.7] Illegality-by-default protects no one.
[P1.8] CON must name a prohibition, not a missing treaty. Silence is not a ban.

[C1.1] The question is not whether lunar material may ever be touched. It is whether, before a full global treaty exists, a firm may turn a finite shared asset into saleable private property. Free use is not a power to create private property. The proposition starts by collapsing that distinction, then calls the result "recognition."
[C1.2] The fish-in-the-net analogy [P1.2] hides the problem. Fish are mobile and replenishable; polar ice sits in cold traps and is finite on human timescales. A company can publish its coordinates, contain its dust, and still remove the very stock that made later use possible. Its equipment and claimed safety perimeter then effectively reserve the site.
[C1.3] Transparency discloses the taking; debris control polices litter. Neither grants authority, caps removal, or protects non-users. No agreed body can verify the conditions, hear objections, or enforce limits against non-licensees.
[C1.4] Sample return and a few national statutes [P1.3] do not cure that. Scientific samples were not a market in planetary assets, and domestic statutes are claims by states whose operators stand to gain; they cannot define the rights of absent states. Article VI supervision [P1.4] binds a licensee, not rival states or purchasers; it cannot settle overlapping permits or make contested title clean. A purchaser needs an internationally defensible right, not a clean logbook and a permissive permit.
[C1.5] A conditional licence is not a global rule.
[C1.6] Nor is waiting inert: it preserves the ice and the bargaining equality of latecomers. Treaties cannot refreeze ice or erase investments made under early titles; later rules will grandfather first takers or trigger expropriation disputes. That interim changes the baseline by launch capability. Silence may not be a ban [P1.8], but it is not a deed; sale needs more than an absence of prohibition.
