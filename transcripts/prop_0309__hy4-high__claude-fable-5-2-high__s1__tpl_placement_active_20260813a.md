[P1.1] Rural hospital closure is not a normal market exit. It removes what turns distance into survival or death. A regional center may have more specialists and lower unit costs, but for a suspected heart attack, a complicated delivery, a child in respiratory distress, or an older patient after a fall, the first question is not which center has the best operating room; it is whether anyone can stabilize the patient before the clock runs out. Another hour on winding winter roads with thin ambulance coverage is not inconvenience.
[P1.2] It is deterioration.
[P1.3] That is why the consolidation ledger is false economy. It counts revenue per bed, staffing ratios, payer mix, and average utilization. It does not count the diabetic patient who skips follow-up because care is two counties away, the employer that cannot recruit workers without nearby emergency care, the family that leaves town, or the public cost of intensive care after preventable delays. Those costs are scattered across households, employers, ambulance services, and public programs.
[P1.4] Governments can count those spillovers and subsidize accordingly; the market calls closure efficient only because the hospital never has to pay them.
[P1.5] Subsidy is also about equal citizenship. Rural residents are often older, poorer, and less able to absorb a long trip. Telling them to drive farther because volume is low turns geography into a rationing device: the patient with a car, flexible work, and money for lodging reaches the center; the homebound widow, farmworker, uninsured parent, or dialysis patient does not. A system built around the first patient and dismissing the second is selective, not efficient.
[P1.6] The subsidy should buy a guaranteed access floor, not freeze every building: local emergency stabilization, primary and chronic care, basic obstetric coverage where distance demands it, telemedicine links, and reliable transfers. Advanced specialties can consolidate. What cannot be left to the market is the proximity-dependent capacity to keep a patient alive, stable, and treated close enough to matter.
