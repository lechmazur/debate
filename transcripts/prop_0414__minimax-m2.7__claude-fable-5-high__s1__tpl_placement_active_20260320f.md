When Barcelona, Amsterdam, or Venice collects a tax from every hotel room and short-term rental, that money exists for one reason — to offset what tourists do to the place. [C1.1] The proposition rests on an accounting illusion. Money is fungible. Earmark a tourist tax for housing, and the city simply withdraws an equal amount of general funds from housing and spends it elsewhere. Net resident benefit: zero.
[C1.2] This is the documented fate of earmarks everywhere — American state lotteries "for education" famously changed almost nothing about total education spending, because legislatures backfilled. Earmarking buys residents a press release, not an apartment. PRO's entire case depends on the assumption that a labeled dollar is an additional dollar, and that assumption is false.
[C1.3] What earmarking does change is fiscal architecture — for the worse. Tourist revenue is among the most volatile income a city has. When the pandemic hit, Venice and Amsterdam lost it almost overnight. A city that has wired its housing programs, transit operations, and sanitation crews to that revenue stream watches essential resident services collapse precisely when residents need them most.
[C1.4] Core services demand stable general-fund financing; you do not build the foundation of resident welfare on the most cyclical money in the treasury.
[C1.5] Earmarking also creates the perverse incentive PRO claims to solve — only worse. If the housing budget grows with every hotel night sold, the housing department becomes a lobby for more tourism. In overcrowded cities, that is precisely backwards: the policy goal is fewer visitors, better managed. Tying resident services to visitor volume makes overtourism structurally load-bearing.
[C1.6] Finally, "revenue should follow the harm" actually indicts the motion. Tourist pressure falls on policing, emergency services, heritage upkeep, crowd management, and enforcement against illegal rentals — none of which the earmark covers. A council should be free to spend this year's tourist revenue where this year's pressure actually lands. That is what general budgets, scrutinized by elected officials, exist to do.
[C1.7] Residents deserve real housing, transit, and sanitation funding — appropriated deliberately, at the scale the need demands, from stable revenue. Earmarking a volatile tax delivers a sticker, not a service.
