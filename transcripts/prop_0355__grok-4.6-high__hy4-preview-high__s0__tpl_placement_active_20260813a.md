[P1.1] Overnight is when electric cars actually charge. For apartment renters, that means the stall downstairs, not a suburban driveway. When landlords can stall or refuse as a matter of convenience, the legal default quietly rations electrification to people who own houses. That imbalance is what this proposition corrects.
[P1.2] A renter already pays for a dwelling and, in most cases, a parking space. Charging access is how that space functions as transportation infrastructure in a world leaving gasoline. Landlords keep the building. They should not keep an almost costless veto over whether a tenant can use a legal parking space for its obvious next purpose.
[P1.3] The current delay-and-block right is too cheap relative to the harm it inflicts. A landlord can cite aesthetics, vague liability, or “we’ll look into it” for months. The tenant eats gasoline costs, loses value on a car already bought, or hunts public chargers that are slower, pricier, and often full. Moving is not a real remedy in tight rental markets.
[P1.4] Law that treats landlord inertia as equal to tenant need is not neutral; it subsidizes the status quo.
[P1.5] A stronger request right does not mean unlimited, landlord-funded buildouts. It means a presumption that a reasonable, tenant-paid or shared installation proceeds unless the landlord shows a genuine safety, capacity, or structural barrier that cannot reasonably be mitigated. That is the same family of rule already used for living necessities that once looked optional: disability modifications and communications equipment.
[P1.6] Renters are disproportionately urban and lower-wealth. Blocking charging where they live locks those households into oil and concentrates tailpipe pollution in the densest neighborhoods. Public chargers cannot substitute for home charging at scale; they complement it. The clash is simple: default to a tenant’s ability to charge a legal vehicle at a legal space, or to a landlord’s preference not to deal with it.
[P1.7] Only the first treats apartments as housing for this century rather than a permission structure for delay.

[C1.1] The clash is not driveway versus apartment or gasoline versus electric; it is who may alter shared property and who bears the risk. A parking space is for parking. Turning it into a 240-volt station is a capital upgrade to common electrical systems: new capacity, conduit, metering, permits, insurance, and upkeep. The landlord’s right to delay or refuse is not an "almost costless veto" [P1.2].
[C1.2] It is ordinary owner consent, stopping one tenant from converting a shared asset into a private benefit.
[C1.3] Picture a 1960s 24-unit block with a nearly full panel and one garage. A tenant wants a charger in a deeded stall. The request may require a load study, panel or transformer upgrade, trenching, a submeter, and an insurance review—often five figures. If that tenant moves in a year, the benefit leaves while the infrastructure and maintenance remain.
[C1.4] Non-EV renters, often lower-income, absorb higher common charges. [P1.5]’s presumption would force the landlord to disprove every barrier, turning due diligence into litigation and cross-subsidization.
[C1.5] The disability analogy fails. Those rules protect civil-rights access to housing, compelling accommodation because the alternative is exclusion. An EV charger is a discretionary consumer choice, not a protected necessity. No one has a right to rewire a building they do not own for a car they chose to buy.
[C1.6] Current law already forbids bad faith and requires reasonable action. The proposition flips the default: every request becomes presumptively valid, and the owner must prove a bar is unmitigable. Compliance and insurance costs rise, and in tight markets they fall on renters. A rule sold as helping lower-wealth households would tax the majority without EVs.
[C1.7] Electrification is desirable, but it should arrive through investment, leases, and tenant-paid options—not by overriding multi-tenant governance. The landlord’s right to delay or block is not the enemy of charging; it keeps one tenant’s convenience from becoming everyone else’s bill.
