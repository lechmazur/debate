[P1.1] When drought hit East Africa in past crisis years, a familiar script played out: Tanzania banned maize exports, prices in Kenya spiked, traders smuggled grain anyway at inflated cost, and Tanzanian farmers—denied their best market—lost income and planted less the following season. Everyone was worse off. That is the predictable arithmetic of export bans, and it is exactly the problem enforceable continental rules exist to solve.
[P1.2] The case rests on three pillars. First, export bans are a textbook collective action failure. Each government bans exports to look decisive at home; collectively, the bans shrink the regional grain pool precisely when scarcity is worst, driving prices higher everywhere. The 2007–08 global food crisis showed this vividly: cascading rice export restrictions amplified a modest supply shortfall into a price explosion.
[P1.3] No single country can fix this alone—only a binding mutual commitment can, because each state restrains itself only if it trusts its neighbors will too. That trust requires enforcement, not communiqués.
[P1.4] Second, bans destroy the long-run foundation of African food security. A trader will not build storage, logistics, or cross-border supply contracts if any government can slam the border shut overnight. Predictable rules unlock investment in regional value chains, which is the AfCFTA's entire purpose. Unrestricted ban powers quietly hollow out the agreement's core promise.
[P1.5] Third, the proposal is rules limiting bans, not abolishing sovereignty. A sensible regime requires notification, time limits, objective trigger criteria, and review—mirroring safeguard disciplines that already exist in trade law. Governments retain emergency tools; they simply lose the power to beggar their neighbors unilaterally and indefinitely.
[P1.6] The alternative—discretion—has been tested for decades. It delivers panic, smuggling, suppressed farm prices, and importing neighbors paying famine premiums. Africa imports tens of billions of dollars of food annually while sitting on vast agricultural potential. Binding disciplines on crisis-era export bans are how a free trade area proves it means something when it matters most.
