[P1.1] The current transparency regime for AI content has a dangerous blind spot: it only labels static, broadly distributed AI-generated material, while ignoring the far more manipulative practice of AI-optimized persuasion campaigns tailored to individual users or micro-groups. This gap leaves the public defenseless against covert, algorithmically calibrated manipulation that exploits personal data to bypass critical judgment.
[P1.2] Existing rules, like the EU AI Act’s limited labeling requirements for deepfakes, fail to address dynamic AI systems that generate thousands of unique, personalized persuasive messages for different users, each tweaked to exploit that group’s specific biases, fears, or lived experiences. For example, in recent election cycles, bad actors have used AI to create micro-targeted campaign ads: one variant for suburban parents stoking fears about school board policies, another for rural voters amplifying concerns about agricultural regulations, each optimized to resonate with that group’s demonstrated preferences, with no label disclosing the AI’s role in crafting the message.
[P1.3] This is not a minor administrative ask. Labeling AI-optimized persuasion is a minimal, low-burden transparency measure that does not ban any content, only requires platforms to disclose when an AI system has tailored persuasive messaging to a specific user or micro-group, much like we already require disclosure of sponsored content. Users have a right to know when the content they see is not just human-created, but algorithmically calibrated to manipulate their individual vulnerabilities—whether that’s a political ad, a health misinformation post, or a predatory financial offer.
[P1.4] The alternative is a landscape where bad actors can weaponize personal data to deliver undetectable, hyper-personalized manipulation at scale, eroding informed choice, electoral integrity, and public trust. This requirement closes that loophole, and is a common-sense step to protect users in an era of ubiquitous AI.
