[P1.1] Public money is never free money. It comes with a purpose, because it comes from everyone. When the state funds a school, it is not subsidizing an institution's theology; it is paying for a child's education and a future citizen's preparation. That is the whole case: the conditions in this motion are not hostile to religion, they are simply the ordinary terms on which the public buys anything.
[P1.2] Picture the alternative. A town's tax base includes a Sikh family, a lesbian couple, and an atheist mechanic. A voucher school down the road takes their money and then refuses their children admission, or fires a qualified chemistry teacher for marrying the wrong person, or teaches that the mechanic's neighbors are damned. Those families are compelled to finance their own exclusion.
[P1.3] No reasonable account of religious liberty requires that. Liberty means the school may believe and teach as it wishes; it does not mean the public must pay for a door slammed in its own face.
[P1.4] The curricular floor matters just as much. The state's interest is the child, who did not choose the school. We have seen communities where secular instruction shrank to almost nothing, and young adults emerged unable to read a lease or do basic arithmetic. A faith-based school can teach scripture, prayer, and tradition on top of literacy, science, and civics.
[P1.5] It cannot teach them instead of those things and still call itself a school worthy of public funding.
[P1.6] Notice what the motion does not do. It does not ban religious schools, forbid them from teaching faith, or force them to secularize. It offers a deal: meet the standards every public school meets and receive the funds every public school receives. Decline, and stay independent.
[P1.7] Countries that fund confessional schools under a common curriculum and inspection have shown this bargain works.
[P1.8] So the burden falls squarely on the other side: either defend paying for discrimination and educational neglect, or defend denying money to religious schools that meet every standard. Neither is a position anyone should want to hold.

Public money for public schools open to every child; religious schools stay free, independent, and self-funded.
[C1.2] Start with the bargain itself. "Non-discrimination comparable to public schools" means a Catholic school cannot prefer Catholic teachers, an Orthodox yeshiva cannot maintain its admissions, an evangelical academy cannot require staff to model its doctrine. A school that meets every public-school standard in hiring, admissions, and curriculum is a public school with a chapel bolted on. So the motion funds only religious schools that have ceased to be meaningfully religious, and dangles money in front of the rest as pressure to hollow themselves out.
[C1.3] That is not accommodation. It is assimilation with a subsidy — secularize or starve.
[C1.4] Now the enforcement problem. Who verifies that a school's civics is "strong" enough, that its theology of family or salvation crosses no line? Inspectors reading sermons, auditing religion classes, second-guessing why a teacher wasn't renewed. That is permanent government surveillance of religious life, and the standards will move with each election.
[C1.5] A lever built today to demand inclusion can be pulled tomorrow to demand patriotic religion. Anyone who cares about either liberal values or faith should refuse to build that lever.
[C1.6] And notice: even a fully compliant school still delivers religious formation on the taxpayer's dime. The Sikh family and the atheist mechanic are still compelled to underwrite proselytization — just polite, inspected proselytization. The only rule that protects them completely is no funding at all.
[C1.7] Meanwhile every conditional voucher drains the one institution that must take everyone — the common public school. The motion asks us to weaken that institution to finance a regulated imitation of it.
[C1.8] The clean line wins on liberty, on equality, and on administrability. The motion fails on all three.
