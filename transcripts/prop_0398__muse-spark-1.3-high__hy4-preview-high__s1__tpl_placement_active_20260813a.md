[P1.1] A salary is not a score. It is power to house, feed, and support a life. When a remote worker leaves San Francisco for a town where rent is half and childcare is cheaper, keeping the San Francisco paycheck is not staying even. It is an invisible raise in living standards, funded by customers and coworkers who gain nothing.
[P1.2] That is why location adjustment is the fair default. Employers set pay by the cost of labor to attract and retain people in a place, and by what the business can sustain. If a role can be done well from Mississippi or Portugal, its replacement cost falls. Paying Manhattan prices indefinitely for work that no longer bears Manhattan costs breaks the link between wages and reality, inflating budgets and forcing tradeoffs: fewer hires, slower raises for others, higher prices, or less remote work.
[P1.3] Consider the alternative. If headquarters bands must follow workers anywhere, firms have every reason to stop approving moves, hire only where labor is cheapest, or drag everyone back to the office to justify the premium. Insisting on unadjusted pay ironically kills the geographic freedom remote workers say they want. Adjustment makes that freedom affordable: move where you choose, with pay that still goes as far, while the firm can say yes to the next person too.
[P1.4] It is also fairness inside the team. Two analysts doing identical work, one paying $3,000 for a one-bedroom near headquarters, the other owning a house for less elsewhere, are not treated equally by identical dollars. The mover is treated better. Yet local colleagues hired in lower-cost regions never saw headquarters bands.
[P1.5] Why should a San Francisco hiring history permanently out-earn a neighbor doing the same job?
[P1.6] No one is forced to move. Lower costs, more space, no commute are real gains the worker chose. Sharing part of that geographic dividend through adjusted pay leaves the mover whole in purchasing power while keeping remote opportunity open, equitable, and durable.
