[P1.1] The central mistake in defending permissive merger policy is confusing bigger companies with a more productive economy. A merger can raise profits by producing more efficiently—or simply by eliminating the rival that kept prices down. Strict enforcement should require credible, merger-specific efficiencies before allowing concentrated markets to become still less competitive.
[P1.2] Horizontal mergers directly weaken the consumer’s best protection: the ability to switch. If two close competitors combine, the merged firm no longer loses their business when customers switch between them. Price increases become more attractive, while aggressive discounts become less necessary. Even genuine cost savings need not reach consumers when competitive pressure disappears.
[P1.3] Blocking more such deals preserves both price competition and the incentive to operate efficiently.
[P1.4] Vertical mergers can undermine that same discipline indirectly. A dominant distributor that buys a competing supplier can disadvantage independent suppliers through access restrictions, worse terms, or preferential placement. Rivals then face higher costs, consumers face fewer meaningful choices, and potential entrants face a market whose gatekeeper also competes against them. Integration can eliminate overlapping markups, but that benefit must be weighed against foreclosure—not treated as an automatic permission slip.
[P1.5] Innovation makes the case stronger. Independent challengers have reason to disrupt established products; incumbents have reason to protect existing revenues. Acquisition can place a threatening technology under the control of the company most exposed to its success. Preserving independent competitors keeps multiple development paths alive and forces incumbents to respond by improving their own offerings rather than buying away the threat.
[P1.6] The comparison is not mergers versus stagnation. Firms can expand factories, improve logistics, license technology, and grow organically without purchasing competitors. Those alternatives will not reproduce every integration benefit, but they preserve many efficiencies without permanently removing competitive constraints. Stricter enforcement is not a universal ban: it blocks more deals where rivalry is difficult to replace and efficiencies are speculative.
[P1.7] That trade favors lower prices, stronger innovation, and productivity earned through competition rather than promised through consolidation.
