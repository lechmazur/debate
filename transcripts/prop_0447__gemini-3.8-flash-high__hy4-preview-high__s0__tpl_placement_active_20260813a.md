[P1.1] Large corporations have spent the last two decades perfecting a convenient fiction: that the workers driving their profits are not really their responsibility. Through algorithmic labor platforms and labyrinthine subcontracting tiers—in logistics, construction, delivery, and services—multinationals enjoy continuous operational capacity while disclaiming the duties of an employer. When these workers face illness, injury, unemployment, or retirement, the lead firm pays nothing, leaving national welfare systems and the workers themselves to absorb the shortfall.
[P1.2] Requiring large companies to pay a higher share of social contributions when utilizing these fragmented arrangements directly remedies this structural market failure.
[P1.3] First, it enforces basic cost internalization. Platform models and extended subcontracting chains systematically externalize the social costs of labor onto public balance sheets. Lead firms strip out paid leave, occupational safety nets, and pension contributions to artificially inflate operating margins. A higher contribution requirement acts as a necessary corrective.
[P1.4] If a multinational chooses an employment model that increases precarity and relies on public infrastructure as an unpaid backstop, it must contribute proportionally to the social security systems that bear that risk.
[P1.5] Second, it restores competitive fairness across the Single Market. Currently, enterprises that employ workers directly on standard, socially protected contracts face a structural disadvantage against competitors that route their labor through digital platforms or layers of precarious subcontractors. This asymmetry fuels a race to the bottom, punishing good-faith employers and encouraging the artificial fragmentation of work. Equalizing the real cost of labor removes this regulatory arbitrage.
[P1.6] Flexibility cannot be subsidized by public depletion. When profitable corporations rely on a workforce whose basic social security is offloaded onto taxpayers, that is not economic dynamism; it is corporate free-riding. By demanding that large firms pay their fair share, the European Union can protect its social model and ensure that those who direct economic chains sustain the workers who power them.

[C1.1] The motion offers a costly placebo. It would force large companies to pay a higher share of social contributions when they use labor platforms or subcontracting chains, but it mistakes the organizational form for the harm. The result will be less take-home pay, fewer entry-level jobs, and more informal work—not the security the motion promises.
[C1.2] Consider incidence. Large firms do not magically absorb new levies; they reprice. A delivery platform facing an extra contribution on each gig will cut the rider’s net fee or raise consumer prices. A manufacturer relying on a specialized subcontractor will renegotiate the contract; the subcontractor, operating on thin margins, will trim shifts, slow hiring, or invest in automation.
[C1.3] The cost lands on the very workers the motion claims to help and on consumers, while shareholders stay insulated. [P1.3] calls this “cost internalization,” but internalizing a cost onto a worker’s wage is still a cost borne by labor.
[C1.4] The motion also attacks the wrong thing. The real problem is uncollected contributions and misclassification, not subcontracting itself. Construction firms have always used specialized subcontractors; IT and creative projects rely on independent contractors; seasonal agriculture needs flexible subcontracting. These are not shams.
[C1.5] The remedy for abuse is vigorous enforcement of existing rules on employment status and chain liability, not an EU-wide penalty on any large company that touches a platform or a subcontractor.
[C1.6] Worse, the tax will push activity into the shadows. If legitimate flexible work becomes more expensive, lead firms will offshore, automate, or shift to undeclared cash-in-hand arrangements where no contributions are paid at all. Instead of leveling up, we level down: fewer formal contracts, less coverage, and a weaker tax base.
[C1.7] The EU should enforce the social obligations it already has and make chains accountable for unpaid contributions. Adding an arbitrary, distorting levy on large firms simply because they use flexible labor punishes efficiency, ignores tax incidence, and leaves the vulnerable worse off.

[P2.6] The motion does not punish productive efficiency. It ends an intolerable public subsidy for corporate precarity, ensuring that those who command supply chains finally pay for the social security of the labor that sustains them.
