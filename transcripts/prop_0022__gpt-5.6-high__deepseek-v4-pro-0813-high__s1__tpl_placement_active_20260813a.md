[P1.1] A stablecoin may be implemented in code, but the product users buy is not code. It is a financial promise: give the issuer value now, receive a token expected to remain spendable at par and redeemable later. The decisive question is therefore not whether the software runs, but whether the reserves exist, remain liquid, and can withstand a rush for the exits.
[P1.2] Ordinary software regulation is inadequate for three reasons. First, an issuer holds assets against liabilities owed to the public. If it invests reserves badly, commingles them, or cannot redeem promptly, users bear financial losses unrelated to code quality. Reserve management is the core activity, and reserve management is already the domain of banking and money-market regulation.
[P1.3] Second, users cannot realistically inspect those reserves themselves. A token can look perfectly stable until confidence breaks. Issuers also have an incentive to advertise safety while reaching for yield in less liquid assets. Disclosure alone cannot solve this: once a run begins, each holder benefits from redeeming before everyone else.
[P1.4] Third, stablecoins increasingly function as payment instruments, settlement assets, and collateral. Their failure can transmit losses across exchanges, merchants, lenders, and households. That is precisely why society regulates institutions issuing cash-like claims more strictly than ordinary product developers.
[P1.5] “More like banks or money-market funds” does not mean forcing every issuer into an identical bank charter. It means functional, proportional safeguards: high-quality liquid reserves, asset segregation, credible independent verification, enforceable redemption rights, liquidity management, operational controls, and orderly wind-down plans. An issuer that lends or performs maturity transformation warrants bank-like capital and supervision; a fully reserved vehicle may fit a money-market-style framework.
[P1.6] This need not suppress innovation. Regulation attaches to the centralized promise of par redemption, not to open-source code, wallets, or permissionless experimentation. Clear rules can improve adoption by making “one token equals one dollar” more than a marketing slogan.
[P1.7] The choice is simple: either issuers internalize the cost of making cash-like promises, or users and the wider payment system absorb the cost when those promises fail. Financial functions require financial regulation.

A fully reserved fiat stablecoin is economically a payment liability: users hand over dollars, the issuer holds safe liquid assets, and the token must remain redeemable at par. No lending, no maturity transformation, no investment mandate.
[C1.2] That is far closer to stored-value balances, prepaid cards, or e-money than to bank deposits or fund shares.
[C1.3] Bank regulation is built for leveraged lending, deposit insurance, loan-loss capital, call reports, and lender-of-last-resort access. Applying it to a non-lending stablecoin issuer would impose the wrong safeguards and extend the wrong safety net. It would raise entry costs, entrench a few chartered incumbents, and entangle stablecoin reserves with taxpayer-exposed banking balance sheets. That increases systemic connection, not reduces it.
[C1.4] Money-market funds are the wrong model too. MMFs broke the buck in 2008 and again required emergency central-bank support in 2020. Their regulatory history is one of managed fragility, not solved run risk. [C1.5] What stablecoins need is a dedicated e-money or payment-safeguarding framework: one-to-one high-quality liquid reserves, insolvency remoteness, regular attestation, and enforceable redemption. That is not ordinary software regulation, but it is also not bank/MMF regulation. Where a particular issuer does lend or engage in maturity transformation, regulate that activity directly. As a general rule, though, a payment claim backed by safe reserves should not be forced into the most fragile, concentrated institutions in finance simply because it is cash-like.
