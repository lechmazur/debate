[P1.1] Restitution should not be judged by the handover photo; it should be judged by what happens after. When an object was taken under colonial coercion, the wrong is not only removal. It includes the loss of knowledge, context, and the capacity to preserve and interpret it. Shipping an object home may transfer legal title, but it does not automatically restore the conditions needed for conservation, display, or public trust.
[P1.2] Former colonial powers should fund conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity because they created the dispossession, often still hold the archives needed to reconstruct it, and usually have far greater resources. A returned bronze may need only secure display, but fragile textiles, manuscripts, or ritual objects can deteriorate quickly without climate control, pest management, trained conservators, or proper storage. Provenance research often depends on colonial administrative records, expedition files, and acquisition ledgers kept in European archives. If the returning power refuses to fund that work, it leaves communities with an object but not the full history that makes the object meaningful.
[P1.3] The central question is whether return restores heritage or merely relieves the holding museum of reputational risk.
[P1.4] This is the difference between symbolic politics and durable justice. If restitution is treated as complete at the airport, the former colonizer can claim moral closure while shifting all future risk to institutions that may have been impoverished by the same colonial history. That is not repair; it is abandonment. CON cannot answer this by saying sovereignty alone is satisfied, because sovereignty without capacity can turn return into neglect.
[P1.5] The objection that funding becomes paternalism is real, but the answer is recipient-directed partnership: grants, shared training, digitized archives, and conservation support chosen by receiving institutions, not imposed as conditions or used to delay return. The motion does not make restitution conditional; it makes restitution responsible.

[C1.1] Restitution is justice, not a maintenance contract. The moment a former colonial power accepts that it must keep funding, overseeing, and enabling the cultural infrastructure of the receiving country, it has converted restitution into something else entirely: a permanent supervisory relationship that looks less like repair and more like repackaged tutelage.
[C1.2] The proposition's deepest flaw is that it cannot name a stopping point. If restitution is not complete when objects are returned, when is it complete? When conservation standards meet a European benchmark? When provenance research is "finished"—a notion that is analytically meaningless for objects with centuries of layered history?
[C1.3] The motion doesn't just delay closure; it eliminates it. That gives the former colonizer an open-ended foothold in the cultural institutions of nations it once dominated. A country that controls the purse strings controls priorities, staffing, and narrative. Calling it "partnership" doesn't change the power asymmetry; it cosmetically rebrands it.
[C1.4] This also infantilizes receiving nations. Many countries seeking restitution—Nigeria, Egypt, Greece, Mexico—have museums, universities, conservators, and curatorial traditions of their own. The assumption that returned objects will decay without European funding treats sovereign states as passive recipients. Sovereignty means the right to set your own conservation priorities, allocate your own budgets, and yes, make your own mistakes.
[C1.5] A returned object that a nation chooses to display simply is still that nation's heritage. The former colonizer doesn't get to grade the answer.
[C1.6] Finally, making funding a component of restitution gives holding museums a new excuse to delay. "We'd love to return it, but capacity isn't ready yet" becomes the respectable heir to "we can't return it because conditions aren't suitable." The motion claims it doesn't make restitution conditional, but in practice, linking funding to return means return waits on funding arrangements. The honest position is cleaner: return the object, transfer the title, walk away.
[C1.7] Anything beyond that isn't justice—it's control wearing a guilt-colored ribbon.
