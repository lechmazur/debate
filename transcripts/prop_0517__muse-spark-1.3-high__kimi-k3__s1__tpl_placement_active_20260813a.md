[P1.1] A state has good reasons to scrutinize who lives and works inside its territory. Physical presence consumes housing, public services, and labor-market space, and raises security and integration questions. Recognizing a credential earned online imposes none of those costs. The barrier should track the burden — and that means the lighter act should face the lighter rule.
[P1.2] That ordering unlocks work that residency systems strangle. Hospitals, firms and universities face shortages not of bodies in general but of specific certified skills — coding, accounting, design, translation, tutoring. A Kenyan data analyst or a Filipino bookkeeper can fill that gap remotely tomorrow if her qualification is recognized, with no visa queue, no relocation cost, no pressure on rents. Making her wait behind someone seeking a residence permit gets the priority backwards.
[P1.3] It is also fairer. Residency pathways reward those already mobile: young, wealthy, able to pay foreign tuition and rent and survive years of paperwork. That drains talent from poorer countries and concentrates opportunity. Easier cross-border recognition of online credentials reverses the flow — workers earn global wages while staying home, paying taxes and mentoring locally.
[P1.4] Skill circulates without people being uprooted.
[P1.5] And it is more verifiable. Physical residency is judged through opaque discretion — funds, intent, quotas. Online professional work leaves a trail: exams, portfolios, supervised projects, employer ratings, renewable micro-credentials that can be audited across borders. States can harmonize standards, test for competence, and revoke recognition if quality slips.
[P1.6] Low risk, high traceability, massive inclusion: that is exactly what should get the fast lane.
[P1.7] To keep residency harder is not to abolish it; it is to stop using the heaviest gate for the lightest contribution. If we want productive, humane mobility, recognize the skill first and debate the move second.
