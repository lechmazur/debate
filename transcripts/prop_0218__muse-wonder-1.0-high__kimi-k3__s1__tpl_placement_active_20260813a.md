[P1.1] A cracked screen should not cost nearly as much as a new phone, and a worn battery should not send a working laptop to landfill. Yet that is what happens when manufacturers alone control parts, manuals, and diagnostic software. A national right to repair breaks that control, and over the next five years its savings and waste reductions will dwarf any added safety or security risk.
[P1.2] First, competition lowers prices. Today manufacturers can pair parts to one device, withhold genuine components, and steer owners to authorized shops charging monopoly rates. When independent shops and owners can buy the same battery or display, use the same calibration tool, and read the same manual, repair becomes competitive again. Shops compete on labor, refurbished parts become viable, and manufacturers must cut their own prices.
[P1.3] Even owners who never self-repair benefit, because a credible cheaper alternative reduces repair bills and the pressure to replace a device early.
[P1.4] Second, cheaper, faster repair cuts e-waste. Most electronics are discarded not because processors are obsolete, but because a battery degrades, a port fails, or a screen breaks. Extending a phone or laptop by a year or two spreads its manufacturing footprint — mining, shipping, and assembly — over far more use and keeps hazardous materials out of the waste stream. At national scale, modest lifespan gains across hundreds of millions of devices produce large avoided waste.
[P1.5] Those risks are real but narrow and manageable. Repair laws do not require publishing trade secrets, encryption keys, or source code; they require the parts and tools already given to authorized technicians. Those technicians are not uniquely safe by employment — independent technicians can follow the same procedures, warnings, and authentication checks. Lithium batteries and device security deserve standards, but standards support regulated access, not monopoly denial.
[P1.6] On scale and frequency, the benefits win: routine savings and avoided waste for millions of people, versus isolated incidents that existing product-safety and cybersecurity law can still police.

[C1.5] The risks, by contrast, arrive on day one. Security is worse: the serialization and pairing these laws target is what binds biometric sensors to the secure enclave and makes stolen phones worthless to part out. Broadly distributed flashing and calibration tools leak; once leaked, every stolen device becomes reusable.
[C1.6] When Massachusetts opened vehicle data access, federal safety regulators initially told automakers to refuse, citing remote-attack risk.
[C1.7] So weigh it: modest, delayed savings spread thin, against immediate, catastrophic tail risks multiplied across hundreds of millions of devices. A forty-dollar cheaper screen does not balance a house fire or a revived market in stolen parts.
