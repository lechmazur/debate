[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies look like help for ordinary people, but they are a fiscal trap that mostly rewards high consumption. Wealthier households drive more, own larger vehicles, and capture the bulk of the benefits, while governments hemorrhage revenue that could fund schools, clinics, or real safety nets. Phasing them out ends that reverse-Robin-Hood transfer.
[P1.2] Targeted cash transfers flip the logic. Prices return to market levels, so scarcity signals work: drivers conserve, fleets modernize, and smuggling becomes less profitable. The savings then reach the actual poor as cash or near-cash support, not as artificially cheap liters of fuel. A low-income family can buy food, pay rent, or cover transport exactly when and how they need it, instead of being forced to capture value only by burning more diesel.
[P1.3] That is both fairer and more efficient.
[P1.4] Broad price controls are the false alternative. They freeze distortions in place, produce shortages, black-market markups, and rationing by queue or connection. They protect neither the environment nor the budget, and they rarely reach the poorest. Cash transfers leave relative prices free to guide behavior while still shielding vulnerable households from the transition shock.
[P1.5] The fiscal and environmental dividends compound. Governments free money previously locked into fuel under-pricing; lower subsidized demand slows emissions and congestion; and transfers can be designed to phase with income, concentrating help where it matters. The choice is not between helping the poor and ending subsidies—it is between wasteful universal under-pricing and precise support that actually reaches those who need it. That is why the phase-out plus cash route is the superior policy.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds clean on a whiteboard. In the real world, it fails on the exact populations it claims to protect.
[C1.2] Start with targeting itself. "Targeted cash transfers" require identifying the poor. In many of the countries that actually run universal fuel subsidies, there is no reliable income registry, informal work dominates, and millions of the poorest lack the ID or bank account needed to receive a transfer. Every targeting scheme has exclusion error, and it is not random—it systematically misses the most marginal: rural households, day laborers, people without fixed addresses.
[C1.3] A subsidy on diesel or cooking gas doesn't ask for a birth certificate. Cash transfer rollouts do, in effect, through the bureaucracy required to enroll.
[C1.4] Second, timing. Removing a universal fuel subsidy is instant—prices jump at the pump the moment the policy changes. Building a functioning cash transfer system—registries, verification, disbursement rails, anti-fraud checks—takes years, not months. That gap is not theoretical; it is where households actually live.
[C1.5] Nigeria's 2023 subsidy removal and Ecuador's 2019 attempt both triggered transport and food price spikes that hit the poor immediately, while promised compensation programs lagged or stalled. The "phase-out first, targeting later" sequence PRO defends is exactly the sequence that produces riots, not relief.
[C1.6] Third, fuel subsidies don't just cushion consumers directly—they stabilize an input that feeds transport, agriculture, and food prices economy-wide. A cash transfer to a targeted household does nothing to stop the price of moving grain to market from spiking, which raises food prices for everyone, including people the transfer never reaches.
[C1.7] None of this is an endorsement of blunt universal subsidies as ideal policy. It is a recognition that the proposition's alternative is fragile, slow, and administratively demanding in exactly the contexts where it's proposed. Broad price stabilization, whatever its inefficiencies, delivers what targeting in weak-capacity states cannot reliably deliver: universal, immediate, low-friction protection.
