[P1.1] When unemployment rises, the economy is already failing the people least able to wait. The right response is not a debate that outlasts the downturn. It is a rule that puts cash in the hands of the newly jobless the moment the labor market breaks, and accepts a temporary rise in public debt as the price of stopping a deeper collapse.
[P1.2] Speed is the first advantage. Discretionary stimulus must survive recognition lags, legislative bargaining, and implementation delays. By the time a package clears, firms have already cut hours, families have already missed rent, and local spending has already shrunk. An automatic cash transfer tied to unemployment does not wait for a majority.
[P1.3] It arrives while the income shock is still reversible.
[P1.4] Targeting is the second. Newly unemployed households spend quickly on food, housing, and bills, not on idle savings. That spending reaches grocers, landlords, and nearby employers before layoffs spread. A stabilizer that expands with the unemployment rolls is therefore both insurance and demand support, aimed where cutbacks would otherwise multiply.
[P1.5] The debt objection mistakes the accounting for the economics. Borrowing rises on the books, but so does the cost of inaction: longer joblessness, skill loss, weaker revenue, and a smaller economy. Debt taken while output is below potential, when borrowing costs are typically depressed, is cheaper than scarring that never appears as a line item. Austerity in a slump can raise the debt ratio by shrinking the denominator.
[P1.6] Nor does the borrowing crowd out investment that firms have already postponed; it absorbs the private saving a downturn creates.
[P1.7] Rules beat politics. Pre-committed transfers cannot be delayed for a headline or stuffed with unrelated projects. They expand when jobs disappear and recede when hiring returns. Governments should adopt them, debt and all, because the downturn allowed to deepen is the more expensive choice.

[C1.1] The case for automatic unemployment-triggered cash rests on a flattering assumption: that the unemployment rate tells government exactly who needs money, when they need it, and for how long. It does not. PRO calls speed “the first advantage” [P1.2]; speed without accuracy is just a faster error.
[C1.2] Unemployment is lagging and noisy. By the time the rate rises, the shock has already hit; by the time revised data confirm a trigger, recovery may be underway. A national trigger then mails checks into a healing economy, adding demand to supply bottlenecks and inflation. That is not stabilization; it is a second shock.
[C1.3] And unemployment can rise for structural reasons—skills mismatch, sectoral reallocation, migration—where cash does not create jobs, it just raises the debt that future taxpayers service.
[C1.4] The targeting story is also weaker than it sounds. If transfers go only to people on unemployment insurance, they miss gig workers, new entrants, and the long-term jobless. If they are broad enough to reach everyone, they become expensive near-universal payments, including to households with savings and secure jobs. Either way, the claim that this is precisely aimed at the vulnerable collapses.
[C1.5] Worst of all is the ratchet. Benefits that expand automatically are politically easy to trigger and politically painful to unwind. Downturns end; spending programs do not. The result is a higher structural deficit, higher interest costs, and less fiscal room for the next crisis—precisely when automatic help is needed most.
[C1.6] Debt is not a bookkeeping illusion. If transfers are mistimed, they do not generate the growth that supposedly shrinks the debt ratio; they simply finance consumption while borrowing costs rise. If they are mistargeted, they crowd out more effective aid. Existing stabilizers—unemployment insurance, food assistance, progressive tax declines—already expand in downturns.
[C1.7] The motion asks us to add a blunt, permanent entitlement triggered by a lagging statistic, and to ignore the debt. That is not a stabilizer. It is a fiscal trap with a compassionate name.
