[P1.1] Housing costs now dominate household budgets in most advanced economies, often exceeding a third of income. When prices outrun wages, the result is not a harmless statistical blip but a structural drag on consumption, savings, and labor mobility. Central banks that ignore this component of inflation betray their price‑stability mandate and, paradoxically, invite the very recession they claim to avoid.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. Elevated house prices push up rents, squeezing disposable income and forcing families into debt. Debt‑fuelled demand inflates asset bubbles, which become fragile. When the bubble bursts, the fallout is a severe, prolonged downturn—exactly what the United States experienced after the mid‑2000s housing boom and what many European economies faced a decade later.
[P1.3] Those recessions were far deeper than any short‑term contraction that might arise from tightening policy to cool housing markets.
[P1.4] Prioritising housing inflation is therefore a pre‑emptive strike. By raising rates or deploying macro‑prudential tools to temper price growth, central banks can flatten the bubble before it reaches a tipping point. The resulting modest slowdown is a controlled recession, one that can be cushioned by fiscal policy and targeted support. The alternative—letting prices run unchecked—produces a larger, more chaotic bust that harms employment, wealth, and financial stability for years.
[P1.5] Housing inflation also entrenches inequality. When ownership becomes a speculative asset, younger and low‑income workers are locked out, eroding social cohesion and long‑term productivity. A central bank that tolerates this inflation sacrifices the broader economic health it is charged to protect.
[P1.6] Thus the rational trade‑off is clear: accept a calibrated, temporary recession risk to prevent the far greater, systemic danger of a housing‑driven collapse.

[C1.2] Housing inflation is predominantly a supply failure—zoning restrictions, construction costs, and regulatory delays constrain supply while demand outruns it. Raising interest rates does not build a single apartment. It crushes aggregate demand, destroys jobs, and pushes marginal workers into unemployment, all to marginally cool a price index that monetary policy cannot fix at the source. A central bank that engineers a recession to lower home prices is using a sledgehammer to fix a plumbing problem, breaking the pipes in the process.
[C1.3] The "controlled recession" framing is dangerous hubris. Central banks cannot calibrate downturns with precision. Once unemployment rises and businesses fail, the spiral becomes self-reinforcing—foreclosures rise, credit contracts, and the very households the policy claims to help face eviction and job loss. Deliberately tightening into economic weakness repeats the errors that preceded past crises, now with eyes wide open.
[C1.4] Moreover, central banks possess macroprudential tools—loan-to-value caps, debt-service limits, and sectoral capital requirements—that can cool housing credit without triggering economy-wide contraction. The proposition's "even if" clause reveals a willingness to abandon these targeted instruments for the bluntest weapon available: mass unemployment.
[C1.5] Housing costs matter, but solving them requires supply-side reform, not sacrificing workers on the altar of price stability. A central bank that prioritizes a specific asset class over full employment has abandoned its mandate.

The opposition's reliance on these tools as a painless alternative is an empirical bet that history has already failed.
[P2.3] On calibration, the opposition assumes central banks cannot manage a slowdown but can somehow manage the aftermath of an unchecked bust. That is backwards. [P2.4] The 2008 crisis did not arise from central banks tightening too hard; it arose from them staying too loose while asset prices inflated. The controlled recession is not a gamble; it is the less dangerous option.
[P2.5] Finally, the suggestion that supply reform solves everything ignores the time horizon. Zoning changes take years, sometimes decades, to affect housing stock. Meanwhile, speculative bubbles compound. Central banks cannot wait for supply reforms to bear fruit while prices spiral.
[P2.6] Their dual mandate requires acting now, not deferring to a political process that may never deliver.
