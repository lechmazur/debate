[P1.2] First, consider the asymmetry of harm. Machine learning risk models operate on statistical probabilities, not certainty. To an algorithm, a false positive rate is merely an acceptable tradeoff on an optimization curve. To a real person, a false positive means immediate destitution or legal jeopardy.
[P1.3] Because administrative appeals take weeks or months to resolve, a summary algorithmic suspension inflicts catastrophic, irreversible damage long before the error is ever acknowledged.
[P1.4] Second, automated risk scoring inherently embeds systemic bias and opacity. When an agency automates adverse action based solely on a model’s score, it converts statistical correlation into summary punishment, stripping away the context that would easily explain an apparent anomaly.
[P1.5] Third, government legitimacy requires individualized reason-giving and accountability. Algorithms cannot exercise discretion, evaluate nuanced life circumstances, or distinguish a clerical error from intentional fraud. A machine cannot listen to a citizen's explanation or review an exculpatory document.
[P1.6] Meaningful human review is not an inconvenient bottleneck; it is the indispensable threshold between legitimate governance and arbitrary administrative harm. Algorithmic tools may assist investigators by highlighting patterns, but they must never possess the unilateral authority to strip people of their livelihoods, legal status, or essential support.
