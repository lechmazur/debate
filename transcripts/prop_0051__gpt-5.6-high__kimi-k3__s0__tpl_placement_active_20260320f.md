[P1.1] Ultra-cheap shopping is not genuinely cheap when its price is lowered by shifting hidden risks onto consumers and workers. The central principle is simple: firms that profit from organizing these transactions must also carry enforceable duties to make them safe and traceable.
[P1.2] Stronger product-safety obligations should require marketplaces to identify sellers and responsible manufacturers, verify compliance for higher-risk goods, preserve records, remove dangerous listings promptly, and contact buyers during recalls. This matters because fragmented overseas sellers may disappear, rename themselves, or sit beyond effective enforcement. The marketplace, by contrast, controls listings, payments, ranking, and customer communication. It is the durable gatekeeper and the cheapest point at which to prevent repeated harm.
[P1.3] Waiting until a child’s accessory contains a hazardous substance or an electrical product overheats turns consumers into involuntary safety testers.
[P1.4] Labor transparency addresses the same structural problem upstream. Transparency is not a guarantee of perfect labor practices, but opacity virtually guarantees impunity.
[P1.5] The likely objection is affordability and convenience. But the proposition does not require banning inexpensive imports or imposing identical checks on every hair clip and winter coat. [P1.6] A slightly slower delivery or modestly higher price is reversible; poisoning, fire, injury, and labor exploitation are not. Cross-border commerce deserves speed only after it meets the basic conditions we already expect from domestic commerce: accountability, traceability, and human safety.
