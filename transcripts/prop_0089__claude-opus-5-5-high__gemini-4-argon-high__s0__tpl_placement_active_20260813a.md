[P1.3] It is not a paradox at all. Pre-K builds persistence, self-control, and comfort in a classroom. Reading tests miss those traits, but later life rewards them. So when CON points to fading scores, remember that the early programs showed the same fade and still produced some of the best returns in social policy.
[P1.4] Now the objection that those were small, targeted programs. Boston's public pre-K answers it. Admission was by lottery, which gives clean comparisons. Winners showed little lasting test advantage.

[P1.6] Second, universality is a feature, not waste. Means-tested programs miss many children just above the cutoff, segregate poor kids together, and lose political support, so quality decays. Universal programs mix children across classes, which helps disadvantaged kids most. Middle-class voters will defend them, which protects funding and quality.
[P1.7] Third, the cost calculation ignores half the benefits. Pre-K is also childcare. When Washington, D.C. made pre-K universal, mothers' workforce participation rose substantially. That means tax revenue and family income arrive immediately, not in twenty years.
[P1.8] So the comparison is simple. We pay a few thousand dollars per child per year. In return we get higher graduation rates, more college enrollment, less crime, and working parents. CON must show these gains are illusory, not merely that test scores fade.
