[P1.1] Dementia strips away the very faculties needed to form a new, authoritative judgment about how one wants to live and die. It does not create a new person with new values—it creates a person with diminished capacity to represent values at all. That distinction is the whole case for PRO.
[P1.2] Advance directives exist precisely because we anticipate this. A person writing a directive while competent is doing something specific: exercising precedent autonomy, binding a future self they know may lose the capacity to reason, weigh trade-offs, and consent meaningfully. This is a Ulysses contract. Ulysses did not want to be untied just because, in the moment, sirens sounded sweet to him.
[P1.3] The whole point of the mast was to make his in-the-moment preference powerless against his prior, considered judgment. If we let "But he seems to want it now" override the contract, we have not respected autonomy—we have destroyed the only mechanism autonomy has for surviving incapacity.
[P1.4] Consider the common case: a woman with advanced Alzheimer's smiles when fed pudding, seems calm, hums along to music. Caregivers say she seems "happy now" and use this to justify overriding her prior directive refusing life-prolonging treatment. But contentment in the moment is not evidence of a value judgment. A toddler is often content too.
[P1.5] Reflexive pleasure responses are not deliberative endorsements of a way of living—they cannot substitute for the reasoned, contextualized values the person held when she could actually understand mortality, dignity, and burden.
[P1.6] This is why the "clear evidence of material change" standard is the only workable line. Without it, any family member, institution, or caregiver motivated by guilt, convenience, or disagreement can claim the patient's demeanor now reflects new values, and directives become advisory suggestions rather than binding commitments. That guts the entire institutional purpose of advance directives and leaves every dementia patient's most vulnerable years governed by whoever interprets their smiles most conveniently. Strong bindingness isn't rigidity for its own sake—it's the only structure that actually protects the person who wrote the directive from being overridden by observers [clipped]

[C1.2] Precedent autonomy cannot justly let that vanished author govern a stranger's dying.
[C1.3] PRO's "clear evidence of material change" standard [P1.6] rigs the outcome. Dementia destroys the ability to articulate new values, so the test is almost impossible to meet. The only evidence left is exactly what PRO dismisses: smiling at pudding, humming to music, seeming calm [P1.4]. PRO calls these "reflexive" and compares them to a toddler [P1.5], but for someone who can no longer weigh mortality or dignity, these experiences are not evidence of absent welfare—they are the entirety of the welfare she can now have.
[C1.4] To require articulate value-change is to mute the only voice the present person has.
[C1.5] The cost is concrete. Suppose this woman develops an easily treatable infection and her directive refuses antibiotics. Honor it, and we withdraw care from someone currently content—killing her not to end suffering, but to obey a past self's horror at a future she cannot foresee. She has no memory of her diagnosis, no dread, only this afternoon's music.
[C1.6] Conversely, had she signed a maximal-treatment directive, strong bindingness would force a terrified, uncomprehending patient through surgery and ventilation. Either way, the document overrides the patient.
[C1.7] Therefore advance directives should be weighty but not strongly binding. They should yield when following them would damage the current person's actual well-being or no longer fit her situation. The default must be the living patient's lived experience, not a dead hand clutching a clipboard.
