[P1.1] For decades, African economies have been locked into an extractive bargain: ship out raw cocoa, crude oil, and unrefined minerals, only to buy back chocolate, fuel, and batteries at many times the price. Continuing to rely on this colonial-era conveyor belt is a recipe for permanent vulnerability. Prioritizing intra-African trade and manufacturing is the only viable path to structural wealth, job creation, and economic sovereignty.
[P1.2] First, raw material dependence surrenders the vast majority of economic value. In global supply chains, extraction is the lowest-margin link; processing, branding, and manufacturing capture the wealth. Worse, capital-intensive mining and oil enclaves employ a tiny fraction of the workforce while leaving national budgets hostage to violent swings in global commodity prices. Africa has the world’s youngest and fastest-growing population.
[P1.3] Exporting unrefined ore to Europe or Asia cannot absorb millions of new workers entering the labor market every year; only factories, agro-processing, and regional value chains can.
[P1.4] Second, intra-African trade is uniquely suited to build that industrial base. When African nations trade with distant rich markets, they overwhelmingly export primary commodities, in part because wealthy importers have long used tariff escalation and regulatory barriers to welcome raw inputs while penalizing finished African goods.
[P1.5] By contrast, when African nations trade with each other, a far higher share consists of manufactured and processed products, from medicines to refined foods and steel. Neighboring markets share similar consumer needs and offer a realistic proving ground where regional firms can achieve economies of scale.
[P1.6] Prioritizing this shift does not mean halting resource exports overnight. It means using current revenues to break commodity dependence: redirecting capital, policy, and infrastructure from colonial mine-to-port railways toward regional integration under the African Continental Free Trade Area. Instead of merely digging up cobalt or harvesting raw cotton for distant assembly lines, African states must build the power grids, cross-border corridors, and industrial capacity that allow African resources to be processed by African workers for African markets.

[C1.1] Prioritizing neighbors over customers who can actually pay is a strategy for shared scarcity, not industrialization. Intra-African manufacturing is a worthy outcome, but putting it ahead of raw-material exports to rich markets would cut the earnings, scale, and investment that make industrialization possible in the first place.
[C1.2] First, rich markets provide what regional markets currently cannot: deep purchasing power and hard currency. Most African economies export similar primary products and import similar manufactures, so neighbors are often competitors, not natural buyers. Zambia cannot sell the bulk of its copper, Nigeria its oil, or Côte d’Ivoire its cocoa to nearby low-income consumers at world prices, nor can those consumers finance refineries and grids. Global sales do.
[C1.3] That foreign exchange pays for machinery, fuel, medicine, and the very power and transport infrastructure regional industry requires.
[C1.4] Second, changing the destination does not fix manufacturing costs. Expensive and unreliable electricity, costly credit, poor roads, customs delays, and small fragmented markets make production uncompetitive even behind regional preferences. Shielding firms to sell mainly to Africa risks repeating failed import-substitution: duplicated mini-plants in every country, higher prices for poor consumers, and politically protected producers that never reach export quality. A factory that cannot survive global competition rarely creates durable jobs; it creates a permanent subsidy bill.
[C1.5] Third, successful industrializers did not turn inward first. They earned from the world, imported technology, met demanding standards, and moved up value chains. Morocco’s auto and aerospace growth is tied to European demand and investment; East Asian manufacturers scaled by selling to rich consumers, not only to neighbors. African states should likewise use resource exports aggressively — diversify buyers beyond former colonial powers to China, India, and elsewhere, negotiate better processing terms, and tax rents wisely — rather than deprioritize their strongest revenue source before a replacement exists.

[C2.1] Nigeria did not lack a policy preference for local refining; it owned refineries for decades. They produced expensively and unreliably, so imported fuel was the cheaper way to keep transport and generators running. [C2.2] The same sequencing problem defeats the copper-cable and fertilizer examples. Cables and fertilizer plants need imported machinery, chemicals, and much reliable energy, all bought largely in hard currency. Today that currency comes from selling copper, oil, cocoa, and minerals on world markets. Deprioritize those sales before regional factories can compete, and you do not finance grids and corridors — you shrink the fiscal and foreign-exchange base that pays for them.
[C2.3] Nor does a population of over a billion equal effective demand. Neighbors have low purchasing power, use weaker currencies, and are often hit by the same droughts and commodity downturns, so regional demand falls when export earnings do. Removing tariffs under an agreement does not pave roads, harmonize standards, guarantee payment, or make a fragmented market behave like one rich customer. And if regional firms are sheltered by priority and preferences, the claim that rivalry will discipline them is wishful: protection, not competition, is doing the work.
[C2.4] Finally, East Asia and Morocco cut against regional priority. Their manufacturers scaled by selling high-value goods to Europe and the United States, attracting investment and technology. The lesson is manufacture for the world, not manufacture mainly for neighbors instead of the world.
[C2.5] Diversifying buyers beyond former colonial powers is not an end in itself. Buyer competition raises prices, finance, and bargaining power to demand processing investment while earnings continue. Keep the revenue engine, tax it, and force it to fund competitive industry. Do not demote it for customers who cannot yet replace it.
