[P1.1] Affordability is not merely the price printed on one protected lease; it is whether ordinary households can find a decent home without bidding against a growing queue. In high-demand cities, that queue is created by scarcity. Rent control redistributes scarcity’s costs; streamlined permitting and zoning reduce scarcity itself.
[P1.2] The mechanism is direct. When rules prohibit apartments near jobs, delay projects for years, or make every approval discretionary, households do not disappear. They compete for the existing stock, pushing up rents and prices. Allowing more homes gives them alternatives.
[P1.3] New housing need not be cheap at first to improve affordability: higher-income renters who move into it stop competing for older homes, while added supply weakens landlords’ pricing power across the market. This “filtering” is not a claim that one tower instantly solves poverty; it is the ordinary chain through which persistent construction relieves pressure.
[P1.4] Rent control’s strongest benefit is immediate stability for some existing tenants. That is real, but it is not a superior affordability policy across the population. A capped unit becomes more valuable precisely because alternatives remain expensive. Incumbents stay longer, mobility falls, and scarce units may be allocated by luck, connections, or waiting rather than need.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, providers can withdraw units, neglect maintenance, or convert buildings where legally possible. Tightening every loophole does not create bedrooms; it simply regulates an increasingly rationed stock. Broader tenant protections can deter arbitrary eviction and improve security, but they likewise cannot overcome the arithmetic of more households than homes.
[P1.6] The contrast is visible in places that permit housing abundantly: even expensive land does not produce the same explosion in rents when construction can respond to demand. Conversely, wealthy cities that combine severe building constraints with tenant regulation still face punishing costs for newcomers, young families, and workers moving for jobs.
[P1.7] Targeted assistance and temporary anti-displacement measures can complement reform. But the motion asks which approach increases affordability more effectively. [C1.6] Third, incidence. New construction is the most expensive housing on the market — it cannot be built cheap at current land, labor, and materials costs. [C1.7] Supply reform can worsen affordability for incumbent renters even while improving the aggregate statistics PRO celebrates. Tenant protections are what keep affordability real during exactly the transition PRO's policy demands.
