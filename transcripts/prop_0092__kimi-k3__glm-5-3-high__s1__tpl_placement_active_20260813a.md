[P1.1] Curb space in a dense city is some of the most valuable land on earth, and today we hand it to the biggest vehicles at the same price as the smallest. That is the case for this motion in one sentence: fees should track costs, and oversized SUVs and pickups impose costs their drivers currently push onto everyone else.
[P1.2] Start with safety. Pedestrian deaths in the United States have climbed sharply over the past decade even as they fell in comparable countries, and the changing shape of the fleet is a major suspect. Vehicles have grown taller, heavier, and blunter. A child struck by a tall, flat-fronted pickup is knocked down and pulled under; struck by a sedan, she is more likely thrown onto the hood.
[P1.3] Extra mass does in car-on-car crashes what a sledgehammer does against a mallet — physics does not negotiate, and the dead are disproportionately people outside the vehicle who never chose the risk. That is a textbook externality: a cost imposed on third parties and left unpriced.
[P1.4] Then street use. Pavement damage rises steeply with axle weight — road engineers use roughly a fourth-power rule — so a heavy truck does far more than its share of the wear we all pay to repair. Longer, wider vehicles consume more curb per parking event, demand wider lanes, and crowd out space that could hold a bike lane, a bus stop, or a row of trees. When something scarce is consumed in larger quantities, the price should say so.
[P1.5] None of this is speculative. Paris voted in 2024 to charge heavy SUVs several times the normal parking rate, and several countries already scale registration taxes to vehicle weight. The machinery exists, and it works.
[P1.6] Note the motion's own discipline: higher fees apply when size imposes higher costs. This is not a war on trucks or taste policing. A driver who doesn't want the fee has a simple remedy — choose a vehicle that fits the city. Price the harm, and the harm shrinks.

[C1.6] This is a cultural gesture at people whose vehicles signal the wrong tribe.
[C1.7] Paris proves the point: a referendum on other people's cars is politics, not pricing. If harm is real, regulate the harm. Fees here are a penalty wearing a spreadsheet.
