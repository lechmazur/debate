[P1.1] The proposition asks whether the EU should let the integrity of its climate policy be hostage to the threat of retaliation. It should not. A carbon border adjustment is not a tariff for revenue; it is the external complement to the EU’s internal carbon price. If European steelmakers pay for emissions while foreign steel enters without paying, the predictable result is leakage: production and jobs shift to dirtier plants, global emissions rise, and EU climate policy becomes politically unsustainable.
[P1.2] Steel, cement, aluminium, chemicals, and paper are obvious cases: trade-exposed, emissions-intensive, and easy to relocate on paper while the pollution remains real.
[P1.3] A carbon border adjustment fixes that incentive. By requiring importers to account for embedded emissions, it makes cleaner production competitive anywhere a supplier wants access to the EU market. That is not protectionism disguised as climate policy; it is preventing a race to the bottom. It also gives trading partners a positive alternative to retaliation: decarbonize, keep access, and avoid the charge.
[P1.4] The EU is one of the world’s largest consumption markets. Firms will adjust processes to preserve sales, especially if the policy is transparent, tied to actual emissions, and phased in.
[P1.5] Retaliation is possible, but the motion says “even if,” and that is the right standard. If retaliation vetoes border carbon pricing, then every jurisdiction with a serious carbon price faces the same pressure, and the world loses a necessary tool for industrial decarbonization. Retaliation can be managed: through targeted exemptions for vulnerable economies, revenue recycling into clean industry, supply-chain diversification, and climate clubs that reward alignment. What cannot be managed is a climate strategy that leaks its own ambition abroad.
[P1.6] The central choice is between a credible climate policy that accepts trade friction and a symbolic one that collapses under the first threat. The EU should choose credibility.

[C1.3] Developing nations bear the brunt. Countries such as India, Nigeria or Brazil cannot instantly retrofit heavy industry; a border charge would lock them out of Europe’s market while offering no real pathway to cleaner production, contravening the EU’s own climate‑justice pledges.

[C1.5] A smarter route is selective, sector‑by‑sector measures combined with climate‑club incentives and targeted support for vulnerable economies. That preserves credibility without provoking retaliation.
[C1.6] Thus the EU should reject a sweeping carbon border adjustment that invites retaliation; the costs to its own economy, its trade partners and global cooperation far outweigh the limited climate benefit.
