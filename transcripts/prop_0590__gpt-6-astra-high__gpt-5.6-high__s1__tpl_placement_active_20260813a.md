[P1.1] A worker denied a job in Europe suffers the same harm whether the screening system’s provider is headquartered in Paris or California. The safeguards should therefore follow the decision, not the company’s address. Otherwise, incorporation becomes a loophole in protection against consequential automated decisions.
[P1.2] The trigger should be substantial, reasonably foreseeable impacts within the EU from high-risk uses, such as hiring, credit, or access to essential services. That means obligations before deployment, not waiting for somebody to prove an injury afterward. It does not mean regulating every foreign model because a European might encounter its output. The relevant connection is a consequential use affecting people in Europe.
[P1.3] This approach wins first on equal protection. If two lenders assess European borrowers using equally consequential AI systems, both should have to secure appropriate testing, documentation, and human oversight. A headquarters rule makes those safeguards depend on a fact unrelated to the borrower’s vulnerability or the system’s danger. People should not have to investigate a supplier’s corporate structure to know whether protections apply.
[P1.4] It also wins on competition and incentives. When obligations follow headquarters, providers can reduce their regulatory exposure by restructuring or locating abroad while continuing to serve the same European market. Responsible European firms bear costs that foreign competitors can avoid. An impacts rule removes that incentive: competing for the same high-risk European uses means meeting the same baseline.
[P1.5] Enforcement need not depend on chasing every foreign developer through foreign courts. The EU can condition access to its market on compliance, require accountable representatives where appropriate, and place complementary duties on European organizations deploying these systems. Those levers are imperfect, but a headquarters boundary would exempt the very providers enforcement needs to reach. Administrative convenience cannot justify that exemption.
[P1.6] The decisive principle is straightforward: when an AI system helps determine people’s opportunities in Europe, European protection should not stop at the provider’s registered office.
