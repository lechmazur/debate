[P1.1] Europe is not selling a product it owns alone. When Malta or Cyprus sells a passport, it sells the right to live, work and move across the entire European Union. When Portugal or Greece sells residency, it sells a key to the Schengen area. One government pockets the fee; twenty-six others inherit the risk.
[P1.2] That lopsided bargain is the heart of this debate, and it is why these programs should end.
[P1.3] First, security and crime. These schemes attract the people who most need a clean second identity: sanctioned oligarchs, corrupt officials, tax evaders fleeing scrutiny at home. This is not a hypothetical. Undercover journalists showed Cypriot officials apparently willing to help a fictional convicted criminal buy a passport, and Cyprus shut its program amid the scandal.
[P1.4] After Russia invaded Ukraine, European governments scrambled to discover how many Kremlin-linked figures had already bought their way in. Due diligence cannot fix a model whose revenue depends on saying yes to rich applicants with complicated pasts.
[P1.5] Second, the economic promise is hollow. Golden-visa money flows overwhelmingly into property, not factories or startups. In Lisbon, Athens and Barcelona, investment thresholds turned apartments into visa tokens, pushing up prices for residents who earn local wages. That is why Portugal removed its real-estate route, Spain moved to scrap its scheme, and Ireland and the United Kingdom closed theirs.
[P1.6] Countries that ran these programs longest are the ones walking away.
[P1.7] Third, citizenship is not a commodity. Europe tells refugees, workers and spouses they must prove residence, language and genuine ties for years. Selling the same status to anyone with a large enough cheque tells ordinary migrants that belonging is earned only if you are poor. The European Commission has pursued Malta precisely because sold citizenship lacks a genuine link to the country, and the EU's highest court has sided against Malta's scheme.
[P1.8] Nothing here requires Europe to reject wealthy foreigners. Investors can still come through entrepreneur visas, skilled-worker routes and ordinary business investment. What must end is the sale of rights themselves. The opposition must defend why [clipped]

[C1.2] Start with what these programs actually deliver. Malta and Cyprus are small economies with narrow tax bases. Investment migration brought in capital that, during the eurozone crisis, Greece otherwise could not attract — hundreds of millions in property and business investment when foreign money had fled. Telling small states to forgo that revenue in the name of abstract principle is easy from a large economy's chair.
[C1.3] The alternative to selling access is not virtue; it is austerity, debt, or selling something else.
[C1.4] Second, the security argument proves too much. Journalists caught Cypriot officials misbehaving — and Cyprus shut the program down. That is accountability working, not a reason to abolish every scheme. Banks get caught laundering money; we regulate them, we do not abolish banking.
[C1.5] Tighten due diligence, publish applicant statistics, add EU-level vetting. Those are reforms. "Bad actors exist, therefore ban the sector" is not an argument anyone applies consistently.
[C1.6] Third, the housing complaint is real but misattributed. Lisbon and Athens had underbuilt housing for decades; golden visas were a accelerant, not the fire — which is why Portugal kept its program while merely removing the real-estate route. That is precisely the surgical fix my opponent pretends is impossible.
[C1.7] Fourth, capital is mobile. End these programs and the applicants do not repent; they buy St. Kitts passports or Dubai residency. Europe keeps leverage over who enters only while it is at the table. Abolition exports the transaction to jurisdictions with far weaker scrutiny.
[C1.8] Finally, states already grant status for non-merit reasons — ancestry, birthright, fast-tracks for athletes. Reforming investment migration into a well-regulated revenue tool is consistent with sovereignty. Ending it is a gesture that costs small states dearly and fixes nothing.
