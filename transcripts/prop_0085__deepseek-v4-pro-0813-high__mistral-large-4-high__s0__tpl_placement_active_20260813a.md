[P1.1] A school that leaves smartphone rules to individual teachers is not offering flexibility; it is offloading a systemic problem onto the people least able to enforce it alone. A student can leave third period, where phones are banned, and enter fourth period, where they are allowed “for emergencies,” and the school has quietly taught that rules are only personal preferences. That is a losing model.
[P1.2] The default should be no smartphones during the school day, with narrow, school-approved exceptions. This is not anti-technology. It is a recognition that the central school activity is learning and face-to-face interaction, not checking a personal device. Even a phone on a desk, face down, imposes a cognitive tax: students monitor whether it lights up, who texted, whether they can steal a glance.
[P1.3] That cost does not disappear just because a teacher chooses to ignore it.
[P1.4] Teacher-by-teacher rules are also impossible to enforce consistently. Students compare notes: “Mr. X allows phones,” “Mrs. Y doesn’t care.” Teachers then become the bad guys rather than educators because they must negotiate, confiscate, or justify their own policy every single day. Parents complain that the same behavior draws different consequences in different rooms. A school-wide default removes that dispute and gives every teacher the same institutional backing.
[P1.5] Worse, much of the harm happens where individual classroom rules cannot reach: hallways, lunch, bathrooms, study periods, and the moments between classes. Those are exactly the spaces where phones fuel social exclusion, secret recording, and comparison. Only a building-wide default creates a consistent social environment.
[P1.6] A default ban can still include controlled, school-owned devices or specific teacher-approved activities. But the baseline must be clear: personal phones away from arrival to dismissal. Otherwise, the school has no real policy—just a collection of thirty different ones.
