[P1.1] Even with perfect reliability, legalizing embryo screening for non-disease traits crosses a fundamental ethical line: it reduces future human beings to consumer products tailored to parental preference, with cascading harms to individuals and society that no technological advancement can fix. First, it violates the reproductive autonomy of the future child. A child selected for height, appearance, or predicted cognition never consented to being engineered for traits that will define the expectations placed on them for life. A boy selected for 6’5” height may face crushing pressure to play professional sports, while a child selected for “high cognitive potential” may be denied support if they struggle academically, because their parents paid for a “guaranteed” trait.
[P1.2] The right to an open future—to define one’s own path without being bound by purchased genetic expectations—is erased before a child is even born. Second, it would create a permanent genetic caste system. Even reliable screening will be expensive at first, accessible only to the wealthy. Over generations, this would produce a hereditary gap in physical appearance, height, and cognitive traits between the rich and the poor, locking in inequality in ways that no social program can undo.
[P1.3] We already see how parental investment in education and nutrition creates opportunity gaps; this would bake those gaps into our DNA. Third, it enshrines discriminatory social norms into the human genome. Screening for “predicted cognition” would almost certainly screen out neurodivergent traits like autism or ADHD, framing neurological difference as a defect to be eliminated. Appearance screening would reinforce Eurocentric beauty standards, while height preferences would perpetuate gendered biases.
[P1.4] Legalizing this practice does not just reflect existing prejudice—it writes it into the genetic code of future generations. These harms are inherent to the practice, not flaws in the technology. Even with perfect reliability, the line between treating disease and engineering consumer humans must be drawn, and legal restriction is the only way to uphold that line.

[C2.6] PRO’s position would ban the embryo choice while leaving the expectation untouched—or, worse, make the child’s very existence a legal controversy.
[C2.7] Finally, “no born people for anti-discrimination law to protect” is rhetorical overreach. Regulated systems can prohibit selection for protected characteristics such as sex or skin color without banning every non-disease trait.
