[P1.1] Strict merger enforcement—blocking more horizontal and vertical deals—would lower consumer prices and raise innovation because it preserves competitive pressure that disciplined markets deliver and monopoly power destroys. When rivals stay independent, they fight on price and product, forcing efficiencies that actually reach customers rather than padding margins or creating barriers.
[P1.2] Horizontal mergers shrink the number of choices. With fewer sellers, residual demand becomes less elastic, markups rise, and list prices climb. Consumers pay more for the same goods, whether airfares after airline consolidations or everyday items when regional grocery chains combine. Blocking those deals keeps the rivalry that keeps prices down.
[P1.3] Vertical mergers look different but can do the same harm. An upstream supplier that buys a key retailer can starve rivals of inputs or shelf space, raising their costs and, ultimately, retail prices. Enforcement that blocks foreclosure preserves open supply chains and the lower prices competition produces.
[P1.4] Innovation thrives under rivalry, not concentration. Firms invent and improve when they fear losing customers tomorrow; dominant firms often slow the pace of invention to protect existing rents. Break-ups and blocked mergers have repeatedly unleashed follow-on product development. Scale economies matter, yet they are overstated.
[P1.5] Many productivity gains come from managerial skill, process learning, or arm’s-length contracts—none of which require merger. Organic growth and joint ventures can capture size without extinguishing competitive incentives.
[P1.6] The net balance is clear: the price reductions and innovation surge from sustained competition outweigh any modest efficiency losses. Productivity and scale can be achieved competitively; lower prices and faster innovation cannot be achieved monopolistically. Strict enforcement therefore delivers higher consumer welfare.

[C1.1] The motion asks you to believe that blocking more mergers helps consumers more than the efficiencies it destroys. That inverts how prices actually fall. Prices fall when firms cut costs—larger plants, shared logistics, pooled R&D—and rivalry forces those savings into lower prices. Mergers are one of the fastest routes to those savings.
[C1.2] Block them as a class and you often keep two subscale firms with higher costs, which means higher prices, not lower.
[C1.3] Vertical deals make this vivid. When a manufacturer buys a distributor, the merged firm stops stacking markup on markup—eliminating double marginalization—and retail prices face mechanical downward pressure. The foreclosure story PRO tells is theoretically possible but empirically rare. Treating it as the default blocks the common, pro-consumer case to prevent the exception.
[C1.4] Innovation is more scale-hungry still. A new drug costs billions to develop; a chip fab tens of billions. Small firms invent, but the path from lab to market runs through large balance sheets—and through acquisition. The venture-capital ecosystem funds startups on the expectation of an exit.
[C1.5] Tell founders that selling to Google or Pfizer is presumptively illegal and you won't get more independent innovators; you'll get fewer startups funded in the first place.
[C1.6] Note also which deals stricter enforcement actually blocks: the ones that already cleared current scrutiny—deals agencies found likely efficient. PRO assumes those marginal deals are mostly harmful without offering a reason. And the error costs are asymmetric: a harmful merger can be undone by entry or disruption, but the efficiencies of a blocked merger are gone forever.
[C1.7] Wireless shows the real world. T-Mobile and Sprint combined; instead of the predicted price hikes, the merged firm used its scale to roll out 5G and undercut the incumbents, and real wireless prices fell. Three strong rivals outcompeted four weak ones. Scale won—and so did consumers.

[P2.1] The CON case treats merger efficiencies as automatic consumer gains and competitive losses as theoretical edge cases. That reverses the mechanism. Cost cuts only lower prices when residual rivalry forces pass-through. Merge rivals and the incentive flips: savings pad margins or fund exclusion rather than discounts.
[P2.2] Blocking horizontal deals therefore does not freeze “subscale” firms; it preserves the pressure that turns productivity into lower prices instead of higher markups.
[P2.3] Vertical double-marginalization is solvable by contract, not ownership. Arms-length supply agreements and most-favored terms routinely eliminate stacked markups without handing the upstream firm the power to raise rivals’ costs. The foreclosure risk is not exotic; once control of key inputs or distribution is concentrated, that option becomes profitable. Treating rare-but-severe foreclosure as the exception therefore underweights the default harm.
[P2.4] On innovation, scale for fabs or trials can be achieved through joint ventures, licensing, or organic growth. Acquisition is not the sole exit channel for startups; public markets and private funds remain open. What disappears under lax enforcement is the competitive threat that forces incumbents to commercialize rather than bury rival technologies. Blocking the deals that already clear weak scrutiny simply withholds the presumption that agencies have already screened out monopoly effects.
[P2.5] The wireless episode does not rewrite the comparison. Price movements after T-Mobile/Sprint reflect multiple concurrent factors; they do not prove that three rivals permanently outperform four, nor that further concentration would continue the gains. Entry can eventually discipline a bad merger, yet entry is slow and incomplete in concentrated industries, while the lost rivalry from a blocked efficient merger can still be replaced by independent expansion. The permanent damage therefore runs the other way: lost competition permanently raises prices and slows invention more than foregone scale reduces productivity.
[P2.6] Strict enforcement restores the net consumer surplus.
