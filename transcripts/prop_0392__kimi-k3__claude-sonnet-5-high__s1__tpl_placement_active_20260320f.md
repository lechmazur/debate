[P1.1] Accessibility online is not a luxury feature. When a blind customer cannot navigate her bank's website with a screen reader, or a deaf user faces a public information video with no captions, they are locked out of daily life as surely as if the building had no ramp. [P1.2] First, goodwill has been tried and has failed. Accessibility guidelines have existed for over twenty-five years; laws in many countries already require accessible services. When standards are clear, fixes cheap, and compliance still rare, the problem is not knowledge.
[P1.3] It is incentives. Accessibility gets cut first because nothing happens when you cut it.
[P1.4] Second, fines fix that incentive. Firms weigh compliance cost against non-compliance cost; today the second figure is near zero. [P1.5] Third, notice how modest the motion is. It targets only repeated failures of basic standards. Nobody is fined for an honest first mistake: a sensible regime warns first, allows time to fix, then escalates to proportionate, revenue-scaled penalties. [P1.6] CON will call this blunt and burdensome. But the burden falls on the repeat offender who was warned; the status quo's burden falls on disabled users every day. [C1.7] PRO frames this as ramps versus no ramps. But a fine doesn't build a ramp. It just makes the building's owner poorer, and sometimes it makes them board the door shut entirely.
