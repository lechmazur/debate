[P1.1] Rewilding abandoned farmland wins because it buys a process, not a product. Active reforestation buys seedlings: nursery stock, crews, site preparation, guards, mowing or herbicide, watering, replanting, monitoring. Rewilding buys the cheap conditions for recovery—stop cultivation and grazing, prevent recultivation, reconnect edges to hedgerows and woods, plug damaged drains, and control invasives only when necessary. On marginal land in Europe and North America, most work is then done by seed rain, surviving roots, soil fungi, birds, insects, mammals, and natural thinning.
[P1.2] Those inputs send no invoices.
[P1.3] Carbon follows that cost curve. A planted hectare may look faster, but nursery emissions and soil disturbance cut net gains, while drought losses, browsing, and replanting drain the budget. Abandoned fields typically have low land value and nearby seed sources; a dollar spent protecting many regenerating hectares—shrubs, wet margins, litter, roots, rebuilding soil—will usually sequester more additional carbon than a dollar spent forcing one hectare into planting rows. Ending fertiliser and tillage, accumulating soil organic matter, leaving deadwood, and renewed peat formation on rewetted ground all store carbon.
[P1.4] The planting program sells a visible stem count; rewilding delivers total sequestered carbon per dollar.
[P1.5] Biodiversity makes the comparison sharper. A retired New England pasture moving through goldenrod, hawthorn, young woods, and gaps feeds pollinators, birds, fungi, and browsers at successive stages. A retired European cereal field with fallow strips, scrub, hedges, and wet hollows creates a mosaic, not a tree crop. Natural regeneration sorts species by moisture, shade, soil, and disturbance, producing uneven ages and local genotypes; a planted block starts as rows chosen by a procurement list.
[P1.6] Most active programs buy control, and control is expensive and biologically narrow: closed canopies shade field flowers, deadwood and edges arrive late, and trees on dry or naturally open habitat can displace species needing recovery. The claim that planting is faster confuses appearance with survival and scale. For the same dollar, biodiversity restored through succession beats seedlings.
