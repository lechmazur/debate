[P1.1] Shipping a crate across an ocean is logistics, not restitution. Colonial powers did not merely relocate physical objects; they severed communities from their histories, concentrated museum wealth in imperial capitals, and physically altered the artifacts themselves. Treating a customs receipt as the finish line mistakes mere possession for repair, forcing formerly colonized nations to pay the bill for damage they did not cause.
[P1.2] First, colonial custody created conservation and research liabilities that origin countries should not subsidize. For generations, Western museums routinely treated organic masks, textiles, and regalia with toxic pesticides like arsenic and mercury, alongside synthetic coatings. Many returned objects are now hazardous to handle or vulnerable to climate shock without costly remediation. Simultaneously, violent looting stripped items of context, burying their identities inside fragmented European military logs and ledgers.
[P1.3] Shipping back chemically contaminated, anonymized objects without funding conservation and provenance research dumps an expensive puzzle onto receiving nations. Corrective justice requires repairing harm done to an object and its record, not relinquishing altered property and walking away.
[P1.4] Second, funding museum capacity remedies institutional extraction without compromising sovereignty. For decades, imperial museums refused repatriation by claiming origin countries lacked climate-controlled vaults, cynically using colonial economic disparities to justify keeping loot. Abruptly offloading fragile collections onto tight domestic budgets, where heritage competes with hospitals and schools, sets receiving museums up for crisis. Yet supporting capacity never requires Western control.
[P1.5] Through unconditional grants, locally directed training, and funded archival access, former colonial powers can underwrite infrastructure while leaving every curatorial and spiritual decision in local hands.
[P1.6] True restitution restores cultural life, not merely legal title. An artifact locked in an underfunded storeroom or stripped of its name remains estranged from its people. Until former colonial powers fund the research, conservation, and capacity required for meaningful reintegration, shipping crates home does not complete justice; it simply outsources liability.
