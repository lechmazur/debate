[P1.1] When a handful of objections can block cheap power for hundreds of thousands, the system is broken. Britain and Europe still ration their cheapest energy source — onshore wind — and strangle the wires needed to move it, in deference to intense local opposition.

The fair answer is not a ban or de facto ban, but streamlined permission with proper consultation and direct local benefits, not a veto.

Planning objections disproportionately come from older, wealthier homeowners with time to organize, while renters, young families crushed by energy bills, and future generations never get a hearing. Treating that file of complaints as the true cost ignores the silent majority — who consistently tell pollsters they support onshore wind, even nearby — and the millions paying the price of blocked turbines.
[P2.2] The portrait of ruined lives beside turbines is overwrought. Setback rules already limit noise, and flicker and health scares have not survived scrutiny where projects operate under normal regulation; what remains is mainly a change of view. That matters, but weigh it against [C1.2]'s forgotten neighbours: the family in fuel poverty after gas prices spiked, the town breathing gas-plant pollution, the coastal village flooding. Those harms are concentrated too — they are simply invisible in a local planning inquiry.
[P2.3] Nor does steamrolling consent accelerate backlash; indulging vetoes does. When one county blocks turbines or pylons, its neighbours learn obstruction pays, and developers face death by a thousand inquiries. Far more politically explosive than a new pylon is another winter of unaffordable bills. Populists thrive not when infrastructure is visible but when governments promise cheap, secure power and fail to deliver.
[P2.4] And the supposed escape routes do not escape. Offshore wind is vital but substantially pricier and slower to connect, rooftop solar cannot heat homes through a dark northern winter or run steelworks, and efficiency, however desirable, does not generate a watt. You cannot be pro-electrification and anti-pylon.
[P2.5] Relaxing restrictions does not mean silencing communities; it means consulting them without granting a veto, and paying direct local benefits instead of surrendering national energy security to whoever shouts loudest.

Those costs are concentrated on named households, often where housing is cheaper and political access weaker, while the power and green credit travel to distant cities.
[C2.2] A benefiting majority is not a consenting community.

[C2.4] The backlash argument misses the deeper danger: procedural illegitimacy. [P2.3] says obstruction breeds resentment, but if governments announce that strong opposition will be relaxed away, every project becomes a mobilization target: opponents know immovability is their only leverage, while supporters have no reason to organize. [P2.5] gives the game away with consultation “without a veto”—if the outcome is predetermined, consultation is theatre and the veto has already been seized.
[C2.5] Finally, [P2.4] proves too much. Every electron needs wires, but not every wire must cross the same contested land. Grids can use existing corridors, be buried where opposition is strongest, follow transport routes, and pair with offshore wind, solar, storage and demand flexibility to shrink the onshore footprint. The choice is not “override the village or freeze”; it is whether to site the transition with the people who must host it.
[C2.6] A transition that treats strong local opposition as an obstacle is faster on paper and slower in reality, because it manufactures the resistance it claims to avoid.
