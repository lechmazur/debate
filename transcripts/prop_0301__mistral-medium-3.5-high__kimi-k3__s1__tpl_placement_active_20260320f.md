[P1.1] Public money must serve the public. When governments fund religious schools, they must demand standards that protect all citizens and the cohesion of society itself. Strong civic and curricular benchmarks—teaching accurate history, scientific consensus, and democratic values—ensure a shared foundation that unites diverse communities rather than allowing them to fracture along lines of belief. Without these standards, taxpayers could involuntarily fund schools that teach some children they are superior to others, or that democratic norms are optional.
[P1.2] This would erode the social trust that makes pluralism possible.
[P1.3] Non-discrimination is non-negotiable. Public funds must not subsidize exclusion based on faith, gender, or sexuality. A state-funded school expelling LGBTQ+ students or refusing to hire teachers of other faiths makes the state complicit in discrimination. Teaching creationism as science at public expense betrays both education and the scientific consensus that taxpayers support.
[P1.4] These standards respect religious freedom; they enable faith to thrive in a pluralistic society. Many religious schools already meet or exceed public benchmarks while maintaining their ethos. The condition is minimal and fair: if you accept public money, you must serve the public. This prevents a two-tier system where public funds support private prejudice or inadequate education.
[P1.5] The alternative is stark: either one standard for all publicly funded education, or public resources used for private division and miseducation. The proposition draws a necessary line. Public funding is a privilege that carries public obligations. This is not hostility to religion but the only way to ensure religious schools, when publicly funded, serve the common good.
[P1.6] This balance protects both faith communities and the society that sustains them.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like moderation: fund, but with safeguards. Read closely, it is something else — an offer to fund religious schools on the condition that they stop being religious.
[C1.2] The key phrase is "comparable to public schools." A religious school is not a public school with a prayer bolted on; its faith shapes hiring, curriculum, and character formation. Require it to hire teachers regardless of belief and treat its own doctrine as one opinion among many, and you have not regulated the school — you have replaced it. Orthodox Jewish, Catholic, and Muslim schools exist to transmit a tradition.
[C1.3] "Funding, provided you abandon transmission" is a polite refusal, not a compromise.
[C1.4] And who writes these standards? "Strong civic benchmarks" and "democratic values" are not plumbing codes; they are contested political content drafted by secular majorities. The proposition fears schools teaching children they are "superior to others" — then hands the state power to decide which theologies are acceptable. That is the majoritarian domination pluralism exists to prevent: minority faiths judged, and defunded, by the majority's definition of acceptable belief.
[C1.5] The complicity argument proves too much. Governments fund religious hospitals, charities, and universities through student grants without demanding secularization. School funding likewise follows the child: a family directing its allotment to a school sharing its faith no more makes the state complicit than a civil servant's tithe. Excluding religious families alone is the real discrimination — and it entrenches the true two-tier system, where the affluent buy the schooling they want and the poor take what the state prescribes.
[C1.6] The fear of division is backwards. Religious schools often serve immigrant and low-income communities, outperforming local public schools; their graduates vote, volunteer, and work beside everyone else. Societies fracture when communities learn their deepest convictions disqualify them from public partnership.
[C1.7] Fund literacy, numeracy, safety, and genuine academic outcomes. But conditions requiring religious schools to become public schools are not standards; they are a cancellation with paperwork.

[P2.1] CON claims the proposition forces religious schools to "stop being religious," but this is a false and damaging straw man. Non-discrimination standards do not require religious schools to abandon their faith identity; they merely prevent public funds from being used to exclude students or staff based on that identity. In many democracies, publicly funded religious schools successfully teach standard, fact-based curricula in science and history while maintaining their distinctive ethos and hiring both co-religionists and others. The state is not demanding secularization; it is ensuring that taxpayer money does not underwrite discrimination.
[P2.2] CON’s fear of majoritarian standards is fundamentally misplaced. The civic and curricular benchmarks in question are not arbitrary impositions, but the same standards that already govern all public schools in any functioning democracy. These are developed through transparent, inclusive processes with input from diverse communities, including religious ones. We are not creating special tests to target faith; we are applying the universally accepted educational standards that taxpayers fund.
[P2.3] Any school—religious or secular—that teaches children some citizens are inherently inferior, or that well-established scientific facts are mere opinions, fails the basic test of public education.
[P2.4] The attempt to equate schools with hospitals reveals a category error. Schools are uniquely charged with socializing the next generation of citizens and preparing them for shared civic life. Public funding for education therefore carries obligations that do not apply to healthcare. The alternative CON implicitly defends—public money flowing to schools that exclude LGBTQ+ youth or reject scientific consensus—is not a recipe for social cohesion, but for the very division they claim to avoid.
[P2.5] Without these standards, we legitimize a two-tier educational system: one set of publicly funded schools open to all, and another set that can reject students based on identity while still drawing on public funds. Societies fracture not when inclusive standards are applied, but when public resources are used to teach some children that others are less than equal. That is not pluralism; it is the exploitation of collective resources for divisive ends.
