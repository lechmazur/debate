[P1.1] Attritable systems are not a downgrade; they restore mass, adaptability, and credible escalation options to a force optimized for a world that no longer exists. The United States should shift a significant share of procurement toward cheap, expendable, and recoverable platforms even at some cost to exquisite programs.
[P1.2] The core problem is arithmetic. Exquisite systems are too few and too expensive to absorb the losses modern missile warfare creates. A carrier air wing or penetrating bomber concentrates billions of dollars in one target; a single hit can erase decades of capability. Dozens or hundreds of low-cost strike drones, decoys, and uncrewed vessels can be dispersed, launched from many platforms, and lost without crippling the campaign.
[P1.3] The adversary faces saturation: every interceptor spent on a cheap drone is unavailable for a crewed fighter or missile.
[P1.4] This is not theoretical. Cheap drones have repeatedly destroyed expensive armor and radars; small drone boats and cruise missiles have forced naval task groups to spend scarce interceptors against threats costing a fraction of the munition used to stop them. The lesson is not that quality is worthless; it is that quality without mass is brittle.
[P1.5] A procurement shift also changes production speed. Exquisite platforms often take decades to build and depend on narrow supply chains. Attritable systems can be produced in competition, iterated rapidly, and rebuilt as tactics change. In a long war, replacing losses fast matters more than any single exquisite capability.
[P1.6] Finally, deterrence strengthens when leaders have usable options. A president may hesitate to risk a carrier or a hundred-million-dollar aircraft; he is far more willing to use expendable swarms to impose costs and hold targets at risk. That lowers the threshold for credible resistance without lowering the threshold for catastrophe.
[P1.7] None of this requires eliminating exquisite platforms. It requires ending their immunity from budget competition and buying mass, replaceability, and the credible threat of denial.
