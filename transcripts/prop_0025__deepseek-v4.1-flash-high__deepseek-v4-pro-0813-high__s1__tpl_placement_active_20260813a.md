[P1.1] The right to disconnect begins with a simple question: when an employer sends a message at 10 p.m., who bears the cost? Today, usually the worker. The employer gets an answer; the worker loses sleep, dinner, or a child’s bedtime. That is not flexibility.
[P1.2] It is an invisible subsidy paid in human health.
[P1.3] The harms are concrete. Constant availability fragments sleep, keeps stress hormones elevated, and makes rest feel provisional. Remote work erased the commute, then erased the boundary. A ping is not harmless when ignoring it can affect promotion.
[P1.4] Workers cannot solve this individually because the person who first logs off becomes the “not committed” one. A legal right changes the default: unless a role has a genuine, defined exception, after-hours communication waits.
[P1.5] This is not a ban on ever contacting employees. “Most employees” plus sector-specific exceptions is the design. Emergency responders, hospital staff on call, utilities crews, and incident engineers may need immediate contact. But then the law can require notice, compensation, rest periods, or rotation. Exceptions are where the burden shifts back to the employer instead of being dumped on the worker.
[P1.6] The opponent will call this anti-competitive. But the competitive advantage built on exhausted people is fragile and fake. Burnout drives turnover, absenteeism, mistakes, and quiet quitting. Reciprocal flexibility—if Saturday work is truly needed, it is paid or traded—produces more sustainable effort.
[P1.7] Countries that have moved toward disconnect protections have not stopped functioning; they have forced firms to manage time better.
[P1.8] The decisive comparison is between a default of total availability and a default of protected rest with negotiated exceptions. One treats every hour as the employer’s option. The other treats rest as the baseline and asks employers to justify intrusion. For a healthy workforce, that is the only defensible default.

[C1.1] The right-to-disconnect proposal mistakes the symptom for the disease. The harm is not the 10 p.m. ping; it is unreasonable workload, unclear expectations, and weak management. A law that discourages after-hours communication does not fix those things. It compresses the same workload into fewer official hours, pushes contact into unofficial channels, and penalizes the flexible arrangements many workers actually need.
[C1.2] The problem begins with treating workers as one uniform class. Some parents want to leave early and answer messages after bedtime. Some employees do their best work late. Global teams must coordinate across time zones.
[C1.3] A “most employees” default with exceptions for emergency responders, on-call hospital staff, and utilities crews sounds clean, but real boundaries are not. Is a product launch an incident? Is a cross-border closing an emergency? Is a lawyer near a filing deadline exempt?
[C1.4] Each ambiguous case produces legal risk, paperwork, and managers who quietly shift contact to unofficial channels to avoid liability.
[C1.5] This mandate also undercuts the better solution: negotiated flexibility. In a tight labor market or a well-run firm, workers can already demand clear expectations, comp time, or leave. Competitive employers already advertise “no after-hours email” because talent wants it. A legal default replaces those context-specific bargains with a one-size rule, making it harder to trade a Tuesday evening call for a Friday afternoon off.
[C1.6] It also does not reduce the underlying workload, so burnout can simply intensify within official hours.
[C1.7] The claim that countries with such protections have not stopped functioning is a low bar. The real costs are slower responses for nonexempt roles, more formality, less mentoring, and fewer spontaneous opportunities. The decisive comparison is not rest versus availability; it is flexible autonomy versus regulatory uniformity. The latter costs more than the ping.
