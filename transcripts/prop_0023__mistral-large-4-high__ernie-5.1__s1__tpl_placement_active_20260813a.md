[P1.1] The phrase "cryptocurrency exchange" is a marketing label, not a regulatory category. When a platform takes custody of customer funds, matches buy and sell orders, offers leveraged trading, and executes trades on behalf of retail investors, it is functioning as a broker-dealer and an exchange. Calling it something else doesn't change the economic reality or the risks.
[P1.2] The past three years have demonstrated the cost of this misclassification. When FTX collapsed, billions in customer deposits vanished because the platform was not subject to segregation requirements or capital reserves that bind traditional broker-dealers. Celsius and BlockFi offered interest-bearing products that were effectively securities, yet operated without the disclosure and custody protections that would have prevented their implosions. These were not edge cases; they were predictable outcomes of platforms performing banking and brokerage functions while escaping banking and brokerage rules.
[P1.3] Securities regulation exists to solve specific problems: preventing fraud, ensuring best execution, mandating asset segregation, and surveilling markets for manipulation. Crypto platforms face identical problems. Wash trading, front-running customer orders, and undisclosed conflicts of interest are rampant precisely because these venues lack the surveillance and conduct rules that govern registered exchanges.
[P1.4] The industry claims that securities regulation would kill innovation, but this confuses innovation with regulatory arbitrage. Legitimate technological advancement doesn't require fractional reserve banking disguised as "yield products" or the commingling of customer and corporate funds. If these platforms want to serve retail investors, they should meet the same standards that have protected investors for nearly a century. The functional test is clear: custody, order matching, and retail execution demand exchange and broker-dealer oversight, not a bespoke regime built for the convenience of the industry.
