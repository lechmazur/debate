[P1.1] In modern research ethics, scientific value never justifies possessing human bodies without consent. If university researchers dug up a local cemetery tomorrow without family permission, no ethics board would let them keep the skeletons just because the DNA might yield major discoveries. Yet thousands of Indigenous and colonized ancestors remain locked in museum drawers because they were taken through battlefield looting, grave-robbing, and unconsented colonial excavation when conquered peoples were treated as specimens rather than human beings. Delaying return because those remains are scientifically useful preserves that exact double standard.
[P1.2] Institutions cannot launder stolen ancestors into legitimate laboratory property by pointing to the data inside them. For living descendants, every year of retention inflicts an ongoing violation of kinship, spiritual duty, and equal dignity.
[P1.3] Allowing institutions to postpone return whenever researchers claim continued study would be valuable also creates a permanent veto against repatriation. Science never runs out of questions. Yesterday, curators demanded retention for skull measurements; today, researchers cite ancient DNA and isotope analysis; tomorrow, they will invoke technologies not yet invented. If potential discovery justifies holding ancestors back, return becomes an empty promise, because new tools will always make stored bones newly valuable.
[P1.4] Museums and universities have already held many collections for decades, often untouched in storage until a repatriation claim arrives. Prompt return—moving directly to identify and restore ancestors to rightful communities without stalling for another study—is the only way to end institutional foot-dragging.
[P1.5] Finally, prompt repatriation does not outlaw science; it subjects researchers to the universal rule of consent. Once stewardship returns to descendant communities, scientists with compelling questions can still ask permission to collaborate, and communities approached as equals rather than obstacles sometimes say yes. What ends is not inquiry, but coerced inquiry built on historic plunder. Human remains are ancestors, not academic raw material, and no research agenda entitles institutions to hold them hostage.

[C1.2] Repatriation done well is slow, and slow for the right reasons. Before an ancestor can go home, someone must establish provenance: where the remains were taken, from whose territory, under what conditions, and which community holds the cultural authority to receive them. That is not foot-dragging; it is the difference between restoration and a second dispossession. Remains returned "promptly," before provenance work is done, can end up with the wrong nation, reburied in the wrong ground, or trapped in disputes between neighboring communities who each hold a legitimate claim.
[C1.3] A rushed handover does not honor the dead; it launders institutional negligence into a press release.
[C1.4] Second, the consent framing is thinner than it looks. The dead consented neither to excavation nor to reburial. Deciding who speaks for an ancestor 150 years removed is itself a contested question, and some descendant communities actively want study—of migration histories, of epidemic disease, of ancestral continuity that strengthens their own land and repatriation claims. "Prompt return" forecloses the collaborative research those communities request, sacrificing their interests to satisfy the conscience of the institution.
[C1.5] Third, destruction is asymmetrical. Reburial is largely irreversible; most analysis is not. A time-limited, community-supervised study period—sampling negotiated, results shared, remains returned on a fixed schedule—has repeatedly produced knowledge and return. The motion's rule makes that middle path impossible, because the trigger is researchers arguing value, which is precisely when the clock starts.
[C1.6] So the real choice is not science versus dignity. It is a managed, transparent process with community authority at its center, versus a rigid mandate that mistakes speed for justice. Promptness is a virtue in answering claims. As a rule for handling ancestors, it is just carelessness wearing moral costume.
[C1.7] We should reject it.
