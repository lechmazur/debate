[P1.1] The real question isn't whether students will ever touch a device—of course they will. It's whether, in the years when their brains are wiring for sustained attention and face-to-face relationships, we should make a personal screen the spine of the school day.
[P1.2] Early secondary grades sit on top of a developmental window that is genuinely irreplaceable. Adolescents are consolidating the neural architecture for impulse control, for reading a peer's face, for tolerating boredom long enough to think a hard thought through. The classroom is one of the last places where that work still happens without a competing feed. A one-to-one program quietly hands every student a portal out of that room.
[P1.3] Then there is the displacement problem. A 1:1 program doesn't sit beside attention and social development; it consumes the schedule that would have produced them. Lesson design, classroom management, even the choreography of the room bend toward the screen. Every minute spent managing, charging, troubleshooting, or recovering from distraction on a device is a minute not spent on what the proposition names.
[P1.4] The case for "both" is comfortable, but in practice, schools that commit to 1:1 do less of what we claim to value, not more.
[P1.5] The attention cost is not hypothetical. Cognitive load research consistently shows that concurrent digital activity drains working memory from the primary task. In a thirteen-year-old whose attentional control is still being built, that drain is the medium doing what the medium does. Teachers know it.
[P1.6] Watch a class with devices open and a class without them. One is teaching; the other is refereeing.
[P1.7] The opponent will gesture at equity and future jobs. But equity is poorly served by issuing every student a portal to the same distractions wealthier peers already navigate, and digital fluency is achievable in a lab or library without turning every class into a screen.
[P1.8] Prioritize attention and social development first. Teach the rest. The order matters.
