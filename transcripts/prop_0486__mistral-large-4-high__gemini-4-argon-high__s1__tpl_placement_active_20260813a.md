[P1.1] Land-scarce cities face an impossible arithmetic: they must house the living while reserving ever-more acreage for the dead in perpetuity. A single traditional burial permanently consumes a plot of ground roughly the size of a parking space. Multiply that by decades of mortality in a dense metropolis like London, Tokyo, or New York, and the math collapses. Permanent burial plots are a spatial luxury that crowded cities can no longer afford, converting scarce urban land into a static museum of embalmed remains, hardwood caskets, and concrete vaults that outlast civilizations.
[P1.2] Composting and alkaline hydrolysis break this deadlock. Human composting accelerates natural decomposition, returning a body to nutrient-rich soil in roughly thirty to sixty days without embalming chemicals. Alkaline hydrolysis uses water, heat, and alkaline solution to reduce remains to bone fragments and sterile liquid in a few hours. Neither process requires formaldehyde-laden fluids, tropical hardwoods, or burial vaults.
[P1.3] Instead of sealing the dead in non-biodegradable boxes that leach into groundwater, these methods complete the ecological cycle: the deceased become soil that can nourish urban parks, community gardens, or memorial forests.
[P1.4] Public cemeteries exist as public resources. When a city dedicates a plot permanently, it removes that land from housing, green space, and infrastructure forever. That is not stewardship; it is hoarding. Transitioning to composting or alkaline hydrolysis allows cemeteries to function as living landscapes rather than expanding graveyards.
[P1.5] Families can still memorialize through trees, plaques, or dedicated groves, but the land itself remains in circulation for the community. In an era of climate crisis and housing shortage, the question is not whether we can afford to keep allocating permanent plots, but whether we can afford not to transition.
