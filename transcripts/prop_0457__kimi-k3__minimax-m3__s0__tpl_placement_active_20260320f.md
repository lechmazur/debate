[P1.1] The cleanest irony in climate policy today: environmental review has become fossil fuels' best friend. Coal plants and pipelines built decades ago face no permitting gauntlet—they're already there. Every wind farm, solar array, and transmission line that would replace them must survive five, seven, sometimes ten-plus years of studies, hearings, appeals, and litigation. Delay doesn't neutralize harm; it picks a winner, and the winner is the status quo.
[P1.2] First pillar: speed is now the binding constraint on decarbonization. The technology is cheap and ready. Capital is available. What kills projects is process.
[P1.3] Transmission lines—the single most important infrastructure for integrating renewables—routinely take a decade to permit, and many die before construction. Projects sit years in interconnection queues. A wind project delayed five years isn't a neutral outcome; it's five more years of gas and coal generation. Climate math is cumulative, so time lost is carbon permanently added.
[P1.4] Second pillar: local veto structures create a catastrophic externality mismatch. A handful of litigants who dislike a transmission corridor's view can impose costs—higher emissions, dirtier air, worse climate outcomes—on millions of people they'll never meet. This isn't grassroots democracy; it's minority rule with a procedural weapon. And it's increasingly weaponized strategically: offshore wind projects on the U.S.
[P1.5] East Coast and transmission lines across the West have been tied up for years by litigation, some of it bankrolled by interests with no environmental motive at all.
[P1.6] Third pillar: streamlining is not abolition. The proposition preserves environmental review—it consolidates overlapping reviews, sets enforceable deadlines, uses programmatic assessments for repeated project types, and time-limits litigation. Germany permitting a highway faster than a wind turbine, and then reforming precisely that imbalance, shows this is fixable governance, not ideology.
[P1.7] The weighing metric: judge this debate on total environmental outcomes—lives, emissions, ecosystems—not on process purity. A review regime that reliably produces the worst environmental result is not environmental protection. It's environmental theater with a body count.

The motion removes that tool and converts opponents into litigants of a different kind: political opponents who fight projects at the ballot box, in zoning hearings, and in street-level resistance.
