[P1.1] Orbital debris is a classic tragedy of the commons, and the deorbit bond is the cleanest fix we have. When a satellite reaches end of life, the cost of failure — a dead hull left in a crowded orbital shell, generating collision risk for everyone — falls on other operators and on future missions, not on the operator who profited from the launch. That is a textbook externality. Right now, nothing makes the operator bear it.
[P1.2] A bond does.
[P1.3] The mechanism is proven in analogous industries. Mining companies post reclamation bonds before they break ground; if they walk away from a stripped pit, the money restores the land. Oil platforms and nuclear plants face decommissioning obligations for the same reason. Orbit is not some exotic domain where liability design is impossible — it is just the newest commons to be industrialized without making the polluter pay.
[P1.4] Three things the bond does at once. First, it changes behavior at the margin: a firm that must post real money against a clean retirement will actually engineer reliable passivation, remaining-fuel margins, and disposal burns rather than declaring end of life and going silent. Second, it funds remediation when behavior fails — the bond is forfeited precisely when the operator does not perform, so the resources to grapple and deorbit a derelict are already sitting in escrow instead of requiring taxpayers or other operators to fund a salvage mission for someone else's profit.
[P1.5] Third, it prices honesty: it separates serious operators from firms that plan to launch, cash out, and leave the shell above them for someone else to dodge.
[P1.6] Note what this is not. It is not a launch ban, not a licensing hurdle designed to freeze out new entrants. It is a refundable bond tied to verifiable retirement. Operators who deorbit as promised pay nothing but the time-value of escrow.
