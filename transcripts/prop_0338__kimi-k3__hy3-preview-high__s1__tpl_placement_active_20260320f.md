[C1.1] The core categorical error in PRO’s framing is equating companion apps with high-risk social products while ignoring the single defining risk driver of regulated social platforms: peer-to-peer interaction and algorithmic amplification of user-generated content. High-risk social rules exist because services like TikTok or Instagram enable stranger contact with minors, spread harmful user-created content to millions, and leverage social graphs to drive peer pressure. Companion apps have no user-to-user interaction, no viral content distribution, no social graphs. They are one-to-one AI interactions, sharing far more operational DNA with ordinary chatbots than with social platforms.
[C1.2] PRO also overstates the uniqueness of companion app persistence: ordinary educational chatbots increasingly retain long-term memory to track student progress, a feature that carries no social-style risk. Persistence alone does not justify social product regulation.
[C1.3] PRO leans on minor vulnerability to justify overbroad regulation, but ordinary chatbot guardrails already address core risks: bans on sexualized content, limits on data harvesting, and mandatory AI disclosures. Imposing high-risk social rules—which include 24/7 user content moderation teams, viral content impact assessments, and cross-user data portability mandates, all irrelevant to AI-only companion apps with no peer interaction—would impose massive compliance costs that would drive most minor-friendly companion tools out of the market. Requirements like mandatory government ID age verification exclude most minors from helpful tools, while bans on persistent personas eliminate beneficial uses like ESL practice or social anxiety coaching, with no meaningful risk reduction beyond narrow chatbot tweaks.
[C1.4] PRO cites extreme edge cases of harm, but the vast majority of minor-companion interactions are benign. High-risk social regulation targets systemic, scalable harms like nationwide viral challenges. Companion app harms are isolated, individual, and already mitigable under existing chatbot standards. Treating them as high-risk social products is regulatory overkill that punishes useful tools for rare, already-addressable failures.
