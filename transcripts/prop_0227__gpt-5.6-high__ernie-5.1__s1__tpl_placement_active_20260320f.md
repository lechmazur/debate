[P1.1] Ransomware survives because payment converts disruption into revenue. For one hospital or company, paying may look rational; across society, that same choice finances better malware, recruits affiliates, identifies profitable sectors, and proves that the next threat may work. This is a collective-action failure: every victim has an incentive to escape alone, while everyone becomes less safe when they do.
[P1.2] A legal prohibition changes the attacker’s expected return. It lets potential targets credibly say that payment is not merely undesirable but unavailable. Criminal groups then face fewer profitable victims, less reliable monetization, and greater difficulty paying their own networks. The ban should cover direct payments and payments disguised through insurers or intermediaries, backed by mandatory confidential reporting and enforcement focused on deliberate circumvention rather than punishing organizations that promptly seek help.
[P1.3] Crucially, prohibition must be paired with victim support. Governments should fund emergency incident-response teams, secure restoration, temporary service continuity, recovery grants or loans, and stronger backup and resilience programs. That replaces the false choice between enriching criminals and abandoning victims. A hospital unable to access records needs technicians, clean systems, and emergency capacity—not a risky cryptocurrency transfer to criminals who may supply a broken decryptor, retain stolen data, or demand more money.
[P1.4] The hardest objection is the urgent case: what if paying appears necessary to restore essential services? But payment offers neither certainty nor speed, and allowing exceptions teaches attackers exactly which targets to hit hardest. A credible ban makes hospitals, utilities, and local governments less attractive over time, while public support limits immediate harm. Exceptions based on severity would perversely reward criminals for creating maximum danger.
[P1.5] No prohibition eliminates every transaction, but policy need not be perfect to transform incentives. Major economies regulate insurers, financial institutions, exchanges, and corporate conduct; raising the cost and reducing the reliability of payment shrinks the market globally. The status quo socializes danger while privatizing desperate decisions. Prohibition plus support reverses that logic: rescue the victim, deny the criminal, and stop today’s ransom from funding tomorrow’s attack.

Prohibition doesn't reduce incentives for ransomware; it reshapes them into something uglier.
