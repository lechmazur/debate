[P1.1] Ninety-five percent of the world's data travels through undersea fiber-optic cables that lie unprotected on the ocean floor. That single fact should end the debate about where defense money goes. A single severed cable can cut off a nation's financial transactions, disrupt hospital systems, and sever military command from its forward bases—yet these cables are thin, unguarded, and increasingly targeted.
[P1.2] The visible defense procurements we romanticize—tanks, ships, fighter jets—are useless if the network that coordinates them goes dark. The United States and its allies have already seen this future in the Baltic Sea, where repeated cable damage, likely from deliberate interference, exposed how easily critical infrastructure can be severed. Undersea cables are not civilian plumbing; they are strategic chokepoints that modern militaries depend on for intelligence, drone operations, and encrypted communications. Protecting them is defense procurement, just the kind that actually wins conflicts before they start.
[P1.3] Shifting resources from visible hardware to cable protection is not a retreat from strength; it is a rational allocation toward asymmetry. Building one more submarine does little if that submarine cannot receive orders because a cable fifty miles offshore has been cut. By contrast, investing in repair ships, surveillance drones, redundant terrestrial backups, and international legal frameworks to prosecute cable saboteurs yields outsized returns. It deters adversaries by raising the cost of aggression and signals that the nation understands where true vulnerability lies.
[P1.4] The motion asks us to prioritize effectiveness over optics. In an era of gray-zone conflict, the decisive battles will be fought over infrastructure, not over parade-ground hardware. Invisibility is exactly what makes cable security valuable—and exactly why it deserves the funding.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false, self-defeating tradeoff: the idea that we must choose between so-called “visible” defense hardware and the security of undersea cables, when in reality most of the capability that protects cables is exactly the hardware the proposal would defund. The ships, maritime patrol aircraft, anti-submarine warfare suites, and persistent sea-control capabilities dismissed as romanticized parade-ground spectacle are the very assets that detect saboteur vessels, interdict submersibles tampering with cables, and escort repair ships to damage sites during crises.
[C1.2] Cut those procurement budgets to fund extra cable-laying ships or redundant routes, and you do not get more cable security—you get unescorted, unprotected cable assets that adversaries can target with impunity, because you no longer have the fleet to stop them. The Baltic incidents cited in the opening case were not resolved by extra cable armor or backup lines; they were investigated and deterred by exactly the kind of visible naval presence the proposal would cannibalize.
[C1.3] Second, the threat is wildly overstated to justify this misallocation. Existing global cable networks already have massive built-in redundancy: when individual cables are damaged, as dozens are every year by fishing trawlers and anchors, traffic reroutes across alternate paths in seconds, with no measurable impact on military command, financial systems, or civilian connectivity. The rare, localized disruptions that do occur are already addressed by a mature commercial repair ecosystem that does not require raiding core defense procurement budgets. Worst of all, the proposal inverts deterrence.
[C1.4] Adversaries probe cable infrastructure precisely when they believe a nation lacks the naval power to respond. Siphoning funds away from the combat capabilities that make retaliation and interdiction credible does not raise the cost of gray-zone attacks—it lowers it, trading away the stick that makes any cable protection investment meaningful.
