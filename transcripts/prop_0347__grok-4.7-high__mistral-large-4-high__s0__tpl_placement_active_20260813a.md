[P1.1] Customer-facing gig workers do not merely receive feedback. For many, a star average is the lock on the door: fall below an opaque threshold and the app that is their workplace stops opening. When that average is strongly shaped by abuse, bias, or harassment, tying survival to it is not quality control. It is coerced submission.
[P1.2] Workers should be able to refuse those systems without losing platform access.
[P1.3] The right is specific. It is not a license to dodge every metric, nor a ban on removing workers who steal, no-show, or endanger people. It is a refusal aimed at rating systems driven by what no worker should have to absorb: a one-star paired with a racial slur, retaliation for rejecting a sexual advance, punishment for declining an unsafe request, or bias against accent, gender, or disability. And refusal must preserve access.
[P1.4] Otherwise the platform answers "no" by deletion, and the protection is theater.
[P1.5] The distortion is structural. Customers rate anonymously and almost never pay for cruelty. Workers face deactivation lines that demand near-perfection, so one biased night is a threat, not noise. They learn the rational response: do not report the harasser, do not end the ride, do not challenge the unsafe drop-off.
[P1.6] The rating then measures obedience to abuse more than service, and it pushes out the workers bias hits hardest.
[P1.7] Honest accountability does not require this trap. Platforms already hold timestamps, GPS, photos, cancellation reasons, and repeat-customer patterns. They can strip scores tied to documented abuse, send disputed ratings to a person, or keep workers with clean operational records on a non-rating track. What they must not do is offer a choice between the tainted score and unemployment.
[P1.8] A warehouse that fired people because a harasser filled out the form would be indefensible. An app does not change the principle.

[C1.2] The phrase "strongly shaped by abuse, bias, or harassment" is doing impossible work. Every worker who receives a three-star rating will suddenly discover bias. The customer who complained about a late delivery becomes a bigot. The rider who docked a star for a dirty car becomes a harasser.
[C1.3] Without an objective standard, the opt-out becomes a universal dodge, and the rating system collapses not because it was biased, but because workers learned to claim bias whenever accountability arrived.
[C1.4] This is not theoretical. Platform trust depends on signals. When a passenger chooses between two drivers, or a diner between two delivery workers, the rating is the only information that distinguishes a professional from a predator, a careful driver from a reckless one. Strip away ratings—or allow workers to selectively reject them—and customers face a marketplace of indistinguishable strangers.
[C1.5] Safety and quality do not survive that fog.
[C1.6] The proposition also misunderstands what "access" means. A worker who opts out of ratings does not keep platform access; they keep a login to a marketplace where they are invisible. Customers filter by rating. Algorithms prioritize rated workers.
[C1.7] An unrated worker is not protected—they are buried. The "choice" is between a tainted score and economic death by obscurity. That is not a right; it is a trap dressed as an escape hatch.
[C1.8] Platforms already have the tools to fix genuine abuse: dispute queues, pattern detection, removal of ratings with slurs or threats. Those are targeted remedies. The proposition demands a sledgehammer that destroys the accountability infrastructure gig work depends on, all to avoid confronting the hard work of filtering bad data from good.
