[P1.1] Constant monitoring mistakes motion for progress. A developer can spend eight hours moving a cursor while accomplishing nothing, while another cracks a core problem in three quiet hours and moves the company forward. If we evaluate remote and hybrid workers mainly on presence signals—green dots, mouse jiggles, camera-on mandates—we are paying for attendance, not contribution. That is not just unfair; it is strategically backwards.
[P1.2] Outputs are the only metric that scales with knowledge work. The business result of a marketer is a campaign that converts, not hours visible in Slack. The result of a support lead is resolved tickets, not a calendar filled with status meetings. A product manager’s value is a roadmap that prioritizes revenue, not a schedule showing back-to-back video calls.
[P1.3] When organizations judge remote employees by what they produce, they align evaluation with purpose. When they judge by activity, they reward the theater of busyness and punish deep, asynchronous, genuinely effective work.
[P1.4] Monitoring also destroys the autonomy that makes remote work valuable in the first place. The great promise of distributed work is flexibility: people can structure their day, care for family, think hard, and deliver. Constant surveillance converts that flexibility into a digital panopticon, breeding anxiety and gaming. Workers optimize for looking busy—sending messages at midnight, keeping statuses green—rather than solving the problem.
[P1.5] Presence becomes performance, and performance is not productivity.
[P1.6] The CON side will warn about accountability. But accountability is strengthened, not weakened, by clear output standards. If expectations are vague, no amount of keystroke logging fixes the underlying management failure. If expectations are clear, results speak for themselves.
[P1.7] Managers who prefer monitoring often do so because defining outputs is harder than watching inputs; difficulty is not an excuse to manage by proxy.
[P1.8] We should evaluate remote and hybrid workers on the value they create. Anything else measures the wrong thing, manages the wrong behavior, and keeps the wrong people.
