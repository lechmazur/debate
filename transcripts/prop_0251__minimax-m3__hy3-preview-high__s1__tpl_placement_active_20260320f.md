[P1.1] Heat kills workers. Not occasionally, not in freak accidents—predictably, repeatedly, and in numbers that grow every year as summers intensify. And the reason it keeps killing them is that we have treated extreme heat as a matter of employer discretion rather than a labor right. That has to change.
[P1.2] Consider a farmworker in California's Central Valley, a roofer in Phoenix, or a warehouse packer in a building where the cooling has failed. When the heat index crosses 110, they do not get a choice. They get a directive: keep working, or lose the shift. They absorb the risk of organ damage, heat stroke, and long-term kidney disease not because they consented freely, but because their economic position made refusal impossible.
[P1.3] That is precisely the unequal power relationship that labor law exists to correct.
[P1.4] A labor-rights frame places the duty to protect on the employer and the state, not on the individual worker's resilience. It gives workers legal standing to stop work when conditions become dangerous, without surrendering their wages or their jobs. And it produces enforceable, predictable standards—such as Wet Bulb Globe Temperature thresholds—instead of vague "be careful" guidance that dies in a binder.
[P1.5] The voluntary model has already failed. Companies compete on cost, and safety is the first thing trimmed when enforcement is soft. We have watched this exact pattern with other hazards: before mandatory scaffolding and ladder rules, construction deaths were routine. The mechanism is identical here.
[P1.6] Without mandatory stop-work protections, heat is externalized onto the bodies of low-wage workers—disproportionately immigrants and people of color—who lack the bargaining power to insist on breaks, water, or shade. Voluntary guidelines will not change this race to the bottom; they never have for any other hazard, and they will not for heat.
[P1.7] None of this is radical. It is the same legal frame already applied to asbestos, to confined spaces, to machine guarding. The only difference is that heat once felt natural and therefore negotiable. It is not negotiable when the body is breaking down on the clock.
