[P1.1] Modern power runs under water before it runs on parade grounds. Well over nine-tenths of intercontinental internet traffic — bank settlements, cloud services, government communications, and military data relayed through commercial networks — travels not by satellite but through a few hundred undersea fiber-optic cables, often converging at a handful of landing stations and narrow straits. Cut enough of them, and a country is not merely inconvenienced. Its markets stutter, its logistics go blind, and its allies become unreachable at speed.
[P1.2] That vulnerability is no longer theoretical. In recent years cables in the Baltic, around Taiwan's offshore islands, and in the Red Sea have been severed or damaged in murky incidents involving dragged anchors and commercial ships — hard to attribute quickly, cheap to attempt, and ideal for gray-zone coercion below the threshold of war. An adversary does not need to defeat a navy to isolate an island, rattle markets, or slow a crisis response. It only needs plausible deniability and a rough map of routes.
[P1.3] Defense spending that ignores this gets the sequence wrong. A fighter wing, another armored brigade, or a prestige hull has little value if targeting data, satellite tasking relayed onshore, fuel orders, and coalition communications move across networks an adversary can degrade on day one. Visible platforms deter only when the nervous system connecting them survives. At present, that nervous system is often thin, predictable, lightly monitored, and slow to repair, with too few specialized ships and too much traffic forced through the same chokepoints.
[P1.4] The remedy is comparatively cheap and unglamorous: persistent seabed monitoring, better burial and armoring near shore, diverse new routes that avoid single straits, hardened and dispersed landing stations, stockpiles of spare cable, and enough repair vessels to restore links in days rather than weeks. For the price of one major platform, a state can buy resilience for the systems every other platform depends on.
[P1.5] That is the right trade. Fund the connections that make power usable, not only the weapons that display it.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a real vulnerability for a budget priority. Undersea cables matter, but the motion demands much more spending on protection and redundancy even if that strips resources from visible forces. That is backwards.
[C1.2] First, cable resilience is primarily an industrial and regulatory problem, not a front-line combat problem. Most undersea cables are commercially owned, repaired by industry consortia, and governed by licensing, route surveys, and insurance. The sensible state role is targeted: require diversity for critical government traffic, harden a few landing stations, stockpile spare cable, and improve repair coordination. That can be done without cannibalizing the defense budget.
[C1.3] Second, protecting cables depends on the very capabilities the proposition would sacrifice. You do not defend seabed infrastructure with fiber alone. You need patrol ships, maritime aircraft, seabed sensors, mine countermeasures, and the diplomatic credibility that comes from visible presence. If an adversary drags an anchor through a cable in a gray-zone incident, the response is not a spreadsheet of redundancy; it is surveillance, attribution, interception, and the ability to impose costs.
[C1.4] Those functions live in platforms.
[C1.5] Third, the trade is strategically dangerous. A fighter wing or frigate may look less essential than a cable-repair ship, but platforms deter the coercion that makes cable cutting attractive. If you visibly downgrade combat power to harden infrastructure, you may invite the pressure you fear. Deterrence is cheap only while it is credible; once broken, no amount of spare cable restores it.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition overstates the fragility of the system. Networks are meshed, traffic can reroute, satellites provide backup for some critical links, and repair capacity—while imperfect—exists. The risk is real, but it calls for calibrated investment, not a large shift away from forces that secure the cables in the first place.
[C1.7] Vote CON: protect the nervous system, but do not disarm the body.

[P2.3] The claim that cable defense depends on those platforms reverses the problem. No navy can patrol thousands of miles of seabed or prove that an anchor drag was ordered. [P2.4] Buried, monitored, and alternative routes turn a cheap act into an expensive campaign that must find and sever many lines at once. That is deterrence by denial, suited to gray-zone attacks where threats of retaliation lack credibility because attribution is murky and escalation over one cable looks disproportionate.

It means buying the patrol sensors, seabed monitors, and repair vessels that actually serve this mission before buying another duplicative visible platform for deterrence. [P2.6] Finally, rerouting is a cushion, not a solution. Mesh networks reroute only where spare capacity exists, and that capacity vanishes when several cables share a chokepoint or landing area. Satellites can carry priority messages, not the volume of financial, cloud, and military traffic cables carry. And repair capacity built for isolated faults stretches to weeks when damage is multiple, distant, or in contested waters.
[P2.7] One accidental cut is manageable. Deliberate, simultaneous cuts are the planning case — and for that case, calibrated tweaks are the gamble.
