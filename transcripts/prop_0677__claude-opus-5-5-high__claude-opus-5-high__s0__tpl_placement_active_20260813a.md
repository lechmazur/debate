[P1.1] A worker applies for fifty jobs and hears nothing. Another is fired after a productivity score dips below a threshold nobody ever explained. Neither knows whether a person judged them or a model did. That ignorance is the problem this motion fixes, and the fix is modest: tell people when software meaningfully shaped a decision about their livelihood.
[P1.2] First, you cannot challenge what you cannot see. Anti-discrimination law already forbids hiring and firing based on race, sex, age, or disability. But those rights are enforced largely by the people harmed, and algorithmic harm is invisible by default. Amazon reportedly scrapped an internal résumé screener after finding it penalized applications that mentioned women's organizations.
[P1.3] That one was caught internally. How many similar tools were never caught because no applicant knew one existed? Disclosure creates no new rights. It makes existing rights usable.
[P1.4] Second, disclosure disciplines the builders. Vendors now sell screening tools to employers who face little reputational risk, because no one knows the tool is there. Once use must be disclosed, employers start asking vendors hard questions: Was this validated? Was it tested for bias?
[P1.5] Can its outputs be explained? Sunlight changes incentives upstream without regulators designing a single algorithm. Places like New York City and Illinois have already adopted notice rules for automated hiring tools. This is workable, not utopian.
[P1.6] Third, the cost is tiny relative to the stakes. We are not demanding source code, trade secrets, or a ban. We are demanding a sentence: "An automated system meaningfully influenced this decision." Employers already give notice before using background-check reports, and hiring did not collapse.
[P1.7] The "meaningfully influenced" threshold keeps spellcheck and scheduling software out. It targets tools that rank, filter, score, or flag people.
[P1.8] So here is the clash. CON must explain why an employer's convenience in staying silent outweighs a worker's ability to know, contest, and correct a decision about their job. Few decisions matter more in ordinary life. If a machine is in the room, the person on the other side deserves to know.
