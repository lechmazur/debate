[P1.1] A town can ration showers while tanker trucks leave the wellhead full of bottles. That image is the whole case. Municipal sources and aquifers exist to keep people alive where they live. Private corporations that own them, trade them, or pump them for commercial bottling reverse that purpose.
[P1.2] They turn a shared reserve into a product shipped to whoever pays more.
[P1.3] Bottling is not ordinary industrial use. It is bulk removal. Water leaves the basin in plastic and does not return. Aquifers recharge slowly; overpumping drops the water table, dries springs, and can permanently compact the rock that once stored the reserve.
[P1.4] A firm maximizing bottle volume has every reason to treat that damage as someone else's problem. Shareholders do not live with the empty well. Residents do.
[P1.5] Trading makes extraction portable. Once a municipal source or aquifer becomes a tradable right, the highest bidder is rarely the neighborhood that needs the hydrant, the clinic, and the kitchen tap. Ownership then licenses withdrawal for profit, not stewardship for the public. Municipal systems were built with public money and public risk.
[P1.6] Selling the source privatizes the surplus and socializes the shortage.
[P1.7] Permits and fees do not fix the incentive. The firm still profits by taking more water out. In dry years the plant can keep running while households face cuts, because the legal claim sits with the corporation, not the residents. Capture is predictable: monitoring lags, exceptions multiply, and the trucks keep rolling.
[P1.8] A ban ends that inversion. It does not outlaw bottled water from other lawful sources. It says civic water and underground reserves are not inventory.
[P1.9] If governments allow this market, they admit that thirst can be an asset class. They should refuse.

[C1.1] A tanker leaving a thirsty town is a vivid image. It is also about one percent of the story. Bottling is a rounding error in most basins — agriculture, thermoelectric cooling, and municipal leakage dwarf it, often by orders of magnitude. A ban on corporate bottling can pass, be celebrated, and leave the water table falling at almost exactly the same rate.
[C1.2] That is the central problem with this motion: it regulates the identity of the owner instead of the volume of the withdrawal.
[C1.3] Judge this on outcomes for the aquifer and the household tap. On that test, the tool that works is a volumetric one: hard extraction caps tied to recharge, metering with public telemetry, drought triggers that cut commercial users first and households last, and pricing that makes the bottler pay for what it takes. Those rules bind everyone — the almond grower, the semiconductor fab, the municipal utility itself — not just the actor with a logo on the truck.
[C1.4] Notice what my opponent concedes: the ban "does not outlaw bottled water from other lawful sources" [P1.8]. That is the escape hatch, and it drains the case. A firm barred from the municipal wellhead drills eight kilometres away into the same aquifer and calls it a private spring. Same water, same drawdown, now outside the permit regime that at least required reporting.
[C1.5] And consider the perverse effect on the most benign practice there is: most mass-market bottled water is purified municipal tap, bought metered at commercial rates from the utility, which subsidizes the network the residents use. Ban that, and you push bottlers off the meter and into raw groundwater.
[C1.6] Then there is the argument from failure. When Flint's pipes poisoned children, when hurricanes take out treatment plants, the pallets arrive from private bottling capacity. Outlawing the industry's access to water does not make it a better neighbour; it makes it absent.
[C1.7] Regulate the withdrawal. Banning the owner is a gesture that lets the pumping continue.
