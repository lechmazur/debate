[P1.1] Training generative AI on copyrighted works without any payment mechanism lets large publishers and studios extract enormous value from the very catalogs they did not create. A compulsory licensing scheme solves this by allowing broad training access while routing revenue back to rights holders through standardized, auditable payments. This structure preserves the incentive to produce new works because creators continue to receive compensation whenever their material fuels commercial AI systems.
[P1.2] Individual permission negotiations cannot scale to the datasets required for competitive models. Each rights holder would hold veto power, fragmenting access and handing decisive advantages to whichever firm already controls the largest libraries. Compulsory licensing removes that bottleneck without eliminating payment. It mirrors the mechanical license that enabled the modern recorded-music industry: anyone may reproduce a composition once statutory royalties are paid, yet songwriters still earn from every copy.
[P1.3] Without the payment condition, the proposition collapses into outright expropriation. Studios could train exclusively on their back catalogs and market new synthetic works that directly compete with the human creators whose material supplied the training data. A compulsory scheme prevents that outcome by ensuring revenue flows continue even as production methods change. It therefore aligns the interests of established rightsholders with technological progress instead of forcing an artificial choice between the two.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to bless a specific trade: strip creators of the right to say no, so long as a check clears afterward. That trade is the wrong one, and it fails on its own terms.
[C1.2] Start with what copyright actually protects. It is not merely a revenue stream; it is a right to control how a work is used. An illustrator whose portfolio trains a model that then floods the market with art "in the style of" them has not been made whole by a royalty check. The harm is competitive and reputational, not just financial.
[C1.3] A compulsory scheme launders that harm into a line-item cost of doing business, and once the fee is paid, the creator has no further say. PRO's own framing at [P1.3] admits the danger, then hands the exploiter a receipt instead of a remedy.
[C1.4] Look at where PRO's central analogy actually leads. The mechanical license for musical compositions did not spring into being at a fair market rate set by songwriters. Statutory rates were fixed by regulators and lobbying, and for decades they lagged badly behind the value labels extracted, because the party with more resources and more lawyers always wins the rate-setting fight. A compulsory AI-training license would be negotiated the same way: major studios, publishers, and the AI firms with the deepest pockets would shape the statutory rate, while independent writers, musicians, and visual artists, the people with the least leverage, would be forced to accept whatever number emerges.
[C1.5] Compulsory licensing doesn't neutralize the power imbalance PRO worries about in [P1.2]; it constitutionalizes it.
[C1.6] There is also a live alternative PRO ignores: voluntary licensing markets are already forming, with stock-image libraries and some publishers striking direct deals for training data. That market lets creators bargain, exclude bad actors, and price their own risk. A compulsory scheme forecloses exactly that market by making refusal impossible. Permission, not payment, is the right gate.

[P2.1] The core flaw in the opposing case is its assumption that an absolute veto right remains workable once generative training requires millions of works at once. Refusal power simply concentrates into whoever already holds large catalogs or can afford armies of lawyers, leaving most illustrators, songwriters, and authors with no deal at all.
[P2.2] The mechanical-license comparison illustrates the point rather than undermines it. That system replaced individual negotiations with a statutory rate and produced both a vast recorded-music market and ongoing royalty streams that small rights holders still collect today. Rate-setting fights exist, yet the alternative—permission on every track—never scaled. A training license would operate on the same logic: access granted, revenue distributed, without handing every independent creator a veto that only the largest firms can survive.
[P2.3] Voluntary deals now forming are the exception that proves the rule. They cover narrow, high-value subsets of data and routinely exclude the long tail of creators whose work would otherwise be used without compensation. A compulsory scheme does not shut those deals down; it sets a floor beneath them so that no creator is forced into a zero-payment outcome simply because their catalog is too small to matter to an AI firm.
[P2.4] The reputational harm claim collapses for the same structural reason. Once a model is trained, style mimicry occurs regardless of whether permission was granted or refused. The only variable the scheme controls is whether revenue reaches the original creator or disappears into the training firm’s margins. Payment therefore mitigates the competitive injury that a pure veto cannot prevent.

[C2.1] PRO's whole rebuttal rests on a false binary: either every creator individually vetoes every use, or a compulsory scheme strips the veto entirely. That binary is fiction. Collective licensing bodies already aggregate small creators' bargaining power for exactly the situation PRO describes, performance rights organizations pool millions of songwriters into a single negotiating bloc, but membership and terms remain voluntary and creators can still exclude specific uses. That model delivers scale without eliminating consent.
[C2.2] PRO never explains why AI training data couldn't organize the same way, because the honest answer is that it could, and a compulsory scheme is chosen instead precisely because it forecloses refusal.
[C2.3] On the mechanical license, PRO's own example cuts against them. Statutory mechanical rates stayed depressed relative to market value for decades, precisely because they were fixed once, by regulators lobbied hardest by the buyers, and never had to be renegotiated by the sellers. That is not a template for fairness; it is a case study in how compulsory rate-setting freezes in the initial power imbalance. Copying a musical composition is also a narrow, defined act.
[C2.4] Training a model on someone's entire visual style so it can generate unlimited competing output is a different order of use, and treating it as a mechanical royalty problem understates the harm.
[C2.5] On reputational injury, [P2.4] concedes the harm is real and then argues payment "mitigates" what a veto "cannot prevent." That gets the causality backwards. A veto prevents the harm by keeping the work out of the training set in the first place; a compulsory license guarantees the harm occurs and merely taxes it afterward. PRO isn't offering a remedy for style mimicry, they're pricing it, and insisting creators accept the price because refusal has been made illegal.
[C2.6] The independent creators PRO claims to protect are the ones with the least ability to shape the statutory rate and the most reason to want the right to say no.
