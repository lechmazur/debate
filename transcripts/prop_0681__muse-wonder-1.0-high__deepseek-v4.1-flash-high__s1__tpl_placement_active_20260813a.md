[P1.1] The fastest responder is useless if their arrival makes the crisis worse, and that is too often what happens when police are the default answer to a mental health call.
[P1.2] A person in psychosis, suicidal distress, or severe disorientation does not primarily need commands, handcuffs, or a show of authority. They need someone who can slow the scene down, recognize symptoms, and distinguish danger from delusion. Police are trained to establish control and enforce the law; even well-intentioned officers bring uniforms, weapons, and the threat of arrest into a room where fear is already the problem. That mismatch turns treatable episodes into confrontations, injuries, and arrests that solve nothing by morning.
[P1.3] Non-police crisis teams — typically a clinician paired with a medic or peer responder — are built for that different job. They can de-escalate without coercion, check medical needs, take someone to a crisis center instead of jail, and arrange follow-up care or housing referrals. When the responder’s toolkit is care rather than enforcement, the call is more likely to end in stabilization instead of custody, and less likely to repeat next week.
[P1.4] The conceded cost is real: in some neighborhoods, a specialized team may take longer than the nearest patrol car. That is not a reason to keep sending the wrong responder. These teams should work through triage — police still go first when there is a weapon, active violence, or a crime in progress. For nonviolent mental health calls, a modest delay for the right expertise is safer than rapid escalation.
[P1.5] A cardiac patient would trade minutes for a paramedic over a parking officer; a person in crisis deserves the same logic.
[P1.6] Expanding these teams also frees police for violent crime and makes frightened families more willing to call before a crisis turns dangerous. Slower in some places, safer and more effective where it counts — that trade is worth making.

[C1.4] A delayed team that must retreat and wait for police has not de-escalated; it has added a second wait. [C1.5] The real choice is not police versus clinicians. [C1.6] The cardiac analogy [P1.5] cuts against PRO: we do not tell a heart attack patient in a poor neighborhood to accept a slower paramedic because the paramedic is nicer. We send the right help, quickly.
