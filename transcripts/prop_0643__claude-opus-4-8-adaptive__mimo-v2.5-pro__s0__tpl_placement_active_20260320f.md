[P1.1] The core problem this motion solves is simple: the people with the most at stake in a company's long-term survival—its workers—are systematically locked out of the room where its biggest decisions are made. A board governs almost entirely on behalf of shareholders, many of whom hold the stock for months and can exit with a click. Workers cannot click out of their livelihoods. Requiring that a significant share of board seats go to worker-elected representatives corrects that imbalance directly, and I'll defend it on three grounds.
[P1.2] First, better decisions. Workers possess granular, ground-level knowledge that never reaches a board dominated by financiers and outside directors—where safety corners are being cut, where a product line is quietly failing, where short-term cost savings will become long-term liabilities. Germany's long-running codetermination system, where large firms reserve substantial board representation for employees, coexists with one of the world's most competitive export economies. That is not a coincidence; voice produces information.
[P1.3] Second, accountability and stability. Boards left purely to shareholder logic chase quarterly numbers, load up on debt, and treat layoffs as the first lever rather than the last. Worker representatives create internal friction against value-extraction that hollows out the firm. They make reckless short-termism harder to execute quietly.
[P1.4] The result is companies that invest in their workforce and weather downturns without gutting their own capacity.
[P1.5] Third, legitimacy. Corporations wield enormous power over communities and people's daily lives. When the only constituency formally represented is capital, we shouldn't be surprised that gains flow upward while risk flows down. A legal requirement—not a voluntary nicety firms will never adopt on their own—gives workers a structural seat, not a suggestion box.
[P1.6] The burden on CON is heavy. They must argue that the people who build the product, run the floor, and bear the consequences of failure should have no formal say in governance—and that the status quo of unaccountable, short-term shareholder dominance is working well enough to defend. I don't think that case can be made.
