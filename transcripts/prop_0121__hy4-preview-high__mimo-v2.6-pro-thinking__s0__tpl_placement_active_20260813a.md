[P1.1] Picture a 20-year-old on her parent's plan. She is having panic attacks and thinking about suicide. [P1.2] Delay is the rational response to a system that treats confidentiality as a luxury upgrade.
[P1.3] The proposition is not about banning parents from ever being involved. It is about removing automatic structural disclosures—parental notification and insurance billing that reveals the visit—from the path to treatment. Mental-health care depends on candor. Therapy works only if a patient can say the unsayable: suicidal thoughts, abuse, addiction, gender identity.
[P1.4] If scheduling or payment auto-alerts a parent, she minimizes symptoms or never books the appointment. She will not disclose the drinking or abuse driving the panic if the first copy goes to Mom's inbox. Confidentiality is not a preference; it is the delivery mechanism for effective care.
[P1.5] Young adults are legal adults, and many younger teens can already consent to outpatient mental-health care. Insurance is a financing device, not a monitoring license; paying a premium does not purchase access to private thoughts. Safeguards already cover imminent danger: clinicians must act. A default that automatically tells a parent about every visit is overbroad—it applies even when the parent is the source of the distress and no risk exception exists.
[P1.6] Automatic disclosure also harms the most vulnerable. For LGBTQ+ youth or abuse survivors, a revealed visit can trigger ejection from home, conversion pressure, or violence. The motion simply aligns payment with reality: the patient is the young adult, not the policyholder. Stop forcing a choice between being treated and being exposed.
[P1.7] Remove the automatic reveal; keep voluntary and emergency involvement. If we are serious about early intervention, privacy cannot be a feature only the wealthy can afford.
