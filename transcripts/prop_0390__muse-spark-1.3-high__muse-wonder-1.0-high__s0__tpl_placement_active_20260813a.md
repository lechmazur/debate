[P1.1] Online games only work if players believe the match is real and minimally safe. A cheater removed from a PC shooter reappears on a fresh account elsewhere; a player permanently muted for threats and slurs in one network rebuilds an audience in another. [P1.3] That teaches exactly the wrong lesson: misbehave at scale, pay at most locally.
[P1.4] Due-process worries are real but misweighted. No one loses liberty, housing, or livelihood when they lose access to major gaming networks for egregious cheating or abuse. [P1.5] Imperfect enforcement that stops most career cheaters and serial harassers is morally preferable to perfect procedure that stops almost none.
[P1.6] The ask is narrow and proportionate: only serious, evidenced violations — confirmed cheat software, match manipulation, sustained hate, threats, sexual harassment of minors — trigger shared consequences across major ecosystems, with limited appeals and time limits. [C1.1] A ban inside one game is a publisher enforcing its own rules. A ban shared across major ecosystems is something else: industry-wide exile imposed by private rivals with no common standard, no independent judge, and no real way to clear your name. That leap is exactly what the proposition asks us to excuse, and it fails on accuracy, proportionality, and power.
[C1.2] First, errors do not stay small when you network them. Anti-cheat systems flag overlays, hardware quirks, borrowed PCs, and compromised accounts; harassment systems often judge clipped voice, reported brigades, slang, and context they cannot hear. Within one title, a false positive is painful but containable. Export it to Steam-scale, console, and publisher networks and one opaque strike becomes a lifetime mark.
[C1.3] The more serious the consequence, the more process is owed — not less.
[C1.4] Second, there is no shared definition of “serious.” One studio treats trash talk, another treats a single slur in a private lobby, another relies on mass reports from a rival clan as proof of sustained hate. Cheating ranges from aimbots to macros, mods, VPNs, and account sharing. Letting the harshest or sloppiest enforcer set the industry standard means players are judged by rules they never agreed to, in games they were never accused in.
[C1.5] Third, this concentrates unchecked power. Major platforms would trade blocklists, link identities across accounts and devices, and decide who may participate in a huge slice of culture and social life, with competitors incentivized to over-ban rather than risk being labeled soft on abuse. Teenagers, players who share computers, and people whose accounts were stolen would carry the burden, while career cheaters — the supposed target — already use spoofers, fresh hardware, and stolen identities to reappear.
[C1.6] Local bans can be tough, even permanent. Cross-ecosystem bans turn a contestable decision into an unappealable sentence. That is not protection; it is punishment without jurisdiction.
