[P1.1] Transit stations are public investments meant to be used. When a municipality surrounds them with low-density zoning and discretionary hearings, it wastes that investment and guarantees scarcity: a few homeowners can delay or kill apartments, costs rise, and only luxury projects survive the gauntlet. Allowing densification by-right simply means housing that meets clear, pre-set rules can be built without begging for permission. That predictability is the precondition for enough homes to matter.
[P1.2] The shortage is the main driver of displacement, not its cure. In a constrained market, higher-income newcomers do not disappear when we block a building near a station; they outbid existing residents for the older homes nearby, landlords raise rents, and families are pushed to car-dependent fringes with longer commutes and weaker services. New supply near transit interrupts that chain. It absorbs demand where jobs and trains already exist, takes pressure off surrounding neighborhoods, and gives displaced-risk households more vacancies to move into without leaving the area.
[P1.3] Yes, building in a vulnerable neighborhood can raise nearby rents in the short term. But refusing to build protects no tenant. It leaves the neighborhood attractive and undersupplied — exactly the conditions for speculation, teardown of cheap rentals into expensive single-family homes, and rent hikes with no new alternatives.
[P1.4] By contrast, by-right rules can be paired in the zoning code itself with requirements that replacement housing, affordable units, or tenant assistance be provided as a condition of approval-by-rule, without reopening every project to years of negotiation. More homes plus clear standards beats scarcity plus promises.
[P1.5] Finally, this is an access question. Keeping transit neighborhoods low-density reserves publicly funded mobility for those who can already afford to buy in. Densification opens that access to renters, younger workers, seniors downsizing, and people with disabilities who benefit most from transit. The choice is not between change and stability.
[P1.6] It is between planned, abundant change near transit, and unplanned displacement driven by shortage everywhere else.

[C1.1] "Short-term" displacement is an economic euphemism for a permanent crisis. When a working-class family is priced out or evicted near a transit station today, they cannot live inside a theoretical vacancy chain ten years from now [P1.2]. Once uprooted to car-dependent fringes—losing their social networks, jobs, and transit access—they do not come back. Blanket by-right upzoning directly triggers this harm.
[C1.2] Overnight, granting automatic rights to build higher density multiplies underlying land values around stations. That windfall goes straight to land speculators. Suddenly, the most profitable use of an older, low-rent apartment building near a train station is acquisition, eviction, and demolition.
[C1.3] PRO treats every transit zone as a wealthy single-family enclave [P1.1, P1.5]. In reality, transit corridors frequently house a city's vital stock of older, unsubsidized affordable rentals and neighborhood businesses. Because new dense construction is expensive, by-right market development overwhelmingly delivers high-end units first, signaling surrounding landlords to hike rents immediately. Tearing down cheap, older apartments to build costly new ones does not just hurt vulnerable families; it cannibalizes the transit system itself.
[C1.4] It replaces low-income, transit-dependent bus and rail commuters with wealthier newcomers who are far more likely to drive or work remotely, undermining ridership on the very public investment PRO claims to champion.
[C1.5] Giving away density "by-right" surrenders the municipality's primary leverage to prevent this. PRO claims pre-set zoning rules can simply mandate replacement housing or tenant protections [P1.4]. Yet rigid, by-right formulas face a fatal bind across diverse neighborhoods: set affordability mandates weak enough for private developers to build profitably on their own, and you unleash speculative teardowns and local displacement; set them strict enough to truly protect existing tenants, and unsubsidized private construction stalls without public financing. Vulnerable transit corridors need conditional, discretionary planning—where cities negotiate site-specific tenant protections, preserve existing affordable apartments, and demand genuine community benefits before handing developers a massive zoning windfall.

If apartments are banned, investors can still buy a cheap rental, evict, and rebuild an expensive single-family home or hold the land hoping for a future deal. [P2.4] Finally, discretionary leverage is largely leverage to delay, not to protect. A clear rule applied everywhere extracts more housing than heroic bargaining over the few projects that survive.

[P5.1] Vote for the policy that makes displacement carry a price, not the policy that pretends refusing permission makes it stop.
[P5.2] A tenant in an older building near a station is already at risk when supply is scarce: a wealthier renter can outbid her, a landlord can raise the rent, and she leaves with nothing — no interim housing, no replacement unit, no right to return. [P5.3] By-right density changes that balance. It makes the extra value created by upzoning automatically pay for replacement homes and relocation, and if those obligations make a teardown unprofitable, the building stays standing while homes still proceed on parking lots and commercial sites. If a teardown is profitable, it proceeds only with that funded bridge attached. Either way, new supply also gives incoming demand somewhere else to go instead of bidding for her apartment.
[P5.4] That is the decisive difference: scarcity displaces without compensation; by-right requires compensation, replacement, and enough additional homes to reduce the pressure itself. Accepting a managed short-term risk is how you stop the permanent, invisible displacement already happening.
