[P1.1] A public research budget is a claim on trust. Taxpayers are told public science produces reliable knowledge, yet incentives reward novelty and positive results while verification stays an afterthought. Funders should reserve a meaningful fixed share for replication, even if it means fewer novel grants. The choice is not replication versus discovery.
[P1.2] It is discovery backed by verification versus discovery resting on fragile, unverified findings.
[P1.3] Replication is not repetition for its own sake; it shows whether an exciting result is a real signal, a smaller effect, or an artifact of one lab's methods. Most novel findings are early and uncertain. When they are inflated or wrong, the waste is not limited to the original grant. Downstream researchers, firms, and agencies spend years and large sums on a base that later collapses.
[P1.4] A promising target that fails independent validation can still trigger expensive development before the flaw becomes clear. A protected replication budget is cheap insurance against those multiplied losses.
[P1.5] This matters because replication is a public good that individual scientists under-supply. Repeating someone else's study brings little credit, career reward, or excitement, while chasing novelty brings all three. A fixed share fixes that incentive. It funds shared protocols, registries, and independent teams, and cannot be quietly sacrificed when a funder wants visible breakthroughs.
[P1.6] Because it is fixed, it survives short-term budget pressure and the constant pull toward splashy new starts.
[P1.7] CON will say every replication dollar is taken from innovation. But an audit is not a tax on discovery; it is what makes discovery worth funding. Skip verification and you get more headlines but weaker foundations, and public trust suffers when failures surface. The comparison is expected return.
[P1.8] A slightly smaller pipeline with checked entries produces more usable knowledge than a larger one leaking false leads. We accept some displacement of novel projects; the system's overall return rises.

[C1.1] Guaranteeing verification by quota sounds prudent until you ask what is sacrificed and what is actually verified.
[C1.2] Public grant budgets are already starved. When only a fraction of strong proposals can be funded, shaving off a meaningful fixed share does not trim fat; it kills live ideas outright. Entire lines — early-career labs, high-risk hypotheses, urgent work on emerging threats — disappear so a quota can be met. Lost novelty is not deferred, it is destroyed, because a team disbanded or a window missed does not return.
[C1.3] Worse, a fixed share pretends all science needs the same audit rate. It does not. Some results cry out for checking because they will guide drugs, policy, or massive follow-on investment. Much else is self-correcting, low-stakes, or exploratory by design — pilot ethnography, archival digging, proof-of-concept engineering where the next experiment is the real replication.
[C1.4] Mandating the same slice everywhere funds busywork repeats of safe, easily replicable studies while starving the frontier where error is normal and iteration already does the work.
[C1.5] The quota also corrupts what it protects. Guarantee money for replication and you create a market for perfunctory repeats chosen because they are cheap and publishable, not because they matter. Genuine verification requires judgment: which claim is fragile, influential, and decision-relevant?
[C1.6] That judgment can be funded without a rigid tax. Require open data and methods, build replication into large novel grants, and run dedicated calls when a high-stakes finding demands independent testing. That targets verification where its return is highest instead of spending it by calendar.
[C1.7] Discovery backed by verification is the right goal. A fixed levy is the wrong means. It shrinks the pipeline scientists and patients depend on to subsidize checks many studies do not need, chosen poorly to satisfy a number.
