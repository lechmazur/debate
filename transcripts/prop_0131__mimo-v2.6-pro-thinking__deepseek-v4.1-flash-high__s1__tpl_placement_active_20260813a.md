[P1.1] Family caregiving is the largest unfunded workforce in the care system. When an adult daughter cuts her hours to full-time care for a mother with dementia, the state books that as a saving. It is not a saving. It is a subsidy, paid in her lost wages, lost pension contributions, and eroded health — and it is a subsidy the government has simply decided to collect from people with no ability to refuse.
[P1.2] The status quo is not neutrality. It is a policy choice to assume that unpaid care will keep arriving. That assumption is now failing on its own terms. Care needs are rising as populations age and as disability care shifts from institutions into communities.
[P1.3] At the same time, fewer people can afford to absorb the burden: families are smaller, more workers are in two-income households, and adult children live further from their parents. [C1.1] The proposition has a noble instinct and the wrong instrument. Nobody denies that family caregivers save the state enormous sums or that many are exhausted, isolated, and financially punished. The question is whether the answer is to convert them into publicly paid workers on a more generous scale. That would entrench the very assumption the motion claims to reject: that care for older and disabled people is a family responsibility first, and a public service second.
[C1.2] A generous caregiver wage sounds like simple justice. In practice it creates a two-tier care system. People with available relatives get paid; people without them—or with relatives unable or unwilling to care—must still rely on thin formal services. That makes access to care depend on family luck and family labor, which is exactly the unfairness we should be dismantling.
[C1.3] It also puts public money into arrangements that are hard to monitor, hard to quality-assure, and easy to exploit. A paid daughter doing wound care or dementia support has no shift limits, no professional backup, no guaranteed training, and often no real ability to say no.
[C1.4] The motion also treats “more generous” as costless. If payments approach home-care wages and cover real hours, the fiscal commitment becomes large and open-ended, while the care supply remains unpredictable: a caregiver can quit, fall ill, or die. If payments stay modest, they are not what the motion promises. Either way, the public still needs a formal care workforce.
[C1.5] Supporting caregivers matters. Respite, pension credits, tax relief, leave protections, and direct funding for home-care workers all do it better. But the core duty to older and disabled people is to build reliable, accountable care—not to write cheques to relatives and call that a system. The alternative to unpaid family care is not paid family care.
[C1.6] It is public care. Vote CON.
