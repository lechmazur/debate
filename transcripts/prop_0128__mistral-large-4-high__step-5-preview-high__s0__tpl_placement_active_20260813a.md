[P1.1] The proposition asks for a simple discipline: match the credential to the work. When a role does not genuinely require a degree, demanding one is not rigor—it is waste. It excludes capable workers, inflates hiring costs, and tells applicants that four years of tuition is the price of entry, even when the actual skills could be demonstrated in a portfolio, a trial project, or a few months on the job.
[P1.2] Consider how screening actually works. Employers often use degrees as a cheap proxy for traits like persistence, basic literacy, or cultural fit. But that proxy has become increasingly blunt. A self-taught developer who has shipped three production applications, or a service manager who has run a multimillion-dollar operation, demonstrates capability more reliably than a diploma in an unrelated field.
[P1.3] When the credential is not necessary for performance, it stops functioning as a quality filter and starts functioning as a class filter. It locks out the majority of the workforce—roughly two-thirds of American adults do not hold a bachelor's degree—including many experienced workers, career-changers, and high performers who learned their craft outside the university.
[P1.4] The performance logic points the same direction. In roles like sales, administrative coordination, customer success, or IT support, output depends on specific competencies—communication, software proficiency, judgment under pressure—not on the completion of a general education curriculum. Firms that have dropped degree requirements for such positions consistently report larger, more diverse applicant pools without measurable drops in performance.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, the requirement imposes real costs: longer vacancies, higher salary demands to offset student debt, and the systematic exclusion of talent that could start contributing immediately.
[P1.6] The standard should be functional and honest: if a candidate can perform the job safely and effectively without the degree, requiring one is an unjustified barrier. Employers should stop demanding credentials that add no value to the work itself.

[C1.1] The proposition treats degrees as wasteful proxies blocking two-thirds of adults. But that framing misunderstands what degrees do. A degree is not a vocational certificate; it is a costly, credible signal of persistence, delayed gratification, and the capacity to master unfamiliar material. Those traits matter most precisely in roles where tasks are not written down in advance.
[C1.2] The diploma filters for dispositions employers cannot easily test in a two-hour interview.
[C1.3] Removing the requirement does not help the disadvantaged; it abandons them. Without a common credential, hiring shifts to worse proxies: unpaid internships, family networks, brand-name schools, and subjective cultural fit. A transparent—if blunt—degree rule at least gives a nontraditional candidate a clear target. Stripped of that screen, a hiring manager drowning in applications defaults to candidates who already look like them.
[C1.4] Nor is "not genuinely necessary" a line outsiders can draw. Performance in coordination, judgment, and client-facing roles depends on tacit knowledge built through years of general education. A service manager benefits from the writing and reasoning demands of a curriculum more than from a checklist of current software tools. The proposition assumes we can neatly separate general formation from job-specific skill; we cannot.
[C1.5] Employers should refine requirements—specifying fields where training adds value, and testing directly where competencies are narrow. But a blanket ban ignores the signaling function of degrees, the costs of removing them, and the right of organizations to define their own standards of trustworthiness. When a health system strips the degree screen from care coordination, it does not simply hire the nearest competent applicant; it gambles on unknown reliability and unknown capacity to absorb complex protocols. That gamble lands on patients, not on the hiring office.
