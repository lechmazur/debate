[P1.1] A voice is not a keepsake like a photograph. A photograph shows what someone did. A grief chatbot makes them do new things — say new words, offer advice, forgive, joke, or plead, in a voice and personality recognizable as theirs. That is not remembrance.
[P1.2] It is impersonation, and impersonation of the intimate self should not begin without permission from the person impersonated, or, when they can no longer give it, from their estate.
[P1.3] We do not take organs, publish private diaries, or use a likeness to sell products merely because someone misses a person or will pay. Loving desire is not consent. Prior consent — in a will, advance directive, or platform setting — lets people decide while alive whether to persist as a simulation, or leave only the record they actually made. If they left no instruction, the estate is the steward we already trust to close accounts, control unpublished work, and resolve family disagreement.
[P1.4] Without that gate, incentives point the wrong way. A company can scrape texts, voicemails, and videos, build a convincing mother or partner, then charge the bereaved monthly not to lose her again. One relative can commission a bot over another’s objection, feed it selective memories, and have “Dad” bless a feud he hated. A hallucinating bot can invent apologies or secrets, and grief makes those fabrications hard to question.
[P1.5] The harm falls both on the dead, whose identity is rewritten, and on the living, who bond with words no one chose to say. Consent creates a named decision-maker and a point where simulation must stop.
[P1.6] This is narrow. Families may keep letters, play recordings, tell stories, and build memorials. They may not manufacture new intimate speech in someone else’s voice without authorization. The dead cannot correct, withdraw, or object to what their simulation says next.
[P1.7] That powerlessness is why permission must come first.
