[P1.1] The animal-model failure is not a secondary inconvenience; it is the condition that makes refusal costly. For many pathogens, mice, ferrets, or macaques do not reproduce the human route of infection, immune evasion, or severe clinical outcome. When regulators use that as the default bar, they do not get safety. They get delay.
[P1.2] Candidates advance on misleading animal signals, then either fail late in costly field trials or never reach people who need them. Meanwhile, the disease continues its own uncontrolled experiment.
[P1.3] The serious ethical objection is real, but it points toward safeguards, not prohibition. Deliberately infecting volunteers sounds harsh until compared with the real alternative. In an outbreak, thousands are exposed without a known dose, without eligibility screening, without close monitoring, without early rescue, and usually without consent. A controlled infection study enrolls informed adults, uses a measured dose, isolates and observes them, and intervenes at the earliest sign.
[P1.4] That is not the riskier path; it is the more controlled one.
[P1.5] Existing studies show this is feasible and not hypothetical. Malaria volunteers can be infected under controlled conditions and treated before severe disease. Cholera and typhoid challenge models have established antibiotic and rehydration rescue. These are not mystery exposures; their upper limits can be explained.
[P1.6] The problem is that permission remains uneven and timid, so diseases with weak animal models are treated as if they have no bench pathway even when infection is treatable. The proposition does not demand permission for pathogens with no rescue option, fatal irreversible outcomes, or vulnerable populations. It demands a broader default: when animal evidence is inadequate and the infection can be managed, regulators should have to justify refusing, not justify permitting.
[P1.7] Opponents treat animal models as neutral and human infection as exceptional. In truth, withholding controlled human data can mean waiting for larger, uglier natural exposure. A cautious regulator should prefer the smaller, monitored risk.
