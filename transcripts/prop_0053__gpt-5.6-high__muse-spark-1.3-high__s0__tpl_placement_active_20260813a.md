[P1.1] A teenager scrolling beauty content may encounter a trusted creator presenting lip filler, rhinoplasty, or body contouring as casually as a new outfit: a dramatic “after,” a discount code, and little discussion of pain, complications, recovery, permanence, or repeated maintenance. That is not neutral information. It is paid persuasion for medical intervention, delivered through parasocial trust to an audience still forming its body image and capacity to evaluate risk.
[P1.2] The danger is not merely that minors might book procedures tomorrow. Repeated promotion establishes altered faces and bodies as normal, defects as urgent, and intervention as the obvious solution. Filters, selective lighting, undisclosed editing, and exceptional outcomes can make both the need and the benefit appear larger than reality.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, the influencer may be rewarded per referral, while the viewer bears every physical, financial, and psychological consequence.
[P1.4] Stronger warnings should plainly identify sponsorship, material risks, possible permanence, image alteration, and the fact that individual suitability requires independent clinical assessment. Age-gating should reduce minors’ exposure to promotional procedure content when an account’s followers, style, or platform data indicate a substantially youthful audience. This need not block general health education, patient support, or adults seeking information. It places friction precisely on advertising, where commercial incentives distort presentation.
[P1.5] Neither measure must be perfect to be justified. Teenagers may bypass gates, and some may ignore warnings; seatbelts and disclosure rules also fail sometimes. The relevant question is whether these safeguards reduce impulsive exposure and correct misleading impressions at reasonable cost. They do.
[P1.6] Advertisers already tailor campaigns by age and audience profile; asking them not to exploit youthful attention for elective medical sales is administratively feasible.
[P1.7] The principle is simple: the more invasive the product, the stronger the duty not to market it like harmless lifestyle content. Cosmetic procedures can carry lasting consequences; influencer advertising can conceal that seriousness. Modest disclosure and age-based friction are therefore not censorship. They are the minimum protections owed when profit, medical risk, and adolescent vulnerability meet on the same screen.
