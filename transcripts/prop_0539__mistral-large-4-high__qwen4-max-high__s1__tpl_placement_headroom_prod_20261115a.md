[P1.1] Cross-border commercial surrogacy is a reality that no border wall can stop. Intended parents travel, gametes travel, contracts are signed across jurisdictions, and babies are born. The only genuine choice is not between surrogacy and no surrogacy, but between surrogacy that operates in daylight under enforceable rules and surrogacy driven underground where no regulator can see it. The proposition draws that line correctly: legality must be conditional, tethered to standards that agencies and clinics can actually be held to.
[P1.2] Those standards are not decorative. Anti-exploitation means more than a signed contract; it requires independent legal counsel for the surrogate, compensation that reflects real medical risk rather than desperation wages, and a cooling-off period so consent is informed rather than pressured. Parentage standards mean the child is not left stateless or parentless by conflicting laws—birth certificates must name the intended parents from the start, while the surrogate’s own parental rights are extinguished only through a court order, not a private clause.
[P1.3] Health protection means mandatory psychological screening, limits on embryo transfers, insurance that covers pregnancy complications, and post-natal care that does not end at delivery. Without these three pillars, commercial surrogacy becomes a marketplace for the vulnerable.
[P1.4] The word "enforceable" is the proposition’s spine. Paper standards are worthless across borders; what matters is licensing with teeth, inspections that can shut down a clinic, penalties that follow agencies into their home jurisdictions, and mutual recognition agreements so a parentage order in one country is honored in another. That is precisely what makes legality defensible. An outright ban does not protect the woman in a clinic abroad—it simply makes her invisible to any authority that could help her.
[P1.5] Conditional legality keeps her within the law’s reach.
