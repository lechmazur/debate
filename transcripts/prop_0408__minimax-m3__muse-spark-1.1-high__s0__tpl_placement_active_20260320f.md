[P1.1] Eighteen-year-olds are handed legally binding contracts—student loans, credit cards, apartment leases—yet most have never been taught to read one. That gap is the central clash in this debate, and it is why the PRO side wins.
[P1.2] The motion asks whether schools should require serious financial literacy rather than leave these skills to elective exposure or family background. The answer is yes, because the "elective or family" alternative is not neutral ground—it is a privilege lottery. Students whose parents understand amortization, compound interest, and tax withholding arrive at adulthood armored. Everyone else walks into a financial landscape rigged against them, often discovering the cost of a mistake over decades rather than days.
[P1.3] Three concrete stakes. Student debt: a borrower who cannot distinguish subsidized from unsubsidized loans, or fixed from variable rates, can make a choice at nineteen that compounds into six-figure regret by thirty. Predatory credit: subprime auto loans, buy-now-pay-later schemes, and high-fee debit products target the financially illiterate, and the marketing is sophisticated. Taxes: many graduates will file as employees, contractors, or freelancers within a year, yet cannot distinguish a W-2 from a 1099, or explain why a refund feels like a windfall rather than a forced zero-interest loan to the government.
[P1.4] Electives fail here. A curious student can find a personal-finance class; an overwhelmed, college-bound teenager rarely will. Family background fails more starkly. Wealthy households transmit financial fluency at the kitchen table; lower-income households often transmit the opposite—distrust inherited from crises like 2008, or simply silence where expertise is missing.
[P1.5] A required course is not a panacea, but it is the only mechanism that reaches every student, not just the lucky or the lucky-born. Schools already mandate health, civics, and physical education on the theory that adults need baseline competence to navigate adult life. Money is not less essential than gym. It is more.

[C1.1] The problem PRO describes is real — young adults get crushed by fine print. Requiring a serious financial-literacy course for graduation is the wrong fix for that problem, because it fails on learning, on equity, and on institutional competence.
[C1.2] Learning first. Personal finance is use-dependent. You do not learn to budget while you have no income, no rent, no taxes to file. You learn when the paycheck hits.
[C1.3] Forcing sophomores to memorize difference between W-2 and 1099 two years before they ever see one guarantees fast forgetting. Research on financial education consistently finds small gains in quiz scores and almost no change in saving, borrowing, or default behavior months later. An elective or family conversation at least meets a motivated learner at a teachable moment; a mandate teaches the unmotivated at the worst possible time.
[C1.4] Equity second. Graduation requirements are zero-sum. Every district that has added a required semester has cut something — often a math lab, a CTE shop period, or flexible credit for students working after school. The students PRO wants to help are precisely those who cannot afford to lose that flexibility.
[C1.5] Adding another gate to graduate does not level the [P1.2] privilege lottery; it adds another hoop for those already closest to dropping out, while wealthier kids get the same worksheet plus a private tutor at home anyway.
[C1.6] Finally, who teaches this serious course? Most schools do not have staff qualified to explain amortization versus predatory lending. So they buy packaged curricula, often written or sponsored by banks and fintech firms eager to brand themselves as educators. The result is not armor against predation; it is marketing with homework.
[C1.7] If we want equity, embed these skills into existing math and economics where they are applied, keep robust electives for those who want depth, and fund unbiased advising when students actually file taxes or sign aid letters — where knowledge sticks.
