[P1.1] Every year, thousands die crossing the Mediterranean not because they lack valid asylum claims, but because there is no legal way to knock on the EU's door before climbing onto an unrated rubber boat. This unified humanitarian visa fixes that core moral and practical failure.
[P1.2] This is not open borders. It creates a single standard application channel at EU consulates abroad. Candidates submit their asylum claim safely, receive a proper impartial assessment, and may only travel to the EU if their claim is approved. No more paying criminal smugglers.
[P1.3] No more drowning while governments watch.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim this will cause overwhelming arrivals. That argument reverses reality. Right now, smuggling gangs are the EU's de facto gatekeepers. A formal system lets the EU screen claims upfront, reject invalid applications before anyone travels, and plan hosting capacity properly.
[P1.5] We already operate this exact administrative model for student visas, work permits, and tourist travel. There is no practical barrier to applying the same basic process to people running for their lives.
[P1.6] Most critically, this restores the actual promise of asylum. Asylum was never meant to reward swimming ability, physical fitness, or the wealth to pay smugglers. It was meant to protect people facing persecution. Right now we run the test backwards: we let the most vulnerable die before they ever get a hearing, and only assess those who survived the crossing.
[P1.7] This policy will save thousands of lives, destroy the smuggling trade's core revenue stream, and return control of entry to EU authorities. There is no decent, workable alternative. That is the case.

[C1.1] The Pro opener sells a comforting image: a frightened family walks into an EU consulate, fills in a form, and flies safely to protection. Reality would be a bigger, sneakier queue outside fewer, more fortified doors.
[C1.2] Start with reach. [P1.5] says we already run this model for students and tourists. But those applicants have passports, fixed addresses, and usually some tie that makes return plausible. An asylum seeker fleeing Syria, Eritrea, or Taliban-controlled Afghanistan often has no functioning civil registry, no passport, and no EU embassy within safe reach.
[C1.3] People would still have to cross checkpoints, bribe officials, and pay fixers merely to arrive at the consulate in a neighbouring capital. Smugglers would not lose their business; they would rebrand it. Boat money becomes visa-facilitation money. That is not destroying the trade’s revenue stream; it is raising its price.
[C1.4] Now take the moral claim. [P1.6] says we would stop testing swimming ability and start protecting the persecuted. In practice, an external visa rewards the opposite of vulnerability. The people who can secure documents, reach a consulate, book appointments, and survive interviews are frequently the better-off, better-connected, and literate.
[C1.5] The rural widow, the disabled elder, the undocumented minority cannot. We replace a physical ordeal with a bureaucratic one, where the most marginalized lose again.
[C1.6] Finally, “unified” is the fiction. Member states cannot agree on recognition rules or relocation. If a consulate approves a claim, who decides the destination? Who pays?
[C1.7] The same vetoes and blame-shifting that crippled relocation will reappear at the visa window. External decision-making also means remote, document-poor assessments prone to fraud and error.
[C1.8] The motion mistakes a form for a solution. A rubber boat and a consulate queue are both rationing mechanisms; the second merely looks tidier while it runs on the same desperation. It would expand demand it cannot meet, hand smugglers a new product, and leave the truly vulnerable further from safety.
