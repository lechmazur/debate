[P1.1] A statue on a courthouse lawn is not a history lesson. It is a sentence spoken by the community in the present tense: this is who we hold up. That is the whole clash. Nobody here proposes erasing anyone.
[P1.2] The motion moves objects from places built for honor to places built for understanding. So the real question is narrow: should a democratic public keep saying, every day, in its most symbolic spaces, that men whose defining legacy is enslavement, conquest, or terror deserve our reverence?
[P1.3] Consider what a monument actually communicates. A Black family walking into a courthouse past a bronze Confederate general is not being educated about 1863. They are being told whose justice this building was built to serve. A pedestal carries a name and maybe a date; it explains nothing.
[P1.4] A museum can show the whip, the ledger, the letters, the later politics of who raised the statue and why. If the goal is remembering history, the museum wins on its own terms. Only if the goal is honoring does the square matter, and that is precisely what cannot be defended.
[P1.5] The history argument also cuts the other way. Most Confederate monuments were not raised by grieving veterans in 1866; they went up decades later, during Jim Crow, as a deliberate claim about who ruled. Leopold II's statues in Belgium celebrated a colonial enterprise that killed on a staggering scale. Keeping them in place does not preserve history; it preserves a later political message.
[P1.6] We know relocation works. Budapest gathered its Soviet-era statues into Memento Park, where they now teach rather than intimidate. Bristol placed the toppled Colston statue in a museum, graffiti intact, and more people learned about him in a year than in a century on his plinth.
[P1.7] The motion is bounded: "primarily associated." That excludes complicated founders and targets men whose fame is the crime. So the burden on the other side is plain: explain why a plinth teaches better than a museum, and why the honor must continue.

[C1.6] A principle whose scope is set by whoever is angriest this decade is not a principle.
