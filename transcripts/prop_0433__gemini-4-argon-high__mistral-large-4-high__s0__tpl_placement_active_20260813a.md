Yet a lease locks a tenant into paying thousands of dollars over a year, backed by severe penalties if they leave early. Because landlords hold exclusive access to a building’s complaint history, letting them conceal chronic livability failures turns a housing contract into a trap.
[P1.2] We already require landlords to disclose hidden physical defects like lead paint or past flooding because applicants cannot spot them during a walkthrough. Recurring short-term-rental parties, severe noise, and chronic package theft are the modern equivalents: hidden operational defects that destroy the core promise of a lease—quiet enjoyment and basic security. A shift worker or a parent with an infant cannot function beneath an unpoliced party flat. [P1.5] Mandatory disclosure breaks that cycle. When landlords must either disclose a chronic problem upfront or fix it—by installing package lockers, enforcing quiet hours, or shutting down rogue rentals—market incentives finally reward honest management over tenant turnover.

Is a package stolen once a month "chronic" theft?

When the law requires disclosure of undefined conditions, it does not inform tenants—it manufactures litigation. Every lease signing becomes a potential lawsuit waiting for a disgruntled tenant to argue retroactively that the landlord should have characterized last March's complaint as "severe" rather than "moderate."
[C1.3] Worse, the mandate creates perverse incentives that harm the very tenants it purports to help. Faced with liability for nondisclosure, landlords will respond rationally: they will stop documenting complaints in writing, settle frivolous noise accusations with quiet cash payments to keep records clean, or avoid renting to anyone who appears litigious. The diligent landlord who maintains an honest complaint log becomes the lawsuit target; the negligent landlord who ignores problems simply avoids the paper trail. Disclosure does not install soundproofing, repair broken lobby locks, or shut down rogue short-term rentals.
[C1.4] It merely forces property owners to label their buildings as defective, stigmatizing units and depressing neighborhood property values without reducing a single decibel or recovering a single stolen parcel.
[C1.5] We already possess workable mechanisms: noise ordinances enforced by police, criminal reports for theft, and the tenant's right to speak with neighbors during due diligence. What is missing is not information—it is enforcement. Mandating disclosure substitutes bureaucratic paperwork for actual peace and security.

Police reports are public, but they do not notify landlords, and they record accusations, not verified facts. When a tenant emails about a loud gathering, the landlord can dispute receipt or characterize it as a conversation, turning "easily provable fraud" into a credibility contest about whether 10 p.m. constitutes "severe noise."

[C2.4] The proposition confuses labeling with solving. We need housing inspectors enforcing existing codes, not landlords confessing defects. Disclosure creates a market for lemons and lawsuits, not quiet enjoyment.
