[C1.1] Start with the test the motion sets: not whether short-term rentals have any effect on rents, but whether restricting them lowers rents and displacement by more than it costs the people who earn from them and the businesses fed by visitors. Measured honestly, the rent side of that ledger is thin and the cost side is fat.
[C1.2] First, the arithmetic. In most high-demand neighborhoods, whole-home short-term listings are a small slice of housing, typically low single digits of stock. A 12% conversion rate, as PRO imagines, is an outlier, not a baseline. Even if every one of those units flowed back to long-term tenants, the added supply would nudge rents by a few percent at most, and much of that is temporary because rents are driven by chronic underbuilding, zoning, and job growth, not by tourists.
[C1.3] Second, they do not all flow back. When New York effectively banned most short-term rentals in 2023, listings collapsed, yet rents kept climbing to record highs. Owners sold to owner-occupiers, kept units as pieds-à-terre, or shifted to thirty-plus-day stays. A displaced renter cannot afford a condo that used to be an Airbnb.
[C1.4] Portland's owner-occupancy permit regime, meanwhile, was notorious for low compliance, so the seven-dollars-saved-per-dollar-lost figure should be treated as an assertion, not a finding.
[C1.5] Third, the costs land on the wrong people. The typical host is not a portfolio landlord but a homeowner renting a spare room or a basement to cover the mortgage that keeps them housed. Neighborhood cafés, corner shops, and cleaners lose customers when visitors are pushed into hotel districts. Hotel rates spike, which means fewer families and fewer long-stay visitors, so spending doesn't simply relocate; some of it disappears.
[C1.6] Restrictions are cheap politics. They let a city look like it is acting on housing while dodging the one thing that reliably lowers rents: building more. The motion asks us to believe a sliver of supply outweighs concentrated, certain harm to residents' incomes. It does not.
