[P1.1] Sports fans are not buffet customers. Most follow one club, one national side, or a single competition. Broadcasters still sell them the warehouse. That mismatch is the problem, and cheaper team-specific or competition-specific passes are the answer.
[P1.2] A Liverpool supporter does not need every mid-table fixture elsewhere. A Chiefs fan does not need a package built around thirty unused teams. When the only legal door is a fat bundle, the price is set by rights recoupment from a captive audience, not by what that fan actually values. The result is overcharging, cancelled subscriptions, password sharing, and illegal streams.
[P1.3] Specific passes would let people buy the games they will watch at a price that reflects that narrower product.
[P1.4] This is not charity. It is honest pricing: charge more to the omnivore who wants every match, charge less to the loyalist who wants one side. That expands the paying audience instead of squeezing the same households until they leave. Students, families, diaspora fans, and casual viewers get a legal on-ramp.
[P1.5] The premium bundle remains for anyone who truly wants everything.
[P1.6] Forcing a product people do not want is not required to fund elite sport. Rights value comes from attention, advertising, and paying customers. Narrower, cheaper products increase volume and legitimacy at once. Broadcasters already unbundle when it suits them: pay-per-view events, out-of-market packages, club channels.
[P1.7] What they refuse to do is make those options actually cheap and widely available, so the games fans care about stay locked behind a wall built for people who consume the whole sport.
[P1.8] Sell the product that matches the fan. Stop making most supporters subsidize a buffet they will never eat.

[C1.1] Strip away the rhetoric and the pledge at the heart of this motion is a fantasy: that slicing a broadcast package makes each slice cheap. It doesn't. The rights bill doesn't shrink because you sell it in pieces. A league that costs a broadcaster billions must recoup billions, whether from one bundle or thirty team passes.
[C1.2] Price a "Chiefs pass" and you're pricing to the most captive audience in sport — the loyalist who cannot switch teams. That's not honest pricing; it's a superfan tax. We saw this logic play out with cable unbundling: à-la-carte pricing was promised as salvation, and every serious analysis concluded per-channel prices would balloon until total spend matched or exceeded the bundle. Fans of a club plus a national side plus a cup run would pay more, not less, across three fragmented passes.
[C1.3] Second, bundles are how sport funds itself equitably. Collective sale means the Kansas City Royals and Brentford exist on television at all. Unbundle demand by team and revenue concentrates on the handful of glamour clubs — the direction Spanish football went when clubs sold rights individually, hollowing out everyone below Madrid and Barcelona until La Liga forced collective sale to fix it. Team-specific pricing is competitive-imbalance machinery.
[C1.4] It slowly kills the league whose drama gives your one team meaning.
[C1.5] Third, the fan's actual product is the competition. Your team's season is inseparable from the title race, the relegation battle, the rival's collapse. A team pass sells you half a story, then paywalls the playoffs, the derby broadcast rights held elsewhere, the cup ties. The result is exactly the app-juggling, mid-season upsell chaos that drives people to illegal streams — the problem PRO claims to solve.
[C1.6] The bundle isn't a warehouse forced on fans. It's the pooling that keeps sport affordable per game, keeps small clubs alive, and keeps the whole story on screen. Break it, and everyone pays more for less.
