[P1.1] The United States faces an overdose crisis driven by fentanyl, a drug so potent that lethal doses fit on a pinhead. The question is not whether we respond, but how. Over the next five years, expanding medication-assisted treatment and harm reduction will reduce deaths more than escalating interdiction and penalties because these approaches address the actual mechanism of death—using alone, without tolerance, without help—while supply-side enforcement cannot stop a drug this compact and this profitable.
[P1.2] Medication-assisted treatment is the single most effective tool we have. Methadone and buprenorphine stabilize opioid receptors, eliminate withdrawal, and allow people to function. Research consistently shows that people retained in these treatments cut their risk of overdose death by roughly half. This is not abstract: when a person stops using fentanyl daily because they are on buprenorphine, they stop dying.
[P1.3] Scaling access—removing waitlists, expanding office-based prescribing, funding mobile clinics—directly converts high-risk users into survivors.
[P1.4] Harm reduction operates at the moment of highest risk. Naloxone distribution puts an antidote in the hands of peers and family; fentanyl test strips let users detect contamination; supervised consumption sites ensure help is present when breathing stops. These do not require sobriety to save a life—they save lives while people are still using.
[P1.5] Interdiction and penalties fail on their own terms. Fentanyl is measured in milligrams; a single truck could carry a year's supply for a city. Seizing shipments does not raise prices enough to deter cartels, and when supply tightens, the market shifts to even more potent analogs—the iron law of prohibition. Criminal penalties do not deter addiction, which is compulsive, not rational.
[P1.6] Incarceration disrupts treatment and raises overdose risk upon release.
[P1.7] Five years is enough time to build treatment capacity that persists. It is not enough time to win a war against a molecule. The choice is between saving lives now through medicine, or chasing a fantasy of interdiction that has failed for fifty years.
