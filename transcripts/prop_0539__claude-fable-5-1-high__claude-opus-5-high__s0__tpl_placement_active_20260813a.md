[P1.1] The children are the place to start, because they are the ones no one asked. A baby born to a surrogate in one country for parents from another can end up with no nationality, no legal parent, and no way home. That has actually happened—Thai and Indian cases where infants sat in legal limbo for months because the destination country refused to recognize the parentage the birth country recorded. Neither prohibition nor free-market surrogacy fixes that.
[P1.2] Only enforceable parentage standards do.
[P1.3] So the motion is not a compromise between two purer positions; it is the only position that takes the harm seriously. Consider what the alternatives actually produce.
[P1.4] Prohibition does not end the practice; it relocates it. When India and Thailand closed their doors to foreigners, the industry moved within a year or two to Cambodia, Nepal, Laos, then Kenya and Georgia—usually to places with weaker medical oversight and no contract enforcement. Demand from infertile couples does not vanish because a legislature votes. Bans simply guarantee the surrogates are poorer, the clinics less accountable, and the babies more exposed.
[P1.5] Deregulation fails from the other side. Where clinics operate without binding standards, the pattern is familiar: women recruited from slums by brokers who take most of the fee, multiple embryo transfers to raise success rates at the expense of the surrogate's body, contracts that let commissioning parents walk away from a disabled child, and no independent counsel for the woman signing. These are not hypotheticals; they are the documented scandals that triggered the bans in the first place.
[P1.6] The motion threads the needle: keep the practice legal, but license only agencies and clinics that can show independent legal advice for surrogates, capped and escrowed payments, health insurance and single-embryo protocols, and pre-birth parentage orders recognized on both sides. If an operator cannot meet those, it does not operate.
[P1.7] Any opponent must defend one of two things: abandoning surrogates to an underground market, or abandoning them to an unregulated one. Both leave the same women and children paying the price.
