[P1.1] Ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks are the leading driver of preventable diet-related disease, killing 11 million people globally each year, and decades of relying on voluntary consumer choice campaigns have failed to curb this crisis. These products are engineered to be hyper-palatable, marketed aggressively to children and low-income communities with limited capacity to weigh long-term health risks against immediate gratification, and buried nutrition labels are designed to be ignored even by motivated consumers. Voluntary consumer choice campaigns assume equal access to clear information and equal ability to act on it—assumptions that are systematically violated in this market.
[P1.2] Prominent front-of-package warning labels cut through this industry noise: Chile’s 2016 law requiring black octagon “high in sugar” stop signs on ultra-processed products drove a double-digit drop in sugary drink purchases within two years, results no comparable consumer education campaign has ever matched. Unlike vague “healthy lifestyle” messaging, these labels communicate risk instantly, no advanced literacy or math skills required, and shift social norms to stigmatize products that cause preventable harm—just as they did for tobacco. The alternative of relying on consumer choice ignores the reality that these products are sold in a rigged information environment, where billions in industry marketing frame soda and chips as fun, convenient, or even natural, while hiding the health costs.
[P1.3] Warning labels put the modest burden of disclosure on the corporations profiting from these products, rather than on overstretched public health systems or individual consumers trying to parse fine print. This is a low-cost, evidence-backed intervention that saves lives, reduces healthcare costs, and levels the playing field for consumers who have been manipulated for decades.

[C1.1] Warnings treat food like tobacco. But eating is not smoking: there is no safe cigarette, while most foods can fit in a healthy diet, and many ultra-processed foods — fortified cereals, whole-grain breads, flavored yogurts, plant-based milks — provide affordable nutrition and shelf stability. An octagon does not say “eat occasionally” or “watch the portion”; it says danger. Applied by crude sugar, sodium, or processing thresholds, it misclassifies mixed products, flattens differences, and teaches fear rather than understanding of a diet.
[C1.2] The evidence for making warnings the main strategy is weaker than it appears. Chile did not test labels alone; it bundled them with bans on child-directed marketing and school sales, so purchase shifts cannot be credited to the octagon. In supermarkets, warnings compete with price, habit, hunger, and time. Once dozens of everyday items carry the same alarming mark — as with California’s chemical warnings — people tune it out, swap to an unflagged but no healthier product, or buy a reformulated version designed to dodge a threshold.
[C1.3] Selling less of one category is not eating better overall.
[C1.4] What changes diets is capability: knowing how to build a meal, judge a portion, cook quickly, and afford a substitute. Serious consumer choice campaigns — through schools, primary care, community cooking and shopping support, plus clear neutral information — build that capability. Warnings do the reverse: they shame the purchase while leaving food deserts, price gaps, and time poverty untouched. A shopper who cannot reach or afford alternatives does not eat better; she shops with more guilt and less trust.
[C1.5] Nor is the burden only corporate. Thresholds invite gaming by large manufacturers able to reformulate around a line and absorb redesign costs, while smaller, culturally specific producers face the same compliance and a scarlet letter on their packs. Governments should inform choice, improve access, and teach skills — not conscript packaging into misleading alarms that blur genuine hazards, invite fatigue, and substitute stigma for empowerment.
