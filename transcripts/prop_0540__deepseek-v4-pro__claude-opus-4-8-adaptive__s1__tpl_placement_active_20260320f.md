[P1.1] Every year, thousands of newborns with treatable disorders are missed simply because their parents did not return a form or were never adequately informed. That is not a failure of medicine—it is a failure of system design. When we know that early detection can prevent catastrophic brain damage, death, or lifelong disability, public health has a duty to set the default to screening.
[P1.2] Opt-in screening consistently produces lower uptake, and the drop-off is not random. It hits hardest among families with less health literacy, language barriers, unstable housing, or limited access to prenatal care—precisely the same families whose children already face higher health risks. An opt-out framework flips the dynamic: it makes screening the standard of care while preserving genuine choice. Parents receive clear notice and the unconditional right to decline.
[P1.3] No one is coerced. But by removing the requirement to actively request the test, we stop penalising the overwhelmed, the uninformed, and the under-resourced.
[P1.4] Crucially, the proposition limits screening to conditions with effective early interventions. This is not a fishing expedition for late-onset probabilistic risks. We are talking about disorders like congenital hypothyroidism, medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency, or severe combined immunodeficiency, where a missed diagnosis can mean irreversible damage before symptoms even appear, and where diet, hormone replacement, or early transplantation fundamentally alters the outcome. In these cases, the evidence for intervention is robust, and the window to act is terrifyingly narrow.
[P1.5] Opt-out with guardrails answers the classic objections. Anxiety from false positives is manageable because the list is restricted to actionable conditions where confirmatory testing is swift and definitive. The information burden on parents is reduced, because the default pathway already aligns with what the overwhelming majority would choose for their child if fully informed. And autonomy is actually strengthened: true parental choice is only possible when the default does not bury a life-saving opportunity behind paperwork and missed appointments.
[P1.6] A system that waits for parents to opt in does not respect their autonomy; it just leaves some children to suffer preventable harm.
