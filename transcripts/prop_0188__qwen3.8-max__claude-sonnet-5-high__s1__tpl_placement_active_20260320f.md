[P1.1] The core of this debate is who bears the risk of research noise: the public, patients, and policymakers, or the incentives of individual researchers. Publicly funded clinical and social-science research is a public good, and its credibility depends on separating the plan from the result. Mandatory preregistration does that. It forces researchers to state hypotheses, sample size, exclusions, and analyses before seeing the data.
[P1.2] That simple step makes p-hacking, endpoint switching, and HARKing visible instead of invisible. In clinical trials, the difference is not academic: if a team quietly shifts from depression symptoms to sleep quality, or drops noncompleters in a way that flatters a drug, clinicians and patients may be misled. In social science, if only surprising positive findings survive, schools and governments may copy programs that never actually worked.
[P1.3] The second half is necessary because preregistration alone cannot fix publication bias. If journals only publish exciting positives, researchers still have every incentive to massage results until they look exciting. A commitment to publish preregistered studies regardless of outcome changes the reward system. It tells scientists: your job is to run a competent test of an important question, not to manufacture a neat story.
[P1.4] Null findings become informative rather than career-threatening. Meta-analyses become more reliable because the file drawer starts to empty.
[P1.5] My opponent will likely defend flexibility. But preregistration does not forbid exploratory work; it requires transparency about what was planned and what emerged. Nor does it demand publishing bad science; it means outcome should not determine acceptance once the study is public, funded, and soundly executed. The real choice is between a system that lets inconvenient results vanish and one that treats publicly funded evidence as a public record.
[P1.6] For medicine, policy, and public trust, the latter is clearly superior.
