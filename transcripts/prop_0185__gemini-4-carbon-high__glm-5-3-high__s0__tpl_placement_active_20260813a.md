[P1.1] Legislative discretion systematically fails at fiscal discipline because electoral incentives are fundamentally asymmetric. Lawmakers win votes today by delivering visible spending hikes and tax cuts while shifting the bill onto unrepresented future taxpayers. In theory, legislatures borrow in recessions and save in booms; in practice, discretion breeds chronic deficits across both. Ordinary statutory rules cannot fix this because a legislature cannot bind itself: the same simple majority tempted to overspend can effortlessly suspend or repeal yesterday’s cap.
[P1.2] Only constitutional entrenchment places fiscal solvency above short-term electoral opportunism.
[P1.3] Crucially, pairing constitutional balance with automatic stabilizer exceptions captures the macroeconomic benefits of countercyclical policy without its political pathologies. Rigid balanced-budget mandates drew criticism because they forced pro-cyclical spending cuts mid-recession. Automatic stabilizers solve that: when growth falters, progressive tax receipts naturally decline and unemployment and safety-net transfers immediately expand. That injects targeted demand into struggling households far faster than discretionary stimulus bills, which arrive months late, get hijacked by special-interest pork, and calcify into structural deficits.
[P1.4] Once recovery arrives, automatic stabilizers recede on schedule, enforcing discipline during booms.
[P1.5] Leaving fiscal discipline to legislative discretion does not preserve flexibility; it destroys it. When lawmakers run unchecked deficits through economic expansions, compounding interest payments steadily devour public revenue, crowding out productive infrastructure, education, and healthcare before a crisis ever hits. Worse, heavily indebted governments face spiking bond yields and harsh market-imposed austerity precisely when downturns strike, leaving vulnerable citizens exposed. Constitutional rules requiring balance outside cyclical stabilizers—exemplified by Switzerland’s business-cycle debt brake—reverse that dynamic.
[P1.6] They compel lawmakers to pay honestly for permanent programs, anchor sovereign credibility, keep borrowing costs low, and ensure automatic safety nets remain solvent. Constitutions exist precisely where majoritarian temptations reliably harm those without a vote. Because future citizens cannot vote against today’s borrowing binge, fiscal discipline belongs in constitutional law, not legislative whim.

[C1.2] Start with enforcement. Who polices a constitutional budget rule? Courts would be dragged into ruling on revenue forecasts and spending paths—technical, contested, political judgments they lack any legitimacy or capacity to resolve. The realistic alternatives are toothless: Germany's debt brake has been repeatedly softened or suspended when it became inconvenient, including during reunification, the eurozone crisis, COVID, and a climate-emergency carve-out.
[C1.3] When the rule binds, it gets bent; when it can't be bent, it does damage. That is the worst of both worlds: all the rigidity of entrenchment with none of the discipline.
[C1.4] Second, rules invite gaming. Faced with a binding cap, legislatures don't become virtuous—they shift spending off-budget, into state-owned enterprises, unfunded mandates on lower governments, tax expenditures, and creative accounting. You get less transparency, not more discipline, while the constitutional text gives markets and citizens false assurance that someone else is watching the till.
[C1.5] Third, the stabilizer exception is a confession of inadequacy. Automatic stabilizers—tax receipts falling, unemployment insurance rising—are a modest fraction of what crises demand. The 2008 financial rescue, bank recapitalizations, pandemic lockdown support, and war mobilization were all discretionary. A rule that says "balance, except for the mechanical bits" tells legislatures they may respond to routine downturns but must risk constitutional crisis to respond to real emergencies.
[C1.6] Finally, the Swiss example proves culture, not text: plenty of disciplined nations—Nordic states with surplus targets, or the UK's fiscal frameworks—achieved solvency through legislated norms and credible institutions. Fiscal discipline is a practice of democratic accountability, and constitutions can't substitute for it.
