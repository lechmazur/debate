[P1.1] Right-to-disconnect laws improve worker welfare more than they damage productivity in knowledge industries because the productivity damage is largely hypothetical while the welfare gains are structural.
[P1.2] Knowledge work is not factory work. Output depends on cognitive clarity, not hours logged. An engineer or writer who checks Slack at 10 PM isn't producing extra value; they are degrading tomorrow's capacity. The "always-on" expectation imposes a standing cognitive tax—workers carry ambient dread even during family dinner or sleep, fragmenting recovery so deeply that even non-working hours fail to restore.
[P1.3] Laws that establish clear boundaries remove that tax.
[P1.4] Consider the concrete stakes. After-hours notifications disrupt sleep architecture, which cascades into worse decision-making, higher error rates, and slower creative problem-solving. Jurisdictions that have adopted such rules have not seen knowledge economies collapse; they have formalized norms that high-performing teams already recognized. The law does not ban overtime; it bans the expectation of availability that makes rest impossible to schedule.
[P1.5] Critics imagine lost innovation from uncontrolled hours. But breakthrough thinking in knowledge industries comes from incubation, not exhaustion. A rested developer solves architecture problems faster than a depleted one who never logs off, and a writer who closes the laptop at six often drafts better prose the next morning than one who answers emails until midnight. Forced disconnection even surfaces the toxic inefficiency of bloated email chains and unnecessary meetings that masquerade as diligence.
[P1.6] The asymmetry is decisive. Welfare gains from protected recovery are immediate and compounding—better mental health, family stability, reduced burnout. Productivity costs, where they exist, appear mainly in poorly managed workflows that the law will improve by forcing explicit prioritization and honest deadlines.
[P1.7] Right-to-disconnect laws do not damage knowledge work. They expose how much supposed productivity was really just anxiety. PRO wins.
