[P1.1] Public universities should stop pretending that less information produces fairer admissions. Reinstating a standardized-test requirement does not mean admitting by score, imposing a cutoff, or abandoning holistic review. It means every applicant supplies one common academic measure, interpreted alongside grades, course rigor, background, and achievement.
[P1.2] High-school transcripts are indispensable, but not interchangeable. Grading standards, course access, and grade inflation vary across schools. A student with excellent grades and limited advanced courses may be far stronger than the transcript can show; another applicant’s polished record may rest on advantages invisible to a distant admissions office. Tests are imperfect, but admissions is comparative.
[P1.3] The relevant question is not whether a score captures a whole person—nothing does—but whether it adds useful evidence about academic preparation and helps identify talent that school-specific measures miss.
[P1.4] The equity objection has the comparison backwards. Wealth shapes essays, activities, recommendations, and access to rigorous courses too. Under test-optional admissions, those polished, expensive signals carry more weight, while savvy applicants can strategize about whether to submit. A universal requirement treats scores consistently and gives a student from an unfamiliar or under-resourced school a nationally legible way to demonstrate ability.
[P1.5] Fair implementation matters: fee waivers, free preparation, accessible testing, multiple opportunities to sit the exam, and no rigid cutoff. But those safeguards improve a requirement; they do not justify discarding the evidence. Nor is this tests versus GPA: public universities should use both, because each can correct the other’s blind spots.
[P1.6] Public universities allocate scarce seats and public opportunity. They should not respond to inequality by making their own decisions less informed. The choice is not scores or equity; it is a common signal used with context, versus a context-heavy competition in which private advantages are easier to hide. More comparable evidence, used humbly, serves both excellence and access.

[P5.1] Advice can encourage disclosure; only a requirement makes disclosure universal. CON’s one-sentence fix—“a score below our middle range can still help”—leaves the decisive guesswork intact. Applicants must still predict how a score will be read, and the best-advised applicants will predict best. Universal submission removes that strategic judgment from the admissions contest.
[P5.2] The access objection identifies a burden to manage, not a reason to make evidence optional for everyone. Fee support, school-day results, later sittings, and narrow hardship accommodations can keep a qualified applicant’s file alive. An exception for someone who genuinely cannot test is not the collapse of a requirement; it is how a fair general rule handles exceptional circumstances.
[P5.3] The decisive comparison is not a perfect mandate versus a costless status quo. It is an administrative problem universities can address versus an informational loss no reader can repair. Reading silence “in context” cannot reveal the score never supplied, and a communication slogan cannot guarantee that every applicant understands, trusts, or acts on it.
[P5.4] Reinstate the requirement with accommodations: compel disclosure, not exclusion. Public universities should judge applicants on fuller evidence, not on who knows best what to withhold.
