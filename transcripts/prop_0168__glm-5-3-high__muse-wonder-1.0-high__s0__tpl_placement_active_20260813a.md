[P1.1] The question is not whether humans in space inspire us. It is what a science agency should buy with a finite exploration budget. On that question, the arithmetic is brutal and one-sided.
[P1.2] Robotic missions deliver more science per dollar because they spend nearly everything on instruments. A crewed mission must spend the overwhelming majority of its mass and budget keeping people alive: food, water, oxygen, radiation shielding, return propulsion, life support redundancy. The Apollo program carried three astronauts and a small package of experiments; today a rover the mass of a small car carries laboratories that would not have fit in Apollo's science bay, and it runs for a decade instead of three days.
[P1.3] Curiosity and Perseverance have been operating on Mars for years, drilling, sampling, and analyzing—continuously, at a fraction of the cost of a single crewed landing.
[P1.4] Telescopes do even better. Hubble, a decades-old instrument, reshaped cosmology, measured the expansion rate of the universe, and imaged thousands of galaxies in a single long exposure. JWST is now probing the atmospheres of exoplanets and the first generation of galaxies. No crewed mission can visit a planet orbiting another star, but a telescope can characterize it.
[P1.5] Most of the universe is simply not a place humans can go; a budget weighted toward crewed flight is a budget weighted away from most of the science.
[P1.6] The crewed alternative consumes budgets precisely because it is expensive. The International Space Station consumed well over $100 billion, and its headline science output—microgravity protein crystals, human physiology—is modest compared to what a fraction of that sum bought in planetary probes. The SLS rocket has cost enormous sums per launch while flying rarely.
[P1.7] Note the motion's modesty: shift *most*, not all. Keep crewed programs alive at the margins. But when the goal is discovery per dollar, robots and telescopes win, and it is not close.

[C1.1] Counting life support as waste assumes the astronaut is overhead. In exploration, the astronaut is the most versatile instrument ever flown — and often the reason the other instruments work at all.
[C1.2] A rover on Mars drives inches at a time, waits minutes for instructions from Earth, and avoids anything its programmers did not anticipate. An astronaut geologist walks, notices, digs, turns over the unexpected rock, and changes the plan on the spot. Apollo astronauts in three days brought back hundreds of kilograms of chosen lunar samples still yielding papers decades later; Perseverance can only cache small tubes and hope a separate expensive mission someday retrieves them. On the Moon or Mars, one crewed season can do the traversing, drilling, sorting, and mapping robots stretch into decades of cautious, partial work — if latency, power, dust, or a stuck wheel does not end them first.
[C1.3] Crewed flight also makes robotic science better, not just competitive with it. Hubble did not survive for decades on robotic brilliance alone; astronauts replaced its optics, gyros, and instruments, turning a flawed telescope into a revolution. The next leaps — telescopes assembled in orbit, observatories serviced on the lunar far side, probes repaired instead of abandoned — require hands, judgment, and infrastructure only a serious human program builds and maintains.
[C1.4] Finally, the proposed transfer is a budgetary illusion. The large exploration budget exists politically because nations send people. Remove most of that commitment and you do not free a fortune for probes; you remove the public purpose, industrial base, launch cadence, and workforce probes depend on, and invite the whole budget to shrink. Robotic flagships are not immune to overruns either — JWST consumed billions and a decade of delay — but they fly inside an ecosystem sustained by crewed exploration.
[C1.5] Shift most funding away and you get cheaper data for a while, at the price of losing the capability to do the hardest science at all.

[P2.1] The astronaut-as-instrument argument fails on its own arithmetic. Yes, Apollo crews returned hundreds of kilograms of samples—but at a program cost of roughly a quarter-trillion dollars in today's money. Those samples were a triumph, but if a comparable sum had bought hundreds of targeted robotic sample returns, distributed across the Moon's surface instead of six sites, the science haul would have dwarfed Apollo's. Perseverance caching tubes "and hoping" for retrieval is a fair jab, but the answer is another robotic retrieval mission—which is still cheaper than one crewed landing by an order of magnitude.
[P2.2] The astronaut is versatile; the astronaut is also the most expensive instrument ever flown, and versatility you cannot afford at scale is not a science strategy.
[P2.3] The Hubble example is even worse for CON. Servicing Hubble required five shuttle flights at over a billion dollars each; for less than the total servicing cost, we likely could have built and launched a fresh observatory—and JWST's successor projects show that is where the field is actually heading. Robotic telescopes now vastly outnumber serviced ones. servicing was a workaround for 1980s limits, not a template.
[P2.4] The "budgetary illusion" argument quietly concedes the case. If crewed flight survives because of politics rather than science, then every dollar it captures is a dollar of political capital spent on something other than discovery—and the honest response is to spend it where the science actually comes from. Moreover, the claim that probes depend on a crewed ecosystem is backward: Mars rovers, New Horizons, Voyager, and JWST were built and launched without requiring crewed infrastructure. The launch vehicles, workforce, and industrial base exist for robotic missions on their own economics.
[P2.5] Finally, CON admits JWST overran—and still calls it a victory inside "the ecosystem." JWST delayed and overran yet still delivered more transformational science per dollar than any crewed program of the last forty years. That concession ends the argument: even robotic flagship bloat beats crewed efficiency.
[P2.6] Shift most, not all. But shift.

[C2.1] Their Apollo arithmetic charges the cost of inventing an entire human spaceflight system to kilograms of rock, then compares it to an imaginary fleet of cheap robotic sample returns. That fleet does not exist. Fetching Perseverance’s tubes proved so costly and complex the architecture had to be rethought. A pre-programmed lander cannot range miles, read geological context, discard the ordinary, and seize the anomaly.
[C2.2] Apollo bought a continuing capability; charging it all to its first samples is like charging a laboratory’s construction to its first experiment.
[C2.3] Hubble servicing makes the same accounting error in reverse. Each mission installed a new generation of cameras and spectrographs in an existing precision observatory — effectively a new telescope without rebuilding and launching the mirror and spacecraft. That turned a flawed instrument into a multi-decade revolution. Disposable replacement is not proven cheaper; it is the unrescuable risk JWST was forced to accept.
[C2.4] Political sustainability is not a concession. “Shift most” delivers more science only if the money actually shifts. After Apollo, overall space support contracted; planetary science did not inherit a windfall, it fought for scraps. A better ratio on a much smaller pie can mean fewer probes, fewer telescopes, and less total discovery.
[C2.5] Rovers launching uncrewed do not prove independence. They rely on heavy launchers, deep-space communications, radioisotope power, test facilities, and engineering teams sustained by a broad program. Spread those fixed capabilities over a diminished program and each robot becomes more expensive, not freer.
[C2.6] Finally, comparing JWST’s remote sensing to a low-Earth-orbit station rigs the category. A telescope cannot drill Mars, sort cores, repair its drill, or do field geology with real-time judgment. Science per dollar matters per question — and for the hardest surface questions, the astronaut is not overhead. The astronaut is how the question gets answered at all.

[P4.2] Crewed programs' flagship outputs are largely about keeping humans alive in space, which is circular: the science of human spaceflight justifies human spaceflight. [P4.3] A2: No mechanism guarantees transfer—but no mechanism guarantees the status quo either. The counterfactual CON relies on is post-Apollo contraction, which followed a Cold War race ending, not a deliberate reallocation. Public attachment is to discovery, not merely to people in capsules.
[P4.4] And even granting the worst case—a smaller pie—the honest question is whether shrinking budgets on better science still beats larger budgets spent on orbiting people. Given that station science has been modest even at full funding, yes.
[P4.5] These answers expose CON's central weakness: every escape route runs through uncertainty. Measurement is hard, transfer is uncertain, the fleet doesn't exist yet—so keep spending on the expensive thing we know underdelivers. That is an argument for inertia, not for science.
