[P1.1] Every data-privacy debate until now has shared one saving grace: you could, in principle, control what you revealed. You chose what to type, click, and buy. EEG headbands and neural earbuds capture involuntary responses—attention spikes, emotional arousal, fatigue, craving—that users cannot suppress, cannot see, and often cannot even articulate.
[P1.2] That difference is the whole debate, and it is why click-through consent, the fig leaf that barely covers ordinary data collection, collapses entirely here.
[P1.3] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, consent requires understanding, and no consumer can understand what brain data will reveal. A user consents to "improve your experience" without knowing the same signals can indicate emotional vulnerability, early cognitive decline, or susceptibility to impulse purchases. You cannot meaningfully waive access to information you don't know you're transmitting. Consent that cannot be informed is not consent—it's a liability shield.
[P1.5] Second, the prohibited uses are precisely the exploitative ones. Advertising and dynamic pricing built on neural data don't just observe preferences; they detect the moment your impulse control dips and strike then. A retailer that can see your craving peak and raise the price in that instant isn't serving you—it's picking your pocket with your own nervous system. Markets presume a bargaining self capable of saying no; neural targeting is engineered to bypass that self.
[P1.6] Consent given yesterday cannot protect the person being manipulated tonight.
[P1.7] Third, we already accept bright-line prohibitions where consent fails structurally. You cannot sell your organs or consent to a rigged loan. Polygraph results are barred from most employment decisions. Society draws hard lines where the power asymmetry makes "agreement" a formality.
[P1.8] The mind deserves at least the protection we give kidneys.
[P1.9] Notice what this prohibition leaves untouched: medical uses, accessibility tools, gaming, wellness features. It bans only the monetization of thought against the thinker. That is a narrow rule protecting the last private space humans have. Defend the line here, or there is no line left.

[C1.2] If the concern is sensitive inference, the answer is strong privacy rules for inferences, not a categorical ban on an entire category of beneficial use.
[C1.3] PRO's case also assumes click-through consent is worthless. But consent is not all-or-nothing. We routinely make consent meaningful where information is complex: layered disclosures, plain-language summaries, purpose limits, revocable opt-ins, independent audits, and heavy penalties for misuse. A user may not understand every algorithmic implication, but she can understand, “We may use your focus data to personalize content and ads; you can opt out.” The right response to weak consent is better consent, not permanent prohibition.

Dynamic pricing is not only price gouging; it can mean discounts for students, off-peak users, price-sensitive shoppers, or loyalty rewards. [C1.5] It would also entrench large platforms that can substitute coarse behavioral data, while blocking smaller neurotech firms from offering the precise, consented personalization that makes their products valuable.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO's bright-line analogies fail. We ban coercion, fraud, and unreliable tests—not voluntary commercial adaptation. The proposition does not target manipulation; it forbids even transparent, opt-in, welfare-enhancing uses. That is too blunt, too paternalistic, and too costly.
[C1.7] The stronger path is regulate abuse, require real choice, and let consenting adults benefit from neurotechnology.
