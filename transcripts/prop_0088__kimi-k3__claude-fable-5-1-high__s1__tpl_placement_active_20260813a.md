[P1.1] A school district that adopts a four-day week is making a trade: student learning on one side, operational convenience on the other. The proposition holds because that trade is lopsided — the learning losses are real and compounding, while the savings are marginal and the retention gains unproven.
[P1.2] Start with the learning harm. Four-day schedules rarely preserve instructional time; they compress it. Longer days hit diminishing returns fast — a nine-year-old's seventh hour is worth a fraction of her first. Meanwhile, a three-day weekend every week widens the gap in which forgetting happens, and forgetting falls hardest on exactly the students schools most need to reach: struggling readers, English learners, kids without books or quiet space at home.
[P1.3] Add what schools quietly provide beyond instruction — a fifth day of meals, supervision, structure — and the weekly cost to low-income students includes hunger and lost stability, not just lost minutes. Learning damage like this compounds year over year; a cohort doesn't get those weeks back.

Districts don't fix structural deficits by turning off the lights one extra day; they paper over them.

Their realistic alternative to four days is five days with a long-term substitute in the only algebra classroom, a librarian teaching chemistry, and a bus route cut anyway.
[C1.2] That reframing matters because the learning evidence is far more modest than the motion implies. Studies across Oklahoma, Colorado, Missouri, and Oregon districts generally find effects clustered near zero, with small losses concentrated in districts that shortened total instructional hours, and nothing measurable in districts that kept hours roughly constant. The mechanism is not the schedule; it is the hours. A district that protects hours protects learning.
[C1.3] That is a design choice, not a fate.
[C1.4] Now weigh the other side properly. Retention is not a "scheduling perk." A vacancy costs a class an entire year of coherent instruction. Districts consistently report more and better applicants after switching, and teacher turnover is one of the best-documented drags on achievement we have.
[C1.5] If the schedule fills the math position, that is a learning gain, not a cost against learning. Attendance rises too, because dentist appointments, sports travel, and family errands migrate to the fifth day instead of interrupting the school week.
[C1.6] The hunger and supervision concerns are real, and the better districts answer them directly: optional fifth-day tutoring, meals, and enrichment aimed at the struggling readers everyone worries about, delivered in smaller groups than a Tuesday classroom ever offers.
[C1.7] So the honest comparison is this: a small, avoidable, and often absent learning effect against the very staffing stability that makes learning possible where these schools actually exist. That is not a lopsided trade against students. Done right, it is a trade for them.
