[P1.1] Coordinated campaigns on social media routinely cause severe offline harm: swatting, doxxing-driven stalking, mob harassment that forces targets into hiding. These outcomes are foreseeable, often intended. Yet platforms face no legal duty to take reasonable steps to prevent them. That failure leaves victims to bear catastrophic costs alone.
[P1.2] The platforms alone have the system-wide view to detect a campaign as it forms—the surge of coordinated posts, the spread of personal data, the swarm of accounts targeting one person. They profit from the engagement, but the victim pays in shattered safety. A legal duty would flip that incentive: platforms must anticipate and mitigate foreseeable offline injury, not just react after the fact.
[P1.3] This duty is not about policing viewpoints. It activates only when a coordinated campaign creates a clear, specific risk of offline violence or serious harm, and only requires reasonable mitigation—timely removal of doxxing, freezing coordinated accounts, alerting authorities. That mirrors the duty a venue owner has to prevent foreseeable physical violence, a duty we already accept.
[P1.4] Free speech does not shield foreseeable violence. When a platform watches a mob assemble and withholds even minimal intervention, it externalizes the danger onto real bodies. Current law, including Section 230, grants near-absolute immunity, leaving victims with no recourse. A legal duty would provide accountability and a deterrent against inaction.
[P1.5] If we require factories to prevent foreseeable spills and hospitals to prevent foreseeable falls, exempting the most powerful amplifiers of social coordination when they host coordinated attacks makes no sense. The offline harm is real. A duty to mitigate it is a long-overdue application of a basic principle: those who can prevent devastating harm must take reasonable care to do so.
