[P1.1] The heart of this debate is one word in the motion: "admit." Everyone concedes remote work has trade-offs. What people underestimate is how badly the losses fall on the youngest workers, and how invisible those losses are until years later.
[P1.2] Consider how a junior actually learns. Not from the formal training module, but from the thousand small transmissions that happen in physical proximity: overhearing how a senior colleague defuses an angry client, being pulled into a meeting last-minute, having your rough draft marked up over your shoulder, catching the offhand "don't do it that way" before you make the mistake. This is apprenticeship by osmosis, and it is precisely what a screen cannot replicate. A twenty-three-year-old at home gets the assigned task and the scheduled call.
[P1.3] They do not get the ambient education that turns a competent beginner into a trusted professional.
[P1.4] Now the "admit" problem. Two forces conspire to hide the damage. First, employers who invested heavily in remote infrastructure have every incentive to declare it a success; admitting harm to juniors means admitting a costly mistake. Second, juniors themselves cannot report a loss they cannot see.
[P1.5] You do not feel the promotion you never got offered, or the mentor relationship that never formed, or the network of senior sponsors you never built. The harm is a counterfactual, and counterfactuals do not show up in the satisfaction surveys that both sides cite as reassurance.
[P1.6] The consequences compound. Careers advance through visibility and sponsorship—someone senior who says your name in the room where decisions are made. Remote juniors are structurally less visible, easier to overlook for stretch assignments, and slower to build the reputational capital that early years are supposed to bank. Miss that window and the disadvantage doesn't reset; it accrues.
[P1.7] I am not arguing WFH is bad for everyone. I am arguing something narrower and more defensible: for the specific population of junior employees, in the specific dimension of long-term career development, the cost is real, largely uncounted, and worse than the comfortable consensus admits.
