[P1.1] A courier delivering groceries in a February ice storm faces exactly the risks unemployment insurance was invented for: demand collapses, the app stops sending orders, an injury takes away the ability to work for six weeks. What she doesn't face is coverage. Not because her risk is different from a warehouse worker's, but because of a contractual label chosen by the firm that routes her work and sets her pay.
[P1.2] That's the whole case. Social insurance is a risk-pooling system, and pools only work if they track exposure to risk rather than job titles. Every hour of economically dependent work that sits outside the pool is a hole in the roof.
[P1.3] Second, exclusion isn't neutral — it's a subsidy. A firm that classifies its workforce as contractors pays nothing into unemployment or pension funds; its competitor that hires employees pays in full. That gap is a straightforward cost advantage for whoever contracts out of the safety net, which pushes the whole labor market in that direction. Requiring contributions from platform earnings doesn't add a distortion; it removes one.
[P1.4] And when platform workers lose income anyway, the cost doesn't vanish — it lands on emergency assistance, food aid, and medical debt, paid by everyone, just later and less efficiently.
[P1.5] Third, why standardized earnings records specifically: because the usual excuse for exclusion is administrative impossibility, and platforms have already destroyed that excuse. They know, to the cent and the minute, what each worker earned, when, and from which company. That is better data than most traditional payrolls generate. Standardization solves the genuinely hard part — a person driving for two apps and cleaning through a third accumulates a single, portable earnings history rather than three unusable fragments.
[P1.6] The pandemic already ran this experiment. Governments improvised emergency programs for gig workers because the existing machinery couldn't see them: slow, leaky, fraud-prone, and built from scratch. Build the plumbing before the next flood, not during it.

[C1.1] PRO treats platform work like traditional employment, ignoring its defining feature: total autonomy. Unemployment insurance relies on a specific mutual contract. The employer controls your schedule, and in return, funds your safety net when they cut your hours. Platform workers control their own hours.
[C1.2] You cannot insure against a loss of hours when the worker holds the clock. If a driver chooses to log off, pursues another gig, or rejects low-paying tasks, they aren't "unemployed" in the actuarial sense; they are exercising autonomy. Forcing independent contractors into a system designed for rigid schedule control creates massive moral hazard and breaks the risk pool.
[C1.3] Furthermore, PRO claims platforms have perfect data to the cent [P1.5], but this conflates gross revenue with insurable wages. A standardized earnings record for a courier doesn't account for vehicle depreciation, fuel, maintenance, or the unpaid time spent waiting for an algorithm. If we mandate social insurance based on gross platform payouts, we are taxing revenue, not actual income. Conversely, if the government tries to standardize net earnings across thousands of highly individualized expense profiles, the administrative impossibility PRO dismisses returns with a vengeance, drowning the system in endless disputes over deductible business expenses.
[C1.4] Finally, this mandate destroys the very flexibility that makes platform work economically valuable. These platforms serve as a crucial transitional buffer for students, caregivers, and individuals between traditional jobs. Mandating payroll-style social deductions significantly increases the marginal cost of labor. This forces platforms to raise consumer prices, reduce market demand, and restrict open onboarding to only the most profitable hours.
[C1.5] By trying to force gig work into a rigid, twentieth-century employment box, PRO will shrink the exact low-barrier income source that keeps vulnerable populations afloat. We should not break the ladder just because it doesn't look like a traditional staircase.
