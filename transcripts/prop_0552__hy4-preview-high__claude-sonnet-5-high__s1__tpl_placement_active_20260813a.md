[P1.1] E-waste export is not trade; it is a toxic subsidy: rich countries buy cheap disposal by shipping poison to places least able to refuse it. When a rich country exports a container of dead phones and circuit boards, the sticker price looks cheap only because the real costs—lead exposure, dioxins, mercury in soil and water—are dumped onto communities with weaker regulators and little political voice. Importing e-waste is not the same as importing a good; it is importing a hazard that often arrives labeled "used electronics" or "donations."
[P1.2] Once it lands, profitable recovery in lower-income destinations means open burning, acid stripping, and unregulated dismantling, not high-tech recycling. That is the core reason for the ban: export shifts unaccountable harm to poorer countries under the cover of recycling.
[P1.3] The usual defenses collapse on inspection. The claim that exports create livelihoods romanticizes survival: pulling copper from flames or picking through toxic residue is not a development ladder, it is occupational disease. Certification sounds better, but hazardous-waste experience shows labels are easy to forge and loads easy to mix; under-resourced customs cannot police thousands of containers for hidden batteries or toxic flame retardants. A ban to lower-income countries removes that loophole rather than pretending paper certificates equal protection.
[P1.4] Requiring domestic recycling or certified regional processing changes the incentive. Rich consumer societies generate the waste and have the capital, engineers, and legal reach to manage it safely. Keeping materials in accountable systems spurs investment in automation, repair, refurbishment, and advanced recovery of copper, gold, and critical minerals. Those are safer, higher-skill jobs than informal burning, and they reduce virgin mining that often scars poor regions.
[P1.5] When producers and consumers must pay true costs, products get designed for longer life and easier disassembly. This is not protectionism; it refuses to let a toxic externality be someone else's problem, and forces the circular economy to start at home with real rules, not exported risk.

[C1.3] Second, PRO's frame collapses "e-waste" into pure hazard, but most of this trade is used electronics with real remaining life. A five-year-old laptop or smartphone shipped to Lagos or Manila isn't dumped poison—it's affordable computing for people a domestic-only regime prices out entirely. [C1.4] Third, "domestic recycling or certified regional processing" already exists as an option under the Basel Convention framework—rich countries can already choose it. [C1.5] Fourth, domestic recycling is not automatically safer—it's just safer to regulate. Rich countries have their own illegal dumping, their own under-inspected scrapyards. [C1.6] The real fix is legal, monitored, certified trade with investment in inspection and capacity—not a wall that hands the whole market to smugglers.
