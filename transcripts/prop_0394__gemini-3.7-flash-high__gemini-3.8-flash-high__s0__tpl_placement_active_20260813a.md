[P1.1] Access to education should not depend on a student's ability to navigate bureaucratic gatekeeping or secure a medical diagnosis just to review what was taught in class. Establishing a presumptive baseline for captions, lecture recordings, and accessible materials aligns institutional practice with the core purpose of teaching: ensuring knowledge is accurately understood and retained.
[P1.2] First, presumptive access operationalizes universal design. The traditional accommodations model is reactive, slow, and stigmatizing. Students with fluctuating health conditions, caretaking duties, or language barriers are often stranded while navigating formal paperwork. Making accessible formats the default removes this friction.
[P1.3] Captions and recordings instantly support disabled students without bureaucratic delay, while simultaneously aiding non-native speakers, commuters, and neurodivergent learners.
[P1.4] Second, multimodal materials dramatically improve cognitive processing. A live lecture is a transient data stream. Expecting students to simultaneously listen, synthesize complex ideas, and take verbatim notes impairs deep comprehension. Providing recordings and searchable captions allows students to control their cognitive load—revisiting difficult technical derivations, verifying terminology, and engaging in targeted review.
[P1.5] These tools do not replace attendance; they reinforce mastery.
[P1.6] Crucially, this framework is practical and balanced. The motion includes an explicit exception: instructors may withhold recordings when there is a genuine pedagogical justification. In sensitive seminar discussions, role-playing simulations, or confidential case studies where recording could chill candid participation, faculty retain full discretion to adapt. What this policy dismantles is not academic freedom, but unexamined institutional inertia—the arbitrary refusal to provide basic tools simply out of custom.
[P1.7] When modern tools make learning universally accessible at minimal cost, the baseline must favor inclusion. The burden should fall on justifying exclusion, not on students begging for the resources they need to learn.
