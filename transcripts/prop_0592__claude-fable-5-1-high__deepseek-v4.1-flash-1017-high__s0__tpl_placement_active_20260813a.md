[P1.1] A sanction that arrives six weeks late is not a sanction; it is a courtesy notice. Aggressor states do not wait for twenty-seven capitals to finish bargaining. They move money, reroute tankers, and dig in. The unanimity rule turns the European Union's single most powerful non-military tool into a weapon with a safety catch that any one member can keep locked indefinitely.
[P1.2] The core problem is structural, not personal. Unanimity hands every member a veto, and a veto is a bargaining chip. Once a government realises it can trade its signature for concessions on unrelated files, it will. That is exactly what happened repeatedly after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine: packages stalled for weeks over exemptions for particular pipelines, particular oligarchs, particular patriarchs, while the aggressor adapted.
[P1.3] In 2020, sanctions on the Lukashenko regime for a stolen election sat frozen for weeks because one member wanted leverage on a completely separate dispute in the Eastern Mediterranean. Nobody disputed that the election was stolen. The hostage was still taken.
[P1.4] Qualified majority voting breaks that incentive. Under the EU's standard threshold, a measure needs a large majority of states representing roughly two-thirds of the population. That is not a steamroller; it is an enormous coalition. Small states retain real protection through blocking minorities, and the treaties already contain an emergency brake allowing a government to escalate to leaders for genuinely vital national interests.
[P1.5] What vanishes is the ability of a single capital to hold everyone else hostage over something unrelated.
[P1.6] Credibility is the second stake. Deterrence works only if an adversary believes the response will come. When a hostile power knows it can split one member off through energy contracts or flattery, the threat of sanctions discounts to near zero before anything is adopted. Majority voting makes the threat believable, which means sanctions may deter rather than merely punish.
[P1.7] The treaties already permit this shift without rewriting them. The question is whether Europe prefers the appearance of unity to the capacity to act.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a faster vote for a stronger sanction. Foreign-policy sanctions are not legislation passed in Brussels and automatically applied; they are coercive measures that must be enforced by customs, banks, courts, and police in every member state. That is why unanimity matters. It is not a courtesy to aggressors; it is the commitment device that makes a sanction stick.
[C1.2] A government outvoted on a measure it believes threatens its energy supply, its shipping industry, or its constitutional neutrality has little reason to enforce it vigorously—and every reason to seek loopholes, delay licensing, or quietly signal that the measure is not really Europe's policy. The result is a sanction on paper with a dissenting state as its weakest link.
[C1.3] PRO's hostage examples prove bargaining happens; they do not prove majority voting cures it. Under QMV the outvoted state does not suddenly become a loyal enforcer. It simply loses the formal veto and keeps the practical one: non-implementation. The emergency brake does not save the proposal either.
[C1.4] If it is used, we are back to unanimity and the delay PRO condemns; if it is too vague to use, it offers no real protection for vital national interests and makes the whole system less legitimate. Either way, QMV does not deliver the decisive, credible sanctions it promises.
[C1.5] There is also a deeper cost: sanctions imposed over a member's sustained objection invite the aggressor to exploit that split. Deterrence depends on the adversary believing Europe will hold the line, not merely that it can assemble a majority on day one. Unanimity forces concessions before adoption, but those concessions buy ownership. QMV may produce a headline faster and a hollow regime sooner.
[C1.6] The choice is not unity versus paralysis; it is binding collective action versus a majority vote that one reluctant state can still quietly unmake.
