[P1.1] Unpaid family care is not a spare public resource sitting unused in the kitchen. It is already stretched thin, and governments that keep treating it as free labor are budgeting for a collapse they will later pay for in the most expensive places: emergency wards, nursing homes, and workers who never return.
[P1.2] When an older parent develops dementia or a disabled relative needs lifting, bathing, medication, and night-time watch, someone still has to do that work. That someone is usually a woman of working age. She cuts hours or quits. Her earnings and pension credits vanish.
[P1.3] Her own health erodes. The public accounts look cheaper this year only because the cost has been shifted onto her body and her future. That is not fiscal prudence. It is an off-the-books tax levied on the people least able to refuse.
[P1.4] The assumption that relatives will keep absorbing the burden is already failing. Families are smaller. People live longer with complex needs. Adult children live farther away and hold jobs that cannot be done from a sickbed.
[P1.5] Formal care is rationed on the quiet premise that kin will fill the gap. Kin do fill it—until burnout, injury, or poverty ends the arrangement. Then the state inherits a crisis it refused to price honestly.
[P1.6] Paying family caregivers more generously is not a sentimental bonus. It is the cheaper way to keep care in the community instead of forcing a late, costly dump into institutions. It treats care as labor rather than a gendered duty. It gives people a real choice between staying at work, sharing care, or providing it without being driven into hardship.
[P1.7] Love is not the issue. A son can love his mother and still need rent. Affection does not pay the bills or replace a lost career. Exhaustion and destitution crowd out care far more effectively than a wage ever could.
[P1.8] If governments want families to keep carrying this load, they have to stop pretending the load has no price.
