[P1.1] Every city that has priced its core faced furious drivers beforehand, and almost none has reversed course afterward. That pattern is the whole debate. Stockholm's trial began with majorities against it; after residents saw emptier streets and faster buses, a referendum kept it. London's charge was supposed to end a mayoralty; it outlived three.
[P1.2] Opposition to congestion pricing peaks on the day before it starts and drains away once people experience the thing itself. A policy whose approval rises with exposure is not one a city should abandon because of how it polls in the abstract.
[P1.3] Consider what "driver opposition" actually represents. In a central district, drivers are a minority of the people present: the majority arrive by transit, by foot, by bike, or simply live there. The driver sitting in gridlock imposes delay on every bus behind him, exhaust on every lung nearby, and risk on every pedestrian crossing. Free road space means rationing by queue, where everyone pays in wasted hours and nobody gets anything back.
[P1.4] Pricing rations by willingness to pay, keeps the road moving for ambulances, deliveries, and buses, and converts the lost time into revenue that can fund the transit alternative. Letting the people who create the cost veto the remedy is not democracy; it is capture.
[P1.5] Nor does "strongly oppose" mean "mostly oppose." Concentrated, vocal losers always outshout diffuse winners. A nurse whose bus now runs on schedule doesn't attend council hearings; the suburban commuter who loses a free lane does. A city that governs by decibel will never build anything with upfront costs and spread-out gains, which describes nearly every worthwhile urban reform.
[P1.6] So the test is simple: does the policy work, and does the public come to support it once it exists? On congestion pricing, the answer to both is yes. Cities should act, absorb the noise, and let results do the persuading.
