[P1.1] Every year, cities pay out large sums to settle lawsuits over excessive force, wrongful arrests, and fabricated evidence. The bills land on municipal budgets, meaning on residents who had nothing to do with the misconduct, and often on the very neighborhoods most policed. The officer who caused the harm typically pays nothing. His paycheck is untouched; his pension is untouched; in many cases his personnel file barely notes it.
[P1.2] That is the status quo, and it is indefensible on its own terms: we have built a system in which the person with the most control over the risk bears the least of its cost.
[P1.3] Required personal liability insurance fixes that mismatch the way we already fix it for surgeons, lawyers, truckers, and contractors. Nobody thinks malpractice coverage means we distrust doctors. It means someone with money at stake continuously evaluates whether a professional is becoming dangerous. Insurers are not ideologues.

[P1.6] Compare that to the current disciplinary pipeline: internal review boards, union arbitration, civilian oversight with no teeth, and chiefs who cannot fire known problem officers because arbitrators reinstate them. [C1.1] PRO’s analogy to surgeons and truckers is seductive, but it collapses on contact with how policing actually works. Doctors and lawyers are private professionals serving private clients. Police are state actors executing statutory duties under public authority. When an officer violates rights, it is the government—not the individual in isolation—that has breached a constitutional duty.
[C1.2] The badge is not a franchise an officer operates; it is a commission from the public. A city cannot outsource that public liability to an insurance policy and call the problem solved.
[C1.3] Worse, PRO concedes the fatal flaw. They admit in P1.7 that “the city remains the deep pocket for catastrophic cases.” If municipal budgets still fund the catastrophic settlements, taxpayers still pay. The policy does not stop costs from falling on the public; it merely adds a middleman who takes a cut.
[C1.4] The “market discipline” mechanism is also backwards. Insurers do not price constitutional judgment; they price claims exposure. Premiums will rise for officers in high-crime precincts, those who work undercover, and those who use force in legitimate but risky arrests. The result is not a purge of dangerous officers.
[C1.5] It is an incentive to avoid the very work communities need, or to litigate every complaint aggressively because a sustained finding now threatens livelihood.
[C1.6] Police accountability is a public function. We already possess the tools: civil-rights litigation, criminal prosecution, federal consent decrees, and stronger local oversight. If discipline is weak, the answer is to build democratic and legal capacity, not to hand private actuaries unaccountable veto power over who may serve. Discipline must answer to voters and courts, not to quarterly earnings targets.
[C1.7] This motion does not allocate costs fairly. It forces taxpayers to fund insurance while leaving them on the hook for the same settlements, and it replaces accountable institutions with profit-driven underwriting.
