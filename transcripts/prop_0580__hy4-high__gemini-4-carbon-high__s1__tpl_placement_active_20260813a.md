[P1.1] Child poverty is an income shortfall: rent, food, utilities, and diapers keep arriving while family income is too low or volatile. A monthly child tax credit targets that shortfall directly, adding cash when bills are due. Childcare subsidies target one possible use of cash, and only when an approved provider, qualifying schedule, paperwork trail, and available slot all line up.
[P1.2] That is why cash is more cost-effective per poverty dollar. An expanded credit needs a payment system and an income test; the marginal dollar reaches the household budget. Subsidy dollars must move through provider reimbursement, eligibility checks, billing, quality rules, and capacity constraints. In tight markets, some spending buys higher prices or new slots, not income relief.
[P1.3] It can miss families whose poverty reflects job loss, disability, irregular hours, or a childcare desert. The transfer is filtered; the credit is direct.
[P1.4] Monthly delivery is also the mechanism. Poverty is not an annual reconciliation problem. A parent facing a Thursday grocery gap or rent deadline needs predictable cash before the crisis, not a subsidy claim after care is arranged. Regular payments smooth volatile earnings, prevent arrears and high-cost borrowing, and let families choose the binding need: food, a utility bill, or the childcare copay that blocks work.
[P1.5] That flexibility is precision; the same dollar solves whichever shortage actually keeps the child poor.
[P1.6] Childcare subsidies can promote employment and care quality, but those are different metrics with longer causal chains. A voucher for formal care does little for a parent between jobs, caring for siblings, working nights, or living where licensed slots are scarce. A modest monthly credit can pay rent or the exact copay that matters; an equivalent subsidy exists only if the right service exists and can be used. Direct, recurring income support beats conditional spending on one service.
[P1.7] For poverty reduction per public dollar, put cash where poverty is measured: the child’s household budget, monthly.
