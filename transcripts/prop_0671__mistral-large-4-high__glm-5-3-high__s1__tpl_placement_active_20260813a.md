[P1.1] The earliest years of schooling are not a testing ground for technology; they are the foundation of how children learn to think. When we put tablets in the hands of kindergarteners and first graders, we are not preparing them for the future—we are short-circuiting the very cognitive architecture they need to build that future. A device-light policy in early grades recognizes that childhood is not a beta test for software.
[P1.2] Paper is not nostalgia; it is neurology. Learning to form letters by hand activates motor and memory pathways that typing does not engage in the same way. Research on learning consistently finds that students who write by hand demonstrate deeper comprehension and better retention than those who type, because the slower pace of handwriting forces the brain to process and synthesize information rather than mechanically transcribe it. In early grades, when brains are wiring themselves for literacy and numeracy, this difference is not marginal—it is foundational.
[P1.3] A child who cannot feel the resistance of a pencil learning to write is missing a tactile anchor for language itself.
[P1.4] Screens also fragment the attention that early learning demands. A tablet is essentially a casino designed for children: notifications, animations, and infinite scroll compete for the developing prefrontal cortex. Early readers need sustained, focused engagement to decode text, build fluency, and develop the patience to wrestle with difficulty. Device-light classrooms protect that cognitive space, teaching children that learning requires effort rather than escape, and that boredom is a signal to dig deeper, not to refresh.
[P1.5] Consider equity as well. One-to-one tablets assume every child has a quiet home, reliable Wi-Fi, and parental tech support to manage digital homework. Paper works everywhere—it requires no charging, survives drops, and functions without broadband. Prioritizing paper in early grades ensures we are not sorting children by their zip codes before they have even learned to read, and it prevents the classroom from becoming a showcase of who has the newest device.
