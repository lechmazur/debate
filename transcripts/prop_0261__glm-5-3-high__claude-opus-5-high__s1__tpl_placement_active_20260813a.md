[P1.1] When someone buys a bookshelf of physical books, no one disputes that those books pass to their children. Yet when the same person spends decades building a digital library—thousands of dollars of ebooks, music, films, and games—the platforms say those purchases vanish at death. This isn't a quirk of technology. It's a legal vacuum, and the proposition is how we close it.
[P1.2] Start with ownership. Consumers pay real money for these assets. They are described as purchases, not rentals, and priced like purchases. The only thing non-transferable about them is a license term drafted by the seller in a contract of adhesion the buyer never negotiated.
[P1.3] To let that boilerplate nullify inheritance—something no physical-goods contract could ever do—is to let private terms override a foundational feature of property law. Estates have passed assets to heirs for centuries precisely because the dead person's wishes deserve respect. A "Buy" button that delivers nothing inheritable is misrepresentation dressed as commerce.
[P1.4] Second, dignity and autonomy. A digital library is often a person's intellectual life: their reading history, their taste, their curiosities. Denying next of kin any claim treats that life as disposable on death, and treats grieving families as irrelevant. The law already recognizes fiduciary access to digital records in several jurisdictions; extending inheritance rights to purchased assets is the natural completion of that principle, not a radical leap.
[P1.5] Third, the counterargument that these are mere licenses fails on its own terms. The remedy is not to surrender to bad licensing—it is to require terms compatible with the legal system they operate within. We regulate what contracts can do all the time: you cannot contract out of minimum wage, of habitability, of consumer protection. Saying "the contract forbids it" proves only that the contract needs fixing.
[P1.6] What would this actually require? A statutory right of succession to purchased digital content, administered by platforms through simple verification—death certificate plus proof of kinship—exactly as banks already handle accounts. The estate receives access or a transfer, subject to the same single-user terms the decedent accepted. No piracy [clipped]
