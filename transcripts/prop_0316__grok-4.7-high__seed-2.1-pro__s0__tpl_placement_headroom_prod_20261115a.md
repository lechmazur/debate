[P1.1] A person uploads photos, writes a few honest lines, and waits. Matches come slowly or not at all. They change the lighting and wonder what is wrong with them. What they are not told is that an AI has already scored their face, predicted their reply rate, and decided how often, and to whom, their profile appears.
[P1.2] That silence is the injury.
[P1.3] Users should have a right to know when a dating app uses AI to rank their profile or shape who gets shown to them. Visibility on these apps is opportunity, and opportunity is intimacy, status, and sometimes safety. When a company inserts a model between two people, it decides whose lives intersect. No one can meaningfully choose an app, a subscription, or a strategy without knowing a machine is doing that sorting.
[P1.4] The right is narrow, not a demand for source code or proprietary weights. It is a clear disclosure that AI ranks profiles and shapes exposure: who is boosted, who is buried, who is shown first, who is filtered out. Without that fact, a quiet inbox is misread as personal failure. People blame their looks or their wit when the cause may be an engagement model built to maximize time on the app, not mutual interest.
[P1.5] Opacity also hides bias. Ranking systems learn from past swipes, and those swipes encode preferences and prejudices about race, body, age, and class. Users cannot contest what they cannot see operating. Disclosure does not erase those patterns, but it makes them legible.
[P1.6] Someone who learns an attractiveness score governs their reach can leave or refuse to rent that visibility back.
[P1.7] Trade secrets do not defeat a right this small. Ads are labeled and credit scoring is disclosed; romance is more personal, not less. A fair courtship does not leave users guessing.

[C1.1] A generic disclosure that AI ranks profiles and curates matches is not a meaningful right—it is a useless, performative checkbox that delivers none of the benefits proponents promise, while opening the door to harms that erode the very purpose of dating apps. Vague notice does nothing to end the self-blame proponents decry, because it tells users nothing about why their profile is buried. [C1.2] It does not enable meaningful choice, either: every major dating platform already uses some form of algorithmic ranking, so there is no realistic opt-out short of abandoning online dating entirely, just as there is no opt-out from search algorithms on Google or recommendation systems on streaming services. Once users know AI ranking shapes visibility, scammers will optimize every part of their profile to trick the model into boosting them—using fake AI-generated photos, scripted bios, and swipe-farming tactics to score high visibility, pushing genuine users further down the queue and overwhelming feeds with people not looking for real connection.
[C1.3] Ad and credit disclosures work because those systems attach to regulated, contestable decision criteria. A vague “we use AI” label on a dating app is equivalent to labeling a restaurant “we use spices”: technically true, totally uninformative, and a distraction from actual guardrails against bias and exploitative design that would help users far more than a hollow right to a useless notice.
