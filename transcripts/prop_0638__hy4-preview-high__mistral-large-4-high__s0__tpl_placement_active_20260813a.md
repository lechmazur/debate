[P1.1] Governments should not run fundamental science like a procurement office. They fund the unknown; you cannot contract the unknown in two- or three-year deliverables. That is the central clash: short-cycle competitive project grants pretend science is predictable, while long-term block grants accept uncertainty and cultivate it.
[P1.2] Under project grants, a researcher must promise specific aims, milestones, and outcomes just to be renewed. Rational scientists respond by proposing safe, incremental work whose results they can already almost see. The truly fertile question—the strange anomaly, the hunch that might rewrite a field—is defunded because it cannot be scheduled. Worse, the system burns a large share of research time on writing, reviewing, and re-reviewing proposals.
[P1.3] Months lost to grant cycles are months not spent thinking, mentoring, or running experiments. Block grants flip this incentive. Stable funding lets a lab pursue a surprising result, maintain scarce expertise, train students over years, and invest in shared instruments.
[P1.4] Consider LIGO. Its detection of gravitational waves rested on decades of sustained detector physics and engineering, most of it before any payoff was visible. A short-cycle grant regime would have strangled a machine whose main product for years was null results. The same logic underwrote CRISPR, which grew from basic curiosity about bacterial defenses, and underpinned the RNA science behind modern vaccines.
[P1.5] None of these were auditable milestones; all were cultivated bets.
[P1.6] The motion concedes less accountability per dollar, but that concedes the wrong kind of accountability. Reporting against guessed milestones measures compliance, not discovery; a negative result can be scientifically vital yet look like failure in a project ledger. Block grants still impose accountability—peer review of institutions and leaders, retrospective assessment, openness, training, and long-run output—but judge the work as science rather than as a purchase order. If the public goal is transformative knowledge rather than auditable busyness, the trade is not merely acceptable; it is essential.

Once a laboratory secures stable funding, it enjoys a funding monopoly regardless of productivity.

[C1.7] The motion concedes "less accountability per dollar." That is not a peripheral cost—it is the price of democratic legitimacy. Science needs freedom to explore, but it also needs discipline to deliver.
