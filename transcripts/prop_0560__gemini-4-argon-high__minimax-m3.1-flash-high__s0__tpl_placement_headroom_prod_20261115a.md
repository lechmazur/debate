[P1.1] Healthcare budgets are finite, meaning every allocation is a choice between competing lives. The question is not whether to care for the dying, since hospice and palliative support remain foundational, but where the next marginal dollar does the most good. Prioritizing preventive public health over marginal expansions of high-cost end-of-life care saves vastly more life-years, preserves human dignity, and corrects a profound structural inequity.
[P1.2] Consider the stark asymmetry of impact. At the end of life, marginal expansions typically mean funding six-figure therapies or prolonged intensive care for terminal patients. These interventions routinely yield only weeks of additional survival, frequently shadowed by severe toxicity, invasive ventilation, and unnecessary suffering. Conversely, directing those same funds into preventive public health, such as hypertension screening, immunization, smoking cessation, or lead abatement, stops disease before it begins.
[P1.3] Prevention secures decades of vibrant, autonomous living for thousands rather than buying painful weeks for a few. Moreover, preventing chronic illness alleviates the crushing downstream hospitalizations that strain health systems in the first place.
[P1.4] Furthermore, prioritizing prevention corrects a fatal flaw in resource allocation: the bias toward visible crises over invisible suffering. A patient in an intensive care unit commands immediate spending, whereas the victims of neglected public health are statistical and unseen. Nobody celebrates the stroke that never happened because a community clinic controlled their blood pressure. When systems chase costly, low-yield terminal interventions, they starve the upstream programs that lower-income communities rely on most.
[P1.5] Those populations suffer disproportionate rates of preventable disease and premature death, often never reaching the advanced age where high-cost end-of-life care even becomes relevant.
[P1.6] Restraint against marginal, low-yield prolongation is not abandonment. It allows medicine to prioritize comfort at life’s end while investing finite resources where they deliver the greatest return: healthy decades across an entire population.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane, but it mistakes a defensible direction for a settled allocation rule. A budget should compare the expected benefit of the next dollar, not award one category an automatic priority. “Preventive public health measures” range from immunization with immediate, durable benefits to poorly targeted campaigns whose uptake, completion, and health effects are uncertain. “High-cost end-of-life care” ranges from burdensome treatment unlikely to help to therapies that materially extend life or relieve suffering for identifiable patients.
[C1.2] Population averages do not settle these marginal choices. A prevention program may reduce disease across a community, yet its additional dollar may purchase advertising rather than effective screening or treatment. Its benefit may also emerge only years later, and it is contingent on access, adherence, and sustained funding. Conversely, cost is not the same as low value.
[C1.3] A terminal patient may gain many months from targeted cancer treatment, while others gain little; the very heterogeneity that makes average forecasts useful also makes blanket dismissal dangerous. Prognosis is imperfect, and an intervention can prevent an avoidable hospitalization or allow a patient to remain at home, benefits that simple survival measures understate.
[C1.4] There is also a fairness problem. Public-health programs often deliver the largest gains to people who have not yet reached the age or health status where terminal care becomes relevant. Automatically privileging them over severely ill, older, and disabled patients can make age and disability a hidden rationing criterion. The fact that end-of-life treatment is expensive does not license a system to treat the dying as if their claims on care count for less.
[C1.5] The defensible policy is narrower: fund effective prevention, reject demonstrably nonbeneficial and burdensome interventions, and preserve palliative care and patient choice. But the proposition requires evidence that the marginal preventive dollar consistently outperforms the marginal end-of-life dollar across relevant cases. No such categorical result follows merely from prevention being upstream and terminal care being costly. [clipped]
