[P1.1] The motion asks whether states that hold children fully criminally liable at ages far below fourteen should change course. They should, because treating a nine- or ten-year-old as a fully punishable offender ignores developmental reality and produces worse outcomes than protection and intervention.
[P1.2] The core of our case is capacity. Children below roughly fourteen are still developing the very faculties criminal law presupposes: impulse control, foresight, understanding of long-term consequences, and the ability to participate meaningfully in their own defense. A child who cannot reliably distinguish fantasy from reality, or who lacks a mature grasp of death or permanence, cannot meet the mens rea standards that justify criminal punishment. When a legal system pretends otherwise, it does not clarify morality—it manufactures a fiction that harms the child and corrodes the system's legitimacy.
[P1.3] Consider the practical consequences. Some jurisdictions, such as England and Wales, prosecute children as young as ten. In places with such low thresholds, children are placed in punitive settings where they are exposed to violence, abuse, and hardened peer networks. A twelve-year-old who shoplifts or sets a fire is not expressing mature criminality; they are signaling unmet needs, trauma, or developmental disorder.
[P1.4] Criminal labels follow them into adulthood, limiting education, housing, and employment. That is not deterrence. It is a self-fulfilling prophecy of recidivism.
[P1.5] The international consensus is moving the same direction. The overwhelming majority of states set the age at fourteen or higher, and child-rights bodies consistently urge raising low thresholds. The few outliers that treat young children as adult offenders do so not because the evidence supports it, but because of reflexive toughness politics.
[P1.6] We do not deny that young children can cause serious harm. But harm demands a welfare and public-health response, not a criminal one. Fourteen is a defensible floor: old enough to grasp wrongdoing, young enough to preserve childhood where it still matters. Raise the age.

[C1.1] The motion pushes a false, one-size-fits-all choice that ignores both developmental nuance and the rights of vulnerable victims. We do not have to choose between treating all children under fourteen as fully punishable adult offenders and imposing a rigid universal cutoff that lets even thirteen-year-olds who commit severe harm off the hook for any meaningful accountability. Existing systems can be reformed to provide proportionate, age-appropriate responses for serious juvenile offending without resorting to adult-level punishment, making the proposed fourteen floor unnecessary and harmful.
[C1.2] PRO’s core capacity claim rests on an overbroad generalization that no child under fourteen can understand wrongdoing or control their impulses. A thirteen-year-old who plans a violent assault, repeatedly torments a younger peer, or commits armed robbery absolutely grasps that their actions are harmful and illegal. Developmental science does not support a universal fourteen cutoff for all offenses: maturity varies far more by individual circumstance, offense severity, and prior moral guidance than by chronological age alone.
[C1.3] PRO’s case also erases the voices of child victims and their families. If a thirteen-year-old sexually assaults a seven-year-old, raising the age to fourteen would eliminate all criminal accountability for the perpetrator, leaving the victim with no justice and signaling to young offenders that severe harm to other children carries no real consequence. That is not a welfare-focused victory—it is a betrayal of the most vulnerable people in the equation.
[C1.4] Finally, PRO’s claim that criminal labels drive recidivism is backwards. Unaccountability is a far stronger predictor of repeat offending: a twelve-year-old who commits serious assault or arson and faces no proportional consequence learns their actions have no stakes, making future severe harm more likely. Proportional, age-appropriate juvenile accountability—restitution, mandated rehabilitation, or tailored sanctions—reduces recidivism far more than blanket non-accountability for serious offenses.
