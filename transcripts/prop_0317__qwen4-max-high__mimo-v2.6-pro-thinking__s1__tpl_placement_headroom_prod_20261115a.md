It converts a visible price into invisible costs: queueing, lotteries, bot contests, and time spent refreshing a page while the show sells out anyway. The scarcity is real, and neither remedy in this motion manufactures supply. They only decide who pays and how the rationing happens.
[C1.2] The face-value channel sounds gentler, but it hides the same coercion. For a platform to guarantee that tickets resell at cost, it must police every transfer, verify every price, and lock tickets to approved channels. That means tighter identity binding, more non-transferable barcodes, and more power for exactly the gatekeepers the proposition complains about. You cannot simultaneously believe platforms are predatory bottlenecks and hand them the authority to approve every resale of every ticket.
[C1.3] Real fans need real resale. Plans change. Travel breaks. A ticket bought in January is sold in June.
[C1.4] Sellers legitimately need to recover fees, shipping, and the package extras bundled into the purchase. A rigid face-value rule either strands those costs on the seller, discouraging resale and leaving seats empty, or invites endless definitional fights about what "price paid" includes. Meanwhile, buyers lose the one thing secondary markets genuinely provide: a guaranteed seat when the primary sale is over. Speculators who hold inventory bear the risk of unsold tickets; a margin is compensation for that risk-bearing, not theft.
[C1.5] The enforcement story is worse. Caps only bind the platforms that comply. Every other channel — messaging apps, forums, cash at the door — stays open, and fans pushed there lose fraud protection and verified entry. A rule that drives transactions underground while enriching incumbent platform lock-in is not consumer protection.
[C1.6] The proposition must show that fans end up better off after accounting for these costs. Scarcity, enforcement, and gatekeeper power all cut the other way.
