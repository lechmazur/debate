[P1.1] Torture is not defined by the instrument. It is defined by what a state deliberately does to a mind it has total power over. Strip a person of contact, of daylight, of anything to do or anyone to answer, and you are not withholding a privilege — you are removing the inputs a human brain needs to stay intact. Clinicians who have examined segregated prisoners describe the same sequence: anxiety, then panic, then perceptual distortion, then the flat, unreachable withdrawal of a man who has stopped expecting the world to exist.
[P1.2] Some hear voices. Some cut themselves to feel something. That is severe mental suffering, imposed on purpose, on people who cannot leave. If a foreign prison did this to a captured journalist, we would call it torture without hesitating.
[P1.3] The motion does not ask whether a prison may separate a violent man from others today. It asks whether that separation may continue, alone, past fifteen days. That line is where the world's prison standards already draw it, and it is defensible for a reason: the first days are crisis management; the weeks after are a policy choice. And by then the damage stops being discomfort and starts being injury — the kind that follows people into the street, into their families, into their inability to hold a job or a conversation.
[P1.4] So the burden is specific. My opponent must name a security need that cannot be met by a separate cell with staff contact, supervised out-of-cell hours, and treatment — and that only endless isolation can solve. There isn't one. What long solitary produces is not safety but quieter, sicker men, and a clock that gets reset by letting someone out for an hour.
[P1.5] Fifteen days is containment. Everything after it is cruelty with a filing system.

[C1.2] Collapse that distinction and you criminalize quarantine, solitary bed rest in a hospital, and any harsh custody condition that happens to distress someone. [C2.1] PRO has replaced purpose with knowledge-plus-power, but that test proves too much to be usable. A doctor intubating a delirious patient, a nurse restraining a suicidal man, a warden extracting an inmate from a riot — each knows severe distress will follow and each acts because the alternative is worse. If foreseeing suffering under state power is enough, force-feeding, emergency quarantine, and every lawful act of compulsion become "torture," and the word stops distinguishing atrocity from necessity.
[C2.2] What actually separates torture from containment is the role suffering plays: in torture it is the instrument — extracted by it, punished by it, broken by it; in segregation it is a risk the state is obliged to manage, not a result it seeks. PRO says segregation "gives the prisoner nothing," but that is precisely why it is confinement rather than interrogation: the state does not use the distress to make him talk, confess, or submit. The same knowledge that would make prolonged isolation criminal under PRO's test is present in chemotherapy, in involuntary commitment, in protective custody — all chosen knowing they harm, none chosen because they harm.
[C2.3] PRO's strongest new claim is the wall versus the void [P2.7]. But for a terrorist coordinator or a gang shot-caller, the wall and the void are functionally identical unless staff contact is continuous and out-of-cell movement is continuously supervised. PRO's alternative is not solitary made gentler; it is solitary made more expensive and more evasive. When staffing fails — and staffing always fails — the prisoner ends up in the void again, only now the unit is called a "separate living area," the duty to review has quietly vanished, and the state has no obligation to justify the isolation at all.
[C2.4] Abolition does not eliminate the void; it hides it. Regulation, medical screening, and enforceable caps preserve the ability to name and check what is being done. PRO offers a relabeling that looks like mercy and functions as concealment.
