[P1.1] Being born on one side of a river rather than the other is luck, not merit. Your passport tracks that luck. Your pain, your liberty, your death do not become less important because your mother crossed that line in the wrong month. Human welfare tracks properties that actually matter morally — sentience, agency, the capacity to lead a life.
[P1.2] Nationality tracks an administrative fact. The first must outweigh the second.
[P1.3] Nations themselves prove this. We don't cherish states as final ends. We cherish them because, at their best, they are uniquely effective machines for securing human welfare: rights, order, health, opportunity. Loyalty is the oil that keeps that machine running.
[P1.4] That means its moral force is derivative and conditional. It exists to serve human welfare. A derivative duty cannot be stronger than the source that gives it meaning. When the machine turns against its purpose — when fidelity demands you bomb a village, turn away a boat of drowning children, hoard vaccines while millions die elsewhere — the justification for loyalty collapses.
[P1.5] The collision test makes the hierarchy visible. Both sides allow partiality. You may fund your own schools before a museum abroad. You may favor neighbors you can effectively help.
[P1.6] But partiality inside a floor is not permission to violate the floor. No decent view says a border guard should shoot unarmed migrants because orders and country demand it. No decent view says you may let strangers starve to preserve a small tax cut for compatriots. Like family love, national loyalty operates inside side-constraints set by universal duties not to harm and to relieve grave suffering.
[P1.7] Make loyalty stronger and you turn every closed-border cruelty, every aggressive war, every climate free-ride into virtue. Put universal welfare first and loyalty still has robust room — to keep promises, pay taxes, defend a just polity — but bounded. Bounded is weaker.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes the source of a duty for its weight. Nationality may begin as an unchosen fact, but many serious obligations enter our lives unbidden: the family we did not choose, the commitments we inherit, the neighbors whose fate is entangled with ours. National loyalty’s moral force comes not from a passport but from continuous cooperation under law—public services, defense, taxation, self-government, and the reliance of compatriots on one another. Those relations create special obligations, and nothing about their origin makes them weak.
[C1.2] Nor does PRO’s “derivative” argument succeed. A duty can be derived from something larger and still be stronger in practice: a parent’s duty to a child is not weakened because care matters for human welfare. National loyalty is not just a tool. It is a bond of reciprocity and fair play.
[C1.3] A citizen who has accepted protection, public goods, and a shared political order owes a particular fidelity that is not automatically outweighed by equal benevolence to everyone.
[C1.4] The real weakness is on the universal side. “Universal human welfare” is abstract, unbounded, and lacks a determinate claimant. A duty owed to everyone at once often becomes a duty to no one in particular. National loyalty, by contrast, attaches to concrete people with shared institutions and enforceable expectations. It can demand military service, taxation, lawful fidelity, and personal sacrifice for those one actually lives among.
[C1.5] That is morally weighty, not weak.
[C1.6] PRO’s collision test is rigged by assuming loyalty must always stay inside universal “floors.” But those floors are exactly what is disputed. A legitimate state that prioritizes its citizens in vaccines, courts, disaster rescue, and entry decisions is often doing what justice requires, not merely what is excused. When forced to choose between a compatriot and a stranger, loyalty can be the stronger moral claim. The claimed hierarchy therefore fails.
