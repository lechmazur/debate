[P1.1] Decarbonization is fundamentally a race against the clock. The climate does not reward transit plans that sit in environmental review or engineering paralysis; it rewards tons of carbon avoided today. For Latin America, prioritizing the rapid electrification of bus fleets over urban rail is the only strategy matched to the urgency of the near term.
[P1.2] Urban rail is notoriously slow, capital-intensive, and financially volatile. Constructing a single metro line takes a decade or more, routinely suffers severe cost overruns, and incurs massive upfront emissions from pouring millions of tons of concrete and steel. By the time a new subway opens in the late 2030s, the critical window for near-term climate targets has already closed. Latin American municipal budgets simply cannot absorb these multi-billion-dollar liabilities while simultaneously meeting immediate public service needs.
[P1.3] Electric buses, by contrast, deploy immediately onto existing street networks and established bus rapid transit corridors. Cities like Santiago and Bogotá have already proven this model: thousands of electric buses can be procured, integrated, and put into commercial service within a single mayoral administration. Crucially, because Latin America boasts one of the cleanest electrical grids in the world, dominated by abundant hydropower, every diesel bus replaced with an electric powertrain yields instantaneous, profound emissions reductions.
[P1.4] Moreover, this is a question of spatial reach. Decarbonization cannot just benefit a single high-density corridor in a wealthy capital city. Rail is economically impossible for the dozens of rapidly growing secondary cities across the region. Bus networks, however, extend to the urban peripheries where millions of lower-income commuters live, directly replacing dirty diesel exhaust with zero-emission transit across entire metropolitan zones.
[P1.5] In the near term, prioritizing rail means sinking billions of dollars into projects that deliver zero carbon abatement for a decade. Prioritizing bus electrification delivers clean transit, cleaner air, and measurable emissions reductions right now.

[C1.1] Decarbonization is not merely a question of fuel switching; it is a question of modal shift. The fatal premise of the affirmative case is treating transit decarbonization as an accounting exercise of swapping diesel engines for batteries. In reality, the primary driver of Latin America’s escalating transport emissions is not public transit tailpipes, but rapid motorization—millions of commuters abandoning slow, congested surface transit for private cars and motorcycles.
[C1.2] An electric bus stuck in gridlock does nothing to reverse this trend. In Latin America’s dense metropolitan hubs—such as São Paulo, Mexico City, Lima, and Bogotá—bus networks face severe capacity saturation, bunching, and speed degradation. Electrifying a bus fleet simply decarbonizes a shrinking base of captive riders while leaving private vehicle miles unchecked. Only grade-separated urban rail delivers the immense throughput—routinely exceeding 50,000 passengers per hour per direction—and dramatic travel-time savings necessary to pull middle-class commuters out of private automobiles.
[C1.3] Mode shift from private vehicles to rail abates vastly more carbon than simply electrifying existing bus passenger-kilometers.
[C1.4] Furthermore, urban rail expansion is not confined to decades-long tunneling. It encompasses light rail, surface tramways, and the electrification and modernization of existing legacy rail corridors, which can be deployed rapidly to unlock massive capacity. By contrast, electric buses lock municipal budgets into short asset lifecycles, requiring expensive battery replacements every six to eight years and continuous fleet turnover without expanding corridor capacity or reshaping urban land use. Rail infrastructure permanently shapes compact, transit-oriented development that curbs sprawl and permanently lowers regional energy demand.
[C1.5] Prioritizing electric buses treats the symptom of bus emissions while entrenching car-dominated street space. To achieve deep, permanent decarbonization, Latin America must prioritize urban rail as the high-capacity backbone of regional mobility.
