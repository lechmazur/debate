[P1.1] Middle-school math placement is one of the most powerful engines of educational inequality. A single decision, often made around age ten or eleven, determines who gets open-ended problem solving, conceptual depth, and acceleration, and who gets a repetitive computation track with a lower ceiling. That placement is not a neutral measure of ability. It reflects earlier tutoring, summer enrichment, adult expectations, and test preparation more than mathematical potential.
[P1.2] It compounds advantage.
[P1.3] The proposal is not lower rigor. It replaces permanent tracks with heterogeneous classes built around rich, grade-level tasks, plus intensive tutoring for students who enter with gaps. Every student sees demanding content. Struggling learners receive small-group tutoring tied directly to the same curriculum, so a gap in fractions or number sense does not become a permanent exit from advanced work.
[P1.4] High achievers are not held back; they explain reasoning, encounter different solution paths, and take extension problems. The key distinction is that detracking without supports can fail, but this motion pairs detracking with tutoring supports. Under that design, the common pattern is meaningful gains for low and middle achievers and little or no loss for high achievers, while traditional tracking mainly protects students who were already ahead.
[P1.5] Tracking also narrows the algebra-calculus-STEM pipeline by race and class. It assigns the least experienced teachers too often to the lowest track, and it turns the top track into a contest of outside test prep. Heterogeneous classes plus intensive tutoring shifts the mechanism from early selection to instruction.
[P1.6] The decisive test is whether a school should identify only a fraction of eleven-year-olds and ration rigorous math to them, or teach every student demanding math and give each one the support to reach it. The second choice is both fairer and better for the system.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a fantasy budget. "Intensive tutoring" sounds compassionate, but intensive means small groups, frequent sessions, and sustained intervention for every student who enters with gaps. In a school of five hundred students, if even a third need that support, you are talking about hiring dozens of additional math specialists, paying for after-school and summer programs, and maintaining that infrastructure indefinitely. Public schools are already cutting arts, music, and librarians to balance budgets.
[C1.2] This plan asks them to conjure a tutoring army out of thin air while pretending the funding exists.
[C1.3] Even if the money materialized, the classroom reality collapses under the weight of the range. A heterogeneous eighth-grade math class might contain students reading at a fourth-grade level alongside others ready for algebra. The teacher cannot simultaneously teach fractions remediation and quadratic functions with the depth either group deserves. Teaching to the middle abandons both ends: the advanced student drills material they mastered years ago while waiting for the class to catch up, and the struggling student drowns in abstraction without the focused scaffolding they need.
[C1.4] "Extension problems" on a worksheet are not acceleration; they are busywork while the real lesson moves at a pace set by the median, and teacher burnout skyrockets as educators attempt to differentiate across a five-grade-level spread in a single period.
[C1.5] Advanced math is cumulative and fast-moving. Calculus requires fluency in algebra, which requires mastery of arithmetic. Students who are ready to move quickly need to move quickly, or they disengage and leak out of the STEM pipeline the PRO side claims to care about. Tracking is not a rationing device that protects privilege; it is an instructional match that lets curriculum move at the pace of readiness.
[C1.6] Heterogeneous classes plus underfunded tutoring promises equity on paper while delivering mediocrity in practice, sacrificing the depth that high achievers need and the concentrated remediation that strugglers require.

[C2.1] The reallocation argument collapses under arithmetic. [P2.2] claims we can shift staff from "separate low, middle, and high tracks" into tutoring, but tracking does not triple the teaching staff—it sorts existing students into different sections of the same course. When you merge those sections into one heterogeneous room, you still need a teacher for every student; you haven't freed up bodies, you've concentrated the spread. Add "intensive tutoring" for the third of the class that enters with gaps.
[C2.2] That requires additional adults, hours, coordination. You cannot staff a tutoring corps by dissolving tracks; you need new resources, which the opening established.
[C2.3] The "multiple entry points" defense [P2.4] fails on facts of mathematics. If an eighth-grader reads at a fourth-grade level and lacks fractions, no entry point allows access to quadratic functions. The tutor is not "supplying missing prerequisites" while the student engages with grade-level tasks; the tutor is teaching a remedial curriculum in parallel while the student sits in a class they cannot follow. That is not heterogeneous instruction—it is a low track wearing a tutoring badge, consuming specialist time that could have gone to remediation before the gap widened.
[C2.4] [P2.6] treats "generalizing" and "justifying" as equivalent to acceleration. They are not. A student ready for calculus needs to encounter limits and derivatives, not spend a year explaining elementary arithmetic in novel ways. The motion sacrifices sequencing—the cumulative spine of math—for a model offering advanced students enrichment worksheets while class crawls at median pace.
[C2.5] This is not replacing tracking with better instruction; it is replacing readiness with paperwork, and calling the wreckage equity. The choice is not between tracking and tutoring; it is between sorting students into rooms where they can learn, or pretending that a worksheet and a tutor can substitute for mathematical readiness.

The fallback when a district cannot yet fund that dosage after redeploying existing remedial sections, credit recovery, pullout interventions, and low-track staffing is phased scale-up: begin with the grade or course where gaps are most consequential, and expand as savings and reallocation free capacity.

[P4.3] A2: My comparison is tracked acceleration as actually delivered—early placement error, outside-test-prep advantage, uneven course quality—not an idealized track given unlimited extra enrichment. [P4.4] The pressure questions expose the real clash.
