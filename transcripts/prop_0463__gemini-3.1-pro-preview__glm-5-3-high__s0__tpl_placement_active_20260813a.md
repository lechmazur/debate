[P1.1] Morality is fundamentally rooted in the equal intrinsic worth of every human being. National borders, by contrast, are mere administrative lines drawn by history, war, and political compromise. They do not—and cannot—alter the fundamental moral weight of a human life. Therefore, duties to universal human welfare must supersede the demands of national loyalty.
[P1.2] First, nationality is a profound moral lottery. No one earns their birthplace. To claim that we owe vastly greater moral consideration to someone simply because they share our citizenship is to distribute basic human rights based on an accident of birth. True justice requires impartiality.
[P1.3] The capacity to suffer, to thrive, and to live with dignity is universal; thus, our highest moral obligations must be universal as well. A life saved from malaria in a distant country holds the exact same moral weight as a life saved in our own hometown.
[P1.4] Second, the stakes involved in these competing claims are radically asymmetrical. Duties to universal human welfare typically address existential threats: absolute poverty, preventable disease, starvation, and global climate collapse. In contrast, when national loyalty demands precedence, it often does so to protect relative luxuries or geopolitical dominance. It is morally indefensible to prioritize a marginal increase in a compatriot's standard of living over the literal survival of a foreigner.
[P1.5] Finally, elevating national loyalty above universal welfare removes the essential constraint on state exploitation. If a nation’s primary duty is solely to its own citizens, then hoarding life-saving vaccines during a global pandemic, exporting carbon emissions to developing nations, or turning a blind eye to atrocities abroad become easily justifiable under the guise of "national interest." Universal welfare is the vital moral backstop that prevents national loyalty from devolving into tribalism and imperialism.
[P1.6] National loyalty may have a practical place in fostering local civic administration. But when the two conflict, the arbitrary lines of a map can never justify abandoning our supreme obligation to humanity itself. The moral claim of universal welfare is not just stronger; it is the foundation of justice itself.
