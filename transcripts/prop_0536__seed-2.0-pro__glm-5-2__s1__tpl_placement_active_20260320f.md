[C1.1] The proposition asks for "stronger legal protection" against algorithmic age exclusion, but the problem it identifies is not a gap in legal protection—it is a gap in enforcement. Age discrimination in hiring already violates the ADEA. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act already bars age-based exclusion in lending. An employer who discriminates does not escape liability merely by pointing to software.
[C1.2] The law regulates outcomes and actors, not tools.
[C1.3] Creating a special legal category for algorithmic decisions targeting older adults fragments anti-discrimination doctrine and creates perverse incentives. If algorithmic age screening triggers heightened liability that human screening does not, firms have every reason to abandon algorithms and return to unaccountable human managers who "just had a bad feeling" about older applicants. Human bias is harder to detect, harder to audit, and produces no paper trail at all. Algorithms are at least auditable.
[C1.4] Penalizing their use more harshly than human judgment pushes firms toward the less transparent option and makes the underlying problem worse.
[C1.5] The proposition also conflates three domains where age functions very differently. In hiring, age is largely irrelevant to capability—already covered by existing law. In insurance, age correlates with genuine actuarial risk. A 70-year-old's long-term care costs are not an outdated stereotype; they are a statistical reality insurers must price to remain solvent.
[C1.6] In financial services, creditworthiness already has objective metrics that operate regardless of age. Blanketing all three with one "algorithmic exclusion" frame obscures legitimate risk assessment that keeps premiums affordable and markets stable.
[C1.7] What is actually needed is better enforcement: audit requirements, transparency obligations, and discovery rules that apply across all protected classes and all decision systems. The motion demands the wrong remedy—demographic-specific, tool-specific law—where universal, outcome-based enforcement would do more good for older adults and everyone else.

An algorithm that screens out older applicants at disproportionate rates is a textbook disparate impact claim, and plaintiffs can use statistical evidence without needing to see source code. Algorithmic decisions are actually *easier* to challenge systematically than scattered human judgments precisely because they apply uniformly at scale—producing the statistical pattern the law requires.
[C2.2] PRO also narrows the proposition when convenient. The motion covers insurance and financial services broadly, but PRO now insists the rule "only stops excluding based solely on birth year, before any individual risk is measured." This is sleight of hand. Algorithms rarely use birth year directly—they use correlated variables like graduation year, employment gaps, or health utilization patterns.
[C2.3] Banning those proxies in insurance and lending would gut legitimate actuarial modeling, because age genuinely correlates with real risk in those domains. You cannot separate "lazy discriminatory modeling" from "actuarial prudence" without a line-drawing exercise courts are poorly equipped to perform—which is exactly why current law focuses on outcomes, not tool design.
[C2.4] PRO also assumes firms will "just adjust the code." But if age correlates with legitimate risk factors, removing them to avoid heightened liability makes models less accurate, not more fair. [C2.5] The urgency argument cuts the wrong way.
