[P1.1] Insurance is not just a product; it is a social mechanism for mutual protection. That is why the motion focuses on essential coverage—health, basic dwelling, disability—where access is a precondition for dignified participation in society, not a consumer luxury.
[P1.2] Actuarial fairness, in its pure form, charges each person their expected cost. But this logic, applied without limit, hollows out the very function insurance exists to serve. The point of pooling risk is that the lucky subsidize the unlucky. If everyone pays exactly what they're expected to cost, there is no pooling at all—just individual prepayment.
[P1.3] The question is never whether to limit actuarial fairness, but where.
[P1.4] For essential coverage, the answer must be: at the point where pricing becomes exclusionary or compounds disadvantage people did not choose. A person born with a chronic condition did not select that risk. A family living in a flood-prone area because housing discrimination pushed them there did not choose it. Charging them more doesn't merely reflect risk; it converts misfortune into financial penalty, then excludes those who can't pay.
[P1.5] This is where social solidarity enters. Solidarity is not sentiment; it is the operational principle that makes risk-pooling work. The healthy pay for the sick, the safe for the vulnerable, not as charity but because everyone benefits from a system that catches them when their number comes up. Unlimited actuarial fairness breaks that bargain.
[P1.6] It transforms insurance from shared protection into individualized pricing, leaving high-risk people uninsured and defeating the purpose entirely.
[P1.7] We already accept limits: community rating in health insurance, prohibitions on genetic discrimination, requirements that insurers cover pre-existing conditions. These work because they preserve a risk pool broad enough to function. The motion states the principle behind those limits clearly: for essentials, law must constrain actuarial fairness to preserve solidarity.
[P1.8] The alternative is a market that looks like insurance but has stopped doing what insurance is for.

[C1.2] Start with what actuarial fairness actually does. Differentiated premiums are not a moral judgment; they are information. They tell us where risk is concentrated, which incentivizes prevention, mitigation, and honest pricing. When a homeowner in a floodplain pays more, that premium is a signal to build differently, elevate structures, or relocate—and a signal to insurers about where capital should go.
[C1.3] Wipe out that signal by law, and you don't eliminate the flood. You eliminate the feedback loop that manages it.
[C1.4] Second, forced pooling without risk adjustment unravels. If high-risk and low-risk people must be charged the same, the price is a bargain for one group and a rip-off for the other. The healthy delay buying; the sick buy eagerly; the pool gets sicker; premiums climb; more healthy people exit. This is the classic adverse-selection spiral, and it doesn't produce solidarity—it produces collapse, leaving the very people the motion claims to protect with an empty pool.
[C1.5] Risk-based pricing is what keeps low-risk people in the system at all. Without it, you don't get the lucky subsidizing the unlucky; you get the lucky gone.
[C1.6] Third, if society genuinely wants to protect those dealt bad hands, it can simply pay. Transparent subsidies, vouchers, public reinsurance, tax-funded support—these transfer resources openly, with the costs visible to voters who decide them. Mandating that insurers quietly overcharge one customer to undercharge another is solidarity off the books: unaccountable, capricious, and resting on whoever the law happens to favor.
[C1.7] And note the motion's logic never stops. Age is unchosen. Sex is unchosen. Genetics, geography, occupation—almost no risk factor is fully chosen.
[C1.8] The "unearned misfortune" test doesn't justify a limit; it forbids pricing entirely.
[C1.9] Precision plus open subsidy beats mandated blur. That's the real solidaristic position.
