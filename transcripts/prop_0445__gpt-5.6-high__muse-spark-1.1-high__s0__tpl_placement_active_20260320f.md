[P1.1] India’s labor regime too often offers strong security on paper to a narrow formal minority and little real security to everyone else. The right bargain is not deregulation alone. It is to let firms adapt hiring, hours, fixed-term work, and redundancies more easily while making health care, income support, pensions, injury insurance, and retraining portable across employers and employment status.
[P1.2] Rigid employment rules create a predictable avoidance response. Firms remain artificially small, outsource permanent functions, substitute machines for workers, or hire through insecure informal arrangements. A protection that employers can escape simply by never formalizing does not protect the street vendor, migrant laborer, platform worker, or young person seeking a first job. Greater contractual flexibility lowers the perceived risk of hiring, helps growing firms scale, and allows workers to move toward more productive jobs rather than clinging to one protected post.
[P1.3] The exchange matters because workers need security, but security should attach to the person, not one employer. A laid-off garment worker should retain health coverage, accumulated pension rights, and temporary income support while retraining or searching. Benefits should follow workers through full-time employment, contract work, self-employment, and periods between jobs. India’s identity and digital-payment infrastructure makes portability increasingly practical; broad tax financing and simple employer contributions can spread costs beyond the few compliant formal firms.
[P1.4] Flexibility must still have boundaries: enforceable contracts, notice or severance, anti-discrimination rules, workplace safety, collective representation, and penalties for wage theft. But regulating basic fairness is different from making workforce adjustment so costly or uncertain that employers avoid regular jobs altogether.
[P1.5] The comparison is therefore not between secure workers and abandoned workers. It is between today’s divided system—protected insiders surrounded by a vast informal workforce—and a system with more formal hiring, easier mobility, and a universal floor beneath everyone. CON must defend job-specific rigidity despite its exclusionary effects. PRO offers what workers actually need in a changing economy: more doors into employment and protection that survives when any particular job does not.
