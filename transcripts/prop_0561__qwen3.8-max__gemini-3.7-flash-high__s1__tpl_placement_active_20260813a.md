[P1.1] The core of this case is simple: when a platform builds a business by directing labor, setting pay terms, and capturing value from hours worked, it should not be allowed to socialize the costs of illness, age, and unemployment onto workers and taxpayers. Prorated benefits tied to hours are the narrowly targeted way to fix that imbalance.
[P1.2] Today, platform work often sits in a legal gray zone. A driver or courier may log full-time hours, follow algorithmic dispatch, face deactivation standards, and depend on app income, yet receive no health coverage, paid leave, or retirement accrual. The result is not flexibility; it is subsidized risk-shifting. If that worker gets sick, needs care, or ages without savings, the cost does not disappear.
[P1.3] It falls on the worker, their family, emergency rooms, or public safety nets, while platforms keep the upside of a cheaper labor model.
[P1.4] Proration is the key. This is not forcing every contractor into a single full-time employment box. It says: for each hour a platform receives labor, it contributes a proportionate amount toward health coverage, leave, and retirement. That contribution can follow the worker across apps, preserve multi-apping, and avoid binary classification fights.
[P1.5] Because platforms already track hours, rides, deliveries, and payments with precision, administration is feasible.
[P1.6] The government’s role is necessary because voluntary standards create a race to the bottom. Platforms that treat labor as an externality gain an advantage over those that do better. A uniform requirement prevents that, establishes a floor, and makes the price of platform services reflect their real cost. It also reduces the distortion between app work and traditional employment, stopping competition based on who can shed more risk.
[P1.7] The question is not whether gig work can remain flexible; it can. The question is whether public policy should let platforms profit from work while denying workers the basic social protections that work should fund.
