[C1.2] First, universal free fares are a subsidy for everyone, including affluent commuters and tourists, paid for by taxes that are often sales and property levies. Those taxes fall hardest on the same low-income households PRO invokes. The progressive alternative is targeted: free or deeply discounted passes for low-income riders, students, seniors, disabled residents. That delivers relief without giving a free ride to people who can pay.
[C1.3] Second, fares are not just revenue; they are a dedicated funding stream. Replace them with general taxation and transit competes every year with schools, police, pensions, and fire services. In a recession, when ridership need rises, tax revenue falls. Permanent fare-free status makes the system more vulnerable, not less.
[C1.4] Third, the real barrier to ridership is service. A bus every 45 minutes, late at night, is not useful whether it costs $2 or $0. If cities spend the money on lost fares, they are not spending it on more frequent buses, better reliability, or safer stations. Modest ridership gains mean a very high cost per new rider.
[C1.5] Fourth, free fares can degrade the experience: crowding on core routes, long dwell times from unrestricted boarding, and enforcement/security costs remain. PRO's administrative savings are overstated; you still need inspectors, security, data, and tax collection.
[C1.6] The central comparison is not fares versus no fares. It is universal free rides versus targeted affordability plus better service. CON wins because the motion spends scarce public money broadly, worsens equity, and risks the service quality that actually gets people on board.
