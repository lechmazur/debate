[P1.1] Unpaid family care is not a gift from the sky; it is a hidden subsidy extracted from ordinary households. When a daughter cuts her hours, a spouse retires early, or a sibling gives up sleep and health to keep a disabled relative out of an institution, the government saves money on paper because the cost is booked to that family's lost income, stress, and future poverty. That is the status quo the proposition rejects.
[P1.2] Paying family caregivers more generously is the fair and practical correction. First, it recognizes real labor: bathing, lifting, medication, feeding, supervision, coordination. If we paid a stranger to do it, we would call it work and pay wages. The accident of kinship should not turn work into an unlimited obligation.
[P1.3] Second, it is cheaper and more humane than the alternative. Formal institutional care is scarce and often far more expensive per person. A modest but reliable caregiver payment can keep people at home, where most say they want to be, while reducing emergency admissions and burnout-driven breakdowns. It also helps the economy by keeping caregivers attached to the labor market rather than forcing them into unpaid full-time dependency.
[P1.4] Third, the current assumption is unsustainable. Aging populations and rising disability mean demand for care will grow, but families are smaller, more geographically scattered, and more likely to have two earners. If policy keeps relying on unpaid labor, it will ration care by who has a willing and financially able relative — and punish those who do.
[P1.5] Opponents may call this expensive or open to abuse. Everything is expensive; unpaid care is simply deferred cost. Generous payment with basic eligibility, assessment, and audits is cheaper than mass institutionalization and more just than serfdom by affection. A society that saves money by exhausting daughters and spouses has not found an efficient care system; it has found someone else to bill.

[C1.1] PRO has correctly diagnosed the problem and prescribed the wrong cure. Unpaid care is a hidden burden. But a bigger cheque to the relative doing it does not end the assumption that families will carry the load. It formalizes that assumption and puts a price tag on it.
[C1.2] First, the care trap. PRO says payment keeps caregivers "attached to the labor market" [P1.3]. It does the opposite. A payment conditioned on providing care rewards staying home.
[C1.3] A daughter weighing a return to work now loses both the benefit and the care she would need to replace. Because even a "generous" government payment will sit near minimum-wage level, we would be paying mostly women a low wage to exit careers, pensions and promotion ladders, often permanently. That is not liberation from "serfdom by affection." It is serfdom with a stipend.
[C1.4] Second, the money buys little new care. Most family care already happens. [C1.5] Cash-for-kin keeps exactly that rationing and makes it official.
[C1.6] Third, quality and safety. When the state pays a stranger, it can require training, inspection and a way out. [C1.7] PRO's "basic audits" [P1.5] cannot see inside a living room.

[P2.1] CON's strongest line is that paying relatives formalizes the family burden. Reverse it: unpaid care already formalizes that burden, with no wage, no pension credit, no recourse. A cheque is not a cage; it is the first bargaining chip a caregiver has. CON calls payment "serfdom with a stipend" [C1.3]; unpaid care is serfdom without one.
[P2.2] If the state says the work is worth paying for, it can also attach conditions: pension credits, respite hours, training, and the right to reduce or share the role. CON assumes a deliberately stingy, all-or-nothing benefit and then calls it the policy. That is an argument for designing payment well, not for refusing it.
[P2.3] The "care trap" [C1.2] is real only if the payment is low and exclusive. A decent caregiver wage, plus pension contributions and portable respite, changes the calculus. The current unpaid arrangement is the real trap: the daughter leaves work, loses seniority, and has no income of her own. Paying her does not force that choice; it cushions it.
[P2.4] And even if some caregivers stay home, that is not automatically waste. Someone is doing the lifting, bathing, medication, and night supervision. CON's "services" [C1.8] do not abolish that work; they hire substitutes at higher cost, with staffing shortages and less continuity. Payment keeps care from collapsing into ER visits and institutionalization—that is new value at the margin, not deadweight.
[P2.5] On exploitation, audits are imperfect, but so are institutions. The better safeguard is an independent income, respite, and outside contact, not financial desperation. And CON's fairness objection—that isolated people get nothing—is a reason to fund services too. It is not a reason to keep unpaid relatives as the default.
[P2.6] The choice is not cash versus services; it is whether family labor is finally recognized or quietly extracted until it breaks.
