[P1.1] Today we stand on a simple premise: children are the most vulnerable users of the internet, and the default settings that platforms choose determine the majority of the experience. Requiring privacy‑by‑design for minors is not a vague aspiration; it is a concrete, enforceable standard that shifts the burden from an individual child to the corporation that profits from their data.
[P1.2] First, the evidence of harm is undeniable. Studies consistently show that minors exposed to unrestricted data collection suffer higher rates of cyber‑bullying, predatory contact, and mental‑health crises. When platforms default to open profiles, location sharing, and algorithmic feeds, they hand predators a roadmap. A default that limits data to the minimum needed for the service—no location, no targeted ads, no public friend lists—directly cuts that roadmap.
[P1.3] Real‑world pilots, such as the European Kids Online experiments, reported measurable drops in unwanted contact when privacy defaults were tightened.
[P1.4] Second, the claim that this equates to censorship is a false equivalence. Privacy‑by‑design does not block speech; it restricts who can see it. A teen can still post, comment, and share—just not to the whole world by default. The difference is analogous to a school hallway: you can speak freely, but you are not forced to broadcast to every passerby.
[P1.5] Finally, the policy is proportionate. Governments already mandate age‑verification for alcohol, car seats for children, and helmet laws. Extending a comparable safety net to digital spaces is a logical, low‑cost regulatory step that does not require content moderation, thus avoiding the slippery slope of state‑driven censorship.
[P1.6] If we accept that protecting minors from measurable digital harms is a legitimate governmental role, then mandating privacy‑by‑design is the least intrusive, most effective tool we have. The alternative—leaving default settings to profit‑maximizing platforms—guarantees continued harm. The choice is clear. Moreover, because the default applies only to minors, adults retain full control, so the policy respects free expression for the majority while shielding those who cannot yet make informed choices.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept two empirical claims: default privacy-by-design will measurably reduce harm to minors, and it will not significantly increase censorship. Both are demonstrably false, and the second collapses on closer inspection.
[C1.2] First, the harm-reduction case is weaker than PRO suggests. The European Kids Online experiments PRO cites in [P1.3] measured contact patterns, not actual harm. Tightening defaults may push minors to less-regulated platforms, encourage age-faking, or train them to bypass safety systems — worsening the very harms regulators claim to prevent. There is no robust evidence that data-minimization defaults change the underlying risk environment: predators adapt, and hiding one profile from another does not solve the architecture of risk.
[C1.3] Second — and this is where the proposition truly fails — the claim that this does not significantly increase censorship cannot survive scrutiny. In [P1.4], PRO concedes the policy "restricts who can see" a minor's speech. That is, by definition, an audience restriction on expression, and audience restriction is a recognized form of censorship. A 16-year-old climate organizer, a young artist seeking an audience, an LGBTQ teen searching for community: each is silenced from the public square by default.
[C1.4] The school-hallway analogy is revealing — schools are not free-speech environments, and applying that model to the public internet is precisely the paternalism this debate must reject.
[C1.5] Third, the enforcement mechanism undermines itself. To apply the rule, platforms must know who is a minor — requiring age verification, ID collection, or behavioral inference. Each path either invades privacy, the very harm the policy claims to prevent, or pushes verification to third-party vendors who become the new data harvesters.
[C1.6] The "least intrusive" framing is a marketing slogan. Default-locking millions of young voices out of public discourse is an enormous intrusion on the speech rights and civic development of minors themselves. The proposition fails on its own terms.
