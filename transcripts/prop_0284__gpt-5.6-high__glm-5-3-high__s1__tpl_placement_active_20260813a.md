[C1.1] Price differentiation is not a glitch in markets; it is one of their oldest and most productive features. Haggling, coupons, matinee prices, student rates, airline yield management, insurer risk pooling—all charge different people different amounts for substantially the same thing. Algorithms simply do faster and finer what merchants have always done: estimate what each customer is willing to pay.
[C1.2] The core economic point is simple. When a firm can charge more to those with higher willingness to pay, it can serve customers who would otherwise be priced out entirely. A software subscription, a flight, a medication priced uniformly must clear its average cost across everyone. With differentiation, the price-sensitive student pays less than uniform pricing would ever allow.
[C1.3] Blanket disclosure or bans push everything toward one uniform, higher price—and the people hurt first are those with the least willingness to pay. The proposition, taken seriously, is a tax on the poor dressed up as consumer protection.
[C1.4] Disclosure, meanwhile, is not the clean remedy it appears to be. A generic notice—"prices may vary by customer"—becomes invisible boilerplate within months, like cookie banners everyone clicks through. A detailed notice invites strategic gaming: customers manipulate device signals and browsing history to game the algorithm, and the firm responds by raising the floor price for everyone. Either way, the disclosure rule fails its own goal while adding friction and cost.
[C1.5] Real harms deserve real remedies—and we already have them. Anti-discrimination law forbids pricing on protected traits. Data-protection regimes govern what inputs algorithms may use. Competition law addresses collusion, including algorithmic collusion.
[C1.6] These tools target actual abuse. What the motion proposes instead is a blunt, usually-applicable rule applied to an ordinary business practice, justified by a hypothetical victim rather than demonstrated harm.
[C1.7] Markets do not owe every buyer the same number. They owe buyers enforceable rules against fraud, discrimination, and collusion. That is what exists. Keep it that way.
