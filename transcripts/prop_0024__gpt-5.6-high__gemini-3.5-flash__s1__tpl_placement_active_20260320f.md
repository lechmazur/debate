[P1.1] Housing affordability is a shortage problem before it is a contract problem. In productive advanced-economy cities, households compete for too few homes near jobs and services. When zoning bans apartments, mandates excessive parking, or makes approval slow and uncertain, it converts demand into higher land prices and rents. Streamlining those barriers lets more households live where they need to be.
[P1.2] That mechanism works at scale. New homes absorb higher-income demand that would otherwise bid for older units; vacancies give tenants alternatives; and competition restrains rents across the market, not merely in regulated leases. Construction is not instantaneous, but reform is durable and cumulative: every additional building expands the stock for decades. “Streamlining” need not abolish safety, environmental review, or infrastructure planning. It means predictable rules, shorter timelines, and legalizing density where demand is strongest, reducing delay costs while preserving legitimate standards.
[P1.3] Rent control, by contrast, changes the distribution of a fixed shortage. An incumbent lucky enough to secure a controlled unit may pay less, but the suppressed price signal reappears as queues, lotteries, informal screening, reduced mobility, and fewer options for newcomers. Stronger controls also reduce expected returns from rental housing, encouraging conversion, under-maintenance, or non-construction. Broad tenant protections can prevent abuse and sudden displacement, but they do not create a single bedroom.
[P1.4] If poorly designed, added risk and compliance costs are priced into new leases or make landlords more selective.
[P1.5] The decisive metric is affordability for the whole population over time: lower market-clearing housing costs, more available homes, and access for young people, migrants, and growing families—not just protection for current occupants. Supply reform addresses the cause and benefits outsiders as well as insiders. Rent regulation can be a temporary shield, but a shield is not a cure. Where scarcity is legally manufactured, removing those constraints is the more effective affordability policy.
