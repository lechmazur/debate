[P1.1] Public transit and ride-hail systems function as essential infrastructure for modern life—they connect people to jobs, healthcare, education, and community. When wheelchair users face wait times dramatically longer than other passengers, the system doesn't merely inconvenience them; it excludes them from full participation in society.
[P1.2] Consider what "longer wait times" actually means in practice. A wheelchair user calling for paratransit might wait 45 minutes to two hours, while a standing passenger summons a ride in five minutes or catches the next bus. That disparity compounds across every trip, every day, every year. It means arriving late to job interviews, missing medical appointments, declining social invitations—not because the destination is inaccessible, but because the journey itself is structured as a barrier.
[P1.3] The core principle here is straightforward: public accommodations must provide meaningful access, not theoretical access. The Americans with Disabilities Act established that people with disabilities deserve equal opportunity to use public services. But equal opportunity requires comparable convenience. A wheelchair-accessible system that takes three times longer to use is functionally separate and unequal.
[P1.4] Ride-hail companies already possess the technology to dispatch vehicles efficiently. The constraint isn't technical—it's that accessibility has been treated as an optional add-on rather than a core requirement. Requiring parity in wait times forces these systems to internalize accessibility as a design constraint from the start, not an afterthought.
[P1.5] The opposition will cite costs. But the cost calculation is incomplete if it only counts dollars spent on compliance while ignoring the costs borne by wheelchair users: lost wages, missed opportunities, diminished independence. We don't allow restaurants to exclude wheelchair users because ramps are expensive. Transportation shouldn't operate under a different standard.
[P1.6] Accessibility with wait times close to standard service isn't a luxury—it's the threshold at which accessible transportation becomes genuinely usable transportation. Anything less is a promise of inclusion that delivers exclusion.

[C1.1] A mandate sounds compassionate until you trace what it actually forces systems to do. "Wait times close to standard service" is not a goal—it's a rigid legal standard, and rigid parity standards in transportation produce three predictable failures.
[C1.2] First, the math of fleets. Ride-hail works because millions of drivers use cars they already own. Wheelchair-accessible vehicles cost tens of thousands more, and almost no individual driver owns one. To guarantee near-parity wait times everywhere, a company must station idle accessible vehicles across every neighborhood, around the clock, waiting for trips that in low-density areas may come once a day.
[C1.3] The marginal cost of shaving an accessible wait from forty minutes to eight is astronomical—and that money comes from somewhere.
[C1.4] Second, where it comes from: exit and leveling down. When parity is legally impossible to meet, the rational corporate response is not heroic investment—it's withdrawing from smaller markets entirely, or letting standard service degrade so the gap technically closes. A rule meant to lift wheelchair users can instead strand everyone, including wheelchair users, in towns where ride-hail simply stops operating. A mandate that drives providers out of the market provides access to no one.
[C1.5] Third, the opportunity cost for disabled riders themselves. A fixed accessibility budget spent chasing the last minutes of wait-time parity buys far less mobility than the same dollars spent on subsidized accessible taxi vouchers, expanded paratransit hours, accessible bus fleets, and curb infrastructure. The motion locks us into the most expensive possible metric—wait-time parity—instead of the metric that matters: total trips actually completed by wheelchair users.
[C1.6] Notice that even the ADA, which PRO invokes, deliberately requires *comparable* and *reasonable* service, not near-identical convenience—because Congress understood that absolutist standards collapse under real-world geography and economics.
[C1.7] The choice isn't access versus exclusion. It's between a brittle parity mandate that shrinks service, and flexible, funded obligations that expand it. CON defends the version that actually moves people.
