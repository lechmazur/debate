[P1.1] Public money should buy public truth. Right now it too often buys a filtered story.
[P1.2] Medical and social researchers face crushing pressure to produce positive, novel, statistically significant findings. That pressure predictably produces p-hacking, shifting hypotheses after results are known, and burying disappointing studies in file drawers. Journals then amplify the distortion by prizing splashy positives. The published record becomes a highlight reel, not the data.
[P1.3] In medicine this kills and wastes. When negative drug trials disappear, doctors and meta-analyses overestimate benefits and underestimate harms. Patients receive treatments that look better on paper than in bodies. Billions in public funding chase dead ends already shown dead but never published.
[P1.4] A preregistered protocol with a commitment to publish regardless of outcome breaks that cycle at its root: the question and method are judged before anyone knows the answer.
[P1.5] The same logic holds in social science, where policies on schooling, policing, addiction, and poverty lean heavily on funded studies. If only successes get printed, governments scale up interventions that do not reliably work, while failures that could teach us are hidden. Preregistration does not ban exploration or surprise findings; it simply labels them honestly as exploratory, so policymakers can tell tested predictions from after-the-fact storytelling.
[P1.6] Mandatory preregistration plus results-blind acceptance is light-touch and liberating. Researchers file their core hypotheses, measures, and analysis plans in advance — a short document, amendable with a timestamp. Journals agree to publish competent work that asks an important question well, even if the answer is “no effect.” That shifts competition from who gets the luckiest p-value to who asks the clearest question with the cleanest design.
[P1.7] Public funding creates a public obligation: show your plan, show your results, let null findings count. That is how public science earns public trust.

[C1.1] The proposal is right that the published record is distorted, but its cure is broader than the disease. Mandatory preregistration treats every publicly funded clinical and social-science study as a confirmatory drug trial. It is not. Adaptive trials, outbreak investigations, longitudinal cohorts, ethnographies, participatory studies, and pilots often must change questions, sites, instruments, or analyses as reality intrudes.
[C1.2] A universal filing requirement freezes methods before researchers know enough to specify them and treats honest adaptation as deviation. The result is transparency theater: vague protocols written for compliance, amended under timestamp pressure, while insights that emerge later stay exploratory or unreported.
[C1.3] Results-blind publication is worse. Journals exist to judge importance, validity, design, ethics, and contribution. A promise to publish preregistered studies regardless of outcome does not remove outcome bias; it replaces a filter with a quota. Underpowered nulls and competent-but-trivial studies would enter the literature with the same imprimatur as major evidence.
[C1.4] Meta-analysts would still have to discount them; clinicians would still ask whether a null came from a failed idea or a failed execution. Guaranteed acceptance lowers the cost of weak design: if registration plus completion earns publication, incentives shift from asking hard questions well to completing registrable units. Journals would lower standards or create captive outlets.
[C1.5] The mandate also carries distributional costs. Registry infrastructure, compliance officers, statistical consultants, and data systems are not free. Large medical centers and wealthy universities can absorb them; community clinics, low-income-country researchers, early-career scholars, and small departments cannot. A rule sold as equity would ration capacity toward institutions already best equipped to game it, while funders police forms rather than fund studies.
[C1.6] Registered reports, data and analysis-code sharing, independent statistical review, replication funding, disclosure of all trials, and post-publication audit attack selective reporting without making flexibility a violation or turning journals into conveyor belts. The motion is not reform; it is a rigid protocol imposed on methods that cannot survive being frozen.
