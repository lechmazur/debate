That “even when” is the problem. A city does not protect health by committing tens of billions and decades of construction to one technology before asking what delivers most clean water per dollar.
[C1.2] Separation is not purity. A separated storm pipe does not treat what it carries; it rushes rain washing oil, fertilizer, tire particles, trash, and bacteria off streets straight into rivers, often in faster, larger pulses. Combined systems fail at peak rain, but the rest of the year they send sewage and much stormwater to treatment. Replace them wholesale and you can trade occasional overflows for chronic untreated runoff, streambank erosion, and downstream flooding.
[C1.3] Overflow management targets the failure point: the peak. Deep storage tunnels, real-time controls that use empty capacity in existing pipes, green streets and detention, and selective separation in the worst basins can capture the majority of overflow volume without rebuilding every street. That is not a Band-Aid; it is triage that gets cleaner rivers years sooner, while preserving funds for lead pipes, treatment upgrades, and flood protection separation alone does not provide.
[C1.4] The cost is also who pays and what is torn up. Full separation means excavating dense, old neighborhoods for years, closing businesses, rerouting buses, and raising water bills to service debt — burdens that fall hardest on the low-income residents invoked to justify it. It also burns enormous embodied carbon before one gallon is cleaner. Money spent duplicating a network that still needs added storm capacity is money not spent where people actually flood: basements, underpasses, and overwhelmed drains.
[C1.5] Aging infrastructure deserves investment, not an unconditional rebuild. Cities should fix combined systems with the mix that removes overflows fastest and cheapest, basin by basin, rather than mandate separation regardless of price.
