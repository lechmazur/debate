[P1.1] The motion is about a gap, not a zero. Nobody has to claim body cameras did nothing. The question is whether they delivered the accountability revolution their champions promised, and the honest answer is that they did not come close.
[P1.2] Remember what was promised. After Ferguson in 2014, cameras were sold as the fix: an objective witness on every chest that would deter misconduct before it happened, settle disputed encounters, and make discipline routine rather than exceptional. A small early study in Rialto, California, reporting steep drops in force and complaints, became the sales brochure. Cities spent hundreds of millions of dollars on that premise.
[P1.3] Then the large studies arrived. The most rigorous one, a randomized trial across roughly two thousand officers in Washington, D.C., found no meaningful difference in use of force or complaints between officers with cameras and without. That is not a minor asterisk. It is the central deterrence claim failing at scale.
[P1.4] Why? Because the camera is controlled by the institution it is supposed to check. Officers decide when to activate, and footage is routinely missing at exactly the moments that matter. Departments decide what to release and when, often withholding video for weeks or months while a narrative sets.
[P1.5] Union contracts frequently let officers review footage before writing reports, converting the "objective witness" into a script-editing tool. And prosecutors, who file the actual accountability, still charge officers at rates that barely moved.
[P1.6] Consider the most consequential police videos of the past decade. George Floyd's killing was recorded by a teenager's phone. The bystander, not the body cam, produced the accountability. Body cameras were supposed to make that bystander unnecessary.
[P1.7] They didn't.
[P1.8] Meanwhile, footage is used heavily to prosecute civilians and to clear officers, which is fine as truth-finding but is not the redistribution of power advocates described.
[P1.9] If CON's best case is "cameras occasionally help," CON has conceded the motion. Occasional help is precisely less than a revolution.

[C1.5] The trial did not show cameras made accountability worse; it showed cameras do not act alone.
[C1.6] The control problem is real, but it is an argument for enforceable activation rules, not proof the technology failed. A camera that is sometimes off still records many encounters that would otherwise be invisible. The alternative is no record at all.
[C1.7] George Floyd proves the power of video, not the irrelevance of body cameras. A bystander’s phone changed the world; body cameras extend that possibility to thousands of encounters where no bystander is present. The reasonable expectation—more truth, more evidence, more verifiable accountability—has been met.
