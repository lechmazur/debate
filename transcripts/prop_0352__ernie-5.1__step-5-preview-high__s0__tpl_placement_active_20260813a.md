[P1.1] The proposition isn't nostalgic romanticism about small towns. It's a practical demand that governments stop treating metropolitan concentration as a law of nature when it's largely a policy choice they helped create. For decades, federal infrastructure spending, tax incentives, and subsidized lending flowed toward cities while rural and small-town economies were left to compete on an uneven field. Accepting that outcome as "inevitable" is simply ratifying decisions governments already made.
[P1.2] Three stakes make reversal urgent. First, national security. A country whose food, energy, and critical minerals all depend on concentrated corridors is brittle. Rural America produces the vast majority of the nation's calories and much of its energy, yet those regions face chronic underinvestment and population drain.
[P1.3] Second, democratic health. When economic life consolidates in a handful of metros, political power follows, and the preferences of most citizens get structurally diluted. Third, human dignity. Communities that lose their tax base lose schools, hospitals, and the basic infrastructure that lets people age in place.
[P1.4] The evidence that governments can shift this is real. Broadband expansion programs, place-based industrial policies, and targeted grant programs have measurably slowed decline in places that received sustained investment. The European Union's regional development funds and similar models show that deliberate fiscal transfer can keep smaller economies functioning. This doesn't require freezing populations in place—it requires ensuring that leaving isn't the only rational choice.
[P1.5] The alternative the opposition implicitly defends—doing less, accepting decline, treating it as natural—is not neutrality. It is an active choice to surrender entire regions. Governments created the asymmetry; they can and should correct it.

[C1.1] The motion asks governments to keep smaller towns and rural regions economically viable. It confuses two distinct goals: helping rural people, and propping up rural places. I accept the first and reject the second, because places follow economics, not sentiment, and the bill for fighting geography is paid by everyone—including the rural residents the motion means to help.
[C1.2] Agglomeration is not mainly a policy artifact. Cities exist because density pays: deeper labor markets, cheaper services, knowledge spillovers, infrastructure shared across more users. PRO credits infrastructure and tax policy with building concentration. Some of that is fair.
[C1.3] But governments have spent decades trying to reverse it—Italy's southern subsidies, Europe's regional funds, Japan's revitalization campaigns—and the results are partial at best. Money flows in; population flows out. If concentration were merely a subsidy artifact, a generation of counter-subsidies would have fixed it. They haven't, because the underlying economics persist.
[C1.4] That failure has a price. Every dollar propping up an uneconomic location is a dollar not spent on schools, research, or healthcare where it compounds. The opportunity cost lands hardest on the poor and the young, who need opportunity, not preservation.
[C1.5] The fairer path invests in people, not places: portable credentials, transport links, broadband that plugs rural workers into urban labor markets, housing where jobs actually are. Dignity does not require a subsidized Main Street. It requires a real choice. Doing nothing to the landscape is not my alternative either—markets and families already adjust.
[C1.6] The question is whether government should spend against that adjustment, and the answer is no. Its job is to make adjustment survivable, not impossible.
[C1.7] Two PRO claims also fail early scrutiny. Rural regions are already overrepresented in most legislatures, so "democratic dilution" is overstated. And food and energy production is land-based and capital-intensive; it does not require dense towns. Concentrating people is not concentrating calories.
