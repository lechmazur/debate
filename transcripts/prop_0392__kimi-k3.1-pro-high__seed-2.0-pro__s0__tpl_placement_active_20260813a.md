[P1.1] A bank that works with a mouse but not a keyboard, a clinic’s booking form that a screen reader cannot interpret, or a video service without captions is not merely badly designed. For disabled users, it can mean missed care, lost independence, or having to disclose private information to someone else just to complete an ordinary task. When the same barriers remain after notification and reasonable opportunities to fix them, continued exclusion is a choice—and public-facing services should face meaningful fines for making it.
[P1.2] The motion is deliberately demanding of persistent failure, not every technical mistake. Basic accessibility includes practical, established features: keyboard navigation, readable structure, text alternatives, captions, and controls assistive technology can identify. A sensible regime gives clear standards, notice, and time to remediate. Fines then apply to repeated noncompliance, scaled to be proportionate yet large enough to matter.
[P1.3] That is accountability for ignoring known obligations, not punishment for imperfect code.
[P1.4] Fines are necessary because voluntary encouragement misprices exclusion. A service captures the savings from skipping accessibility work, while disabled customers absorb the costs in wasted time, dependence on others, and denied opportunities. Competition does not reliably correct this: switching may be impossible where a service is essential, dominant, or tied to an employer, landlord, school, or public agency. Even where alternatives exist, telling disabled people to shop around accepts a separate and lesser digital life.

Keyboard operability, semantic design, and captions are basic engineering disciplines, often improving services for everyone.

[C1.1] This policy does not fix the exclusion it claims to address. It shifts harm onto the very people it purports to help. Meaningful fines never land on the executive who signed off on skipping accessibility work. They land on operational budgets.
[C1.2] When a small municipal library, a rural health clinic, or a community food bank gets a five-figure fine for a broken booking form they already asked their only part-time tech volunteer to fix, that fine does not buy a screen-reader compliant rebuild. It cuts the disabled transport stipend, the drop-in support hours, or the phone line that people without internet use instead. That is not accountability. That is punishing the organisations already operating on zero margin to serve vulnerable users.
[C1.3] Pro claims this only targets wilful, repeated failure. But there is no clean line between an organisation that chooses not to fix a barrier and one that cannot prioritise it. Every single digital service runs a backlog of a dozen critical unresolved bugs at all times. This rule does not punish malice.
[C1.4] It punishes having finite engineering capacity. Worse, this regime will spawn a compliance industry, not accessible services. Operators will not hire accessibility designers. They will hire lawyers to argue about what counts as 'repeated', how much notice was sufficient, and whether a bug technically meets the definition of 'basic standard'.
[C1.5] We have seen this dynamic play out with every fine-based regulation that punishes failure instead of supporting capacity. Disabled users do not get better services out of fines. They get services pulled entirely, or budgets diverted away from the tangible support they actually need. That is the tradeoff this motion will never acknowledge.

[P2.1] The opposition’s central move is to turn a penalty for repeated failure into one for a single broken form. Their own example exposes the trick: a library that has engaged someone to repair a barrier is not automatically fined under notice and cure. The trigger is persistence after clear standards, warning, and reasonable time. A backlog may explain delay; it does not justify indefinite exclusion.
[P2.2] [C1.2] also assumes an arbitrarily crushing penalty. “Meaningful” does not mean identical fines for a food bank and a dominant platform. Proportionate scaling makes sanctions bite without becoming confiscatory. If any financial penalty is illegitimate because it might reduce services, no accessibility duty owed by a constrained organisation could ever be enforced. Finite budgets become a permanent exemption, and disabled users alone are expected to wait indefinitely.
[P2.3] Nor is the choice “fines or accessibility.” Fines make accessibility the cheaper, more predictable choice before budgets are consumed by repeated neglect. A provider can invest in keyboard navigation and usable forms, or continue funding workarounds, complaints, and eventually penalties. Enforcement can prioritise remediation rather than revenue. The compliance-industry objection in [C1.4] is an argument for clear basic standards and consistent process, not for letting repeat offenders ignore them.
[P2.4] The withdrawal threat is especially weak. An essential clinic cannot make exclusion lawful by threatening to leave, and a provider that abandons users rather than caption videos or repair forms demonstrates why voluntary requests are insufficient. Regulated services cannot acquire veto power over accessibility merely by predicting their own noncompliance.
[P2.5] Finally, weigh certain harm against speculative harm. CON offers possible budget cuts; the status quo delivers actual missed appointments, inaccessible transactions, and forced dependence on others. Warnings alone preserve that bargain. Repeated failure shows the warning has failed.
[P2.6] A proportionate fine changes the incentive while preserving the opportunity to comply. That is not punishing scarcity; it is refusing to make disabled people finance scarcity with their rights.
