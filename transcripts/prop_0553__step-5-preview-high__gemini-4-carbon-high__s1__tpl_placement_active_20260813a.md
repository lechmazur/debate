[P1.1] Ownership should not expire when a warranty ends. An Indian family that buys a laptop or smartphone owns the object; it should not be forced into permanent repair dependency because the manufacturer controls parts, diagnostics, and documentation. Today, simple failures—battery degradation, charging-port damage, speaker failure, hinge breakage—can require proprietary software authorization, paired components, or board-level replacement. The device may be repairable in principle, but inaccessible in practice.
[P1.2] A national right-to-repair law changes that default by requiring manufacturers to provide genuine parts, necessary tools, service manuals, and diagnostic access on fair, reasonable terms to consumers and independent technicians.
[P1.3] Nor is this a call for unsafe hacking or counterfeit parts. Regulation can distinguish legitimate repair from dangerous modification: require quality standards, protect sensitive security functions, and restrict genuinely hazardous procedures. The question is not whether oversight is possible; it is whether manufacturers alone should decide what counts as repairable. When firms control parts and information, they also control prices, timelines, and the lifespan of the product.
[P1.4] That is not consumer sovereignty; it is a private lock-in on property.
[P1.5] The stakes are concrete. Independent repair shops and technicians are an important source of affordable service and employment. Giving them legal access to tools and documentation expands competition, lowers repair bills, and keeps useful devices in service longer. A national law provides a common, enforceable floor rather than a patchwork of state rules or voluntary corporate programs that can be withdrawn or narrowed.
[P1.6] It also reduces pressure to replace devices prematurely, limiting electronic waste and the environmental cost of manufacturing. For lower-income households, a repair bill exceeding a device’s resale value is not an inconvenience; it is forced replacement.
[P1.7] The central choice is whether India treats repair as part of ownership or as a licensed privilege. PRO chooses ownership; CON would preserve a system where the buyer pays for the device but the manufacturer still owns the repair.
