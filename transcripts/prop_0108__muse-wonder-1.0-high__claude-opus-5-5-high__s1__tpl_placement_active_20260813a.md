[P1.1] Winner-take-all does not just count votes; it distorts who gets to govern. In a single-member plurality district, a candidate can win with 35 percent, 60 percent can split between similar opponents and lose, and a popular third option is punished as a spoiler. The rational response is not to vote your conscience but to cluster into two big tents and demonize the other, because any defection helps your worst outcome win.
[P1.2] Inside those tents, the decisive election is often a low-turnout primary in a safe, gerrymandered seat. A legislator does not fear losing to a moderate in November; she fears being primaried for compromising. So compromise becomes betrayal, voters outside the two bases become irrelevant, and legislatures fill with members who represent factions, not districts. That is polarization by design, not by accident.
[P1.3] Ranked-choice and proportional systems break that trap at its source. Ranked-choice in multi-member districts — the single transferable vote — lets voters rank sincere first choices, transfers votes from eliminated or surplus candidates, and elects several winners in proportion to support. A group with roughly a fifth of the vote in a five-seat district earns roughly a seat, instead of zero if it finishes second everywhere.
[P1.4] Change the math and you change the behavior. Candidates need second-choice support, so trashing every rival backfires. Broad coalitions beat narrow mobilization. Conservatives in cities and progressives in rural areas finally win representation instead of permanent shutouts, and legislatures must bargain across more than two warring camps — bargaining that produces durable policy because no single faction was handed total power on a minority vote.
[P1.5] Keeping plurality winner-take-all asks Americans to accept minority rule, wasted votes, and engineered extremism as normal. A ranked, proportional legislature would make every vote count toward someone the voter actually chose, force winners to earn broad support, and reward coalition over combat. Representation should reflect the country that voted, not accidents of district lines and split fields.

[P2.2] The Senate proves this, not the opposite. Senators still face winner-take-all primaries and statewide plurality logic, where alienating the base is fatal and courting the other side is optional. Gerrymandering sharpens the House problem; it was never the whole mechanism.
[P2.3] The coalition horror stories also attack a system not proposed here. Israel's nationwide list with a very low threshold invites fragmentation. Multi-member ranked-choice districts do the opposite: in a five-seat district, a fringe needs roughly a fifth of the vote to win, not a sliver, while a group shut out under plurality finally gets its proportional share. Bargaining afterwards is not a betrayal of voters either.
[P2.4] Bargaining already happens — inside party primaries, leadership fights, and must-pass bills — only now just two camps can veto everything. A legislature with several parties must assemble majorities publicly around policies, and voters can punish coalition partners at the next election. [P2.5] On accountability, a safe single-member seat is the weakest accountability of all: the incumbent is chosen in one party's primary and November is a coronation. With several members, voters gain a choice of representatives to approach, reward, or replace without surrendering their views to the other party. Exhausted ballots are likewise dwarfed by plurality's wasted votes — every vote for a losing candidate, and every surplus vote for a landslide winner, elects no one. Alaska's repeal attempt failed after voters had actually used the system.
