[P1.1] When the last fluent speaker of a language dies, something dies that no donation box will ever recover. That is the heart of this motion: cultural memory is a public good, and public goods are exactly what markets and charities systematically fail to supply.
[P1.2] The economics are clear-cut. An endangered language's value is diffuse—it accrues to future scholars, to descendants not yet born, to humanity's store of ecological knowledge and oral literature. No private actor can capture that value, so none funds it adequately. Charity is worse than insufficient; it is structurally mismatched to the task.
[P1.3] Grants run one to three years; language transmission takes a generation. Community archives held together by a volunteer's spare bedroom collapse the moment that volunteer moves, falls ill, or dies. We have watched this happen to small historical societies and minority-language newspapers for decades. Private responsibility, in practice, means public amnesia.
[P1.4] We already accept this logic everywhere else. Governments fund national archives, libraries, and museums because a society's memory is infrastructure—everyone benefits, no one can be excluded, and collapse is irreversible. The motion simply extends the same principle to communities too small or too poor to bankroll their own preservation. Why should a majority culture's history rest in publicly funded vaults while a minority's history rests in a shoebox?
[P1.5] Success stories exist, and they are public. Government-funded immersion schooling in Wales and New Zealand helped pull Welsh and Māori back from decline; Hawaii's public immersion program rebuilt a speaker base that was nearly extinguished. These were not charity projects. They were standing commitments sustained across decades—the only timescale on which language revival works.
[P1.6] CON will warn of politicization and waste. But arms-length funds modeled on arts councils—with peer review and communities themselves setting priorities—already manage that risk in analogous domains. The real choice is not government versus community. It is standing institutions versus quiet extinction.
[P1.7] A fund that outlasts any single donor is the only tool that matches the permanence of what we stand to lose.

[C1.1] The last speaker of a language does not die for lack of a government account. Languages die when children stop speaking them at home. That is the flaw at the centre of PRO's case: it treats cultural memory as a storage problem to be funded, when it is a living practice that must be owned.
[C1.2] First, whoever holds memory holds power. The same states PRO now asks to be custodians spent generations suppressing these languages, through Welsh schoolchildren punished for speaking Welsh and Indigenous children taken into residential schools. A standing public fund makes the state the permanent gatekeeper of which languages count, which elders get recorded, and which histories qualify as "heritage." The histories most worth preserving are often testimony against the state itself.
[C1.3] Do we expect a ministry's fund to prioritise archives documenting that ministry's abuses? Many Indigenous communities explicitly refuse to deposit sacred or restricted knowledge in government systems. PRO's model makes their memory a line item in someone else's budget.
[C1.4] Second, standing funds embalm instead of reviving. Permanent money builds permanent institutions: archivists, databases, grant officers. The result is beautifully catalogued recordings of a language nobody speaks. Money flows toward the professionals who apply for it, not the grandmother teaching her grandchildren.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's own examples prove our point. Māori revival was ignited by communities themselves, through grassroots language nests run by families. Welsh survived because activists fought for it, often against government reluctance. Hawaiian immersion began with community-run preschools.
[C1.6] In each case, communities led and the state eventually followed under pressure. Those communities won because they owned the effort, not because a fund owned them.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO promises permanence, but a standing fund is only as permanent as the next austerity budget. A community that depends on one public tap collapses when that tap is turned off. Memory spread across communities, universities, diaspora networks, and donors is harder to kill than memory resting on a single appropriation.
