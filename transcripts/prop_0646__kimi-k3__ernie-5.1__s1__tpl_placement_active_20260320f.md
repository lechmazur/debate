[P1.1] Private equity's playbook is not a secret: buy a company with borrowed money, push the debt onto the company itself, cut costs hard, sell the real estate out from under it, and exit within five to seven years. Applied to a retailer, that model is tolerable. Applied to a nursing home, it is a machine for extracting value from people who cannot leave, cannot comparison-shop, and cannot complain to anyone with power.
[P1.2] Care quality in elder facilities is almost entirely a function of staffing—how many nurses and aides are on the floor at 2 a.m. Labor is also the dominant cost. So the most reliable way for an owner to hit its return targets is precisely the most reliable way to harm residents: fewer staff, cheaper staff, more antipsychotics standing in for supervision. Research on Medicare nursing-home data has linked private-equity takeovers to lower nurse staffing and measurably higher mortality in the years after acquisition.
[P1.3] That is not an aberration; it is the business plan working as designed.
[P1.4] The asset-stripping variant is worse. HCR ManorCare—one of the largest chains in America—was bought in a leveraged deal, its buildings sold off and leased back, and the operating company bled for rent until it collapsed into bankruptcy. Residents absorbed the risk; investors had already taken their money out.
[P1.5] Markets cannot discipline this. Demand is guaranteed by Medicare and Medicaid—public money that flows regardless of quality. Families choosing a facility in a crisis cannot audit staffing ratios or pierce an ownership structure stacked three shells deep. Where the customer is captive, the payer is the taxpayer, and the product is the safety of the frail, ownership rules are the appropriate instrument.
[P1.6] And note the narrowness of the ask: not nationalization, not a ban on private operation—restrictions on one ownership structure, through transparency mandates, staffing floors, and limits on debt-loading and sale-leasebacks. The capital private equity brings is capital the sector does not need, because the "efficiency" it buys is measured in unpaid care.
