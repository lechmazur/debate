[P1.2] Every time we feed antibiotics to a herd that is not sick, we run a vast evolutionary experiment. Bacteria that survive pass on resistance. Those bacteria do not stay on the farm. They move through meat, manure, water, soil, and workers.
[P1.3] So the cost of cheap pork or poultry is partly paid by a patient in an ICU whose infection no longer responds to first-line drugs. A yield gain booked by agribusiness becomes a mortality risk externalized onto everyone else.

[P1.7] Voluntary restraint fails because the cost falls on the careful farmer while the resistance risk is shared by all. A ban sets a common floor and prevents a race to the bottom. [C1.1] PRO treats every preventive dose as abuse, but prophylaxis is not the same as indiscriminate growth promotion. A veterinarian who gives a high-risk herd a short, targeted antibiotic course before a known disease challenge is practicing medicine: preventing suffering, reducing sepsis, and avoiding later mass treatment. The motion bans that too. That is not protecting public health; it is replacing judgment with a slogan.
[C1.2] The decisive question is whether a blanket ban is the right tool. It is not. Resistance is real, but it is managed by narrowing which drugs are used, excluding highest-priority human medicines, requiring veterinary oversight, enforcing dose and duration rules, improving sanitation, vaccination, and diagnostics. Those measures cut misuse while preserving prevention where it is necessary.
[C1.3] A ban assumes every healthy animal in every setting is identical. It is not. Crowded weaning, transport stress, outbreak exposure, and herd history matter.
[C1.4] PRO says accept higher prices and lower yields. That is easy for affluent consumers, harder for low-income households, school meal budgets, and small farmers who may be priced out. Reduced yields also mean more land, feed, and animals needed per unit of meat, creating environmental and welfare tradeoffs. If domestic production shifts to countries with weaker standards, resistance risk is exported, not solved.
[C1.5] The motion also ignores perverse incentives. If legal preventive use is banned, producers may delay treatment until animals are visibly ill, increasing suffering and mortality; use unregulated black-market products; or substitute less effective, unmonitored alternatives. That can worsen disease spread and increase the need for rescue antibiotics later.
[C1.6] The better principle is stewardship, not prohibition. Ban medically critical antibiotics as growth promoters where possible; permit prophylaxis only under veterinary prescription, with records and time limits; invest in farm hygiene and vaccines. That preserves antibiotics and food security. The proposition overreaches, and overreach is costly.
