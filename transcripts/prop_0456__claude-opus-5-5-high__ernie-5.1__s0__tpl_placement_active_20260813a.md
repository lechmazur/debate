[C1.1] The proposition sounds reasonable on the surface—take without asking, but pay. But that "but pay" hides three fatal problems that make this scheme worse than the alternatives it claims to improve.
[C1.2] First, compulsory licensing assumes you can price what AI training takes from a work. You cannot. The value of a novel, a painting, or a song lies in its expression, its cultural meaning, its place in a creator's body of work. Stripping that into raw statistical patterns destroys exactly what makes it valuable—and no fee can compensate for that destruction.
[C1.3] A statutory rate set by regulators would inevitably be a fraction of what the trained model generates, turning creative labor into a subsidized input for billion-dollar products.
[C1.4] Second, the scheme is administratively unworkable. Modern models train on billions of tokens from millions of sources with no clean attribution. How do you determine which works were "used," in what proportion, and what each is worth? The music cover-song analogy collapses here: a cover is one recognizable work performed by one identifiable artist.
[C1.5] AI training is an opaque, mass-scale process where the contribution of any single work is unknowable. You cannot build a fair distribution system on an unknowable foundation.
[C1.6] Third—and this is the core of my case—compulsory licensing treats creator consent as a nuisance to be bought off rather than a right to be respected. It tells photographers, novelists, and musicians: your work will be ingested, your style will be mimicked, your livelihood will be threatened, but here is a check. That is not protection. That is a buyout at fire-sale prices that legitimizes displacement.
[C1.7] The proposition gives incumbents exactly what they want—legal certainty to train at will—while giving creators a compensation mechanism that will almost certainly underpay and be riddled with disputes.
[C1.8] Free use is theft. Permission is cumbersome. But compulsory licensing is not the middle path. It is theft with a receipt.
