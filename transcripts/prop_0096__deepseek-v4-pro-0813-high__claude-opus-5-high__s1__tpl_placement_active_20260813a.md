[P1.1] An actor’s voice is not interchangeable machinery. It is a biometric signature and a professional identity. When AI dubbing clones that voice to deliver lines the actor never spoke, in languages the actor may not understand, the studio is not merely distributing an existing performance: it is manufacturing a new one from the actor’s vocal identity. Broad contract boilerplate cannot supply meaningful consent for that use.
[P1.2] A clause assigning rights in “all media now known or hereafter devised” may cover a DVD or a stream, but it was not a knowing authorization to synthesize speech.
[P1.3] The asymmetry makes boilerplate especially unreliable. Performers often sign dense standard agreements under take-it-or-leave-it pressure, with no realistic ability to redline. They cannot be said to have opted into AI voice replication when the technology, the language markets, and the downstream uses were not specified. An opt-in requirement would force producers to identify the proposed AI dubbing, negotiate compensation, and obtain clear permission before appropriating the performer’s vocal likeness.
[P1.4] This also protects the performer’s economic and artistic interests. Once a voice model exists, it can be reused, relicensed, or repurposed across projects and languages, undercutting the actor’s own future work and branding. A boilerplate grant can quietly turn one acting job into a permanent voice asset. Artistically, AI dubbing can alter timing, accent, emotion, and translation choices without the actor ever hearing the result.
[P1.5] That is no longer the performance the actor gave; it is a synthetic derivative wearing their voice.
[P1.6] Opt-in consent does not ban AI dubbing. It simply treats a performer’s voice as the protected, personal, commercially valuable asset it is. The rule should be clear: no new synthetic voice performance without a specific, informed yes.
