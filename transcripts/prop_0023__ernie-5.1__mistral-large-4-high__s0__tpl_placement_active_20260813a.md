[P1.2] The consequences are not theoretical. FTX, one of the world's largest crypto exchanges, collapsed in 2022 after commingling billions in customer funds with proprietary trading. Thousands of retail investors lost everything. That was not an accident of bad management.

[P1.4] Bringing these platforms under securities exchange or broker-dealer regulation does not require reinventing financial law. It requires applying existing law honestly. Broker-dealer registration means fiduciary obligations, capital requirements, and audit trails. Exchange registration means listing standards, market surveillance, and investor recourse.
[P1.5] These are not burdens invented to crush innovation—they are the infrastructure that lets traditional capital markets function at scale.
[P1.6] The opposition will argue that crypto is fundamentally different, that innovation demands flexibility, that rigid securities frameworks will drive activity offshore. [C1.1] The proposition rests on a false premise: that most cryptocurrency is secretly a security. Bitcoin and Ethereum—the assets driving the vast majority of trading volume—are commodities and functional networks, not investment contracts. Slapping securities labels on them doesn't make Howey apply; it manufactures regulatory fiction. When regulators pretend every token is a stock, they don't protect investors—they create a compliance maze that only Wall Street incumbents can afford, while retail traders get pushed to unregulated corners.
[C1.2] Securities exchange and broker-dealer rules were built for markets where intermediaries custody assets and trade on your behalf. They don't map onto crypto's reality: 24/7 global markets, self-custody wallets, and decentralized protocols with no CEO to register. Forcing platforms into a broker-dealer framework designed for traditional finance ignores that crypto users often control their own keys. Applying 1930s securities law to 2020s blockchain technology isn't investor protection; it's a regulatory mismatch that drives innovation offshore and leaves American retail investors with fewer safeguards, not more.
[C1.3] The FTX collapse proves this framework is wrong. That was fraud and commingling, not a failure of securities classification. A broker-dealer license wouldn't have stopped the looting of customer funds any more than exchange registration would have. What would have helped is a distinct crypto framework with mandatory proof-of-reserves, segregated custody, and real-time on-chain transparency—rules tailored to blockchain technology, not borrowed from a securities playbook designed for paper stock certificates.
[C1.4] "Lightly supervised" is a strawman. A distinct asset category doesn't mean regulatory anarchy; it means serious guardrails—custody standards, disclosure, and anti-fraud enforcement—adapted to digital assets. We don't regulate gold like Apple stock, and we shouldn't regulate Bitcoin like a penny stock. The tailored approach protects investors better because it actually fits the market it governs, matching oversight to the unique risks of digital assets rather than forcing them into a template that predates the internet.
