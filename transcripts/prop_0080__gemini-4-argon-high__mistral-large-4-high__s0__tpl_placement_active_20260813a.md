[P1.1] When a university cuts a direct check from its media and ticket revenues to a player, the fiction of amateurism is over. At that moment, schools are compensating labor. The only question left is how to govern that commercial relationship: through mutually negotiated rules, or through the current mix of opaque booster deals and unilateral administrative mandates.
[P1.2] First, collective bargaining is the only way to rescue college sports from permanent legal and competitive chaos. Under basic antitrust principles, competing institutions cannot collude to cap compensation, restrict player movement, or enforce standardized contracts on their own. Whenever schools try to impose unilateral "compliance" guardrails, courts strike them down, fueling the exact wild-west environment administrators complain about: constant tampering, broken NIL promises, and chaotic roster turnover. In every other major revenue sport, stability comes from a collective bargaining agreement.
[P1.3] By giving athletes a seat at the table, schools gain the legal ability to negotiate enforceable transfer windows, transparent revenue splits, and multi-year commitments that hold up in court.
[P1.4] Second, ad hoc NIL and school-run compliance fail the vast majority of athletes. An unregulated market rewards the star quarterback who can hire sophisticated representation, while leaving the backup lineman or women's basketball player to navigate shadowy collectives, predatory contract language, and arbitrary institutional pay scales. Unilateral compliance departments exist to protect the university from liability, not the athlete from exploitation. Formal bargaining replaces individual vulnerability with collective floor standards.
[P1.5] It allows athletes to secure audited revenue numbers rather than trusting university accounting, set binding limits on grueling off-season demands, guarantee long-term medical coverage for lingering injuries, and create neutral arbitration when a coach forces a player out.
[P1.6] Schools cannot build a direct revenue-sharing economy while clinging to paternalistic governance. Ad hoc NIL breeds chaos; unilateral compliance breeds lawsuits and exploitation. Only formal collective bargaining delivers the legal durability universities need and the baseline protections athletes deserve.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a governance tool for a solution. Formal collective bargaining is not merely a stronger version of NIL compliance; it is a legal category change that reclassifies athletes as employees, triggering a cascade of harms that ad hoc systems avoid.
[C1.2] First, employee status destroys the tax-advantaged scholarship. Today, athletic scholarships are generally treated as educational benefits, not taxable wages. Under a CBA, compensation becomes salary, subject to income tax, FICA, and potentially jeopardizing the nonprofit status of athletic departments. For a swimmer on a partial scholarship, the tax bill could exceed the revenue share, turning a benefit into a liability that forces athletes to pay to play.
[C1.3] Second, collective bargaining imposes uniformity across incomparable markets. A CBA negotiated primarily for revenue-generating football and men's basketball would set roster sizes, practice hour limits, and compensation floors that make non-revenue sports economically impossible. Gymnastics, track, and women's volleyball would face football's labor costs, forcing departments to cut teams to balance budgets—ironically violating Title IX while destroying the Olympic pipeline.
[C1.4] Third, the adversarial structure is corrosive to the educational mission. College athletics depends on coaches and administrators making rapid, collaborative decisions about player development, academic support, and safety. A union introduces grievance procedures, work stoppages, and adversarial negotiations into a relationship that requires trust and flexibility. When a coach needs to adjust practice schedules for mental health or manage roster depth, a CBA turns every discretionary call into a potential labor dispute.
[C1.5] Finally, the legal landscape is fragmented beyond repair. Labor laws vary dramatically between states; a national bargaining unit faces insurmountable jurisdictional conflicts, especially in right-to-work states where union security clauses are illegal. Ad hoc NIL and compliance systems adapt to local conditions and evolve with the market; a rigid, multi-year CBA cannot.
[C1.6] The choice is not between chaos and order, but between flexible, evolving market mechanisms and a blunt labor instrument that treats education as employment. We should fix compliance systems, not import the strike.
