Similar stories exist for Pacific Islander skulls that clarified patterns of disease, or for African remains that helped trace the spread of sickle‑cell traits.
[C1.3] These are not trivial curiosities; they are data that can improve health outcomes for living descendants.
[C1.4] Second, the claim that non‑destructive techniques make further study unnecessary is false. [C1.5] Third, museums provide preservation environments that most communities cannot match. Climate‑controlled storage, professional conservation, and legal protection against looting are services that safeguard the remains for future generations, including the descendant communities themselves.
[C1.6] Moreover, the forensic field relies on reference collections of human remains to identify disaster victims and solve crimes; stripping museums of these specimens undermines public safety. The PRO’s analogy to organ harvesting ignores that many of these remains are already sampled; the remaining material is often the only source for future questions that no one can yet anticipate.
[C1.7] Finally, the self‑determination argument assumes all Indigenous groups speak with one voice. In practice many nations negotiate collaborative research agreements that keep remains in institutional care while granting cultural oversight. Imposing an unconditional “prompt” rule eliminates these nuanced partnerships and replaces them with a one‑size‑fits‑all mandate that harms both science and the communities it purports to serve.
