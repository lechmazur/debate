[C1.2] First, credibility is not a luxury good; it is the mechanism that makes low-cost disinflation possible at all. Inflation expectations are not passive numbers that drift gently while a central bank "gradually" returns to target. They are forward-looking judgments about whether the bank means what it says. Once households and firms conclude that above-target inflation will be tolerated "for now," wage demands and price-setting adjust to that belief, and the inflation the bank was fighting becomes embedded in contracts and expectations.
[C1.3] At that point, disinflation requires a larger, not smaller, dose of tightening, because the bank must not only slow demand but re-earn credibility it spent tolerating drift. The 1970s are the cautionary case: repeated gradualist attempts to disinflate without inflicting real pain failed precisely because markets learned the bank would blink, requiring the much deeper Volcker-era recession later to finally break entrenched expectations. Front-loaded credibility is cheaper than serial half-measures.
[C1.4] Second, inflation itself is not a benign backdrop while the bank waits patiently. It is a continuous, regressive tax on wages, savings, and fixed incomes, and it degrades the information content of prices that firms rely on to invest efficiently. "Tolerate longer" does not mean tolerating a static, bounded cost; it means compounding that cost every additional month, borne disproportionately by people with the least ability to hedge against it.
[C1.5] Third, the recession PRO fears is not fixed in size. A credible, decisive central bank that moves early needs less total tightening than one that lets expectations drift and must later shock the system to regain control. The real choice is not "mild slowdown now versus deep recession never." It is "smaller pain now versus larger pain guaranteed later."
[C1.6] That comparison favors resolve, not patience dressed up as caution.
