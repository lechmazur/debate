[P1.1] Consider a hospital that uses a new antibiotic just twice in a year, each time because cheaper drugs will not work. For the hospital, that may be exactly the right number of courses. For a company deciding whether to develop the drug, that pattern across hospitals is a reason to invest elsewhere. Patients need a treatment whose commercial prospects get worse the more carefully doctors use it.
[P1.2] An advance market commitment addresses that contradiction before the investment decision is made. A country can guarantee a minimum purchase at an agreed price if a drug meets a specified clinical need and the supplier keeps it available. Unlike a research grant, that promise need not pay out for a failed candidate. And guaranteeing a purchase does not require doctors to prescribe the guaranteed quantity.
[P1.3] The buyer is paying for the ability to treat the next patient, not setting a quota for finding patients.
[P1.4] A high price per course matters, but the right comparison is not with the price of an antibiotic to which the infection is resistant. It is with the cost and harm of leaving the patient without effective treatment. Nor should that patient, or a ward with a depleted pharmacy budget, have to absorb the premium. A national purchaser can cover clinically indicated use while restricting it to cases that justify it.
[P1.5] Permission is not a blank cheque. Buyers can cap their total obligation, demand evidence of benefit over available treatments, and refuse terms that would displace more valuable care. What they should not do is discard a worthwhile agreement solely because dividing its cost by a handful of lifesaving courses yields a high number. Keeping that handful small is part of what success looks like.
