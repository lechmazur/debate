[P1.1] A person's voice, manner, and intimate character are not raw material for a product. When a company builds a chatbot that answers in a dead parent's cadence, recalls private jokes, or comforts a spouse in the register of the marriage, it is not offering a generic memorial. It is manufacturing a simulation of someone who can no longer refuse. That simulation should not exist unless the person consented in life, or their estate consents after death.
[P1.2] The requirement is modest. It is not a ban on obituaries, photo albums, or ordinary remembrance. It is a gate on one specific act: reproducing intimate personality or voice traits so the living can converse with a counterfeit of the dead. Those traits are the closest things we have to a continuing self.
[P1.3] Using them without authorization treats the deceased as inventory: warmth, sarcasm, and private speech harvested because grief is a reliable market.
[P1.4] Consent is the right rule because the interests collide and the dead cannot renegotiate. A widow may want the bot; an adult child may find it a desecration; a company wants daily engagement. None of those parties is a neutral steward of the deceased's dignity. Prior consent settles the conflict while the person can still speak.
[P1.5] Estate consent is the fallback when no directive exists: the legal successor already controls remains, correspondence, and publicity. Personality simulation is at least as intimate as those.
[P1.6] Without that gate, the default becomes permission by silence. Most people never imagine their voice will be cloned for a grieving relative or a subscription service. Silence is not agreement. A bot that never tires, never disagrees, and always sounds like the person you lost can stall grief, invite dependency, and rewrite memory to fit the model's training.
[P1.7] The living deserve honest mourning. The dead deserve not to be drafted into a conversation they never joined.
[P1.8] Require consent first. Then build the memorial. Not the other way around.
