[P1.1] A job is a bargain for labor, not a lease on a private life. When a firm fires someone for lawful off-duty conduct that does not materially affect job performance, it is not protecting the work. It is policing the worker.
[P1.2] That line already does the hard screening. It leaves out crime, on-the-clock misconduct, harassment of coworkers, conflicts of interest, and any genuine disruption that wrecks the job. What remains is residual at-will power: punishment for politics, a legal weekend protest, union talk after hours, a dating life, legal cannabis where the state allows it, or an unpopular hobby a manager simply dislikes.
[P1.3] That is not efficiency. It is surplus control. Most people need the paycheck more than any one firm needs any one employee, so “just find another job” is not a remedy. It is a tax on privacy and dissent.
[P1.4] The result is a chilled citizenry. People mute themselves, skip lawful civic activity, and hide legal conduct because HR might treat it as a firing offense.
[P1.5] Reputation arguments fail the same test. If off-duty conduct truly tanks the work—lost clients, inability to perform, documented disruption—it already materially affects performance. What the other side wants is a looser veto: speculative brand anxiety, customer prejudice, or managerial taste. That is not business necessity.
[P1.6] It is outsourcing social punishment to the workplace.
[P1.7] Stronger protection does not freeze employers. It requires them to show the job was actually harmed. That is the ordinary liberty rule: coerce only for real effects, not dislike. Without that floor, at-will employment means the boss owns the hours you do not work.
