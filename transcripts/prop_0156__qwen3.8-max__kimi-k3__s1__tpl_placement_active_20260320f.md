[P1.1] The eurozone’s post-2010 response saved the currency in the narrowest financial sense, but it did so by turning a monetary union into a disciplinary regime. That is why the proposition stands: the response deepened political distrust more than it preserved European solidarity.
[P1.2] The core problem is that crisis management was organized around conditionality, not mutual insurance. Bailouts were not grants among equals; they came with memoranda that dictated budgets, pensions, privatizations, and labor markets. In Greece, Ireland, Portugal, and later Cyprus, elected governments were told that the price of liquidity was policy surrender. The message received by debtor societies was not “we are in this together,” but “trust us while we supervise you.”
[P1.3] This created a two-tier political order. Creditor states, above all Germany, gained leverage over domestic choices; debtor states were cast as moral failures requiring correction. That moralization was toxic. In the north, publics were told their taxes were bailing out irresponsible southerners; in the south, publics saw foreign technocrats imposing recession and unemployment.
[P1.4] Both narratives fed resentment, not solidarity.
[P1.5] The mechanism is visible in political outcomes. Mainstream parties that accepted austerity were hollowed out; anti-EU movements surged in Greece, Italy, Spain, and France. The eurozone itself became an electoral liability, not a shared achievement. Even where the currency survived, citizens began treating Brussels, Frankfurt, and Berlin as adversaries rather than common institutions.
[P1.6] CON will likely say institutions prevented collapse and later built banking union or recovery funds. But preserving a system is not the same as preserving trust. A response that requires emergency loans, memoranda, and threats of exit proves that solidarity was thin and conditional. The euro survived as an architecture; the political bond was damaged.
[P1.7] That is the decisive comparison.
