[P1.1] White-collar work is no longer insulated. Generative AI already performs the core of many knowledge jobs: drafting, summarizing, coding, research, analysis, correspondence, and first-pass legal work. Those tasks are not a small slice of office labor. They are the job.
[P1.2] Previous technology mostly automated muscles or routine clerical steps, then generated whole new categories of cognitive employment. This wave attacks that employment itself. A model can be copied infinitely, deployed across thousands of firms at once, and improved without hiring. Firms do not adopt it to keep headcount constant.
[P1.3] They adopt it so one remaining worker plus software replaces several. A document-review team of twenty becomes three. Those seventeen do not become supervisors.
[P1.4] The creation story is thinner than it sounds. Product managers and workflow designers are real and better paid. They are also few. You do not need a specialist for every displaced copywriter, junior analyst, translator, bookkeeper, or first-year associate.
[P1.5] Complementary demand offsets losses only if extra output still requires people rather than more inference. When the bottleneck is already cognition, cheaper cognition often concentrates work among fewer people.
[P1.6] The shock is unusually simultaneous. Law, software, media, finance, consulting, and administration are hit on overlapping timelines, not sequenced over generations. Labor markets do not reallocate that cleanly. Skills are specific, mid-career workers cannot all become model trainers, and wages fall before new occupations appear at scale.
[P1.7] That is why policy cannot wait for the body count. Insurance, portable benefits, wage support, and retraining that maps to remaining demand take years to build. If we wait until payrolls fall, we will be designing programs in a professional-class recession. Preparing early is not panic.
[P1.8] It is acknowledging that white-collar disruption will be faster than the last industrial adjustment, and politically more explosive if we treat it as just another productivity boom.

[C1.3] This is the lump-of-labor fallacy with better marketing. PRO assumes the quantity of white-collar work is fixed, so machines doing "the job" means fewer jobs. But the job is not the output. [C1.4] Cheap drafting means more contracts, more compliance, more disputes handled. AI attacks the task, and tasks are elastic.

[C1.6] On policy: "prepare early" sounds modest, but there is nothing to prepare for that is known. Durable programs built for a speculative mass displacement become permanent transfers chasing a phantom, while pulling resources from the concrete, slow-motion problems we already have.
