[P1.1] Everywhere else in a minor's life, we accept a simple principle: parents decide until children are ready to decide for themselves. Thirteen-year-olds can't buy alcohol, sign contracts, or drive, because we trust parental judgment over adolescent impulse. The smartphone is the bizarre exception — the one space where the default is that an engagement-optimized algorithm outranks the people legally and morally responsible for the child.
[P1.2] The motion closes that exception at the only level where control is actually possible. App-level parental controls lose an arms race: any teen can find a thirty-second tutorial on bypassing app timers — delete and reinstall, switch to the mobile browser, open a second account. Each app is a separate configuration battle against companies whose business model depends on the parent's failure. The operating system is the chokepoint.
[P1.3] One authenticated layer beneath every app means the rule follows the child across apps, browsers, and reinstalls. Note the operative phrase: "cannot easily bypass." Not a perfect lock — just one a fourteen-year-old can't pick in the school bathroom. A parent can lock the liquor cabinet; today she cannot lock the recommendation engine.
[P1.4] Recommendation limits matter as much as age limits. The deepest harm isn't what teenagers seek out; it's what gets served to them — autoplay rabbit holes, appearance-amplifying feeds, doom cycles tuned by systems no parent can negotiate with. A parent should be able to say "no algorithmic feed" for their child the way they say no to other defaults that exploit immature judgment.
[P1.5] And notice what CON must defend. The motion says *allowed*. It creates a capability, not a mandate; parents who prize teen autonomy can leave every switch off. Opposing it means insisting that no parent should ever possess effective tools — that corporate access to a child's attention is constitutionally superior to the family's authority.
[P1.6] That isn't teen freedom. It's the status quo where the algorithm always wins.

[C1.2] A recommendation limit is a political judgment: which feed, which news, which clinic page, which identity forum, which dissenting video. Once that judgment lives in the operating system, one adult and two platform monopolies become the censors of a developing mind.
[C1.3] Teenagers are not toddlers with better passwords. Thirteen-to-seventeen is the period in which judgment is practiced under imperfect conditions — peers, mistakes, uncomfortable ideas. An unpickable OS layer does not teach that skill. It postpones it, then outsources the postponement to Apple or Google, who must now decide what counts as a “recommendation,” what counts as age-appropriate, and which independent messenger or browser is allowed to exist only if it submits to the parent API.
[C1.4] PRO’s “just a capability” line is how infrastructure becomes obligation. The moment the lock exists, schools, platforms, and family courts treat using it as ordinary care. The parent who refuses looks negligent. The teen whose device is locked does not become safer; they borrow a friend’s phone, buy a cheap second handset, or stop telling anyone what they are actually seeing.
[C1.5] Silence is the safety failure. The same device that serves appearance spirals is the device a kid uses to search self-harm help, contraception, coming-out resources, or news that contradicts the household.
[C1.6] And not every household deserves a choke point with no appeal. Controlling, abusive, or ideologically sealed parents get the same unbypassable switch as careful ones. The motion gives them a tool to erase secular news, queer information, rival religion, or evidence of their own harm — and it tells third-party apps they may not offer an easy exit.
[C1.7] App timers are clumsy. This architecture is worse: it treats adolescence as a permissions layer and calls the result authority.
