[P1.1] A dead person cannot say no. That fact decides the rule. If companies may take a lifetime of emails, posts, recordings, and interviews and train a chatbot that answers as if the person still exists, then death itself becomes a consent form. It is not. The only rule that treats a person as more than training data is an explicit opt-in while they can still give it.
[P1.2] This motion is narrow. It does not freeze archives, biographies, or ordinary search. It stops a specific act: building a mimic. A mimic does not quote.
[P1.3] It improvises. It greets a child, sells a product, or revises a public record in a voice that sounds continuous with the dead. That is not commemoration. It is identity capture.
[P1.4] Opt-out after death is a fiction. The person is gone. Families are grieving, divided, or simply outmatched. Firms have every reason to treat leftover traces as free raw material and hide residual objections in fine print.
[P1.5] Once the model is trained, the copy cannot be walked back. The scrape is the decision.
[P1.6] We already refuse to let a face or a name become free commercial property the moment someone dies. Personality is more intimate than a photograph. A private parent turned into a companion bot, a worker whose messages become a helpful ghost, a public figure sanded into brand-safe opinions—these are not edge cases. They are the product once the default is permission.
[P1.7] People who want a digital afterlife can choose it. Companies can contract for living likenesses. Fiction and composites remain available. Requiring consent costs delay.
[P1.8] Skipping it is irreversible: someone who never agreed is made to keep speaking. Law should not let silence after death do the work of a signature.
