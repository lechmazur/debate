[P1.1] Antitrust law exists to protect competitive markets from distortions of power. For decades, merger review has operated with a fatal blind spot: obsessing over pennies added to consumer prices while turning a blind eye to dollars stripped from worker paychecks. Competition policy must treat labor-market monopsony with the exact same analytical rigor and enforcement priority as consumer-price effects.
[P1.2] First, the economic harm is identical. Monopsony is the structural mirror of monopoly. When two hospital systems or logistics giants merge in a region, they do not just gain leverage over customers; they often become the only viable buyer of specialized labor. Just as a monopolist restricts output to inflate prices, a monopsonist depresses compensation below competitive levels and restricts employment.
[P1.3] Both generate classic deadweight loss and misallocate economic resources. Distinguishing between a cartel that inflates retail prices and a consolidated buyer that suppresses wages is economically indefensible.
[P1.4] Second, workers are vastly more vulnerable to market power than consumers. A consumer facing an inflated price can typically substitute brands, buy less, or defer a purchase. A worker whose local labor market consolidates cannot simply substitute their livelihood. Geographic immobility, specialized skills, and family commitments trap workers in place, leaving them defenseless against suppressed wages and deteriorated workplace safety.
[P1.5] Third, ignoring monopsony perverts merger review itself. When regulators examine only consumer effects, merging firms routinely disguise labor-market leverage as "cost synergies" and "efficiencies." Slashing payroll by exercising buyer power is not productive innovation; it is wealth extraction enabled by a lack of outside options for workers.
[P1.6] Competition policy cannot claim to defend dynamic, competitive markets while treating the destruction of labor competition as an irrelevant externality. If a merger degrades the competitive process, that harm is fatal whether it strikes at the checkout counter or on the paycheck.

[C1.1] PRO's case commits a category error: it proves labor monopsony can be harmful, then assumes merger review must weight it co-equally with price effects. Harm is not the test; administrability and merger-specific causation are. The question is whether a legal test can identify merger-specific labor harm reliably and prospectively without turning every transaction into wage speculation.
[C1.2] Consumer-price effects, for all their limits, are anchored in observable product overlaps, customer switching, and standard price-concentration analysis. Labor monopsony requires defining a labor market, estimating worker mobility, and separating merger effects from licensing, noncompetes, commuting costs, union bargaining, and economy-wide wage trends. In a hospital merger, price effects can be modeled through patient flows and payer contracts; wage effects may depend on nurse commuting zones, spousal employment, specialty shortages, and state licensing rules. Give labor effects co-equal weight and regulators must either guess or demand vast new evidence for every merger.
[C1.3] That is not rigor; it is a false-positive machine. Merger review is a one-shot decision with real costs: delayed or blocked transactions that could lower costs, improve service, or expand employment. PRO's “cost synergies” point cuts both ways: some payroll changes are genuine efficiency, some are monopsonistic extraction. Equal priority lets speculative extraction claims defeat demonstrated consumer benefits, even when merger-specific labor harm is small or unproven.
[C1.4] Nor does worker vulnerability prove parity. Vulnerability argues for effective labor-market policy—noncompete reform, licensing reform, portable benefits, direct conduct enforcement—not for making merger review the all-purpose wage regulator. Labor monopsony should be considered where evidence is concrete and merger-specific. But the proposition demands equal seriousness and enforcement weight.
[C1.5] That subordinates administrable price effects to speculative wage effects and weakens competition policy.
[C1.6] The choice is not ignore workers or treat labor exactly like prices. It is disciplined screening versus parity by slogan. Merger review must weigh harms it can prove and remedy, not every harm it can imagine.
