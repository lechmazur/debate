[P1.1] Start with opportunity cost. Active reforestation in Europe and North America is capital-intensive: nursery stock, mechanical site prep, planting, fencing, and often irrigation. Rewilding abandoned farmland asks succession to do the work for free. That cost gap should anchor every comparison.
[P1.2] On carbon, planted programs usually optimize for timber volume, not total ecosystem storage. Monocultures lock carbon into merchantable wood while neglecting soil carbon, fine-root networks, and coarse woody debris—the pools that endure. Site preparation itself disturbs and oxidizes existing soil carbon. Passive rewilding avoids that damage while rebuilding multi-layered, self-organizing vegetation with deeper roots and richer organic horizons.
[P1.3] Across vast tracts from the Carpathian foothills to the Appalachian plateau, natural regrowth is already converting former cropland and pasture into durable carbon stocks without nursery invoices or planting crews.
[P1.4] On biodiversity, the contrast is starker. Plantation forestry is agriculture with trees: narrow niches, uniform age classes, and simplified structure. Rewilding lets pioneer herbs, thickets, mid-successional woodland, and veteran trees emerge in a shifting mosaic, creating microhabitats that planting cannot replicate. That heterogeneity supports pollinators, cavity-nesters, ungulates, and even returning predators, while abandoned-field corridors reconnect fragmented landscapes.
[P1.5] You cannot purchase that complexity with a seedling.
[P1.6] The proposition does not claim rewilding is universally optimal. It claims per-dollar superiority over most active programs. Because active reforestation spends heavily to supply what nature provides gratis—seed dispersal, competition, self-thinning, and succession—rewilding dominates the efficiency frontier. The cheapest ton of carbon and the most resilient habitat are the ones we stop manufacturing and simply allow to return.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a sleight of hand: dividing by near-zero dollars and calling the result efficiency. If a field is simply left alone and regrows anyway, a "rewilding program" that spends nothing has not sequestered a gram or restored a single species. It has watched. Carbon and biodiversity gains that would have occurred regardless are not returns on investment; they are the baseline.
[C1.2] Per-dollar comparisons only mean something where money changes outcomes, and that is precisely where rewilding stops being free.
[C1.3] Because abandoned land does not reliably become forest. Across much of Europe and eastern North America, succession stalls. Old pasture locks into bracken, bramble, or dense invasive thickets—buckthorn, autumn olive, honeysuckle—that can persist for decades with low canopy carbon. Compacted, nutrient-stripped soils and the absence of seed sources in landscapes cleared for generations mean that "self-organizing vegetation" often organizes into very little.
[C1.4] Deer browsing finishes the job. Fixing arrested succession takes fencing, invasive control, and enrichment planting, which is active restoration under another name.
[C1.5] Worse, in the Mediterranean, abandonment produces fire-prone shrubland. Spain, Portugal, and Greece have watched abandoned terraces convert to continuous fuel loads that burn repeatedly, releasing stored carbon and resetting the clock. A carbon stock that combusts every fifteen years is not "durable."
[C1.6] On biodiversity, PRO has the direction of harm backwards for Europe. The continent's richest terrestrial habitats—chalk grasslands, hay meadows, wood pasture—are products of low-intensity farming. Their orchids, butterflies, and farmland birds are collapsing because fields are being abandoned and scrub closes in. In Europe, abandonment is among the leading drivers of biodiversity loss, not a cure for it.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO argues against a caricature. Most active reforestation today is not Sitka spruce monoculture; it is mixed native planting, assisted regeneration, and direct seeding, often costing a few hundred dollars per hectare. That modest spend delivers canopy decades sooner, in species we choose, on sites where nature alone would stall. Paying a little to guarantee a forest beats paying nothing to gamble on bramble.

[P2.1] CON’s baseline objection concedes more than it denies. If passive regrowth would build woodland anyway, active planting must prove it adds enough carbon and biodiversity to justify the spending. Most programs fail that test because they incur costs rewilding avoids: site prep that oxidizes soil, fencing, beating-up failures, weed control, and later thinning.
[P2.2] The stall cases in [C1.3] are real but narrower than claimed, and they indict active planting too. Degraded pasture, invasive thickets, and seed-poor landscapes are exactly where expensive establishment most often fails or requires repeated treatment. Rewilding can use targeted, low-cost nudges—seed trees, invasive removal, or grazing—rather than full planting. Even arrested scrub often stores more carbon and offers more structure than mown pasture.
[P2.3] The Mediterranean fire point in [C1.5] is equally double-edged. Active forestry has repeatedly planted flammable pine and eucalyptus across the region. Natural succession toward mixed broadleaf woodland can break fuel continuity, and grazers can maintain an open-woodland mosaic. A carbon stock reset by harvesting every few decades is no more durable.
[P2.4] [C1.6] attacks a different proposal. Europe’s chalk grasslands and hay meadows are not marginal abandoned fields, and rewilding need not mean indiscriminate abandonment. Extensive grazing can preserve heterogeneity—open sward, scrub, veteran trees, and regenerating woodland. Planting trees does the opposite faster: it closes canopy, simplifies structure, and eliminates open-habitat species sooner than succession.
[P2.5] So the decisive comparison remains per dollar. Active programs purchase timber canopy quickly, but not total ecosystem carbon, deadwood, root networks, or resilience. Rewilding lets nature supply those for a fraction of the cost, leaving funds for genuine problem sites.
