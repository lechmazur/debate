[P1.1] When elections are decided by the subset of citizens who happen to show up, the result is not full popular rule; it is rule by the mobilized, the affluent, the older, and the intensely partisan. Stable democracies should therefore require voting, enforced by modest, proportional civil penalties, because the first duty of democratic law is to make the electorate resemble the people.
[P1.2] Voluntary turnout is never random. It is shaped by income, education, age, mobility, and campaign spending. Candidates rationally chase likely voters, not all citizens. That means policy tilts toward those whose participation is already predictable, while low-income workers, young adults, and marginalized communities become electorally invisible.
[P1.3] A legal duty to vote flips the incentive. If nearly everyone must cast a ballot, parties cannot win by merely energizing a base or buying turnout. They must persuade the broad public.
[P1.4] This is not a demand for coerced opinion. The secret ballot remains intact. Citizens can be required to present themselves and submit a ballot, while remaining free to choose a candidate, cast a blank or spoiled ballot, or register protest. The obligation is participation, not conviction.
[P1.5] That distinction is familiar: jury service, tax filing, and census participation are compulsory not because the state owns the mind, but because shared institutions collapse if everyone can opt out at no cost.
[P1.6] Proportional civil penalties keep the mandate legitimate. A small income-adjusted fine, with exemptions for illness, caregiving, disability, or unavoidable absence, is less intrusive than criminal sanction and far less distorting than a system where only motivated factions decide elections. The goal is not punishment but norm formation: voting becomes a routine civic act, like renewing a license.
[P1.7] The decisive question is simple: should democratic outcomes reflect the whole public, or only those who clear the hurdles of a voluntary system?

[C1.2] Compulsion can manufacture a turnout statistic; it cannot manufacture consent.
[C1.3] Jury service, tax filing, and census forms demand labor, money, or facts needed to administer government. Voting authorizes rulers and chooses among rival visions of public power. The secret ballot protects choice inside the booth; it does not protect the prior judgment that none of the choices deserves endorsement. Abstention can be political speech: a refusal to legitimate a corrupt slate or unresponsive party system.
[C1.4] Stable democracies answer that with persuasion and reform, not a summons.
[C1.5] The proportional fine makes the injury concrete. Income adjustment requires inspecting incomes, policing excuses, and deciding whose crisis counts. Broad exemptions swallow the rule; strict ones punish vulnerability.
[C1.6] Even civil fines generate records, notices, and collection pressure, teaching the least secure citizens that the ballot box is another office that can bill them.
[C1.7] If representative outcomes are the aim, lower hurdles: simple registration, accessible polling, early and postal voting, and let parties earn support instead of waiting for the state to deliver an electorate. Those reforms widen participation by making voting worthwhile; compulsion hides alienation behind turnout. The dilemma in [P1.7] is false. The real choice is between citizens who vote because public power is worth answering and citizens who vote because refusal has a price.
[C1.8] The first is representation; the second is administered compliance.
