[P1.1] The motion asks a comparative question, so let me put the comparison on the table plainly: for adolescents who have been carefully screened and whose dysphoria has persisted, the documented mental-health benefits of gender-affirming care are substantial and consistent, while documented regret in this population is rare. That asymmetry is the debate.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism, because it is not mysterious. Persistent gender dysphoria is a source of severe distress — clinically, these adolescents show elevated rates of depression, anxiety, self-harm, and suicidal ideation. Puberty is a one-way clock: it permanently masculinizes or feminizes the body in ways that are difficult or impossible to reverse later. For a screened adolescent whose dysphoria has endured through assessment, watching that clock run is itself a harm.
[P1.3] Affirming care — starting with reversible puberty blockers, then hormones only after further evaluation — interrupts an escalating source of distress. It would be surprising if that did *not* improve mental health, and clinical experience since the original Dutch protocol has repeatedly found improved psychological functioning, reduced dysphoria, and wellbeing approaching that of peers among carefully assessed youth who transitioned.
[P1.4] Now the other side of the ledger. Regret exists, and honesty requires saying so — but among adults who transitioned after rigorous adolescent assessment, regret rates in follow-up research have consistently been very low, typically in the low single digits, far below regret rates for many routine medical procedures we consider uncontroversial. Detransition stories that dominate headlines overwhelmingly involve people who were *not* carefully screened, or who faced social hostility — precisely the failure modes the motion's screening condition excludes.
[P1.5] Notice what the motion does not ask. It does not ask whether every gender-questioning teen should medicalize, or whether screening is always done well. It stipulates careful screening and persistence. Within that population, the evidence points one direction: large, recurring mental-health gains against a small, well-characterized rate of regret.
[P1.6] That is the case CON must overturn.

If you narrow the definition enough, of course rates look low.
[C1.6] Persistent dysphoria in adolescence is real distress, but persistence is not permanence. Distress deserves intensive psychological care, not a bet that irreversible medicalization is safer than waiting. The balance here is uncertain short-term relief against certain long-term bodily change, and that is not a win for mental health.
