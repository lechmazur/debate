[P1.1] The current system of locked, non-transferable digital tickets is a raw deal for ordinary fans, and blanket resale bans punish legitimate users while failing to stop scalpers. When a fan purchases a ticket, they acquire a legitimate right to access the event, and restricting their ability to transfer that access—whether to a friend who can’t attend, or to recoup costs if a life emergency forces them to miss the show—is an unjustified infringement on their property rights, unless organizers can prove a specific, narrow scalping risk that can’t be addressed with less restrictive rules.
[P1.2] Scalpers already bypass most transfer restrictions by using fake accounts and bot networks to buy tickets at scale, so blanket bans do nothing to stop their activity. They only harm regular fans: a parent who buys a family ticket to a live show months in advance, then has to cancel last minute due to a child’s illness, loses the full cost of the ticket if they can’t resell it, and the seat sits empty, costing the venue and performer revenue too.
[P1.3] This is a lose-lose for everyone except bad actors.
[P1.4] The proposition already builds in a critical carve-out: organizers may implement narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits, such as resale price caps set at face value plus administrative fees, or restrictions on bulk resales by single buyers. These tools target the actual harm of scalping without penalizing fans making legitimate transfers. For example, many European football leagues use capped resale platforms that let fans transfer tickets safely while blocking price gouging, with no meaningful spike in scalping activity. Blanket transfer bans are a sledgehammer where a scalpel is needed, and fans deserve the legal right to use their tickets as they see fit, within reasonable, targeted guardrails.

[C1.1] A digital ticket is not property; it is a revocable license to occupy a specific seat at a specific time under terms set by the venue. The proposition mistakes a conditional service contract for a chattel, and in doing so hands scalpers a legal infrastructure they currently lack. Peer-to-peer transfer creates an untraceable secondary market that no "narrowly tailored" rule can effectively police. Once a ticket leaves the primary issuer's ecosystem, the chain of custody disappears.
[C1.2] A scalper can flip a ticket through five pseudonymous wallets before it reaches the gate, and the organizer cannot distinguish a gift to a friend from a five-thousand-dollar markup without invasive surveillance of every fan's private transactions.
[C1.3] The proposition also inverts the burden of proof. Rather than organizers demonstrating why they need control, they must prove a specific scalping risk justifies restricting transfer. That is backwards. Venues have legitimate interests in controlling who enters their space, preventing fraud, and ensuring revenue reaches artists rather than bots.
[C1.4] When an act prices tickets below market value to reward true fans, an unrestricted peer-to-peer right destroys that strategy overnight; the face-value subsidy flows to the reseller, not the fan.
[C1.5] Furthermore, "narrowly tailored" anti-scalping limits are a mirage in a peer-to-peer context. Price caps require knowing the transaction price; bulk-purchase limits require knowing the buyer's identity. Both collapse when transfers happen wallet-to-wallet, off-platform. The only way to enforce the carve-outs is to build a surveillance apparatus tracking every fan's resale—hardly narrow.
[C1.6] The status quo already permits transfers through official channels when organizers allow them. A legal right to peer-to-peer transfer does not liberate fans; it legalizes the extraction layer between artists and audiences.
