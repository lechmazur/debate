[P1.1] This rule is a low-cost, high-impact consumer protection fix for a pervasive exploitative practice on short-term rental platforms. Right now, platforms intentionally hide mandatory cleaning fees, resort fees, and binding house rules until the final checkout or booking confirmation step, trapping users who have already invested time filling personal and payment information, creating artificial pressure to accept unexpected costs or terms they never reviewed. The first core harm is deceptive hidden fee pricing, a textbook bait-and-switch. A user browsing a $180 per night lake cabin sees a seemingly affordable listing, only to find a $120 cleaning fee, $90 daily resort fee, and $50 local tax added at checkout, pushing the effective nightly rate up 58% with no prior warning.
[P1.2] This is not a rare edge case: multiple consumer surveys find hidden fees add an average of 20% to advertised rental prices, with some users reporting total costs 2 to 3 times the listed rate. This practice disproportionately harms low-income travelers, students, and families on fixed budgets, who may be forced to either absorb the unexpected cost or abandon their trip after wasting hours of search time. The FTC has already banned similar hidden resort fees for hotels, explicitly recognizing that hiding mandatory costs until the final step is inherently deceptive to consumers.
[P1.3] The second core harm is undisclosed binding house rules, which create avoidable conflict and financial loss. A user booking a rural cottage for a friend’s small birthday dinner may find out only after paying that the property bans all gatherings of more than 4 people, leading to a $300 penalty or loss of their $400 deposit. A pet owner may book a place that silently prohibits dogs, forcing them to pay last-minute kennel fees or cancel with a non-refundable penalty.
[P1.4] These are binding contract terms users cannot consent to if not disclosed upfront. The proposed rule imposes zero meaningful burden on platforms, which already collect and store all this information. [clipped]

A disclosure regime that drowns its own signal makes consent worse, not better.
[C1.6] And "zero meaningful burden" is simply wrong: platforms would be warranting the accuracy of thousands of hosts' idiosyncratic prose on their most compressed screen.
[C1.7] The defensible rule is total price and material terms before commitment. That protects the traveler PRO is worried about. This motion instead freezes interface design at an arbitrary click, and calls it protection.
