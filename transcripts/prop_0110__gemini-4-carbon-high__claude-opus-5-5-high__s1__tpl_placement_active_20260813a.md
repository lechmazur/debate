[P1.1] Healthcare violates the basic rules of consumer markets, which is why routing medical spending through fragmented private insurers systematically wastes money and costs lives. Judging population health outcomes per dollar comes down to two questions: how much medicine each dollar buys, and whether care arrives before illness turns catastrophic. On both fronts, public systems hold a decisive structural advantage.
[P1.2] Start with what a dollar buys. In predominantly private systems, a massive share of spending never touches a patient. It vanishes into corporate profits, marketing, underwriting, and billing warfare between competing insurers and providers. Worse, fragmented payers lack unified bargaining leverage against hospital monopolies and drugmakers, letting prices skyrocket.
[P1.3] Public systems pool populations broadly, cutting middleman bureaucracy and using bulk purchasing power to secure lower prices for identical drugs, scans, and surgeries. When care costs far less to administer and buy, every dollar stretches much further.
[P1.4] Now consider population health outcomes. Because workers regularly switch jobs and plans, private insurers face constant subscriber churn. Private carriers have weak incentive to fund lasting prevention; paying to manage early diabetes today mostly saves a rival insurer from funding dialysis decades later. Public systems cover people continuously, aligning fiscal stewardship with primary care and early screening.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, private systems ration through premiums, deductibles, and coverage gaps. Pricing patients out of cheap hypertension pills until they suffer an emergency stroke is both lethal for population health and ruinously expensive.
[P1.6] Empirical reality confirms these mechanics. Across industrialized democracies, universal public systems in Europe, Canada, and East Asia consistently achieve longer life expectancy and lower infant and maternal mortality than the predominantly private American model, for far less per capita. Even hybrid countries using private carriers only contain costs when strict public rules outlaw underwriting, cap prices, and mandate universal pools. Whenever private insurance predominates, overhead swells and early care shrinks, leaving public systems far ahead on health delivered per dollar.
