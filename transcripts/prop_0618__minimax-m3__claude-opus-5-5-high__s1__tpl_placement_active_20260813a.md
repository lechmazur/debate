[P1.1] Three reasons outcomes deserve the dollars.
[P1.2] First, public higher education's purpose isn't filling seats. It's transforming enrollees into graduates with real economic mobility. When funding follows enrollment alone, we reward recruitment and ignore results. The for-profit sector's collapse is the cleanest cautionary tale: aggressive enrollment, dismal graduation rates, and students buried in debt.
[P1.3] Head-count metrics would have kept the money flowing right up until the doors shut.
[P1.4] Second, the two proposed metrics — graduation and post-graduation earnings — measure what we actually claim to care about. Graduation captures whether an institution teaches well enough to bring students across the finish line. Earnings captures whether the credential translates into economic value. Either metric alone is gameable; combined, they force balance.
[P1.5] Easy degrees won't satisfy the earnings test. Résumé-padding programs won't satisfy the graduation test.
[P1.6] Third, on the access objection the opposition will raise: outcomes-based funding need not punish schools serving disadvantaged students. The motion's word is "improvement," not absolute level. A community college lifting graduation rates on the same population is doing heroic work and should be rewarded. Enrollment funding flattens that distinction — same dollar whether the school is moving the needle or not.
[P1.7] The decisive question is simple. Do we want public money tied to what taxpayers are told they are buying — graduates who can support themselves — or to a head count that can be padded indefinitely? Enrollment is a means. Graduation and earnings are the ends.
