[P1.1] Legal personhood is a legal tool, not a metaphysical riddle. Corporations, ships, and municipalities all hold rights and duties without being human. When a river is treated as a rights-bearing entity, it can sue polluters, hold title to its own bed, and be represented by guardians. The sky has not fallen anywhere this has been tried.
[P1.2] New Zealand's Whanganui River has had legal personhood since 2017, represented by guardians appointed jointly by the Crown and the iwi who fought for over a century to have the river recognized as an ancestor rather than property. Canada's Magpie River received personhood at the request of the Innu community of Ekuanitshit. These are not utopian experiments; they are functioning legal arrangements.
[P1.3] The proposition's core is consent. It does not say every river becomes a person; it says personhood should be granted when Indigenous or local communities who live with that river ask for it. That condition does real work. Communities that depend on a watershed for food, flood protection, and identity are its best-informed stakeholders.
[P1.4] Denying their request means telling them their river is a resource to be managed by others, for others—a direct continuation of the dispossession that characterized colonization. Granting it is a modest act of self-determination, and governments that ignore it tend to end up in court anyway.
[P1.5] The objection is that this "complicates infrastructure development." It does, deliberately. Current law already weights development heavily: rivers can be dammed, diverted, and dredged with permits issued through processes that communities often lose. Personhood rebalances the ledger by forcing proponents to treat destruction of the river as harm to a party with standing, not merely a cost line in an environmental assessment.
[P1.6] Complication is what meaningful scrutiny looks like. We did not reject labor rights because they complicated factories.
[P1.7] A river that cannot speak in court cannot defend the people who depend on it. Give it a voice when those people ask.

[C1.1] The motion is not modest recognition. It is a transfer of public authority. Legal personhood is not a plaque; it gives an entity standing, rights, and the power to sue, block permits, and demand remedies. But a river cannot instruct its lawyers.
[C1.2] Human guardians do—and the motion requires governments to empower those guardians whenever a requesting community asks. That is where PRO's case collapses. "Request" is not public consent, and a community is not a single voice. Corporations have charters, shareholders, and finite purposes; a river-person has no such limiting structure.
[C1.3] Indigenous and local communities are plural. A council, iwi, tribe, or environmental group claiming to speak for a watershed does not automatically speak for farmers, fishers, towns, downstream residents, or rival claimants. The proposition outsources a contested public decision to whichever group organizes first and calls that self-determination. Democratic governments can consult, negotiate, and co-manage; they should not be legally bound to convert one faction's request into a trump card over everyone else.
[C1.4] PRO's examples actually prove the danger. Whanganui and Magpie emerged from bespoke, negotiated settlements with defined guardians, funding, and political buy-in. They are not evidence that a general request-triggered rule works; they are evidence that river personhood is so contentious it requires treaty-like bargaining, not automatic entitlement.
[C1.5] Nor is infrastructure complication incidental. Flood barriers, water storage, hydropower, transmission, ports, and climate adaptation often serve the very communities involved. Personhood creates open-ended litigation: what does the river want? Courts must invent answers, and the safest answer for guardians is "no." That is a veto, not scrutiny.
[C1.6] We can protect rivers through enforceable water quality standards, co-management, trust duties, and liability for damage—without inventing a rights-holder that always needs human ventriloquists. The real choice is between transparent collective decisions and a metaphysical legal fiction that lets some people speak for nature against others. CON.
