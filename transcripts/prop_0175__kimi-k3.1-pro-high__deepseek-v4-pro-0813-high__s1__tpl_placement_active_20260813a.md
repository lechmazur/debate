[P1.1] Public-safety rules should be judged by how closely they track actual danger, not by the reassuring word “license.” In these fields, the fit is often backwards: entry is expensive and slow, while the specific risks licensing supposedly addresses could be handled directly. A hair braider who uses no chemicals or heat may be pushed into broad cosmetology training rather than a short sanitation requirement. An interior designer selecting furnishings and finishes is not thereby performing structural engineering; dangerous building, electrical, and gas work can remain governed by codes, permits, and specialist licenses.
[P1.2] That mismatch reveals the primary function of these regimes. Genuine safeguards focus on the hazardous act: infection control, fire safety, inspection, or competence in a particular technical task. Barriers focus on the person seeking to compete: years of education, unpaid experience, examinations covering unrelated work, recurring fees, and approval by boards commonly drawn from the occupation itself. When experienced practitioners are grandfathered in while newcomers must clear costly hurdles, the policy protects market position without applying the same alleged safety standard to everyone.
[P1.3] The economic mechanism is straightforward. Restricting supply allows incumbents to charge more and reduces pressure to improve service. Meanwhile, aspiring workers with limited savings—often people seeking flexible, self-employed work—are kept out even when customers would willingly hire them. Consumers do not necessarily become safer; they may pay more, delay work, attempt it themselves, or use an informal market with less recourse.
[P1.4] None of this requires abolishing safety regulation. Targeted sanitation rules, inspections, insurance or bonding where warranted, enforceable anti-fraud laws, and voluntary certification address identifiable harms without granting incumbents control over entry. Certification can inform consumers while still allowing different routes to competence.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is therefore not “licensing or chaos.” It is broad occupational gatekeeping versus regulation aimed at the risk itself. Where the burdens are broad, the hazards are narrow, and cheaper safeguards are available, licensing operates primarily as protection from competition. Public safety becomes its justification; incumbent protection is its predictable design and effect.

[C1.1] Licensing is not perfect, but the motion needs more than cost and inconvenience to show that these rules are primarily cartel protection. In these occupations, the central function is still to reduce risks that customers cannot easily evaluate before harm occurs.
[C1.2] For hair braiding, the hazard is not only chemicals. Reused combs, clips, razors, and unwashed hands can transmit bacterial or fungal scalp infections; excessive tension can cause traction alopecia and permanent hair loss. A consumer cannot inspect sanitation while seated in the chair. A short licensing requirement focused on hygiene, scalp health, and infection control is a direct safeguard.
[C1.3] If a state piles on unrelated cosmetology hours, that is overbreadth—not an argument against licensing as a safety tool.
[C1.4] Interior design has public consequences. Designers choose finishes, clearances, egress paths, lighting, and material specifications that affect flammability, smoke spread, falls, and accessibility in schools, clinics, offices, and apartments. Structural engineering licenses do not catch these choices. A license can require non-structural code literacy precisely where fire and egress risks live.
[C1.5] The same applies to many trades called low-risk. Small electrical, plumbing, or refrigeration work can create fire, carbon monoxide, contamination, or water damage that clients cannot detect, and failed informal work often leaves them without remedy. A license can condition, suspend, or revoke practice; a lawsuit after the damage is a weaker protection.
[C1.6] Incumbent benefit is a weak test. Every regulation raises entry costs, and every incumbent benefits from less competition. That does not prove intent or primary effect. Licensing bodies commonly impose inspections, continuing education, complaint handling, and public members—features a cartel would not bother designing.
[C1.7] The right response is to reform bad licensing, not to label professional licensing in these fields primarily a barrier. The motion turns legitimate examples of overreach into an overgeneralization.
