[P1.1] Start where congestion bites: dense city cores, when roads are already near capacity. Ride-hail is sold as convenience, but it injects extra vehicle trips and empty miles into the most fragile part of the network. It did not just replace taxis or private cars; in big cities it pulled riders off buses, subways, bikes, and walking, turning space-efficient trips into single-occupant car trips. The apps did not create new road space; they created new reasons to use it.
[P1.2] That substitution drives the problem.
[P1.3] Congestion is competition for scarce road space. Transit moves dozens in one vehicle; ride-hail usually moves one or two. Pulling commuters from buses to app cars replaces one efficient vehicle with many inefficient ones. Each trip also carries hidden mileage: empty driving to the pickup, cruising between fares, and circling for curb space.
[P1.4] That is added travel with no passenger. And because traffic breaks down nonlinearly once roads reach a tipping point, extra low-occupancy cars in a downtown core add disproportionate delay for everyone. That is the difference between a merely busy road and a failed one.
[P1.5] In New York, San Francisco, and Chicago, app cars concentrate downtown, stop in travel lanes, double-park, and block bus stops. A short hop that once carried thirty people by bus, or needed no vehicle at all, becomes one car, one passenger, plus an empty approach leg—especially at rush hour—stuck in the jam it helped create.
[P1.6] Con may cite lower car ownership or less drunk driving. Those are real, but they sidestep congestion. A former transit rider now taking an Uber adds a car; a former driver who would have driven anyway is about a wash once deadhead miles are counted. The urban pattern is more cars, fuller roads, worse occupancy.
[P1.7] On the motion's test—overall congestion in major cities—ride-hail made traffic worse.
