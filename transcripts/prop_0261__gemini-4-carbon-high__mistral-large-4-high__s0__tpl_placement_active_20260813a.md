[C1.1] The proposition asks us to treat a software license as a family heirloom, but that category error collapses under basic legal scrutiny. When a consumer clicks "Buy" on a digital storefront, they are not purchasing a copy of a book or film; they are purchasing a revocable, non-transferable license to access content hosted on corporate servers. This distinction is not buried "fine print"—it is the entire legal architecture that makes digital distribution viable. Mandating that these licenses pass to next of kin does not modernize inheritance law; it voids valid contracts and destroys the consideration behind digital sales.
[C1.2] Worse, the assurance that privacy remains untouched is illusory. A digital library is never just files. It is a comprehensive metadata dossier: exactly what the deceased read, when they read it, how long they lingered on each page, their private annotations, their purchase timing, and the specific devices they used. Transferring this "library" to next of kin does not transfer a physical object; it transfers intimate behavioral data that the deceased never consented to disclose to anyone, let alone relatives who may have conflicting interests.
[C1.3] Physical books contain marginalia; digital libraries contain surveillance records.
[C1.4] Add the enforcement nightmare. Guaranteeing transfer requires platforms to verify death, adjudicate competing claims from next of kin, and hand over credentials—creating a fraud magnet. Death certificates can be forged, inheritance disputes are common, and every transferred account becomes a target for criminals harvesting passwords and payment methods. Platforms face impossible liability: transfer too readily and they enable identity theft; transfer too cautiously and they violate the statute.
[C1.5] Finally, the economic logic is perverse. If purchased digital assets must be perpetually inheritable, platforms will eliminate the purchase option entirely, retreating to subscription-only models that the proposition excludes. This law would not preserve digital libraries; it would guarantee their extinction by making the one-time purchase legally untenable.
