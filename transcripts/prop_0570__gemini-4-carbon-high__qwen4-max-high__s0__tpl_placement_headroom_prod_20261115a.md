[P1.1] Effective secondary education matches the tool to the cognitive task. Mastery practice, whether factoring quadratics, balancing chemical equations, or drilling verb conjugations, operates within bounded rules and verifiable targets. What learners need there is immediate, step-by-step repetition. Waiting days to discover a procedural mistake cements bad habits into memory.
[P1.2] Structured AI tutoring excels at this convergent work: it diagnoses specific slips instantly, adapts problem difficulty on the fly, and gives struggling students patient, judgment-free scaffolding that one teacher facing thirty adolescents cannot deliver simultaneously.
[P1.3] Secondary school writing, however, is fundamentally divergent and communicative. A quadratic equation has a definite answer; a student's essay on Macbeth or civic justice does not. Writers at this stage are moving beyond basic sentence assembly to build sustained arguments, weigh contested evidence, and forge an authentic voice. Language models do not comprehend lived meaning, test subtle logical leaps, or sense what a hesitant teenager is trying to articulate beneath clumsy phrasing.
[P1.4] Instead, they pull prose toward statistical convention. Made the main source of feedback, AI rewards safe, formulaic polish while flattening risky nuance and original style, often praising fluent paragraphs built on hollow reasoning.
[P1.5] Above all, writing is an act of persuasion aimed at a real mind. Teenagers invest in revision when a teacher or peer genuinely wrestles with their claims and takes them seriously as thinkers. Handing primary evaluation to software turns composition into cynical rubric-gaming for an audience nobody actually persuades. Rejecting AI as the main respondent still permits auxiliary support, like flagging surface mechanics before submission, while keeping human readers central to revision.
[P1.6] Indeed, both commitments reinforce each other: delegating routine mastery practice to AI reclaims scarce teacher time from repetitive grading so educators can remain the primary readers of student thought.
