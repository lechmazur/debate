[P1.1] Copyright law has always rested on a simple premise: those who profit from reproduction bear responsibility for it. When a platform deploys an AI system that can reproduce substantial portions of copyrighted work on demand, and when that platform profits from every query, the premise applies with full force.
[P1.2] The core issue isn't whether users press the button. It's who built the machine, trained it on protected material, and monetized the output. These platforms aren't passive conduits like a telephone company routing calls. They constructed and deployed systems that actively synthesize content, they set the parameters that determine what those systems can produce, and they collect subscription fees or advertising revenue from the results.
[P1.3] That is active participation in potential infringement, not passive hosting.
[P1.4] Traditional safe harbor frameworks like the DMCA were designed for a world where platforms stored and transmitted content created by human users. The platform didn't write the infringing post — some person did. That logic breaks down entirely when the platform's own AI system generates the content. Saying "a user prompted it" is like a photocopier manufacturer arguing it bears no responsibility for mass reproduction of books because a customer pressed "copy."
[P1.5] The mechanism of reproduction is the platform's product.
[P1.6] Shared liability — not exclusive, not strict, but shared — creates the right incentive structure. If platforms face no consequence, they have every reason to build maximally capable systems with minimal safeguards. Introduce liability and suddenly it becomes commercially rational to invest in output filtering, licensing agreements with rights-holders, and meaningful opt-out mechanisms. Copyright holders, who often lack the resources to pursue individual users, get a viable path to remedy.
[P1.7] The creative economy that feeds the AI training pipeline in the first place gets some protection.
[P1.8] Without shared liability, we hand platforms an extraordinary gift: extract value from creative work, deploy systems that can reproduce it, and face zero accountability because a user typed the prompt. That is not a principled legal position. It is a loophole large enough to collapse the copyright system entirely.
