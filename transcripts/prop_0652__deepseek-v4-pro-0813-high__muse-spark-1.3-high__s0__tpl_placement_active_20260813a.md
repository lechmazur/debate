[P1.1] When a platform turns a prompt into an image or text, it is not hosting someone else's upload; it is manufacturing the copy. Liability should follow that role. The user supplies the request; the platform supplies the model, the stored patterns, the generation, and the distribution. If the output reproduces protected expression, the infringement is a joint product.
[P1.2] Shared liability simply matches shared causation.
[P1.3] This is not a marginal case. A user can prompt for the opening of a well-known novel, a character design, or a song fragment, and the system can emit substantially similar material. The platform then stores and serves that output to others. Without the platform, the user has a string of words, not an infringing copy.
[P1.4] The platform is the entity best able to detect the copied material before it spreads, compare outputs against known works, filter high-risk prompts, license training data, or block reuse.
[P1.5] Incentives are the core. If only the user is liable, infringement remains cheap for the platform. It collects engagement, advertising, subscriptions, and data while the copyright holder chases millions of difficult-to-identify users. That is a losing enforcement model.
[P1.6] Making platforms share liability forces them to internalize the cost of the outputs they profit from. They will build better safeguards only when the alternative is legal exposure.
[P1.7] Critics will call this a blow to innovation. But the proposal is shared liability, not automatic punishment for every surprising output. A platform that takes reasonable precautions and responds to valid infringement notices should not face the same treatment as one that optimizes for imitation and hides behind a prompt box. The law routinely asks high-volume commercial intermediaries to police the harms their systems predictably produce.
[P1.8] The deeper error is treating generative AI as a neutral pipe. It is not. The platform selects the model, trains it, deploys it, and monetizes it. When that machine reproduces existing work, the platform is part of the infringing act and should share the legal consequence.

[C1.2] A photocopier can reproduce a book, Photoshop can forge a poster, a search engine can surface pirated text. We never made Xerox share liability for every infringing copy because the machine did the printing. Generative platforms are the same: they execute instructions. [C1.3] Causation without volition is not authorship.
[C1.4] Shared liability would force platforms to police imagination in advance. The cost falls on ordinary users and artists experimenting with new tools, while copyright holders gain a heckler's veto over transformative work.

Startups, open-source projects, and niche creative tools cannot, so they never launch. Innovation consolidates, competition shrinks, and culture becomes permission-based.
[C1.6] The fairer rule is clear: hold the prompting user responsible for infringement, and hold platforms to responding to valid notices about distributed copies — not to insuring every keystroke before it happens.
