[P1.1] Traffic deaths are a preventable public health crisis, and the most effective response directly addresses the conditions that produce crashes rather than their legal aftermath. Lower default urban speed limits work because speed is the primary determinant of crash survivability: a pedestrian hit at 20 mph has approximately a 95% chance of survival, while at 40 mph that probability falls to around 20%. Street redesign takes this further by making safe speeds the path of least resistance. Narrowed lanes, speed humps, chicanes, and protected bike infrastructure don’t just ask drivers to slow down—they engineer the road environment so that speeding becomes physically uncomfortable, visually unappealing, and practically difficult.
[P1.2] These are systemic, upstream interventions that prevent the collision from occurring in the first place. Post-crash penalties, by contrast, can only respond after lives have been lost or permanently altered. Their deterrent effect is fundamentally limited because most traffic crashes result from momentary human errors—distraction, misjudgment, or brief lapses in attention—not from deliberate, calculable risk-taking that harsher punishments would influence. The empirical record is clear: London’s citywide expansion of 20 mph zones reduced fatal and serious injuries by roughly 40%.
[P1.3] Portland’s traffic calming initiatives delivered a 50% reduction in collisions in treated areas. Sweden’s Vision Zero program, built on lower speeds and safer street design, cut traffic deaths in half. These results demonstrate that designing safety into the road network works, while punitive measures can only react to tragedies that proper design would have prevented. The choice is stark: we can engineer streets that make deadly crashes impossible, or we can continue to rely on a justice system that can only respond after the fact.
[P1.4] For the goal of reducing traffic deaths, prevention through lower limits and redesign is not just better—it is the only approach that addresses the root cause of the problem.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes traffic death is primarily an engineering problem. It is not. The decisive share of fatalities comes from a minority of drivers—aggressive speeders, impaired drivers, repeat offenders—whose behavior is not a momentary lapse but a pattern. Lower default speed limits and street redesign are passive tools aimed at the average, law-abiding road user.
[C1.2] They slow down people already inclined to comply. They do little to stop someone driving 50 through a school zone or barreling onto a sidewalk after eight beers. For the goal of reducing deaths, the system must identify and incapacitate that high-risk minority, and only post-crash penalties do that.
[C1.3] Harsher penalties are not merely retributive; they are protective. A driver with multiple reckless-driving convictions who faces license revocation and real incarceration is a driver who cannot kill again next month. That is a direct, mechanical reduction in risk. No roundabout or speed hump removes a dangerous driver from the road; the justice system can.
[C1.4] Without that backbone, redesign operates on a population that was mostly not the problem.
[C1.5] Redesign also has structural limits. Traffic calming is expensive, takes years, and can shift danger rather than eliminate it. Narrow a busy arterial and some reckless driving spills onto quiet residential side streets where children play, unless enforcement is there to stop it. Passive design invites adaptation; penalties keep the boundary firm.
[C1.6] The choice is not between engineering and punishment; it is between nudging the compliant and restraining the dangerous. If the goal is fewer funerals, the marginal dollar belongs with the enforcement and penalty architecture that removes killers from the wheel—not with another speed sign that a killer can ignore.

The justice system remains the backbone; without it, street redesign relies on the compliance of the very drivers least likely to comply.
