[P1.1] The future is already disappearing. Not in a distant dystopia, but through dead links, obsolete formats, bankrupt publishers, and orphaned works whose owners cannot be found. If traditional copyright enforcement must mediate every act of digital preservation, much of humanity’s recent heritage will be deleted by default. Nonprofit digital libraries such as the Internet Archive should be exempt from traditional copyright enforcement because their work fulfills the public purpose copyright was meant to serve.
[P1.2] Copyright is instrumental, not sacred. It grants temporary monopolies to encourage creation, not to guarantee that culture evaporates when enforcement becomes inconvenient. Traditional enforcement assumes a living market and a locatable rights holder. Digital heritage often has neither.
[P1.3] A 1990s website, an out-of-print monograph, a deleted recording, or the only crawl of a small community archive: there is frequently no license to buy and no owner to ask. When a library can preserve such a work only by risking liability, the law is not preventing theft; it is mandating loss.
[P1.4] Nonprofit libraries change the calculus. They have no profit motive, strong norms of access and attribution, and a mission that aligns with the public good. Unlike pirates, they do not flood markets with competing copies; they rescue works markets have already abandoned or never served. Conditioning exemption on nonprofit status, transparency, and noncommercial use answers legitimate fear of abuse without sacrificing preservation.
[P1.5] The stakes are civilizational. We are the first generation that can record culture comprehensively and the first that can lose it in one format shift. If we force digital preservation through enforcement machinery built for albums and bestsellers, we will protect mainly what is already profitable and lose the fragile, the obscure, and the ordinary. Exempting nonprofit digital libraries is not a loophole for infringement; it is the only realistic way to keep the modern record from becoming a blank space.

The real controversy—and what this motion demands—is granting digital platforms blanket immunity to scan, host, and distribute in-copyright works to the entire world without a license, without compensation, and without consent.
[C1.2] That is not preservation; it is unauthorized mass republication.
[C1.3] Copyright is not an enemy of culture; it is the economic foundation that makes culture possible. Writers, researchers, and independent creators rely on digital licensing—including legitimate library e-book markets—to earn a living. If establishing a nonprofit mission confers total exemption from copyright enforcement, those markets disintegrate. Digital libraries would become global, substitute distribution platforms competing directly against the very creators whose works they circulate.
[C1.4] You cannot sustain human heritage by starving the people who produce it.
[C1.5] Moreover, a blanket exemption is a reckless remedy. True market failures, like unlocatable rights holders or obsolete formats, warrant targeted solutions: clear statutory carve-outs for orphan works, extended collective licensing, or secure preservation archives. It lets private platforms decide unilaterally that their distribution goals override an author's property rights.
[C1.6] The Internet Archive was not sued for archiving dead hyperlinks; it was sued for distributing modern, in-print books for free. An exemption from enforcement does not merely rescue the obscure; it expropriates the active. To safeguard digital heritage, we must preserve both the artifact and the creative ecosystem that brings it into existence. Blanket legal immunity destroys the latter.
