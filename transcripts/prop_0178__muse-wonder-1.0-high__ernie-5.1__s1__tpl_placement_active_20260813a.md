[P1.1] Population health is decided less in clinics or classrooms than in factories. Ultra-processed foods supply a huge share of calories, and their salt, sugar, and fat are invisible by design. You cannot read your way out of sodium baked into bread, sugar in sauces and cereals, or fat engineered for shelf life and craving. Reformulation mandates change the product itself; labeling and education ask people to navigate a harmful product more carefully.
[P1.2] That difference determines reach and equity. A label works only if a shopper notices it, understands it, has time to compare, can afford an alternative, and can resist price promotions and marketing in that moment. Each step loses people, disproportionately children, low-literacy shoppers, shift workers, and low-income families at highest risk of diabetes and heart disease. A cap on salt in bread or sugar in drinks requires none of that.
[P1.3] The healthier choice becomes the default choice, delivered passively to everyone who eats, including those no campaign will ever reach.
[P1.4] It also determines magnitude. Tiny reductions per serving, repeated across billions of servings, move population blood pressure, blood sugar, and calorie intake in ways individual willpower cannot match. Removing industrial trans fats through restrictions virtually eliminated a lethal ingredient without requiring a single consumer to learn chemistry. Gradual salt cuts work similarly because taste adapts when the whole market moves.
[P1.5] By contrast, labels typically produce small, uneven shifts among already motivated shoppers, while companies game serving sizes and health halos faster than consumers learn rules.
[P1.6] Finally, mandates solve the industry's race to the bottom. A company that unilaterally cuts sugar or salt risks losing on taste to competitors; a uniform cap removes that penalty and lets taste adapt across the market. Education, meanwhile, pits a leaflet against billions in advertising for hyperpalatable, cheap, ubiquitous food.
[P1.7] Labels ask millions of people to win the same fight every day. Reformulation ends the fight before it reaches the shelf.

[C1.1] Reformulation mandates sound like a silver bullet, but they are a blunt instrument that can backfire in ways no labeling campaign ever could. When you cap salt, manufacturers replace it with potassium chloride or flavor enhancers that some consumers find intolerable, driving them toward unregulated alternatives or simply eating more of the product to chase satisfaction. Cap sugar in one category and industry shifts it into adjacent products that escape the regulation. The result is not a cleaner food supply but a game of whack-a-mole that leaves consumers confused and trust eroded.
[C1.2] The proposition's fatal weakness is its absolutism: "more than any labeling or education campaign." That is an impossible bar. We already know that well-funded, culturally targeted education initiatives — think of sustained public health campaigns around sugary drinks that combined media, school curricula, and community outreach — have shifted consumption patterns measurably in multiple countries. These campaigns do not merely inform; they reshape norms.
[C1.3] A teenager who internalizes why excessive sugar matters does not need every yogurt reformulated.
[C1.4] Mandates also carry equity costs the proposition ignores. Reformulation can raise production costs, and those costs land on the cheapest products that low-income families depend on. If a cap makes shelf-stable bread more expensive or less palatable, the people most at risk of diet-related disease may simply buy less or turn to unregulated street food. Education campaigns cost far less per capita and reach people through channels they already use.
[C1.5] Most importantly, mandates treat symptoms. They adjust one ingredient in one product category while leaving the broader food environment untouched — marketing budgets, portion distortion, cooking skill erosion. Education attacks the root: it equips people to make better choices across every aisle, every meal, every day, long after any regulation is repealed or undermined by lobbying. The proposition asks us to believe a top-down cap beats every possible bottom-up effort.
[C1.6] That claim cannot survive contact with how people actually eat.

That is not a neutral engineering tweak; it is a new health risk that a label would never introduce. If a mandate creates a hidden danger, the net population benefit evaporates, and the proposition’s claim of “improving health more than any campaign” collapses.
[C2.2] PRO’s absolutism defense in [P2.2] rests on the idea that a cap reaches every bite while education must be repeated. Yet the proposition compares mandates to “any labeling or education campaign,” and the most potent campaigns already combine mandatory front‑of‑pack traffic‑light labels with school curricula and media bursts. Chile’s label‑plus‑education rollout cut sugary‑drink purchases far more than the country’s modest sodium limits, showing that a well‑designed label system can out‑perform a narrow cap across multiple nutrients at once.
[C2.3] Equity is turned on its head in [P2.4] and [P2.5]. Production adjustments raise the price of staple breads and cereals that low‑income households buy in bulk; the cost is regressive because the cheapest items bear the burden. A pictorial label, by contrast, costs nothing to the consumer and can be understood at a glance, even by low‑literacy shoppers. Mandates therefore impose a financial penalty on the very groups the proposition claims to protect.
[C2.4] The “root‑versus‑symptom” claim in [P2.6] ignores that reformulation is vulnerable to industry lobbying and category‑shifting, whereas education builds lasting agency. When people understand why excess sugar harms, they choose better across every aisle, not just the reformulated shelf. A cap that merely masks one ingredient cannot match the breadth of a comprehensive, culturally tailored education effort that reshapes norms, cooking skills, and demand for healthier products.
[C2.5] In short, mandates risk substitution harms, regressive costs, and limited scope, while the best possible labeling and education campaigns already demonstrably achieve equal or greater health gains. The proposition’s absolute claim cannot survive these realities.
