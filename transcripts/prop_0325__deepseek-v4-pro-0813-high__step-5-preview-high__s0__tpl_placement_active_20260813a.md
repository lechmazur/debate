[P1.1] A sports fan who follows one team is not buying a television network; they are buying access to that team. Yet most streaming models force them to pay for dozens of teams, leagues, and shoulder programming they will never open. That is not a premium experience; it is a tax on loyalty.
[P1.2] The core failure is price bundling that ignores actual demand. A Milwaukee Bucks fan in Oregon does not need every NBA game, but the cheapest legal path often requires a full League Pass or a cable-replacement bundle carrying national networks. A tennis fan who only watches the four majors must buy a general sports package that includes rugby, college lacrosse, and talk shows. The result is predictable: some fans overpay, many refuse to pay, and others turn to illegal streams.
[P1.3] Team-specific and competition-specific passes fix this by matching price to value. A lower-priced single-team NBA pass, a Champions League club pass, or a Grand Slam tennis pass would convert casual viewers into paying customers. The marginal cost of delivering one more stream is tiny; the rights holder already produces the broadcast. Revenue lost from a few bundle subscribers is replaced by volume from fans who were never going to buy the full package.
[P1.4] This is not speculation. It is the same logic that made single-song purchases work alongside albums, and single-article subscriptions work alongside full digital access.
[P1.5] The objection that bundles subsidize niche sports confuses a business model with a moral obligation. If a league wants to fund lower-profile competitions, it can price the specific pass to include a small contribution, or keep the broad bundle as an option. No one is arguing to ban bundles. The proposition is that fans should not be forced into them as the only legal route.
[P1.6] The debate should turn on consumer welfare and market access. Cheaper specific passes expand the paying audience, reduce piracy, and respect the basic principle that you should not have to buy the whole stadium to watch your team.
