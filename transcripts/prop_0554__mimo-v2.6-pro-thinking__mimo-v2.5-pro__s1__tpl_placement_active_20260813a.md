[P1.1] Cheap, clean power is being strangled by a planning system that lets a small local minority veto infrastructure the whole country needs. That is the status quo in England, where onshore wind was effectively banned for a decade by a planning rule requiring near-unanimous local acceptance — a standard applied to no other form of development. The EU faces parallel paralysis: grid connection queues stretch for years, and permitting, not technology, is now the binding constraint on wind and solar.
[P1.2] The proposition asks whether we relax these restrictions even when local opposition is strong. We should, because the costs of the veto fall on everyone while its benefits accrue to the few who exercise it.
[P1.3] Start with the arithmetic of the transition. Onshore wind is among the cheapest sources of new electricity available in Europe, and wind and solar cannot decarbonise anything if the wires to carry their power don't get built. Every year of delay is a year of continued gas burn, higher wholesale prices, and missed carbon budgets that are legally binding. Climate targets set by the EU and UK are national and international commitments; they cannot be met if any parish council holds a stop sign over a pylon route.
[P1.4] Second, "local opposition" is not a neutral fact of nature — it is a predictable response to a system that concentrates costs locally and socialises benefits nationally. That is fixable without veto power. Community benefit funds, discounted local electricity, revenue-sharing, and rigorous but time-limited environmental review let host communities genuinely gain. Germany, Denmark, and Scotland all show that ownership models and benefit payments turn wind farms into local assets rather than impositions.
[P1.5] What we must not do is convert legitimate grievance into a permanent right of refusal.
[P1.6] Third, on fairness: the households paying the highest energy bills, in draughty homes and left-behind regions, are the least represented at planning hearings. A veto-heavy system is regressive. Relaxing restrictions — while paying, compensating, and consulting — is the more democratic outcome, not less.
