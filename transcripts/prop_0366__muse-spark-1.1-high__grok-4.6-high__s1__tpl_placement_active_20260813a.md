[P1.1] The default is a choice to exclude.
[P1.2] Right now studios treat a theatrical-only window as normal and accessible home viewing as an afterthought. For disabled audiences, that means if you use a wheelchair and your local theater has no accessible seating left, if you are Deaf and need open captions that your multiplex shows once at 10am on Tuesday, if you are blind and the audio description headset is dead or not handed out, if you are autistic and cannot tolerate 30 minutes of blaring trailers with no way to pause or dim, you simply do not get to participate.
[P1.3] You wait months.
[P1.4] That wait is framed as patience. It is actually segregation.
[P1.5] Theaters cannot fix this with retrofit. An accessible experience requires control: over captions, volume, description, light, sound, pace, breaks, sensory input. Theatrical exhibition by design removes control. You cannot rewind a caption you missed.
[P1.6] You cannot turn up description without asking a manager. You cannot pause when overwhelmed. And for immunocompromised people or those who need personal care attendants, the logistical cost of a theater trip can be prohibitive even if the building technically complies.
[P1.7] A simultaneous release with robust options at home does what theaters structurally cannot. Accurate open captions and descriptive audio built into the file, not a separate glitchy device. Ability to adjust, replay, bring your own support. That is not a lesser experience, it is a more accessible one, and often the only independent one.
[P1.8] And it grows rather than shrinks the audience. People who crave the big screen will still go. But millions more will pay day-one at home because they can finally watch on equal terms. Making culture immediate for disabled people instead of delayed should be the default for more big movies, not the rare exception.

[C1.1] Theatrical-first is not a decision to lock people out. It is how a big movie becomes a big movie: a scarce public event that pays for the work, the marketing, and the later home versions. Collapsing that window as the default does not make culture more equal. It makes the thing being shared smaller, poorer, and less widely available in public.
[C1.2] Accessibility and day-and-date are not the same policy. Captions, description, better seating, working headsets, quieter screenings, and faster home arrival can all improve without treating every tentpole as a living-room premiere. The proposition binds a real duty to a destructive distribution choice and then calls the package justice. That is a false trade.
[C1.3] Disabled viewers deserve access to cinema, not a mandate that cinema stop being cinema first.
[C1.4] Home is also not the automatic equalizer. Broadband, a quiet room, a working screen, a paid subscription, and the ability to navigate settings are not universal. For many people the local theater is still the only large, shared screen they can reach. If simultaneous release becomes normal, those rooms lose the exclusive draw that keeps them open.
[C1.5] Fewer screens, thinner schedules, and dead downtown houses are not accessibility gains. They are lost public options, including for wheelchair users and caption-seeking audiences who do want a communal night out when the theater actually works.
[C1.6] Nor does the wait equal segregation. A release window is a pricing and presentation sequence, not a caste line. Calling delay “exclusion” treats every staggered good as a civil-rights violation and leaves no room for the economics that fund the next film. People who crave the big screen will not “still go” at the same rate once the same file is at home tonight.
[C1.7] Urgency dies. Revenue thins. Mid-budget titles vanish. What remains is cheaper product with fewer robust options of any kind.
[C1.8] Keep theatrical-first as the default for big titles. Fix the rooms. Speed the afterlife. Do not burn the window to prove the point.
