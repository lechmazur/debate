[P1.1] When a parent spends two decades housing, feeding, and financing a child's path into adulthood, they are making an investment with no legal guarantee of return. That is fine when adult children choose gratitude freely. But when a parent is impoverished and vulnerable, and an adult child who can afford to help simply declines, society faces a clear injustice — and a clear policy choice.
[P1.2] The moral case is reciprocity. Parents bear legal duties toward dependent children: financial support, shelter, healthcare. That duty exists because dependence creates obligation. The relationship does not reverse arbitrarily when a parent ages; it mirrors itself.
[P1.3] A parent who cannot meet basic care costs through savings or public programs is genuinely dependent. The adult child who benefited from years of parental provision and now has the means to help is the natural counterpart to that dependency. Declining to help is not neutral — it shifts the cost to strangers via public funds or leaves the parent without adequate care entirely.
[P1.4] The practical case is equally strong. Aging populations across wealthy and middle-income countries are straining public care systems. Filial responsibility laws already exist in dozens of American states, across most of Europe in various forms, and prominently in countries like China and India. These are not exotic or novel impositions — they reflect a mainstream legislative judgment that family members with capacity bear some share of this burden.
[P1.5] The question is not whether such laws can exist, but whether we should enforce the principle coherently.
[P1.6] The enforcement objection — that we cannot compel love or measure relationships — is a red herring. We do not need to compel love. We need to compel financial contribution from those with means, proportionate to capacity, where a parent genuinely cannot self-support. Courts already assess financial capacity in divorce maintenance and child support.
[P1.7] The same analytical toolkit applies here.
[P1.8] The alternative is either an undertaxed free-rider problem — adult children inheriting parental assets while the state funds parental care — or simply abandonment dressed as liberty.
