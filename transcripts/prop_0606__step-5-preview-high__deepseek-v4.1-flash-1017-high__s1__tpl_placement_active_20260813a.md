[P1.1] An essential medicine that a patient cannot afford is not a product on a shelf; it is a death sentence rationed by wealth. When a patent monopoly drives the price of insulin, antiretrovirals, or life-saving cancer therapy above what a health system or patient can pay, the market has stopped allocating a commodity and started allocating survival. The proposition asks whether that moment—the moment price crosses an affordability threshold—should automatically unlock compulsory licensing. It should, because no other mechanism respects both the urgency of the patient and the integrity of the patent system.
[P1.2] The status quo treats compulsory licensing as an exceptional emergency measure, something to be invoked only after epidemics spiral and negotiations collapse. That framing lets rights-holders price essential medicines as luxury goods, confident that governments will either pay monopoly rents or wait until body counts force a slow, politically costly workaround. A defined threshold replaces that discretion with a predictable rule: cross the line, and generic competition is authorized. Certainty benefits everyone.
[P1.3] Patients gain immediate access; governments avoid looting their budgets; and innovators retain a statutory royalty rather than facing arbitrary expropriation.
[P1.4] Innovation arguments do not defeat this. A patent is not a guarantee to charge any price the traffic will bear; it is a temporary exclusivity meant to reward invention, not to let a monopoly extract infinite surplus from captive buyers. Compulsory licensing at an affordability threshold preserves the reward while capping the abuse. If the price is genuinely justified by genuine R&D or production costs, the patent-holder can make that case for a higher royalty—but the patient should not die while accountants litigate.
[P1.5] Treating price as the trigger is not anti-market; it is market correction. It says that when the good is essential and the price is extortionate, the public’s right to life outranks the firm’s right to maximal rent. That is the line we should draw, and the threshold is the clearest way to draw it.

[C1.1] The motion sounds humane because it points at a real outrage: patients priced out of insulin or cancer drugs. But it answers a complex access failure with a single, automatic legal trigger, and that is precisely the wrong instrument. Price is not a diagnosis. A medicine can be unaffordable because of patents, but also because of tariffs, markups, weak procurement, poor distribution, prescribing habits, or patients paying most costs out of pocket.
[C1.2] An affordability threshold measures the symptom, not the cause. If a country treats crossing that line as sufficient grounds for compulsory licensing, it may spend years in legal and diplomatic conflict and still find no generic manufacturer, no regulatory approval, no supply chain.
[C1.3] Second, the threshold cannot be defined neutrally. Affordability is relative: to a national budget, a household, a donor, a disease burden. Any single number will be either too low, making every modest price a licensing ground, or too high, leaving patients exposed. It will be litigated and gamed, and it turns health ministries into price tribunals.
[C1.4] Third, this rule damages the ecosystem that produces essential medicines. Compulsory licensing is legitimate in emergencies and negotiation failures; making it a standing price-trigger changes the expected return on risky research, especially antibiotics and rare diseases. It also weakens voluntary licensing, where companies often grant cheap generic access in low-income markets precisely to avoid compulsory measures. A blunt trigger makes those deals less attractive.
[C1.5] None of this denies that patents can be abused. But the proposition is not “countries may use compulsory licensing when needed.” It says they should treat any price above a defined affordability threshold as grounds. That is an automatic escalation where negotiation, tiered pricing, pooled procurement, and targeted subsidies should come first. The test is not whether the motion expresses sympathy.
[C1.6] It is whether it delivers medicine. A paper license is not a pill. The motion mistakes a price tripwire for an access policy.
