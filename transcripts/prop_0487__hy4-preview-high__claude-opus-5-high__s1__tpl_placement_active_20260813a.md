[P1.1] Treating planetary defense as a research niche mistakes the problem. We are not short of papers; we are short of time when the alarm sounds. A threatening object may be found with only months of warning, because surveys still miss sizable bodies until they are almost on us. [P1.2] That is the shape of the risk: low frequency, catastrophic consequence, brutally short decision windows.
[P1.3] The right analogy is fire services, not pure science. Cities fund fire crews not because fires are frequent, but because response time is everything. DART proved we can change an asteroid's orbit. That was the experiment; one mission is not a capability.
[P1.4] Research-niche thinking leaves proof with no engine: bespoke spacecraft, launch campaigns, and testing would begin only after detection, while the countdown is already running. Permanent infrastructure—pre-staged launch capacity, upper stages or propellant depots, maintained interceptor craft, tracking and command networks, regularly exercised missions—turns validated physics into an operational response measured in weeks, not budget cycles.
[P1.5] Critics will say the probability is too low. But tail risk is not governed by averages. A 100-meter impact can erase a metro area and cause economic shock; a larger one can dim skies and disrupt harvests worldwide. The expected cost of preparedness may be modest; the avoidable catastrophe from unpreparedness is not.
[P1.6] Permanent capacity also pays dividends before any crisis: stronger space situational awareness, deep-space logistics, satellite servicing, and industrial base. That is resilience, not a niche boondoggle. The motion asks whether governments should deploy deflection infrastructure rather than treat defense mainly as research. They should, because knowledge without readiness is postponed failure.
[P1.7] The clash is not research versus none, but whether we convert validated science into standing capacity before warning arrives, not after. Research finds the rock; only infrastructure moves it.
