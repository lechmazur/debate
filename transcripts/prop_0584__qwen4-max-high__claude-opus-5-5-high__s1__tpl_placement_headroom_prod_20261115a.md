[P1.1] Notice what the motion actually asks: a modest rise in unemployment, over three years, in exchange for keeping inflation near target. It does not ask for a depression, and it does not ask for permanent tightness. The comparison is between a small, temporary, largely cyclical cost and a durable one — the loss of the belief that prices will be stable.
[P1.2] That belief is the whole asset. Once firms and workers conclude that two percent is negotiable, every future supply shock gets baked into wages and prices rather than absorbed. The stop-go pattern of the 1970s is the warning: letting inflation run did not buy lasting employment; it bought both higher inflation and higher unemployment, and the eventual disinflation cost far more jobs than early firmness would have. A modest rise now is the cheap version of a bill that only grows if you defer it.
[P1.3] Second, the tools are not symmetric. Unemployment is something fiscal policy can cushion — income support, retraining, targeted transfers reach the person who lost the job. Inflation has no such remedy; it erodes cash savings, fixed pensions, and every wage that adjusts slowly, and it hits hardest the people with least bargaining power to catch up. Asking monetary policy to tolerate inflation is asking the one institution that can control prices to stand down, while hoping someone else handles jobs.
[P1.4] That is the wrong division of labour.
[P1.5] So the opposition must claim one of two things: either inflation reliably fades on its own — which the post-pandemic surge and its persistence argue against — or that a central bank can quietly accept above-target inflation for years without anyone repricing accordingly. Neither survives contact with how expectations form. Three years is precisely the horizon on which the employment cost fades and the credibility dividend compounds.
