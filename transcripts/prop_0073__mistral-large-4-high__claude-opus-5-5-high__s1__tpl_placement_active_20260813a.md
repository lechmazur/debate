[P1.1] The land beneath our public institutions was not given; it was taken. State capitols, land-grant universities, and public infrastructure stand on territory acquired through broken treaties, coerced sales, and outright seizure. Today, those institutions generate billions in value from that land—through tuition, research grants, property taxes, and natural resource extraction. The theft happened generations ago, but the enrichment is happening now.
[P1.2] When a public institution continues to profit from stolen property, it inherits the obligation to make the owner whole. This is not charity; it is the baseline requirement of unjust enrichment law applied to states.
[P1.3] The proposition does not demand an impossible fantasy of returning every acre to its pre-contact configuration. It offers a practical triad: physical land return where feasible, long-term revenue sharing where the land serves essential public functions, and equivalent restitution where neither is possible. A land-grant university built on seized territory can pay an annual land fee to the descendant community—a fraction of the tuition it charges on that ground. A state can share resource or gaming revenues with the tribe whose watershed or territory it occupies.
[P1.4] A city can transfer surplus public land back to tribal stewardship. These mechanisms recognize that the harm is ongoing, not merely historical.
[P1.5] Opponents will claim this is too costly or too complex to administer. But administrative difficulty is not a defense of theft. A state that cannot afford restitution for stolen land cannot afford to keep the profits from it. The question is not whether justice is convenient; it is whether public institutions may continue to monetize dispossession while calling themselves neutral.
[P1.6] Every year without revenue sharing or restitution is another year the state collects rent on a crime. The only honest settlement is one that ends the free ride.
