[P1.1] Every autumn in cities like Sydney, Toronto, and Amsterdam, the same scene repeats: rental vacancy rates hovering near one percent, queues around the block for a single viewing, and incoming students sleeping in hostels, on couches, in some reported cases in tents. That is what an unconstrained recruitment pipeline delivers into a constrained housing market.
[P1.2] The principle is simple. A public university is chartered and funded to serve its community, and housing is a finite resource it shares with that community. When recruitment grows faster than the stock of homes, the cost does not land on the university's balance sheet; it lands on the lowest-income renter, who gets outbid and displaced. An institution that exists by public license cannot grow by quietly taxing its neighbors' shelter.
[P1.3] Notice how modest this motion is. It asks for a cap only when shortages become severe — a trigger, not a ban. In ordinary years, recruitment continues. It is the opposite side that defends the extreme: no limit, ever, even when students arrive to find literally nowhere to live.
[P1.4] That arrival is the second harm. Dutch universities have had to warn admitted students not to board a plane without a confirmed room. Recruiting someone you cannot house is not generosity; it is a duty-of-care failure dressed up as openness. International students deserve a bed before a sales pitch.
[P1.5] Finally, sequencing. A university controls exactly one lever on the relevant timescale: its intake. Housing supply takes years of zoning, financing, and construction. The rational policy matches intake to the homes that exist, then expands recruitment as the city expands capacity — the university growing as a partner in that build-out, not a rival to its residents.
[P1.6] The choice is not openness versus closure. It is growth that is planned versus growth that is dumped. Cap at the breaking point, recruit to capacity, and international education keeps both its students and its social license. Leave it unconstrained, and it loses both.

[C1.3] Start with causation. Severe housing shortages are built over decades by restrictive zoning, slow permitting, underbuilt public housing, and investor speculation. International students are a marginal slice of total rental demand in these cities. Cap them and vacancy rates barely move — but you have now told voters the problem is "solved," letting the officials who refused to reform zoning off the hook entirely.

[C1.5] Second, follow the money. The motion demands you slam the brakes on the engine that builds the extra housing.
[C1.6] That is self-defeating.
[C1.7] Third, the burden falls on the defenseless and the arbitrary. Why international students and not domestic enrollment growth, not tourism, not short-term rentals, not the general population increase? Because they cannot vote and cannot object. A public institution's duty of care is not honored by turning away the applicant who can be housed with proper planning; it is honored by planning.
[C1.8] So the real choice is this: cap and stagnate, or recruit and build. My opponent will say "just pause until supply catches up." But under a cap, supply never catches up — because you have quietly cut the very budget that would have expanded it.
