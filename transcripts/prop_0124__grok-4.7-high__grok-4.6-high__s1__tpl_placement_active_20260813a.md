[P1.1] A competent adult facing unbearable terminal illness or irreversible degenerative decline should not be legally compelled to endure a death they have clearly rejected. The choice is not between living and dying. For these patients, death is already arriving or function is already collapsing. The real choice is between a medically guided, peaceful end and a prolonged course of pain, suffocation, paralysis, or total loss of control.
[P1.2] The law should permit the former under strict safeguards.
[P1.3] Bodily autonomy does not stop at the hospital door. We already allow competent patients to refuse life-sustaining treatment, ventilation, and nutrition even when that refusal will hasten death. Medical assistance in dying simply extends that same principle to people whose suffering cannot be adequately relieved and whose request is settled, informed, and voluntary. To deny them that option is not neutrality.
[P1.4] It is the state choosing a worse death on their behalf.
[P1.5] Safeguards are the answer to abuse, not a reason for prohibition. Eligibility should require adulthood, decision-making capacity, a terminal or clearly degenerative condition, unbearable suffering, repeated voluntary requests, independent medical assessment, and exclusion of treatable psychiatric illness or external coercion. Waiting periods and documentation make the decision deliberate rather than impulsive. The people most often helped are older patients with cancer or progressive neurological disease who have exhausted meaningful options, not vulnerable people being steered toward death.
[P1.6] A ban does not protect life so much as it outsources dying to worse methods. Patients already stop eating, stockpile drugs, or attempt violent self-harm, often alone and in greater agony. Others who can afford it travel to jurisdictions where assistance is lawful. Bringing the practice inside medicine, with physicians, records, and review, reduces error and secrecy.
[P1.7] Fears of a slippery slope justify tight limits and oversight. They do not justify forcing lucid adults to suffer past the point they can bear. A safeguarded right is the more humane and more honest law.

[C1.1] The law should not authorize physicians to cause death, even at a competent adult's request. Real suffering does not turn a lethal act into care. Once the state licenses that act, it recasts medicine and quietly cheapens lives that look too dependent, too expensive, or too far gone.
[C1.2] Refusing ventilation is not prescribing a fatal dose. One lets disease take its course; the other makes death the treatment. We do not treat a suicidal demand for a lethal overdose as an extension of refusing dialysis. Autonomy stops where the request is for killing.
[C1.3] Neutrality is refusing to make doctors agents of death, not forcing a worse one.
[C1.4] Safeguards cannot carry this weight. Competence, unbearable suffering, and terminal or degenerative disease are judgments made under fear, isolation, depression, and the dread of being a burden. Prognoses are estimates; people outlive them. Treatable despair often wears the face of a settled request.
[C1.5] Waiting periods and dual assessments produce a file, not certainty. An irreversible error cannot be undone by paperwork. The person most in need of protection is the one who can still sign the form.
[C1.6] Legalization is not a narrow mercy. It changes the menu of care. Patients hear that death is now a medical option. Families, hospitals, and strained systems acquire a quieter interest in that option.
[C1.7] Rules that start as strict and terminal-only expand because the logic of unbearable suffering has no natural boundary. Disabled and elderly people then face a new social hint: perhaps you should not stay.
[C1.8] A ban does not outsource dying to violence. Crisis needs interruption and care, not a medical path that ratifies despair. Compassion is pain control and presence—not a statute that tells the vulnerable medicine's last gift can be their death.

[C2.7] Secret stockpiles and lonely attempts are reasons for presence and pain control, not for licensing physicians to cause death. A safeguarded statute does not protect the person whose suffering made the request. It ratifies despair and recasts dependent life as a problem medicine may finish.
