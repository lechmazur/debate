[P1.1] Loneliness is not a notification deficit. It is often an access problem: people lack places to go where they can be around others repeatedly, cheaply, and without having to announce that they are lonely. That is exactly what libraries, recreation centers, and late-hour public spaces provide.
[P1.2] The central comparison is between changing the environment and selling an individual an opt-in tool. An app-based intervention requires a lonely person to recognize the problem, find the app, download it, disclose personal information, learn the interface, and keep engaging. That filters out many of the people who most need help: older adults, shift workers, teenagers without privacy, people without reliable phones or data, and anyone who finds the stigma of a “loneliness app” easier to avoid than a familiar front desk.
[P1.3] Social infrastructure reaches people before they self-identify as lonely. The library homework room, the drop-in basketball hour, the late-night plaza bench, the rec-center coffee morning—these create low-stakes repeated contact, the weak ties and familiar faces from which deeper connection actually grows.
[P1.4] Apps also tend to facilitate one-off matches or scheduled meetups; they struggle to create the unplanned, recurring encounters that make a place feel like a third home. A city can subsidize those encounters cheaply because it already owns the footprint: a branch library, a community hall, a park. Add staffed hours or a small program budget and the marginal reach is broad. Public money should buy defaults, not subscriptions.
[P1.5] App-based tools can be useful adjuncts, but they are self-selecting, churn-prone, and often aimed at digitally comfortable users. The municipal bet should be on the shared physical commons that serves everyone regardless of whether they are ready to say, “I’m lonely,” or simply ready to be somewhere.
