[P1.1] A mandatory charge is part of the price. That single sentence decides this debate. If a fee cannot be avoided, then a menu, ticket listing, or app that omits it is not showing you the price at all—it is showing you a number designed to look like the price while the real one hides behind the checkout button. Requiring disclosure before final checkout simply demands that the advertised price be true.
[P1.2] Three reasons this standard should win.
[P1.3] First, drip pricing exploits people rather than informs them. By the time a diner has ordered, a fan has picked seats, or a hungry customer has built a delivery cart, they have invested time and emotional commitment. Behavioral economics calls this sunk-cost pressure: most people push through the surprise fee rather than restart the search. The late reveal isn't an accident of interface design—it works precisely because it converts hesitation into resignation.
[P1.4] A practice that only succeeds when customers feel trapped is not one CON should be comfortable defending.
[P1.5] Second, hidden fees corrupt competition itself. Markets discipline prices only when consumers can compare them. When one ticketing platform advertises $50 and charges $68, while an honest rival advertises $65 all-in, the honest firm loses the click despite being cheaper. Late-reveal fees don't just harm the individual buyer; they punish transparent businesses and reward whoever hides the most.
[P1.6] Early disclosure levels the field so firms compete on real value.
[P1.7] Third, the ask is trivially cheap. These charges are mandatory and known in advance—a fixed percentage or flat amount. Printing "18% service charge applies" on the menu, or folding fees into the displayed ticket price, requires no new technology. Airlines already show all-in fares under existing rules, and the sky did not fall.
[P1.8] So the clash is simple: I defend the principle that customers deserve the real price before they commit. CON must defend the strategic ambush. Hold them to that.

[C1.1] The proposition treats every mandatory charge like a fixed sticker price. In these industries, many mandatory charges are transaction-specific. A delivery fee depends on address, order size, and sometimes demand; a ticket fee may depend on seat section, delivery method, or a venue levy; a restaurant service charge may apply only to large parties, private rooms, or dine-in but not takeout. Before the customer has made those choices, the only honest disclosures are estimates, ranges, or worst-case totals.
[C1.2] The motion therefore forces businesses to choose between misleading precision and intimidating all-in prices.
[C1.3] The final checkout screen is not a trap; it is the first moment when the exact transaction is known. It lets the buyer see the base price, taxes, service charge, delivery fee, and total before authorizing payment. That is more transparent than folding every possible charge into an early number, where the components become invisible and comparisons become harder. If the consumer can see the total and walk away before paying, PRO’s “ambush” disappears.
[C1.4] This also flips PRO’s competition argument. A universal pre-checkout mandate favors large platforms that can build dynamic calculators, update thousands of listings, and absorb compliance risk. A neighborhood restaurant or local venue cannot. If it must disclose every mandatory charge before the customer has selected the relevant options, it risks under-disclosing or scaring customers with maximum prices.
[C1.5] The rational response is fewer menu variations, fewer delivery zones, or padded prices. That makes markets less dynamic, not more honest.
[C1.6] A mandatory charge is part of the total, but not always part of a knowable advertised price before the transaction conditions exist. Requiring the exact total before payment is reasonable; requiring all charges before final checkout is premature, error-prone, and anti-small-business. Consumers still get the protection that matters: the total is visible before consent. The proposition should fail.
