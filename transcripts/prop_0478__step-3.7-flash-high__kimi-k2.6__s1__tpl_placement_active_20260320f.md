[P1.1] Gig workers are the backbone of on-demand economies: they deliver food, drive riders, complete freelance tasks, and generate billions in annual revenue for platforms that classify them as independent contractors to dodge the cost of basic employee benefits. For workers who rely on gig and short-term contract work as their primary substantial income, the current system is a raw deal: they have no paid sick leave, no workers’ compensation if injured on the job, no employer contribution to health insurance or retirement, and no safety net if a platform cuts their hours overnight.
[P1.2] A government-mandated portable benefits floor fixes this gap without upending the flexibility that makes gig work valuable. The policy would require all platforms hosting workers earning above a substantial full-time income threshold to contribute a small, predictable percentage of each worker’s earnings to a universal, worker-controlled benefits account. Funds can be used for health coverage, retirement savings, paid time off, or injury claims, and the account travels with the worker across every platform they use, eliminating the risk of losing coverage when switching between gigs or taking a short break between contracts.
[P1.3] Opponents will claim this policy will kill gig jobs by raising costs for platforms, but that argument ignores reality. Platforms already pass far larger operational costs to consumers regularly, and a modest benefits contribution is a small price to pay to ensure workers generating platform revenue are not one injury away from financial ruin. The “substantial income” threshold also ensures we do not burden casual, part-time gig workers doing occasional side work, targeting the policy only at people who rely on this labor to make ends meet.

[P2.1] CON’s core argument collapses under a basic category error: it equates a small, earmarked earnings contribution with the full set of controls that define employment, but portable benefits explicitly preserve independent contractor status. Platforms retain no authority to set worker schedules, require minimum log-in hours, or control how workers complete tasks under this policy—only to remit a fixed share of each worker’s platform-based earnings to a worker-controlled account. This is nothing like the de facto employee reclassification CON warns of, leaving gig work flexibility fully intact.
[P2.2] CON’s claim that the cost will be passed to workers as a hidden pay cut fails on competitive dynamics. Platforms already extract roughly a quarter of every gig as their own operational take, and a 2 to 3 percent benefits contribution is a tiny fraction of that cut. If a platform slashed per-task rates to offset the cost, workers would simply shift to competing platforms offering better net terms, since the universal floor applies evenly to all platforms in a jurisdiction, eliminating any incentive to exit small markets as CON predicts, and preventing a race to the bottom on pay.
[P2.3] CON’s demand sensitivity argument also overstates risk: core gig services like food delivery and commuter rides have relatively inelastic demand for regular users, so small, spread-out cost increases do not trigger the mass exodus needed to make CON’s pay cut scenario real.
[P2.4] CON’s cliff and compliance fears are similarly overstated. The “substantial income” threshold is set at a full-time equivalent to exclude casual side hustlers, and platforms only remit contributions based on their own platform’s earnings data for each worker—no cross-platform tracking is required, as workers self-report total annual earnings once per year to confirm eligibility, eliminating the administrative burden CON claims would crush small platforms and entrench monopolies. CON’s preferred universal tax-and-transfer alternative is far less targeted, imposing broad costs on all taxpayers to cover even occasional side gigs, while this policy directs support exclusively to workers who rely on gig labor for their primary income.
