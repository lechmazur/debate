[P1.1] The three predictions named in this motion are not ordinary business analytics. They are attempts to know an employee's future intentions and loyalties before the employee acts on them—and that is exactly why they must be banned.
[P1.2] Start with the clearest case: predicting unionization likelihood. Deciding whether to organize is a protected right precisely because it must be free from employer surveillance and reprisal. An AI that flags which workers are "likely to unionize" is a machine built to do what the law already forbids: identify and neutralize organizing before it happens. You cannot chill a right more effectively than by predicting it and quietly reassigning, passing over, or dismissing the people the model fingers.
[P1.3] A ban here isn't heavy-handed; it's the only way to keep an existing right from being hollowed out by prediction laundered as data science.
[P1.4] The productivity and quitting predictions fail for a related reason: they punish people for futures that haven't happened. A worker flagged as a "likely quitter" gets frozen out of promotions and training—which, of course, makes them quit. A worker scored as "low future productivity" is managed toward the exit before they've underperformed at all. These are self-fulfilling prophecies dressed as foresight, and the person scored almost never sees the score, cannot contest the inputs, and cannot appeal a verdict about something they never did.
[P1.5] And the inputs are exactly the traits we should worry about: age, health signals, caregiving gaps, disability, pregnancy—all statistically correlated with turnover and output, all legally protected. A prediction engine doesn't discriminate on purpose; it discriminates on correlation, which is worse, because it comes with a veneer of objectivity that makes bias hard to challenge.
[P1.6] So the standard I'll defend is simple. When a tool's core function is to pre-judge protected activity and personal futures, with no meaningful transparency or recourse, and with discriminatory correlations baked in, the correct response is prohibition, not disclosure. My opponent must show these harms can be tamed. I say the function itself is the harm.
