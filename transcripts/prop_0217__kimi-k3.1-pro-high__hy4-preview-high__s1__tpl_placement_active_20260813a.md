Blunt universal restrictions fail that test. [C1.1] PRO wrongly treats a water shortage as a pricing problem rather than a quantity problem. A city in drought does not need heavy users to pay more; it needs the water to stay in the reservoir. Sharply rising prices alter the cost of waste, but for the discretionary uses that drive scarcity—irrigating lawns, filling pools, washing cars—demand is often inelastic and backed by ability to pay. PRO concedes this in [P1.5]: a user determined to maintain extravagant use “may pay more.” Paying is not conserving.
[C1.2] If the same volume leaves the pipes, the shortage deepens no matter how large the bill.
[C1.3] Universal restrictions work because they are blunt. A ban on outdoor irrigation, pool filling, and nonessential washing does not ask whether a resident is willing to pay; it removes the use now. That yields immediate, observable, systemwide savings. It also avoids the administrative lag of metering, tiered billing, and case-by-case hardship adjustments.
[C1.4] Response must be certain before the next billing cycle. Waiting to see if a wealthy household answers a price signal gambles with a resource that cannot be invoiced back.
[C1.5] PRO calls heavy-user pricing fairer, but shared sacrifice is the fairer principle in a commons emergency. Universal restrictions ask everyone to stop the same nonessential uses; there is no price at which affluence buys exemption. That clarity sustains compliance. Tiered pricing turns a civic duty into a market: if the rich keep green lawns by paying, resentment spreads and enforcement gets harder.
[C1.6] A lifeline plus assistance sounds humane, yet it relies on paperwork and appeals that vulnerable households cannot quickly navigate.
[C1.7] Use price, if at all, to reinforce the rules. The metric is water actually saved, not revenue raised or marginal cost imposed. Because universal restrictions cut discretionary demand now and heavy-user pricing mainly shifts cost, regions should rely mainly on blunt restrictions.
