[P1.1] The status quo is not "no drug use." It is drug use in stairwells, public toilets, and parks, where the person who overdoses is found by a child or a street cleaner an hour too late. Every city already has consumption sites; the only question is whether anyone with naloxone is standing nearby.
[P1.2] Opioid overdose is uniquely reversible. Breathing slows, and a trained person with naloxone and oxygen can bring someone back in minutes. That is why supervised sites work: across roughly three decades and well over a hundred sites in Switzerland, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain, Denmark, Norway, and France, the widely reported record is that nobody has died of an overdose inside one. Not because the drugs are safer, but because someone is watching.
[P1.3] Every death prevented is a person who remains alive to enter treatment, which these sites also broker, alongside wound care, hepatitis and HIV testing, and housing referrals. They are the front door to services for people who trust nothing else.
[P1.4] Public disorder points the same direction. Zurich's Platzspitz in the early 1990s was an open-air drug scene with hundreds of people injecting in view of the train station. Police sweeps simply moved it to the next park. What ended it was a package of consumption rooms, heroin-assisted treatment, and housing.
[P1.5] Frankfurt saw the same pattern: public injecting and discarded syringes fell once people had somewhere indoors to go. Vancouver's Insite, the best-studied site in the Americas, showed lower overdose mortality in the surrounding blocks and no rise in crime.
[P1.6] Latin America now faces this urgency directly. Fentanyl is arriving in Tijuana and other northern Mexican cities; São Paulo's Cracolândia has survived every eviction for decades. Repeating the sweep-and-displace cycle guarantees the same failure.
[P1.7] The motion asks only that cities be permitted to try a regulated, zoned, staffed option with a proven record. Opponents must show that unsupervised use in public is somehow safer and more orderly. It is not, and no city's experience says otherwise.

[C1.2] Neighbors understand this instantly, which is why proposed sites reliably trigger the same fight: users bussed in, residents locked out of parks, shopkeepers left to absorb theft and needles.
[C1.3] The celebrated zero-deaths-inside record is an accounting trick, not a public-health victory. Of course nobody dies under direct oxygen and naloxone watch. The same person can overdose the next night in an alley with a stronger dose, because the site taught nothing about stopping and changed nothing about supply. Sites supervise a tiny fraction of a city's injections while normalizing continued use.
[C1.4] Keeping an addict alive in the room while losing him outside is not success; it is managed decline dressed as rescue.
[C1.5] Zurich and Frankfurt are misremembered. Platzspitz did not end because the city provided couches for injecting; it ended when tolerance was paired with relentless police closure of the open scene, dispersal, housing with rules, and treatment with expectations. Strip out enforcement and conditional help, as many advocates want, and you keep the magnet and lose the exit.
[C1.6] That distinction is fatal for the cities in this motion. In Tijuana or São Paulo, where police are outgunned, courts are clogged, and trafficking groups prey on open addict populations, a zoned drug house cannot stay zoned. It becomes a protected retail zone, extortion point, and recruitment ground. In strained European cities, it becomes a permanent budget line that crowds out detox beds, abstinence programs, and neighborhood policing.
[C1.7] Cities reduce deaths and disorder by shrinking addiction, not furnishing it.
