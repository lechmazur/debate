[P2.1] The claim that commercial redundancy and satellite backups already solve the problem ignores how wartime attacks differ from random faults. Coordinated cuts at a few chokepoints overwhelm existing reroutes, saturate backup capacity, and force traffic onto higher-latency satellite links that cannot sustain the data volumes modern targeting and logistics require. Militaries have trained for degraded environments precisely because they recognize this risk, yet training cannot create bandwidth that does not exist.
[P2.2] That same degradation turns the opponent's visible platforms into liabilities rather than assets. A carrier group without continuous, high-capacity links loses coordinated air operations, updated threat pictures, and resupply timing. Its organic sensors still function locally, but the platform can no longer act as part of a larger force. Adding another hull or aircraft squadron does not restore that lost coordination; protecting the cables that carry it does.
[P2.3] Deterrence comparisons also favor the investment. An adversary weighing whether to strike will compare the cost of covert cable operations against the cost of confronting observable forces. Raising the former cost directly lowers the chance that conflict begins in the first place. Leaving the cables exposed while buying more visible systems simply presents the attacker with a cheaper opening move.
[P2.4] The marginal dollar therefore delivers greater security when it prevents isolation of the entire defense apparatus than when it adds another platform that cannot operate once isolation occurs.

[C2.7] PRO's case requires the cable catastrophe to be both easy to execute and impossible to mitigate. It is neither, and paying for that fantasy by hollowing out visible forces makes every contingency—including cable attacks—harder to answer.
