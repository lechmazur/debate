[P1.1] Privacy-by-design for minors is one of the narrowest interventions a government can require. It does not ask platforms to remove a post, ban a user, or moderate an opinion. It asks them to flip a switch: when a child signs up, the account opens private, not public; data collection defaults off, not on; targeted advertising defaults off, not on. The child can change any of these settings.
[P1.2] The regulation only changes the starting point.
[P1.3] That starting point matters enormously. Decades of behavioral research show that the overwhelming majority of users never change defaults. Most teenagers, like most adults, accept what they are given. A default private account is not symbolic; it is the difference between a 13-year-old's photos, location, and search history being broadcast publicly versus shared only with people they have actively chosen.
[P1.4] That is a measurable difference in exposure, not a speech restriction in any ordinary sense.
[P1.5] The distinction between privacy and censorship is the heart of this debate. Censorship is the suppression of expression. Privacy-by-design does not suppress what a minor can say, write, post, or search. A child can still publish a manifesto, a song, a coming-out post, a political opinion; they simply do so to a chosen audience, not the entire data-brokerage industry.
[P1.6] The content is untouched; the platform still hosts it; the user still authors it. Only the default audience and data flow change.
[P1.7] This principle is already mainstream law. The United States has restricted data collection from children under 13 since 2000, with no credible charge of resulting censorship. The European Union has required parental consent for under-16s' data processing for years. The empirical record from these regimes shows the predicted effect: less harvesting, no measurable reduction in minors' expressive activity.
[P1.8] So the central question for this debate is simple: can our opponents name a coherent account on which "the account starts private" is the suppression of speech? If they cannot, the motion stands: measurable harm reduction, at no meaningful cost to expression.

Research on “default effects” shows that opt‑in rates are dramatically lower than opt‑out rates, especially for adolescents who lack the digital literacy to navigate privacy settings. The net result is not less harm; it is a different, harder‑to‑measure harm—greater invisibility for the very youths who need exposure the most.
[C1.3] Second, the assertion that this policy does not significantly increase censorship is false. Privacy defaults are not neutral switches; they are gatekeepers. When a platform’s default is private, any content that relies on public reach—political speech, artistic expression, peer‑to‑peer counseling—is structurally suppressed. Platforms, fearing liability, will further prune content that could attract scrutiny, turning a privacy setting into a de‑facto content filter.
[C1.4] The EU’s experience with consent‑based models shows a measurable dip in minors’ public posting, especially among marginalized groups, contradicting the “no censorship” claim.
[C1.5] Thus the motion’s promised trade‑off collapses: harm is not measurably reduced, and censorship is materially increased. The policy substitutes one set of risks for another while granting governments a powerful lever to shape what minors can say. That is not a narrow, harmless intervention; it is a structural restriction that fails the test the proposition sets.
