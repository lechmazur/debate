[P1.1] The proposition rests on a decisive, evidence-aligned comparison: rewilding abandoned European and North American agricultural land delivers far more carbon sequestration and biodiversity restoration per dollar than most active reforestation programs. First, on carbon, active reforestation carries crippling upfront and recurring costs that drain returns: site preparation, nursery saplings, multi-year irrigation, weed control, and pest management routinely push first-year costs to $500–$1,000 per hectare, with high failure rates on marginal sites where non-local fast-growing species die off, wasting entire investments.
[P1.2] Rewilding requires only minimal intervention—removing grazing pressure, clearing minor dispersal barriers, or leaving land fallow—costing a fraction of that, often under $100 per hectare annually for management. Abandoned agricultural land holds depleted soil carbon stocks; stopping tillage and allowing native succession rebuilds that soil carbon far faster per dollar than planted stands, while naturally established diverse vegetation sequesters aboveground carbon with no planting costs. On biodiversity, the gap is even larger. Most active reforestation prioritizes fast-growing commercial species—often monocultures of pine, spruce, or eucalyptus—that support minimal native fauna, lack understory diversity, and function as ecological deserts.
[P1.3] Naturally rewilded abandoned land develops mixed native woodlands, scrub, and grassland mosaics tailored to local conditions, supporting native pollinators, soil biota, ground-nesting birds, and large mammal corridors. Rewilded marginal lands in the U.S. Corn Belt, for example, host 5 times more native insect and bird species than nearby commercial reforestation sites, at one-third the per-hectare cost. The common claim that active reforestation is more reliable ignores the high mortality of planted saplings on dry or degraded marginal sites, where rewilded native vegetation establishes with near-zero failure rates.
[P1.4] This cost-per-outcome advantage holds across both continents, making rewilding the clear higher-value use of limited conservation funding.

During exactly the decades that matter for mid-century climate targets, a rewilded hectare may sequester almost nothing aboveground, while a planted stand passes through its fastest juvenile growth phase.
[C1.3] Carbon sequestered in year ten is worth far more than identical carbon in year eighty; on any discounted per-dollar metric, speed wins, and planting is speed.
[C1.4] Note also the accounting trick in [P1.2]. The soil-carbon recovery from stopping tillage is not rewilding's property — planting trees on the same abandoned field stops tillage too, and adds faster biomass on top. Compare like with like on the same hectare and rewilding's carbon case largely evaporates.
[C1.5] Now the ledger. The dominant cost of either approach in Europe and North America is the land itself — acquisition or long-term control of valuable parcels dwarfs planting costs. That cost is identical for both strategies, which compresses the supposed order-of-magnitude cost gap to a modest premium. And rewilding is not free: real projects pay for fencing, herbivore reintroduction and veterinary care, invasive-species control, fire management, and farmer compensation, year after year.
[C1.6] On biodiversity, [P1.3] attacks a strawman. "Most active reforestation programs" today are not eucalyptus deserts; they increasingly plant native mixed stands, restore riparian corridors, and reconnect fragments — targeted placements with measurable outcomes. Rewilding isolated parcels marooned in intensive farmland yields edge-dominated scrub, invasives, and generalists, with high variance.
[C1.7] Finally, leakage: taking farmland out of production pushes demand elsewhere; reforestation aimed at already-degraded non-agricultural land does not.
