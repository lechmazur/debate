[P1.1] Esports has become a global industry generating billions in annual revenue, yet its workers—frequently teenagers—labor under conditions that would be illegal in virtually any other professional field. We are not seeking special privileges; we are demanding that the baseline protections of labor law finally apply to an industry that has long outgrown its unregulated adolescence.
[P1.2] First, underage exploitation is systemic rather than incidental. Sixteen-year-olds routinely sign multi-year contracts binding them to punitive buyouts they cannot afford, often without independent legal counsel or parental oversight that genuinely protects the child. When a fifteen-year-old spends fourteen hours daily grinding mechanics in a cramped team house, society recognizes that as child labor in any other context. Relabeling it "competitive practice" does not erase the reality of sleep deprivation, social isolation, and developmental harm.
[P1.3] Second, abusive contracts create modern economic captivity. Standard agreements feature unilateral termination rights held exclusively by organizations, non-compete clauses that bar released players from rival teams, and penalty structures that price young talent out of their own careers. These are not rare anomalies but industry templates exploiting information asymmetries, language barriers, and the desperation of aspirants willing to sign anything to reach the main stage.
[P1.4] Third, extreme schedules destroy the human capital the industry depends upon. Back-to-back international tournaments, months-long bootcamps without rest days, and scrimmages running past 4 AM produce repetitive strain injuries, chronic burnout, and psychological crises. Unlike traditional athletes protected by unions, medical staffs, and regulated off-seasons, esports players often train through injury because any pause risks immediate replacement by the next hungry teenager.
[P1.5] The industry's self-regulation has demonstrably failed; voluntary codes collapse the moment profit pressures mount. Mandatory contract review for minors, legally enforceable limits on practice hours, guaranteed medical leave, and genuine collective bargaining rights are not anti-competitive luxuries. They are the minimum standards required to keep the talent pool physically and mentally viable, and to preserve the legitimacy of a sport that increasingly defines itself by the wellbeing of its players.
