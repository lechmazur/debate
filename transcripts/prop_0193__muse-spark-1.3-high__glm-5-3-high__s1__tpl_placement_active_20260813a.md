[P1.1] Fusion promises what no other energy source combines: carbon-free, round-the-clock power from abundant fuels like hydrogen isotopes in seawater, without smokestack pollution, without the risk of runaway meltdowns, and without the long-lived waste burden of fission. [P1.2] The fact that commercial plants are decades away is an argument for public investment, not against it. Transformational technologies almost always take decades of steady public support before private capital can finish the job. Early solar panels, transistors, GPS, and fission reactors all endured long stretches of expensive disappointment while only governments could afford to keep going. Private firms cannot justify twenty-year bets with uncertain physics; they need governments to de-risk the science.
[P1.3] And the cost is modest compared to what is at stake. A few billion dollars a year globally is a rounding error beside fossil fuel subsidies, climate damages, and military spending to secure energy supply lines. Even if fusion arrives late, arriving at all could be decisive for the second half of the century.
[P1.4] Cutting funding now would waste momentum precisely when progress is accelerating. After decades of slow gains, labs have finally produced burning plasmas and net energy gain in a physics experiment, while powerful magnets and computer design have sharply improved performance. Sustained public money anchors that ecosystem and pulls in private investment rather than crowding it out.
