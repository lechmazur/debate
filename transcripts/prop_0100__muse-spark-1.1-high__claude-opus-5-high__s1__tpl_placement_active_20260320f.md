[P1.1] Decarbonization no longer has to wait for nuclear because renewables have won on the two metrics that define a central strategy: cost and speed.
[P1.2] Since 2010 solar costs have collapsed by roughly an order of magnitude and onshore wind by more than two-thirds, while new nuclear costs have risen and routinely double initial budgets. Battery costs have followed the same learning curve, making the classic intermittency objection solvable at prices still below new nuclear. A strategy must be affordable and replicable; renewables are, nuclear is not.
[P1.3] Second, time matters. Climate deadlines are set for 2030 and 2035, not 2050 alone. A utility-scale solar or wind farm goes from permit to electrons in one to two years, with modular scaling that learns as it goes. A nuclear plant averages a decade in the West, longer with litigation and financing, before a single ton of carbon is avoided.
[P1.4] In the race that actually counts, nuclear is too slow to be central.
[P1.5] Third, the old baseload story has been retired by grid operators themselves. Modern reliability comes from diversity, interconnection, demand response, and storage, not one inflexible megaplant. Countries from Denmark to Portugal to Kenya already run annual electricity mixes dominated by renewables without nuclear, and they are cutting emissions faster than nuclear-heavy peers.
[P1.6] Finally, most countries simply cannot make nuclear central. Only about thirty nations operate reactors; the rest lack the industrial base, regulatory capacity, capital depth, and public acceptance. They do have sun and wind.
[P1.7] Nuclear may remain a niche choice for a few large, rich grids. For most of the world, renewables are now sufficient, faster, and cheaper. That is why nuclear is no longer necessary at the center.
