[P1.1] Methane is the fast lever on the climate machine. It traps far more heat than CO2 molecule-for-molecule—on the order of 80 times more over a twenty-year horizon—but it breaks down in roughly a decade instead of lingering for centuries. That single fact should decide this debate: if we want to slow near-term warming, the fastest way to do it is to stop methane from leaking in the first place, not to spend the same dollar chasing carbon that has already been emitted.
[P1.2] Leak detection and repair, LDAR, is cheap and mechanically simple. Oil and gas operations leak methane through corroded valves, faulty seals, and unlit flares—problems you find with infrared cameras or continuous sensors and fix with a wrench and a work order. Much of this leaked gas is product the company was going to sell anyway, so tightening LDAR rules often pays for itself through recovered gas, inspection efficiency, and avoided fines. The technology exists today, deploys in months, and scales across thousands of existing wells without inventing anything new.
[P1.3] Carbon capture and storage is the opposite profile. It requires capturing dilute CO2 streams, compressing them, building dedicated pipelines, and injecting the gas into geological formations that must hold for centuries. Each project takes years of permitting and construction before it captures a single ton, and even now, decades into CCS development, the technology captures only a sliver of what its promoters projected, at a cost per ton that dwarfs the cost of fixing a leaking valve.
[P1.4] So the comparison is stark: one dollar spent on LDAR removes a super-potent warming gas within months, using proven low-tech methods, often while paying for itself. One dollar spent on CCS buys a slow-moving, capital-intensive bet on infrastructure that mostly isn't built yet and targets a weaker, longer-lived gas anyway. If the question is which spending delivers larger near-term climate benefit, methane rules win by construction—faster action, on the more potent gas, for less money.
