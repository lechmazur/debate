[P1.1] The core of this debate is simple: when a buyer pays for a car, the physical equipment inside it should belong to them, not be held hostage by a monthly billing cycle.
[P1.2] The proposition is narrow. It does not demand free cloud services, streaming, map updates, or other genuinely ongoing external services. Otherwise automakers can sell the same car twice: once as a product, and again as a series of permissions.
[P1.3] That is bad for consumers in three ways. First, it violates ordinary ownership expectations. A purchaser can reasonably expect to control functions built into the machine they own. Turning installed hardware into a rental converts a durable good into a fragile license.
[P1.4] Second, it creates safety and reliability problems. Imagine a driver who loses access to heated mirrors in winter, suspension modes suited for rough roads, or towing capacity because a payment failed or a service plan expired. Even if the feature is not always life-critical, making vehicle capability depend on billing status introduces needless instability.
[P1.5] Third, it harms competition and resale. If the manufacturer controls hardware through software gates, it can foreclose independent repair, disable useful features after purchase, and reduce a used car’s value simply because subscriptions lapsed. The manufacturer can also extract rent from every subsequent owner.
[P1.6] The CON side may say subscriptions fund innovation. But there is a clean line: charge upfront for hardware, or lease genuinely ongoing services. The proposition does not ban subscriptions; it stops companies from disabling equipment already in the car. That preserves honest pricing, real ownership, and durable consumer rights.
