[P1.1] Your movements, messages, health questions, and late-night scrolling compiled into a dossier sold to strangers you never met. If a private investigator followed you that way, the law would intervene. When software does it at population scale for ad targeting, courts shrug and call it business. That double standard is indefensible.
[P1.2] Scale transforms a private transaction into extraction. One cookie remembering a login is not the issue. The issue is persistent tracking across apps, sites, and devices, pooled by brokers and broadcast in ad auctions to hundreds of potential bidders, building inferences about pregnancy, debt, sexuality, or political vulnerability. The individual cannot see it, audit it, or undo it once leaked.
[P1.3] So-called consent today is fiction. Buried policies, pre-ticked boxes, dark patterns, and take-it-or-leave-it screens do not signal agreement; they manufacture it. Behavioral evidence is clear: defaults decide outcomes, and companies that profit from surveillance design the defaults. An opt-out regime therefore guarantees harvesting regardless of what people actually want.
[P1.4] Making harvesting presumptively unlawful without explicit opt-in simply puts the burden where it belongs: on the party profiting. This is not a ban on advertising. Contextual ads thrive without dossiers, and any firm offering genuine value in exchange for data can still ask — clearly, separately, and without penalty for saying no. If the deal is fair, people will say yes.
[P1.5] The industry warning about the end of free content is self-serving: the web ran on contextual ads for years, small publishers suffer most under surveillance monopolies, and nothing stops a site from offering a paid or ad-light choice honestly.
[P1.6] The harms of the opposite default are concrete: price and job discrimination, manipulation of anxious teenagers and indebted gamblers, massive breach surfaces, and political microtargeting that corrodes shared truth. Freedom requires the right to be un-tracked by default, with tracking only by authentic choice.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a checkbox for protection and a sledgehammer for tailoring. Large-scale ad tracking can be creepy, and some of it should be illegal. But making it presumptively unlawful unless every user explicitly opts in does not follow. It treats the collection of data as the wrong, while most serious harms—discrimination, manipulation, breach, and black-box inferences—come from how data is used, combined, secured, and acted upon.
[C1.2] A clear rule can target those directly: ban sensitive inferences, prohibit discriminatory ad delivery, require data minimization and retention limits, demand meaningful transparency and deletion rights, and punish dark patterns. Those duties apply whether or not someone clicked “accept.”
[C1.3] Explicit opt-in is not the autonomy cure the other side assumes. People face endless consent walls; they click through because refusing is costly and understanding is near impossible. An opt-in regime simply invites more elaborate consent theater, with the same tracking hidden behind a button. Worse, consent can legitimize abuse: a person can be nudged into agreeing to manipulative targeting without grasping the consequences.
[C1.4] If the problem is power and information asymmetry, consent alone cannot solve it.
[C1.5] The presumption also concentrates market power. Requiring opt-in for third-party tracking pushes advertisers toward the few platforms with logged-in, first-party relationships—the giants. Small publishers, independent sites, and new entrants lose the ad revenue that funds open content, while incumbents can bundle consent into accounts. That is not privacy; it is entrenchment.
[C1.6] And it is overbroad. Targeted advertising is not one thing. A retailer showing a customer an ad for the category they browsed is not the same as a broker inferring a health condition and selling it to anyone. Law should distinguish risk, purpose, and sensitivity.
[C1.7] The proposition cannot. A presumption tied to one legal basis replaces accountable use with paperwork—and paperwork is exactly what surveillance advertisers are good at.
