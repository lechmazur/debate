[P1.1] A fair tax system must measure what it claims to tax. For ordinary households: wages are withheld; purchases are taxed. For very wealthy households, the base often vanishes. If a founder's shares rise by hundreds of millions, she has vast new command over resources, yet no salary need be paid, no sale need occur, and no consumption need spike.
[P1.2] Borrowing against the portfolio can finance a lavish life without taxable income. Income and consumption taxes chase wealth's footprints while leaving its accumulated stock nearly untouched.
[P1.3] The result is structural, not incidental. Large fortunes compound through ownership, control, and asset appreciation; those gains depend on courts, contracts, infrastructure, educated workers, and stable money. The annual cost of maintaining that system falls heavily on wages and everyday spending, while the greatest capacity to pay is often reached only when the owner chooses a realisation event. A high-exemption net worth tax fixes the clock: each year, measure assets minus debts above a very large threshold and take a modest contribution from the stock itself.
[P1.4] That is a better tax base, not merely revenue. Annual assessment reduces the payoff to indefinite deferral, narrows the advantage of borrowing against unrealised gains, and keeps public finance independent of the rich deciding to sell, die, or spend. It also shifts part of the burden away from work and consumption, which discourages ordinary activity, toward concentrated holdings that can absorb the charge without reducing labour or basic consumption.
[P1.5] Valuation and avoidance objections are design problems, not reasons to exempt the base. Wealth is already valued for collateral, insurance, estate planning, divorce, and regulated funds; illiquid assets can be assessed, averaged, or paid over time; a high threshold keeps the tax on households with professional advisers. The choice: taxing the stream while sparing the reservoir is not neutrality. A large net worth tax asks those who hold the reservoir to maintain the society that makes it secure.

[C1.1] A net worth tax sounds fair in theory, but its real-world flaws make it a far worse alternative to targeted reforms of existing income and consumption taxes than PRO admits. First, the valuation problem is not a minor “design” quirk—it is a fundamental, unworkable barrier. Wealth held by high-net-worth households is overwhelmingly tied up in illiquid, hard-to-value assets: private business stakes, real estate in volatile markets, art, and private equity holdings. Unlike publicly traded stock with a clear daily market price, these assets require subjective, often contested appraisals that swing wildly based on appraiser choice or temporary market conditions.
[C1.2] PRO claims valuations are already done for collateral, but collateral assessments are one-off, negotiated for a specific loan—not annual, binding, penalty-eligible valuations that determine tax liability for every holding. The administrative cost of these annual assessments, for both households and the government, would consume a large share of revenue, creating massive deadweight loss.
[C1.3] Second, this tax creates dangerous perverse incentives. Because the base is net worth—assets minus debts—households have a powerful incentive to take on excessive, unproductive debt to lower their taxable value, increasing systemic financial risk that echoes the over-leverage fueling the 2008 crisis. Worse, capital is mobile: jurisdictions that have implemented net worth taxes have seen sharp outflows of wealthy residents and investment, as founders and executives move assets and operations to lower-tax regions, taking jobs and future tax revenue with them.
[C1.4] PRO’s “fix the clock” framing ignores that you cannot tax a reservoir that dries up and flees the jurisdiction entirely. The modest fairness gain of taxing unrealised gains is far outweighed by the economic harm and administrative impossibility of this policy.
