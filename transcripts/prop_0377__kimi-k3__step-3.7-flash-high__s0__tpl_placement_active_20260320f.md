[C1.1] PRO's framing rests on a false split between "earned" high income and "unearned" inheritance, but the distinction is far messier than their opening suggested. Most large intergenerational wealth transfers are not idle handouts to useless heirs: they are the fruit of prior generations' risk, labor, and long-term sacrifice. A small business owner who builds a local manufacturing firm over 40 years, employing 50 local workers, does not create a pernicious "dynastic caste" by leaving that firm to a child who has spent 15 years working in the business, learning its operations.
[C1.2] That wealth is earned across generations, not a random windfall.
[C1.3] PRO also errs in claiming high annual income poses no equal threat to equality of opportunity. A top 1% earner can fund private schooling, unpaid internships, down payment assistance, and entrepreneurial seed capital for their children just as effectively as an heir can. The starting line gap is driven by household wealth, regardless of how that wealth was originally accumulated. Singling out inheritance ignores that high current earnings also fund intergenerational advantage, making the proposed tax a poorly targeted Band-Aid that misses the bulk of the problem.
[C1.4] PRO's claim that aggressive inheritance taxes are "non-distortionary" ignores severe harm to capital formation and small business continuity. When owners know most of their life's work will be seized at death, they have no incentive to reinvest in their companies, hire more workers, or build long-term value. That shrinks the economic pie, reducing opportunity for low and middle-income workers far more than any theoretical gain from cancelling inherited advantage.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO's brushoff of threshold concerns is naive. Arbitrary "large" inheritance thresholds will inevitably creep to capture more middle-class families over time via inflation, punishing families who saved for decades to leave a home or small nest egg to their children.
