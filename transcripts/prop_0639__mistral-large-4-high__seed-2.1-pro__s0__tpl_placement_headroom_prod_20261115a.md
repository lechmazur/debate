[P1.1] Advisory power is a polite fiction. When a sortition assembly spends months deliberating and produces a recommendation, elected politicians can simply file it, water it down, or ignore it entirely when it clashes with party interests or donor pressure. The entire point of replacing electoral politics with random selection is to break that capture. If the outcome remains merely consultative, the assembly becomes an expensive focus group whose findings compete for attention with lobbyists and opinion polls, and the democratic renewal we were promised evaporates.
[P1.2] Binding power changes the game. When citizens know their decision actually settles the question, they deliberate with the gravity of legislators rather than the detachment of commentators. The mandate is narrow and specific—a single policy question, not open-ended governance—so the assembly does not usurp the general legislative role of parliament.
[P1.3] Instead, it resolves the precise knot where representative democracy is most gridlocked: issues where elected officials face conflicts of interest, such as electoral reform, climate policy, or constitutional change. A randomly selected microcosm of the public has no party whip to appease and no campaign to fund; its only incentive is to reach a considered judgment that the whole population can see was made by their peers.
[P1.4] Consider the alternative: in advisory models, implementation depends entirely on the goodwill of the very politicians the assembly was meant to check. That is not accountability; it is hope. Binding authority ensures that the considered judgment of ordinary citizens translates directly into law, giving sortition the teeth it needs to be more than symbolic. The legitimacy of such a decision rests not on electoral mandate but on descriptive representation and democratic deliberation—qualities that binding power actually strengthens, because it forces both the assembly and the public to take the outcome seriously.

[C1.1] Binding decision-making power for sortition assemblies does not fix democratic capture—it trades one set of unaccountable decision-makers for a worse one, with none of the guardrails that make even flawed elected systems legitimate. Random selection creates a descriptive snapshot of the public, but that is not the same as accountable representation. Unlike elected officials, assembly members cannot be voted out if they rely on bad information, cut parochial deals that harm marginalized groups, or cave to targeted pressure, because they face no re-election and disband immediately after issuing their decision.
[C1.2] There is no mechanism to correct bad calls after the fact, no ongoing responsibility to fix unintended consequences when a policy rolls out and harms the very people the assembly was meant to represent. PRO claims binding power insulates assemblies from lobbyist influence, but the opposite is true. A small, fixed group of ordinary citizens with no professional staff, no prior experience navigating pressure campaigns, and no mandatory public disclosure of every meeting and persuasion attempt is a far softer target for well-funded special interests than a sitting legislature, where lobbying is tracked, members have teams to vet claims, and public positions are a matter of long record.
[C1.3] Lobbyists do not vanish when power moves to a sortition body; they simply operate below public view, with far less scrutiny. Narrow, specific policy questions do not eliminate this risk. Even questions like climate policy or electoral reform carry cascading, high-stakes tradeoffs: costs that fall disproportionately on low-income households, rules that can lock in advantage for specific groups, and details that require iterative amendment as consequences come into view. Ceding binding power to a transient, unaccountable microcosm surrenders the core democratic right to hold decision-makers responsible for the outcomes of the laws they pass.

[P2.1] The CON's case rests on a false equivalence between accountability and re-election. [C1.1] assumes that because assembly members cannot be voted out, they are unaccountable. But accountability in a binding assembly is structural, not electoral. When a sortition body issues a binding decision, the chain of responsibility is transparent: these specific citizens, deliberating in public, made this specific choice.
[P2.2] Voters can judge that decision in the next election and instruct representatives to reverse it if it fails. In an advisory model, responsibility diffuses into nothing—politicians can ignore the recommendation and blame the assembly, or water it down while claiming credit.
[P2.3] On lobbying, [C1.2] has the mechanism backwards. Random selection is precisely what makes capture harder, not easier. You cannot donate to a lottery, gerrymander a random sample, or primary a citizen who will never stand for office. A transient group with no career to protect has no lever for sustained pressure; once they decide and disband, the influence dies.
[P2.4] In a legislature, lobbying works because members need funding and votes forever. The assembly's "softness" is its immunity.
[P2.5] [C1.3] warns that binding power surrenders the right to correct bad calls. But advisory power surrenders something worse: the right to any decision at all. Gridlocked parliaments produce no policy to correct; they produce drift. Binding decisions on narrow questions can include sunset clauses or review, but even without them, they create a concrete outcome that democracy can evaluate and overturn through elections.
[P2.6] The alternative is not accountability—it is permanent stalemate where no one is responsible for inaction.
[P2.7] The choice is between a decision with a visible author that the public can judge, and a recommendation with no author that the public cannot. Binding power gives democracy a target; advisory power gives it a fog.
