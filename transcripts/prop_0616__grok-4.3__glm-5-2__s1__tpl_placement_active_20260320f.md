[P1.1] Advanced semiconductors enable the training and deployment of AI models that confer decisive edges in military targeting, logistics, and autonomous systems. Exporting the equipment required to produce them at the leading edge therefore supplies adversaries with the physical means to close that gap rapidly. Once those capabilities exist, they cannot be recalled.
[P1.2] The scale of the risk justifies accepting commercial losses. Military AI does not merely improve existing weapons; it compresses the observe-orient-decide-act loop and multiplies the effectiveness of cheaper platforms. A country that pulls ahead by even a few generations of chips can field systems that render legacy defenses obsolete. Commercial revenue from equipment sales cannot offset the permanent shift in the balance of power that follows.
[P1.3] Targeted restrictions on the most advanced manufacturing tools exploit a narrow but real bottleneck. Only a handful of firms produce the lithography systems needed for sub-5-nanometer nodes. Blocking those sales delays adversary foundry progress by years, not quarters, without requiring perfect global enforcement. The resulting slowdown preserves a window for defensive countermeasures and alliance coordination.
[P1.4] Arguments that emphasize lost market share or inevitable circumvention miss the threshold question. The relevant comparison is not between restricted and unrestricted trade volumes, but between a world in which one side fields mature military AI first and a world in which both sides reach that threshold later. The former outcome carries higher expected costs in deterrence failure and escalation pressure.
