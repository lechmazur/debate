[P1.1] Every student will eventually cook a meal, deal with a broken faucet or a blown fuse, and make financial decisions with real consequences. Not every student will need the quadratic formula, yet we teach it as core. The question is not whether these skills are nice to have; it is whether we organize them as core requirements or leave them to chance. Chance is a bad curriculum.
[P1.2] Look at what "optional extras" means in practice. Electives are chosen by students whose schedules allow them, whose parents know which courses matter, and who already have enough confidence to sign up. A teenager from a household where money was always tight, where nobody taught budgeting because nobody had budgeting to do, is exactly the student least likely to self-select into personal finance. Making these skills optional bakes in the inequality we claim schools exist to reduce.
[P1.3] Universal exposure is the whole point of calling something core.
[P1.4] The case for core status rests on three legs. First, universality of use: cooking, basic repair, and personal finance are the only domains here where the near-term application rate approaches 100% within a few years of leaving school. Second, real stakes: a mishandled credit card or payday loan at eighteen compounds for a decade; a small leak ignored becomes a four-figure repair; a diet built on takeout because a kitchen feels unfamiliar is a health cost paid daily. These are not hypotheticals; they are ordinary life.
[P1.5] Third, and this is the decisive comparison: core status is about protected time and universal access, not about crowding out academics. Schools already carry skills that only some graduates will use. The ones everyone will use deserve the stronger claim on scheduled time.
[P1.6] My opponent must defend a system where the skills with the widest reach and the most immediate consequences are the ones we leave to parental inheritance and accident. That is not a defensible allocation of a public education.

Trading her scarce academic time for a unit on comparison-shopping is not closing a gap; it's giving her a worse version of the same credential while telling her that poverty is a budgeting problem.
[C1.4] Income fixes money troubles. Lecture-hall thrift does not.
[C1.5] Then there's timing. These skills are learned at the moment of need and forgotten otherwise. [C1.6] Cooking and faucet repair are the most learnable-on-demand skills in modern life; a person with decent literacy can follow a recipe or a repair video at the moment the pipe leaks. That asymmetry, not the frequency-of-use count in [P1.4], is what should decide what gets compulsory status.
[C1.7] Finally, mandating means assessing. Mandated cooking becomes a worksheet about the food pyramid. Mandated repair, bounded by liability and local codes, becomes a slideshow about tools.
