[P1.4] When workers can walk with their reputations intact, platforms must compete on pay and treatment, not on the size of the moat they've dug around workers' own histories.
[P1.5] Second, portability corrects a fairness asymmetry at the heart of gig work. Platforms insist these workers are independent contractors, not employees. Fine—but a genuine independent contractor owns her track record. A plumber's references follow her; a freelancer's portfolio is hers.

[P1.7] Third, this is technically routine, not radical. Ratings and completed-job counts are structured data. Standardized transfer tools with worker consent are a solved engineering problem; the only barrier is incumbent self-interest.
[P1.8] Expect the reply that ratings aren't comparable across platforms or that fraud will spike. But receiving platforms remain free to weight imported history however they choose—portability mandates access, not blind acceptance. [C1.3] So the question is what a mandate actually builds. It builds a permanent, portable labor file. [C1.5] Second, the data being ported is not the worker's résumé. It is a co-produced, context-bound signal. A rating reflects a platform's customer base, its dispute process, whether it drops the worst score, whether tipping is prompted. Grade inflation differs wildly between apps.
