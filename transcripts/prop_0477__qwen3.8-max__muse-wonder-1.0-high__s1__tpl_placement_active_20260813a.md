[P1.1] Countries should invest much more in undersea cable protection and redundancy, even at the expense of marquee defense procurements, because the first casualty of modern conflict is likely to be connectivity, not territory. Intercontinental communication runs through a thin web of fiber-optic cables on the seabed. These carry finance, cloud services, government messaging, military coordination, and everyday internet traffic. If that layer fails, the platforms we can see—jets, ships, armored vehicles—become less useful because they rely on the data, logistics, and economic continuity that cables provide.
[P1.2] The threat is not hypothetical. Cable cuts in the Baltic Sea and Red Sea in recent years have shown how a few failures at chokepoints can reroute traffic, raise costs, and expose how fragile repair capacity is. Accidents from anchors and trawlers already happen; in conflict, adversaries would have every incentive to exploit those vulnerabilities quietly, using sabotage, submarines, or proxy shipping. Attribution can be slow, while disruption is immediate.
[P1.3] That makes cables a coercion tool: pressure can be applied below the threshold of open war.
[P1.4] Protection and redundancy are a better hedge than additional high-profile weapons. Redundant routes, spare capacity, cable-monitoring systems, rapid repair vessels, and stockpiled parts reduce the payoff of attack. If an opponent cannot cheaply blind our communications or isolate economies, deterrence is strengthened. By contrast, buying another squadron or frigate while leaving the information backbone exposed risks investing in capability that cannot be coordinated, supplied, or sustained.
[P1.5] This is also the cheaper hedge. Cable resilience is comparatively inexpensive relative to major platforms, yet its benefits extend across every service and every civilian sector. The right standard is not whether defense procurement is visible, but whether the state can maintain secure communication under stress. Countries should therefore shift resources toward the invisible infrastructure whose failure would make all other defense problems harder.

[C1.1] Connectivity matters, but that does not make it the best place for the next defense dollar, let alone a reason to raid major procurements. The network described as fragile has repeatedly shown the opposite: when cables in the Baltic and Red Sea were damaged, traffic rerouted, latency and costs rose, but economies did not go dark and militaries did not go blind. That is redundancy already working, built largely by private operators who lay diverse routes because customers pay for uptime.
[C1.2] Adding still more cables and patrols therefore buys diminishing returns at the hardest margin to defend. Thousands of miles lie in deep water beyond practical surveillance; a determined state with submarines or a deniable merchant vessel does not need to defeat a sensor network, only to find one unguarded stretch. Governments cannot armor the seabed, and extra repair ships help after the fact whether the cut is accidental or hostile. Expensive protection that cannot prevent the attack it is sold against is not deterrence, it is reassurance spending.
[C1.3] Visible platforms, by contrast, are what make sabotage costly and repair possible. Ships and aircraft patrol chokepoints, escort repair vessels, gather attribution evidence, and credibly threaten consequences — the actual deterrent to grey-zone coercion. They also do what cables never can: deter invasion, defend airspace, keep sea lanes open, and fight if deterrence fails. Trading a frigate or squadron for marginal extra bandwidth leaves the state better connected to a crisis it is less able to control.
[C1.4] The sensible state role is targeted and cheap: faster repair permits, intelligence sharing with operators, stockpiles of cable and connectors, and diplomatic pressure on suspicious shipping. That does not require a major reallocation. If governments outspend the market on politically chosen backup routes, they duplicate commercial capacity, freeze yesterday's geography, and crowd out the conventional strength that protects cables, ports, satellites, and territory alike. Resilience is one requirement among many; it should not cannibalize the forces that make resilience enforceable.
