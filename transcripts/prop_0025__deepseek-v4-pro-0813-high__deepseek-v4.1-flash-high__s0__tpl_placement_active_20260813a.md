[P1.1] The after-hours ping is not an innocent message; it is a claim on unpaid time, attention, and sleep. A right to disconnect sets the default: for most employees, off hours are off unless there is a genuine emergency, and ignoring routine after-hours communication cannot count against them.
[P1.2] The first problem is that digital availability has become an implicit job requirement without being recognized as work. When a manager can message at 9 p.m., the employee who does not respond is often judged as less committed. That turns every evening into low-grade monitoring, unpaid and unbounded. The right to disconnect targets this expectation, not the technology itself.
[P1.3] Second, recovery is a safety and productivity issue, not a lifestyle preference. Constant after-hours contact fragments psychological detachment, disrupts sleep, and raises burnout risk. A tired employee checking messages is not more productive than a rested employee who returns focused the next morning. Rest periods already exist for factory floors and truck drivers; knowledge work should not be the only domain where recovery is optional.
[P1.4] Third, individual “just ignore it” advice ignores power asymmetry. A marketing coordinator who ignores a 10 p.m. request to “quickly check” a draft may lose the next promotion. A junior employee cannot safely opt out when the team norm is always-on. A statutory default changes the baseline, so availability becomes something to be negotiated and compensated—or excepted—rather than silently extracted.
[P1.5] The sector-specific exceptions answer the obvious objection. Emergency medicine, public safety, critical infrastructure, and genuinely time-sensitive roles can be carved out, along with true crises. The proposition is not a ban on urgent contact; it is a rule that most communications are not urgent and should not be treated as such.
[P1.6] The right to disconnect does not remove flexibility. It protects the people who currently have the least power to claim any.
