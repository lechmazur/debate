[P1.1] AI-generated voices and digital replicas create a distinct deception problem: audiences hear what appears to be a personal endorsement, but no human may have performed it. A creator’s voice could be cloned without consent, or a celebrity’s likeness scripted to say words they never approved. The commercial then borrows not merely a performance style but an identity. Audiences cannot reliably detect synthetic substitution, so clear legal disclosure is needed.
[P1.2] This is not a ban on AI production. It is a narrow transparency rule: whenever a host-read ad or endorsement uses an AI-generated voice or scripted likeness replication, audiences must be told clearly and conspicuously before the endorsement begins. Legitimate uses remain possible, while material deception does not.
[P1.3] The rule also protects creators. If a company clones a host’s voice and makes them appear to endorse a product, disclosure exposes the fraud rather than letting fabricated intimacy cause reputational damage. Consent must matter: authorized likeness use should also require permission. Synthetic persuasion must not masquerade as authentic human persuasion.
[P1.4] Ordinary ads already use hyperbole, edits, and undisclosed performers, but that analogy fails. A dramatized endorsement does not falsely present itself as a personal testimonial; a cloned voice manufactures the impression that the named creator personally delivered the message. Audiences spend and trust based on who appears to speak, and synthetic identity changes that source of credibility.
[P1.5] The strongest objection is that labels are ineffective or burdensome. Yet “AI-generated voice” is easier to understand than the technical distinctions enforcement must draw. Platforms can provide a standardized disclosure field, and creators can add one sentence before an ad. Minor friction is a reasonable price for preventing fabricated trust and protecting creator identity.
[P1.6] When synthetic speech seeks monetary influence, audiences deserve to know whether the speaker performed the endorsement—or was manufactured for the sale.

[C1.2] Start with PRO's worst case [P1.3]: a company clones a host's voice without consent and makes them appear to back a product. That is already unlawful. False endorsement, misappropriation of likeness, right-of-publicity claims, and consumer-protection rules requiring endorsements to reflect the endorser's genuine views all apply. A disclosure mandate adds nothing here, because someone willing to steal a creator's identity will not pause to recite a compliance sentence.
[C1.3] Mandatory labels bind honest people. Fraudsters ignore them.
[C1.4] So who does this law actually reach? Creators who consent. Picture a podcaster who writes her ad copy, uses the product, approves every word, and lets a licensed clone of her own voice record it while she is sick, traveling, or producing ten episodes a week. Nothing material is false.
[C1.5] She endorses the product, and the words are hers. PRO's own test [P1.4] asks whether the named creator "personally delivered the message." She did, in every sense that matters to a listener's wallet. Which vocal cords vibrated is no more material than whether a ghostwriter polished a CEO's letter.
[C1.6] Now look at the line-drawing. Does patching one flubbed word with voice-editing software trigger a label? Does AI dubbing a Spanish version of an ad the host approved? Does a creator who lost her voice to illness and banked it beforehand have to announce her disability before every sponsor read?
[C1.7] "Scripted likeness replication" sweeps in all of this. The result is either ruinous vagueness or labels so ubiquitous that audiences tune them out, destroying the very signal PRO wants.
[C1.8] The question that should decide this debate is consent and authorship, not technique. Enforce the laws against fake endorsements. Do not compel disclosure of how an honest one was produced.
