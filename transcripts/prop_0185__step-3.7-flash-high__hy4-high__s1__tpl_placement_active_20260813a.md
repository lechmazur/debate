[P1.1] Legislative discretion over fiscal policy is structurally biased toward deficit spending: elected officials face short electoral time horizons, so they prioritize tax cuts and new spending that win votes today while pushing costs to future generations and future legislatures. This bias produces chronic deficits, unsustainable debt, and recurring partisan fiscal crises that harm ordinary households and long-term economic growth.
[P1.2] A constitutionally required balanced-budget provision (BBR) with automatic stabilizer exceptions fixes this structural flaw without the rigidity that plagues unnuanced fiscal rules. The automatic stabilizer exception is critical: it exempts built-in countercyclical tools—unemployment insurance, progressive income taxes, food assistance—that automatically increase outlays or shrink revenue during recessions, eliminating the risk of pro-cyclical austerity that would deepen downturns. No legislative override is needed for these programmed responses, so the rule does not tie policymakers’ hands during crises.
[P1.3] Real-world evidence of the failures of legislative discretion is unambiguous. In the United States, the federal government has run a budget deficit in 46 of the last 50 fiscal years, not primarily due to wars or recessions, but because bipartisan political incentives consistently prioritize short-term wins over long-term sustainability. The recurring debt ceiling standoffs—from the 2011 credit rating downgrade to the 2023 near-default that spiked short-term Treasury yields—are direct products of leaving fiscal discipline to annual partisan bargaining, creating entirely avoidable economic volatility.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim a BBR is too rigid, but that objection collapses under the terms of this proposition. Unlike the strict deficit rules that forced pro-cyclical spending cuts during the Eurozone crisis, this rule allows deficits to fluctuate naturally with the economic cycle, preserving countercyclical flexibility while eliminating the structural bias toward permanent deficit spending.
