[C1.1] The proposition mistakes an origin signal for a trust signal. Cryptographic provenance can establish that a file passed through a particular generator or editor; it cannot establish that the content is true, unaltered, or published by the person who signed it. A signed clip can be cut, re-encoded, dubbed, or placed beside a false caption, while the label continues to describe only the earlier production step. If the chain breaks, the regulator has merely standardized the vocabulary of uncertainty.
[C1.2] That uncertainty is fatal to the motion's claimed benefit. Without identity verification, a signing key is a bearer instrument: whoever controls it can mint apparently valid provenance. The adversary does not need to defeat cryptography; they can use an authorized tool correctly and lie about context. Even a perfectly signed statement that “this video was generated by tool X” leaves open whether the video depicts an event, whether the clip is complete, and whether the speaker consents to its use.
[C1.3] The very case PRO imagines—a phone-call recording—can be accompanied by a valid label and still be used to impersonate someone.
[C1.4] Moreover, a universal mandate can make bad evidence look like protection. Once platforms display standardized badges, users may treat a missing or malformed label as suspicious and a present label as a guarantee. Re-encoding through ordinary messaging apps can strip metadata, so responsible creators are punished for using normal tools while fraudulent creators enjoy a simple evasion route. The mandate then rewards organizations that can afford compliance infrastructure and pushes smaller or anonymous creators toward opaque workarounds, reducing the very supply of transparent media it hopes to improve.
[C1.5] Standardization may aid voluntary infrastructure, but without identity it identifies artifacts, not liars; this proposition promises a safeguard while removing the basis for accountability.
