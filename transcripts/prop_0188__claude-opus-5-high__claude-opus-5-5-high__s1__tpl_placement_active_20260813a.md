[P1.2] Start with what we already know happens. When a drug trial comes out favorable, it gets written up, submitted, and published. When it comes out null or unflattering, it disappears into a filing cabinet. The antidepressant literature is the classic case: the published record looked far more favorable than the full set of trials regulators had actually seen.
[P1.3] Oseltamivir stockpiling proceeded for years while a substantial body of manufacturer trial data sat unreleased. The clinician reading the journals was not reading the evidence; she was reading the marketing-shaped half of it. And in the social sciences the same machinery produces the replication crisis — when psychology's large-scale replication effort tried to reproduce headline findings, a large share simply failed, exactly the pattern you'd expect if the literature is selected on surprise rather than on truth.
[P1.4] Preregistration attacks both mechanisms at once. Declaring your primary outcome and analysis plan in advance makes outcome switching visible — you can no longer run twenty measures, find the one with p<.05, and narrate it as your hypothesis. And a journal commitment to publish regardless of result removes the incentive to bury the boring answer. That commitment is the load-bearing half: registration alone leaves null results registered and unread.
[P1.5] This is not utopian. Clinical trial registration is already a condition of publication at major journals and already legally required for many US trials; results-blind review already exists as Registered Reports at dozens of journals.
