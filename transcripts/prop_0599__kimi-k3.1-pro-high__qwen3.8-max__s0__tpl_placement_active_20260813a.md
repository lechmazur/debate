[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept more surveillance risk in exchange for service delivery, but it assumes the African Union can build and enforce a rights-respecting continental identity regime where many states cannot or will not enforce basic privacy rights at home. That is the wrong trade, and the wrong sequence.
[C1.2] The motion’s danger is not a hypothetical central database; it is interoperability itself. Once national ID systems are linked across borders, a credential issued for health care or benefits becomes a rail for locating people beyond their home jurisdiction. For refugees, dissidents, journalists, or ethnic minorities, that can mean the difference between escaping a hostile state and being found by it. Fragmented systems are inefficient, but they also raise the cost of abuse.
[C1.3] A continent-wide framework lowers that cost and creates a more attractive target for hackers, political manipulation, and mission creep.
[C1.4] PRO’s answer is standards: data minimization, audits, remedies. But the AU cannot reliably discipline member states that abuse police powers, shut down internet access, or detain critics. Without courts capable of punishing violations, a privacy standard is a promise, not a shield. And the proposition concedes the risk increases.
[C1.5] That concession should be decisive where oversight is weakest and the people enrolled are least able to refuse.
[C1.6] Better service delivery does not require this bargain. Many gains come from simpler, less dangerous reforms: offline service cards, local verification, civil-registry cleanup, portability of paper records, and national systems with strict purpose limits. Biometric mismatches, lost devices, and connectivity failures can turn legitimate claimants into ghosts; the poorest are least able to correct errors. If identity systems are built before trust, connectivity, and remedies, the likely result is not inclusion with safeguards but exclusion with surveillance.
[C1.7] The AU should not trade civil liberties for administrative convenience when the poor and displaced will pay first.

Calling this “administrative convenience” disguises the stakes: interrupted treatment, denied assistance, and repeated proof requirements are not minor inconveniences for displaced people. [P2.6] The choice is therefore not liberty versus efficiency. It is whether cross-border identification develops under common rules and visible access controls, or through fragmented arrangements whose failures CON romanticizes as protection.
