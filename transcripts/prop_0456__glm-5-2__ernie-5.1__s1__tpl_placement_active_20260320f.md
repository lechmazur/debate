[P1.1] Compulsory licensing is the only model that solves both sides of the AI training problem at once. The alternative isn't a functioning permission market—it's paralysis or piracy.
[P1.2] Training a competitive generative model requires exposure to millions, often billions, of copyrighted works. No publisher, label, or studio can realistically clear that many rights individually. The transaction costs alone—identifying every rightsholder, negotiating every fee, tracking every use—make voluntary licensing at scale a fiction. And when voluntary licensing is a fiction, two things happen.
[P1.3] Powerful firms scrape everything and dare creators to sue, while smaller developers are shut out entirely because they lack the legal war chest to either clear rights or absorb litigation risk. That's the world we get without compulsory licensing: concentrated market power and uncompensated creators.
[P1.4] The proposition breaks this deadlock. A compulsory scheme says: you may train on copyrighted works without per-work permission, but you must pay into a fund that compensates rightsholders according to a transparent, administrable formula. This mirrors mechanisms already operating in adjacent industries—mechanical licenses for cover recordings, statutory licenses for cable and satellite retransmission. Those regimes exist precisely because lawmakers recognized that individual negotiation was impractical at scale and that creators still deserved compensation.
[P1.5] The AI context presents the same structural problem, only sharper.
[P1.6] Critically, the proposition pairs permission with obligation. It doesn't grant developers a free pass. It conditions access on payment. That condition is what distinguishes a legitimate innovation pipeline from raw appropriation.
[P1.7] Creators whose work forms the training substrate receive a revenue stream they would never secure through bilateral negotiation against firms with vastly more leverage. Developers receive legal certainty to build without facing a thicket of individual infringement claims. And the public receives AI tools trained on the full breadth of creative expression rather than only public-domain scraps or whatever a single corporation owns outright.
[P1.8] The real choice is not between compulsory licensing and some idealized free-market alternative. It's between compulsory licensing and the status quo—where the powerful take and the powerless lose. The proposition replaces that with a workable bargain.
