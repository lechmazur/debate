[P1.1] Critical minerals are the upstream choke point for everything a modern state needs to defend itself: batteries for transport and storage, rare earths for precision weapons and motors, graphite and lithium for supply chains that cannot be improvised in a crisis. Whoever controls the mine, the processing rights, and the offtake contracts controls whether those chains run, stall, or are diverted.
[P1.2] That is why acquisitions by firms closely tied to rival states are categorically different from ordinary foreign investment. A rival government does not need to nationalize a mine to weaponize it. Ties through state finance, party influence, subsidies, or legal obligations to cooperate with intelligence and industrial policy give it levers: slow an expansion, prioritize its own buyers, gain geological and operational data, or deny supply when tensions rise. By the time the leverage is used openly, the dependency is already built.
[P1.3] Presumptive blocking is the right response because the cost of error is wildly asymmetric. Wrongly blocking one deal costs time and some capital; another buyer, domestic fund, or allied investor can be found, and the ore remains in the ground. Wrongly approving one deal can lock in foreign control for decades. Mines and refineries are long-lived, hard to replace, and painful to expropriate or unwind later.
[P1.4] When an asset is strategic and a mistake is near-irreversible, the burden should not be on the government to prove a future act of coercion. It should be on the acquirer to prove it is independent and the risk can be contained.
[P1.5] This is not a blanket ban, and it is not xenophobia.
