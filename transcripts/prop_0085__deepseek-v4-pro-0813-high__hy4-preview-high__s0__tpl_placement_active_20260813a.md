[P1.1] Smartphones are engineered to capture attention. Notifications, infinite feeds, and variable rewards do not pause because a teacher says “put it away.” Leaving phone rules to individual teachers means a student meets one standard in math, another in English, and no standard in the hallway. That patchwork teaches students that rules are negotiable and that the strict teacher, not the distracting device, is the problem.
[P1.2] A default ban changes the social dynamic. The rule is predictable, impersonal, and consistent: phones are away during the school day unless a specific educational or medical exception is granted. The teacher no longer spends the first ten minutes of class bargaining about whether phones are allowed today. The teacher who enforces the policy is not the villain; she is simply applying a school-wide norm.
[P1.3] That matters because teacher-level discretion invites pressure, favoritism, and inconsistent discipline.
[P1.4] The school day also does not end at the classroom door. Lunch, passing periods, assemblies, and substitute-teacher days are exactly where unsupervised phone use causes the most damage: filming fights, social exclusion, and constant comparison. Individual classroom rules cannot touch those spaces. A schoolwide default can.
[P1.5] This is not an argument against limited, structured exceptions. Schools can still permit a teacher to use phones for a planned activity or accommodate a student who needs one. The question is where the baseline sits. Under the proposal, permission is the exception; under the alternative, phone access is the default and teachers must individually justify restricting it.
[P1.6] That stacks the fight against every teacher who wants a focused classroom. A default ban is not a rejection of teacher judgment. It is a school taking responsibility for the one device most intentionally designed to undermine it.
