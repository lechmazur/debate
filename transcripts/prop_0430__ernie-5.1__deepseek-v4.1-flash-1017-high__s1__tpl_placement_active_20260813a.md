[P1.1] Exclusion in private group chats is not a harmless playground squabble; it is a form of relational aggression that corrodes a student’s sense of belonging, grades, and mental health. Neuroscientific work on social pain shows that being deliberately left out activates the same brain circuits as physical injury. When a seventh‑grader is removed from the class‑wide study group that exists only in a WhatsApp thread, the ostracism follows them to school the next day—lowered participation, missed assignments, rising anxiety. That harm is as real, and as measurable, as any hallway shove.
[P1.2] Schools already accept responsibility for off‑campus conduct that spills into the school day. Hazing, drug use, or threats made on social media are routinely investigated when they affect attendance or safety. Digital exclusion fits the same pattern: the act occurs off‑site, but the damage is evident on‑site. Ignoring it creates a loophole that bullies exploit, knowing they can wound without ever stepping foot in the building.
[P1.3] Treating this as a purely off‑campus issue also ignores the practical reality of student life. Most adolescents coordinate homework, extracurriculars, and social plans through invitation‑only platforms that are woven into school routines. The “off‑campus” line is a fiction; the group chat is the modern schoolyard.
[P1.4] Legal and policy frameworks support extending anti‑bullying mandates. Many jurisdictions already hold schools accountable for cyberbullying that disrupts the educational environment. Exclusion is a subset of cyberbullying—silent, systemic, and often invisible to teachers unless they look for the tell‑tale signs: sudden disengagement, deteriorating grades, or reports of feeling “left out.” By explicitly naming digital exclusion, schools close the current policy gap, give victims a clear reporting channel, and send a preventive message that relational cruelty is unacceptable wherever it occurs.
[P1.5] If schools refuse to act, they cede the digital social sphere to unsupervised peer power, allowing a new form of bullying to fester unchecked. The proposition asks only that schools treat this harm with the seriousness it already demands; refusing does so at students’ expense.

[C1.1] Every childhood friendship involves exclusion. Not being invited to a birthday table, a clique’s lunch, or a private chat is often painful—but pain alone does not make conduct bullying, and it certainly does not make it a school’s business. The proposition collapses those distinctions. It points to real social pain and leaps straight to anti-bullying jurisdiction.
[C1.2] That leap is the central issue.
[C1.3] Private group chats and invitation-only circles are, by design, private associations. Schools cannot see them, cannot reliably reconstruct context, and cannot distinguish a sustained campaign of cruelty from three friends deciding they want a smaller space. Anti-bullying systems carry consequences: investigations, discipline, records, mandatory reporting. Applying that machinery to private digital exclusion means schools must police who invited whom, who muted whom, who formed a smaller group.
[C1.4] That is not child protection; it is social surveillance. It will chill ordinary friendship formation and fall hardest on awkward, private, or marginalized students who need small trusted circles.
[C1.5] The proposition says the harm spills into school, so location does not matter. But the off-campus line was never meant to say schools ignore distressed students. A school can support a student who feels left out, contact parents, teach social skills, and address observable disruption—without declaring every private chat slight an anti-bullying offense. The trigger should remain a genuine school nexus: threats, targeted harassment, coercion, or conduct that materially disrupts school operations.
[C1.6] The proposition’s standard has no stopping point. If exclusion from a private chat is bullying, every omitted invitation becomes a potential case file.
[C1.7] And enforcement will be arbitrary. Schools cannot discipline what they cannot see; they will rely on screenshots, hearsay, and whoever is most articulate or popular with staff. That is not a serious anti-bullying policy. It is a recipe for selective punishment and eroded trust.
[C1.8] Schools should respond to cruelty they can observe and to clear threats that follow students through the gates. They should not convert private social exclusion into a disciplinary category.
