[P1.1] A street name is not a textbook. A holiday is not an archive. When a government names a school, a square, or a national day after someone, it is not preserving history. It is awarding honor.
[P1.2] That distinction decides this debate. The standard is names that primarily honor those figures, not every remnant of a complicated past.
[P1.3] Historical continuity does not require us to keep celebrating colonizers, slaveholders, or dictators. Continuity of memory lives in records, museums, curricula, and sites that tell the full story. Continuity of honor is different: it tells living citizens whose violence still deserves public applause. Those are not the same project, and governments should stop treating them as if they were.
[P1.4] Public names shape belonging. A child who enters a school named for a slaveholder, or a city that still marks a holiday for the colonizer who seized the land, receives a civic lesson before any teacher speaks. The lesson is that the state's highest compliments still belong to those who inflicted the harm. Descendants of the enslaved, the colonized, and the disappeared are asked to treat that as neutral tradition.
[P1.5] It is not. It is an official preference for one community's pride over another's equal standing.
[P1.6] We already rename. After dictatorships fall, streets once dedicated to party bosses and military occupiers are routinely retitled. Cities drop the names of fallen emperors and disgraced commanders. Nobody calls that amnesia when the figure is a twentieth-century tyrant.
[P1.7] The sudden piety about continuity appears mainly when the honored figure is a colonizer or slaveholder whose victims remain politically inconvenient.
[P1.8] Keeping the name does not keep the past honest. It keeps the honor cheap and the victims' place in civic life second-class. If we want history, teach it in full. If we want continuity, keep the documents, the complexity, and the scars.
[P1.9] Do not keep the salute.
