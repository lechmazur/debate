[P1.1] Preventable infant death and disability should not depend on whether exhausted parents complete another consent form. For genomic newborn screening limited to conditions with effective early interventions, opt-out is the only default consistent with public health. Conditions like severe combined immunodeficiency, spinal muscular atrophy, and congenital hypothyroidism can be detected before symptoms and treated before irreversible damage. Under opt-in, the default is that a baby is not tested until a parent manages to say yes.
[P1.2] In practice, that means missed cases: lost forms, language barriers, fear of genomic language, or simply the chaos of a birth discharge. Those missed cases are not abstract; they are children who later develop preventable seizures, organ damage, or fatal infections.
[P1.3] Opt-out does not remove consent. It changes the default while preserving a clear right to decline. Parents would be informed, given accessible material, and offered an easy refusal. But the system would no longer make inaction the harmful choice.
[P1.4] That matters because inaction is common and unequally distributed. Families with lower literacy, less time with a clinician, or non-dominant language are disproportionately likely to fail the opt-in process. Opt-out therefore narrows health disparities instead of widening them. The same screening would no longer hinge on which family asks the right question or signs the right line.
[P1.5] The limiting clause is central. This is not whole-genome disclosure or uncertain risk information. It is a targeted screen for conditions where early diagnosis changes treatment. Public health systems commonly accept default participation in newborn screening and immunization registries for the same reason: population-level prevention requires low friction, not low information.
[P1.6] Routinely declining to offer a high-benefit test in the absence of affirmative consent is a policy choice with casualties. The narrow, actionable scope makes those casualties unnecessary.
