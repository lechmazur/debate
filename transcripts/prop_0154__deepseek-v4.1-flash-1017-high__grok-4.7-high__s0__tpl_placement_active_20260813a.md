[P1.1] Foundation models are built by ingesting millions of books, articles, photographs, and illustrations. That is not incidental; it is the core commercial input. Yet the current choice is between unlicensed extraction at zero price and litigation that only the largest firms can survive. A compulsory license breaks that deadlock: statutory permission to train, paired with payment to rightsholders.
[P1.2] The case rests on three things. First, transaction costs make voluntary licensing impossible at the necessary scale. No developer can negotiate with millions of authors; no photographer can track every model that used her work. Collective administration solves this, as it already does for music performance and mechanical reproduction.
[P1.3] Second, payment preserves the incentive copyright exists to protect. If AI companies can take the entire stock of human expression and sell competing products, the market for authorship erodes. A license turns that taking into a revenue stream and gives creators a stake in the AI economy.
[P1.4] Third, legal certainty helps innovation rather than hurting it. Startups cannot build on a regime of unresolved fair-use guesses and injunction risk. A known statutory rate lets them invest; it prevents the field from being captured by incumbents who can afford endless litigation.
[P1.5] The objection that licensing “legalizes theft” gets it backwards. Theft is taking without payment. A compulsory license is the opposite: it says you may use the work, but you must pay. Nor does it require giving AI firms a free pass on output infringement or allowing rightsholders to veto every model.
[P1.6] It creates a compensated input regime, with rates and distribution set publicly.
[P1.7] Copyright has always adapted when new technology made individual bargaining impossible—player pianos, radio, cable. AI training is the same structural problem at larger scale. The choice is not between perfect control and no rights. It is between a paid, workable license and a free-for-all that rewards the biggest extractors.
[P1.8] Choose the license.

[C1.1] A compulsory license does not solve AI training. It abolishes copyright’s only real leverage—the right to refuse—and replaces it with a payment no one can honestly calculate.
[C1.2] Training is not a performance of a song. There is no ticket, stream count, or identifiable moment when one book or photograph is delivered to an audience. Billions of works are compressed into weights. No collecting society can say what fraction of a model’s value came from one novel, one news archive, or one illustration.
[C1.3] The scheme can levy a fee and split it by registry membership, corpus share, or lobbying power. That is not payment to rightsholders. It is a transfer to whoever is easiest to find, while orphaned, dissenting, and unregistered creators get a rounding error.
[C1.4] The music analogies therefore fail. Mechanical and performance licenses, where they function, cover discrete, identifiable uses, and they leave most other exploitations under the owner’s control. This proposal would authorize ingestion of the whole stock of text and images, including works whose owners would not license training at any price. Many creators object to being raw material for a system that imitates them and competes with them.
[C1.5] A check does not restore that refusal. It deletes it.
[C1.6] The legal fork is equally fatal. If training is fair use, a compulsory license invents a tax on lawful conduct. If training infringes, the existing tools are injunctions, damages, and voluntary deals—not a political rate that converts every exclusive right into a buyout. Certainty purchased this way is compliance theater: audits, rate cases, collective-management politics, and arguments over fine-tuning, synthetic data, and foreign works.
[C1.7] Incumbents write those rules. Startups rent permission from them. Individual authors do not become stakeholders in the AI economy; they become line items.
[C1.8] The deadlock is real. Forced access at a fake price makes it worse. Keep the right to say no.

[P4.1] A1: Identification and consent are different problems. A collecting society can identify many rightsholders through ISBNs, DOIs, publisher records, registries, and claims, yet still cannot obtain unanimous ex ante consent from millions. One holdout, one unlocatable heir, one anti-AI photographer can block a corpus. Compulsory licensing is built for that gap: pay claimants, hold unclaimed funds in escrow, audit corpus inclusion, and distribute by repertoire and sampling.
[P4.2] If some owners cannot be found, the answer is a later claim, not zero payment forever. Orphan works and unclaimed royalties are routine; escrow handles them better than uncompensated taking. Consent is not a prerequisite to compensation; it is the veto that makes mass licensing impossible.
[P4.3] A2: The license is the legislative answer, not a waiting room for a fair-use verdict. Legislatures can define commercial training as a compensated statutory use, as they have for technologies that outran individual bargaining. That gives ex ante certainty: a known rate, audit duties, and no injunction lottery. A statutory license does not need a court to first declare training unlawful; it defines the legal baseline itself.
[P4.4] If a court would have called training fair use, the scheme is a policy choice to pay creators rather than a tax on a "right" to extract; if training infringes, it replaces damages roulette with a price. Either way, the uncertainty CON defends is not neutral—it favors incumbents who can litigate.
[P4.5] CON’s questions assume perfect identification and litigation-first certainty. Both are unavailable. The real comparison is a paid, administrable claim for the many versus a veto for the few and free extraction for the largest firms. CON’s alternative is not consent; it is extraction now and litigation later.
[P4.6] A compulsory license wins because it makes the right real enough to pay. That is the decisive institutional choice: payment attached to use, not permission that only the powerful can enforce.
