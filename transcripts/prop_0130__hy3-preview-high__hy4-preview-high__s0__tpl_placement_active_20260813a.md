[P1.1] The proposition is straightforward: artifacts taken by force during colonial domination belong to those from whom they were taken, and that claim survives any assertion that Western museums are safer custodians. Retention on preservation grounds treats a moral debt as a curatorial preference.
[P1.2] Colonial looting was not acquisition; it was extraction under threat of violence. When British forces seized Asante regalia or French troops removed Ethiopian manuscripts, the act exploited vulnerability rather than rescued heritage. A museum that keeps such objects because it claims better climate control is effectively arguing that the original owners’ rights expired the moment they became unable to protect themselves from the looters. That logic rewards the looting itself.
[P1.3] The preservation argument is also empirically shaky. Many source countries now operate professional conservation facilities, and others can build them with the same international cooperation that currently funds Western museums. The claim that foreign institutions are uniquely skilled ignores that conservation knowledge is global and teachable. The Benin Bronzes, stripped from Nigeria in 1897, are increasingly returned, and Nigerian institutions have demonstrated capacity to exhibit and care for them.
[P1.4] To say “we keep them because you might scratch them” is less about conservation than about who controls the narrative.
[P1.5] Moreover, the harm of non-return is not merely symbolic. Objects central to a community’s identity—ancestral figures, regalia, sacred items—function only in relation to that community. A climate-controlled vitrine in London cannot replace the living context of origin. The loss of that connection is a continuing injury that “better preservation” does not offset.
[P1.6] Finally, the preservation caveat creates a permanent excuse. If “we protect it better” is enough to veto return, then every former colonial power can indefinitely retain plunder by citing its own superior infrastructure. That converts museums into warehouses of imperial privilege. The correct standard is simple: looted must go back, and capacity can be built through partnership, not used as a barrier.
