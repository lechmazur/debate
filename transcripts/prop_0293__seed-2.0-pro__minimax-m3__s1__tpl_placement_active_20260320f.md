A statue of a colonial governor outside a nineteenth-century courthouse is, today, an artifact we can argue with, plaque, walk past, and protest. The PRO side wants us to believe that the only honest relationship with such a figure is to lift him off his pedestal and place him behind museum glass.
[C1.3] But that move does not make history more accurate. It makes the public square more comfortable, and comfort is not the same thing as truth.
[C1.4] Consider the standard they propose: remove anyone "primarily associated with" slavery, colonial domination, or mass repression. Apply it honestly. By that logic, we would have to remove statues of most American founders, of Churchill for the Bengal famine, of figures celebrated for one reform and complicit in another. Either the principle is universal—and would gut public memory of everyone except a small handful of saints—or it is selective, in which case it is not a principle at all but a political choice dressed up as moral clarity.
[C1.5] [P1.7]'s claim that we should not vote to erect these statues today proves too much: by that logic, the founders go too.
[C1.6] And contextualization can happen in place. The choice between [P1.5]'s pedestal-and-museum binary is false. The choice is between hiding the past and confronting it where it stands.
[C1.7] The strongest point: removing the difficult figures does not make our cities more honest. It makes them less so. A public square filled only with people we now agree with is not a record of civilization. It is a poster.
