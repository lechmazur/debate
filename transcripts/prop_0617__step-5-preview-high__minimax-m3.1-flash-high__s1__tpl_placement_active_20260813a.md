[P1.1] In digital markets, the acquisition is often the competitive act. A dominant platform that cannot out-innovate a rival may buy it; one that wants ecosystem lock-in may buy a complement. The canonical concern is not a merger creating a stronger competitor, but the elimination of a future one. The acquisitions that turned Instagram and WhatsApp into pillars of today’s Meta illustrate how firms can be absorbed before they mature into serious threats.
[P1.2] The ordinary approach asks whether a deal is likely to harm competition after it happens. That is structurally backwards. Platform competition is dynamic and opaque; harm appears in nascent rivalry, data access, developer dependence, and future innovation. Prices may look stable while quality, privacy, and interoperability quietly deteriorate.
[P1.3] Authorities should not have to reconstruct a completed foreclosure story when a dominant gatekeeper is acquiring more control over distribution, attention, or infrastructure.
[P1.4] A presumption is not a ban. It is a burden allocation matched to asymmetric information and asymmetric risk. The parties control integration plans, traffic data, experimentation results, and internal projections; the agency must infer the counterfactual from outside. Missing a killer acquisition risks durable market power, while demanding proof risks only delay or abandonment of a deal whose benefits cannot be shown.
[P1.5] “Substantial consumer benefit” is the right test: not speculative synergies, but merger-specific, verifiable gains likely to reach users and not achievable without the transaction. That standard still permits efficient deals. What it rejects is the fiction that incumbent scale alone is innovation. It forces the acquiring platform to explain why users are better off under its control, rather than forcing the public to prove what the market has already lost.

[C1.1] The proposition starts from the presumption that an acquisition is lawful and reverses it without an evidentiary foundation. Dominance may increase scrutiny, but it cannot substitute for evidence that a deal eliminates meaningful competition, forecloses a credible rival, or transfers control of essential inputs. Antitrust should not condemn an acquisition because of the acquirer’s identity.
[C1.2] PRO’s dynamic concern is legitimate but proves too much. A categorical rule would cover acquisitions of complementary products, developer tools, security firms, and infrastructure providers—assets that may improve service and reduce dependence on any single rival. A cloud platform buying a specialist, for example, might integrate features, improve security, and lower switching costs. Yet firms cannot prove a counterfactual no outsider can observe: what would have happened had the target continued independently.
[C1.3] Internal forecasts are not neutral evidence; they reflect the deal team’s incentives.
[C1.4] PRO’s information-asymmetry claim also cuts against its case. Authorities can compel documents, data, and testimony and inspect commercial plans; structured review is not “post hoc” merely because it occurs after a deal is proposed. Nor is “substantial consumer benefit” a workable escape hatch. Must it mean lower prices, better quality, privacy, or convenience?
[C1.5] Must it be caused by the merger and absent without it? Such causal proof at the moment of review privileges whichever side presents the most flattering forecast, making the standard unpredictable and chilling beneficial consolidation.
[C1.6] Killer-acquisition risk is real, but it does not justify categorical guilt. Authorities should target acquisitions that combine dominance with a nascent rival, use interim measures where evidence is strongest, and address foreclosure through interoperability, data access, and non-discrimination rules. Those measures attack the mechanism rather than presume harm based on corporate identity. The burden should remain on the authority to show likely substantial harm, not on the firm to prove that every acquisition by a dominant platform delivers substantial consumer benefit.

[P4.2] A2: The motion does not “excuse” an acquisition merely because benefits outweigh harm [C3.2]. It creates a rebuttable presumption and fixes who must prove what. [C4.1] A1: The evidence that can verify “substantial consumer benefit” is not limited to the buyer’s projections. Authorities can examine the transaction rationale, require enforceable commitments, test whether claimed price, quality, privacy, or interoperability improvements are merger-specific, and assess whether safeguards survive independently of the acquisition. The buyer need not prove what would have happened had the target remained independent; that would make every evaluation impossible. It must identify concrete gains that consumers would not receive from the transaction alone.
[C4.2] More importantly, if authorities can compel documents and testimony to test harm [C2.3], they can use the same process to test benefit.
[C4.3] A2: The motion does not excuse a deal merely because it offers benefits; it creates a rebuttable presumption. My objection is not a strawman. Even if the presumption is rebutted, the transaction must still satisfy ordinary antitrust requirements, including the prohibition on substantial lessening of competition. The proposition instead makes an acquisition presumptively anticompetitive because of the acquirer's status, while requiring the firm to overcome that presumption with a benefit that may itself be difficult to isolate.
[C4.4] The information-asymmetry argument therefore does not justify reversing the burden. If the agency cannot test claims about benefit, PRO has conceded that pre-closing review is inadequate; if it can, the firm should answer with evidence. And the practical consequence matters: a presumption against dominant firms raises regulatory risk, discourages legitimate consolidation, and may prevent transactions that improve security, interoperability, or service quality.
[C4.5] PRO also continues to label every acquisition of a competitor, complement, or developer tool alike. Those transactions present different foreclosure mechanisms. A targeted rule—focused on acquisitions involving plausible future rivals or essential inputs, with interim measures where necessary—addresses the danger without making an acquirer’s identity the legal test. The authority should retain the burden of proving likely substantial harm; the firm should have to substantiate efficiency claims, but not prove that every acquisition is beneficial before scrutiny can occur.

[C5.1] The decisive issue is not information asymmetry but legal logic. PRO assumes that because the buyer creates uncertainty, the buyer must bear it. But a presumption must identify what is presumed and how it is rebutted. This motion presumes anticompetitive effects across acquisitions by dominant platforms, yet allows rebuttal through “substantial consumer benefit.” A transaction can deliver lower prices, better security, or more convenience while eliminating a nascent rival and strengthening market power.
[C5.2] Consumer benefit therefore does not necessarily answer the anticompetitive theory.
[C5.3] PRO’s claim that a targeted rule “leav[es] the irreversible harm done” also confuses a difficult case with a categorical one. Authorities can investigate foreclosure, use interim safeguards, impose conditions, or require divestitures before closure. They need not wait for competition to be extinguished before responding.
[C5.4] The better framework is mechanism-specific: the authority must establish a plausible foreclosure theory, while the firm must substantiate efficiency claims with evidence. That is not identity-based leniency or hostility to consolidation; it is a workable division of proof.
[C5.5] PRO wants uncertainty itself to establish guilt. Antitrust should require a demonstrated mechanism of harm, not make every dominant-platform acquisition presumptively unlawful.
