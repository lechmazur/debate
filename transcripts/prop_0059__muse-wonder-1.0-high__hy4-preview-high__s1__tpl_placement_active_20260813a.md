[P1.1] The dominant model — an endless swipe feed, a few photos and prompts, algorithmic ranking, and paid boosts to be seen — does not primarily reward finding a partner and leaving. It rewards staying, swiping, and paying. That misalignment is why, for most users, it makes relationship formation harder, not easier.
[P1.2] First, the interface trains the wrong judgment. Relationships begin with curiosity about a particular person; swiping trains instant comparison across hundreds of cards. Appearance, height, job, and a witty line crowd out kindness, reliability, and chemistry. When the next option is always one thumb movement away, users learn to optimize rather than commit, to keep browsing after good dates, and to treat ambiguity — the normal start of intimacy — as a reason to exit.
[P1.3] Second, attention on these platforms concentrates brutally. A small share of profiles collects most likes, while the majority compete for scraps of visibility unless they pay. For many men that means long stretches of rejection or silence; for many women it means an unmanageable inbox from low-effort contacts. Both experiences corrode formation: scarcity breeds desperation and resentment, overload breeds defensive filtering and burnout.
[P1.4] Most people do not meet a curated set of compatible partners. They meet a sorted hierarchy that tells them their market value.
[P1.5] Third, the model strips away the social scaffolding that helped relationships start. Meeting through friends, work, or community supplied context, repeated contact, and accountability. A cold match supplies none of that: profiles can mislead, conversations vanish without cost, and ghosting becomes rational because neither side expects to be seen again. Trust starts lower, effort starts lower, and fragile early connections die at higher rates.
[P1.6] More introductions are not better formation if the system that delivers them teaches comparison, concentrates attention, and removes accountability. For the median user — not the few who thrive in the feed — the dominant model turns dating into exhausting shopping, and shopping rarely produces commitment.
