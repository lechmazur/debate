[P1.1] Long-term care is the rare risk that is both near-certain and uninsurable privately. Roughly half of people over 65 will need substantial help bathing, feeding, moving, or with dementia for months or years. The bill runs thousands per month for years, yet you cannot know if you will need zero or five years. Private long-term care insurance has collapsed in the US and elsewhere because only those who expect to need it buy it, premiums spiral, and insurers exit.
[P1.2] Telling families to just save assumes everyone can set aside a second house worth of liquid wealth for a risk that may never hit.
[P1.3] What fills that hole today is brutal. Means-testing forces middle-class families to impoverish themselves before help arrives, penalizing anyone who saved. And "family care" is a euphemism for unpaid daughters and wives quitting jobs, losing pensions, and burning out. With fewer children, more geographic mobility, and more women working, that informal workforce is disappearing.
[P1.4] The result is unmet need, hospital boarding, and quiet neglect.
[P1.5] A universal or near-universal system solves the insurance problem at its root. Like health insurance, mandatory social insurance - funded by payroll contributions as in Germany and Japan - pools the risk across generations. Everyone contributes while working, everyone is covered when frailty hits, regardless of wealth. Because the state is the dominant purchaser, it can set basic quality standards, train and decently pay care workers instead of relying on a gray market, and shift care toward home and community options that people actually want.
[P1.6] The alternative isn't cheaper. It just hides costs: lost female labor force participation, caregiver health crises, emergency room visits because no one was there to help. A universal system makes those costs visible and manageable. It is the only way to give aging dignity without bankrupting families.

[C1.1] Universal long-term care sounds humane until you do the math of aging. When one in six people is over 65 and heading toward one in four, promising comprehensive care to everyone turns an uncertain risk into an unlimited fiscal commitment. Costs don't pool away; they multiply because government coverage invites everyone to claim formal paid services for needs families once met informally.
[C1.2] Germany and Japan, often praised as models, have repeatedly raised payroll rates and tightened eligibility because promises outran workers and caregivers. Expanding that model now, just as populations age fastest, locks in the worst timing for a pay-as-you-go promise. That is why targeted help beats universal entitlement. Most elderly never need years of intensive care, and many who do hold substantial wealth, above all housing equity.
[C1.3] Sending public checks to heirs of homeowners while taxing struggling young workers to pay for it is reverse redistribution. Means testing plus a safety net concentrates scarce public money on the truly destitute without bankrupting budgets or starving schools, hospitals, and childcare.
[C1.4] Universal systems also corrode what they claim to save. If the state will pay for a care aide regardless of family effort or savings, families save less, adult children step back, and private insurance withers completely. Demand for expensive formal care explodes just as the worker-to-retiree ratio collapses. The result is not dignity but rationing: waiting lists, fifteen-minute visits, underpaid staff, and standardized packages that ignore what elders actually want.
[C1.5] Family care is not just unpaid exploitation. For many it is a chosen obligation, culturally valued and more responsive than a bureaucracy. Policy should support caregivers with respite, tax relief, and flexible work, not replace them with a monopoly provider. Private savings and means testing are imperfect, but they preserve incentives, target need, and respect diversity.
[C1.6] Universal care promises everything and delivers fiscal crisis plus crowded-out families.
