[P1.1] The state should not help manufacture human beings whose biological origins are permanently sealed by contract. Donor conception offers a profound service to families facing infertility, but that service cannot extend to erasing the biological record of the person created. A society does not treat genetic identity as a premium feature to be toggled off for market convenience, and it does not let such convenience override the interests of the adult who must live with that erasure.
[P1.2] The core of this case is a rights claim that reaches deeper than policy efficiency. Every adult deserves access to the identifying facts of their own conception. Genetic history is not a sentimental luxury; it is a medically necessary baseline, a safeguard against accidental consanguinity in an era of genetic testing, and a precondition for coherent self-authorship. To deliberately withhold this information from the only person who could never consent to its deletion—the resulting child—is to make biological truth a tradable commodity.
[P1.3] CON must defend the indefensible: that a person’s right to know their own origins can be signed away by strangers before that person draws breath.
[P1.4] The motion’s decisive clause is “even if anonymity would reduce donation supply.” This forces the fundamental question: can a basic entitlement be held hostage by market incentives? We already reject that logic elsewhere. We do not legalize kidney sales to expand transplant supply, nor do we seal adoption records to boost foster recruitment. If authenticating a child’s biological parentage causes some prospective donors to withdraw, society absorbs that cost rather than legislating ignorance into human lives.
[P1.5] And the empirical premise is weaker than CON will admit; countries that have removed anonymity have generally seen supply stabilize as norms evolve, but even a lasting shortfall would not justify institutionalized secrecy.
[P1.6] We are choosing whether donor-conceived adults are autonomous citizens with an inviolable interest in their own biology, or products whose specifications were set by contract before they were born. Anonymity chooses the latter. Their right to identity must prevail, whatever the market price.
