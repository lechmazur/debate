[P1.1] Bankruptcy exists to end debts that cannot be paid and return honest debtors to productive life. Student loans are the glaring exception: even borrowers facing long-term inability to repay must clear an undue-hardship bar that almost no one clears in practice. The law should make discharge much easier in exactly those cases.
[P1.2] That bar demands something close to lifetime hopelessness, proven after years of payments that often fail to reduce principal. Chronic illness, low-wage credentials, and caregiving burdens do not reliably qualify. A test that rejects the genuinely unable is not a careful screen for abuse. It is a near-ban dressed as equity.
[P1.3] The costs are concrete. Balances capitalize while wages are garnished and ruined credit blocks housing, relocation, and small enterprise. A home-health worker whose loan will outlast any realistic earnings path is not a strategic defaulter. Forcing collection does not resurrect the principal.
[P1.4] It skims subsistence income, discourages extra hours, and spends public money chasing funds that will never arrive. Insolvency is not cured by declaring the debt sacred.
[P1.5] Moral hazard does not answer the motion, because this is not a general amnesty. Discharge would still require long-term inability—judged by earning capacity, disability, payment history, and realistic prospects—not regret or clever timing. Trustees already separate inability from unwillingness for credit cards and medical bills. Education debt merits no harsher presumption, particularly where loans were marketed to teenagers as safe bets on credentials the labor market never paid for at the promised rate.
[P1.6] Waiting periods, income tests, or partial discharge can police the margins without restoring impossibility. What cannot be justified is a lifetime claim against borrowers who have already lost the ability to pay. Easier discharge for the long-term unable ends futile collection and restores the fresh start bankruptcy was built to provide.

[C1.1] Bankruptcy discharge works when the lender priced in the risk of loss against collateral or a track record. Student loans have neither. A nineteen-year-old borrowing for a nursing degree offers no house, no car, no repayment history — only a legal promise to pay from future income. Make that promise easy to escape and you have not fixed lending, you have broken it.
[C1.2] Lenders will not simply write off losses; they will price them into everyone else. Cosigner requirements, higher interest rates, and tighter caps on loan amounts will hit exactly the low-income, first-generation students the PRO side claims to protect, because they are the ones without family wealth to fall back on when private capital retreats.
[C1.3] The deeper problem is verification. "Long-term inability to repay" sounds objective, but for young borrowers it is a prediction, not a fact. Income, health, and career trajectory can all shift within a decade. A test built to be "much easier" than undue hardship will necessarily be easier to satisfy strategically — timed right after a rough year, before a promotion or recovery that was already likely.
[C1.4] Unlike medical debt, which arrives from an unplanned accident, or credit card debt, which reflects a spending decision already made, student debt is taken deliberately as an investment in a future income stream. Loosening discharge specifically for this category rewards precisely the borrowers with the most private information about their own prospects.
[C1.5] None of this leaves the "genuinely unable" out in the cold. Income-driven repayment caps payments near zero for the truly low-earning, federal disability discharge exists for those who cannot work, and closed-school or borrower-defense discharge already covers fraud victims. These tools target hardship without inviting borrowers with strong future earnings to game a system built on trust in future income. The honest fix is to strengthen and speed up those channels — not to gut the presumption of repayment that makes student lending to poor and working-class kids possible at all.
