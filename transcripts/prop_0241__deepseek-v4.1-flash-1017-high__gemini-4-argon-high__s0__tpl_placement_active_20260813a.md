[P1.1] Criminal punishment is the state deliberately imposing hard treatment. The first question is not “what does this offender deserve?” but “what should this power accomplish?” Retribution looks backward and asks whether suffering matches the crime. Rehabilitation looks forward and asks what will stop the harm, repair the offender’s place in society, and protect future victims. The forward-looking aim is more important because it is the only one that changes what happens next.
[P1.2] Consider a familiar case: an offender whose theft is driven by untreated addiction. A purely retributive sentence can be perfectly proportional and still release the same untreated person into the same conditions. The harm repeats. A rehabilitative sentence—supervised treatment, skills, housing support—has a measurable goal and a public safety payoff.
[P1.3] That is not softness; it is the state taking its own coercive power seriously. If we are going to lock someone up or supervise them, we should organize that intrusion around reducing future harm, not around producing a culturally satisfying amount of misery.
[P1.4] Retribution also has a weak claim to priority because it offers no criterion beyond desert for designing prison life. Once the proportional sentence is set, retribution is silent about whether the prisoner gets treatment, education, or nothing. Rehabilitation gives the system a purpose every day. It also respects the basic fact that offenders are people who can change, while retribution tends to fix them in their worst act.
[P1.5] This does not erase proportionality or victims. Desert can set limits and rights can block abuses. But when the aims conflict, the more important aim is the one that makes the public safer and the sentence do constructive work. Retribution may express condemnation, but it does not repair harm; rehabilitation prevents future victims.
[P1.6] A justice system that can only inflict pain is not fulfilling its deepest responsibility; one that tries to end the cycle is.

[C1.3] Take the addicted thief [P1.2]. Why may the state forcibly confine that thief, yet cannot lock up and "rehabilitate" addicted citizens who committed no crime? [C1.5] Rehabilitation also has nothing to say when offenders need no fixing. Consider an aging war criminal caught decades later, a disgraced executive who cannot embezzle again, or a killer who acted in a one-time rage and poses zero recidivism risk. [C1.6] Yet letting them walk free is an outrage. We punish them because crimes are not mere social hazards; they are moral wrongs against victims that demand public condemnation.
[C1.7] Rehabilitation treats offenders as problems to be managed. Retribution treats both victims and offenders as moral agents—vindicating the harmed, holding wrongdoers accountable, and strictly limiting state power.

Rehabilitation is the only aim that asks whether the state’s hard treatment actually protects the public and restores a life.
