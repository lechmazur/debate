[P1.1] The case for expanding vouchers does not stand or fall on a single test-score coefficient. It stands on who decides, who is served, and what a pluralist education system is for. When evidence is mixed, the question is not “can we prove vouchers raise math scores everywhere?” It is “is it better to keep a child trapped in an assigned school that is failing her family, or to let that family choose?” Mixed evidence is a reason to expand carefully—with transparency, anti-fraud safeguards, and public reporting—not to preserve a monopoly by default.
[P1.2] Public education is not synonymous with a particular building. It is a public commitment that every child gets an education. Vouchers can serve that commitment by funding students rather than plants, especially low-income and special-needs families who cannot buy their way into a better ZIP code. The resource objection proves too much: money follows the child because it was meant for the child.
[P1.3] Districts losing students also lose the cost of educating them. Fixed costs are real, but they are a transition-management problem—phasing, temporary hold-harmless support, administrative consolidation—not a reason to deny families exit forever. And if a district loses students because it is not serving them, the answer is to improve, not to lock the door.
[P1.4] Mixed academic evidence also cuts against the status quo. We have decades of experience with conventional public schooling and persistent gaps; we do not demand perfect proof before funding it. Vouchers create immediate benefits that test scores miss: safety, values fit, curricular specialization, religious or cultural identity, and the dignity of parental agency. Competition can pressure schools to respond even when gains are uneven and slow.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is between imperfect choice and unaccountable assignment. Expand vouchers, with guardrails and honest evaluation, because the burden is on those who would deny a family an exit to show that compulsory assignment is better. They cannot.

[C1.2] Start with the evidence. The pattern across voucher research is uncomfortable for PRO: small pilot programs serving low-income families show modest gains or neutral effects, but the large-scale expansions this proposition demands—the statewide, near-universal programs in places like Louisiana, Indiana, and Arizona—have repeatedly been associated with *negative* effects on test scores, sometimes substantial ones. Mixed evidence is not symmetric noise. It is "small programs look fine, the thing we're actually debating looks worse."
[C1.3] [P1.1] treats mixed evidence as a tiebreaker for choice; it is a warning label on scale.
[C1.4] Second, the "money follows the child" line [P1.2] is accounting fiction. A district losing ten percent of its students does not lose ten percent of its costs. Building maintenance, transportation, debt service, and staff contracts are largely fixed in the short and medium term. Per-pupil funding rises for the remaining students only in a spreadsheet; in reality, programs get cut, counselors disappear, and the students whose families *cannot* or *do not* navigate a voucher market—disabled students, English learners, the least mobile families—inherit a thinner school.
[C1.5] Vouchers select for the parents with time, information, and transportation. That is not pluralism; it is sorting.
[C1.6] Third, public money is flowing to institutions exempt from public accountability: no elected boards, selective admissions, and, for special-needs students, often a waiver of the legal rights and services public schools must provide. [P1.4] counts "values fit" as a benefit while declining to count rights lost at the door.
[C1.7] [P1.5] frames this as exit versus a locked door. But the actual alternative to expansion is not compulsory assignment forever—it is the system educating ninety percent of children, which this proposition asks to shrink on purpose, on unproven grounds, at their expense.

[P2.5] Public schools are not exempt from sorting merely because they are public.
[P2.6] And “public accountability” is not a reason to deny choice; it is a design requirement. Voucher schools can be required to meet transparency, testing, safety, and civil-rights conditions. CON’s alternative is to keep the door locked because some private schools might be bad actors.
