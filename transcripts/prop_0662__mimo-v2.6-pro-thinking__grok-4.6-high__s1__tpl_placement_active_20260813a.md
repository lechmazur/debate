[P1.1] A delivery driver who loses a week of shifts to a bad knee and a warehouse picker who loses a job to a plant closure have both lost earnings. Yet in most social-insurance systems, only the second worker has a documented attachment to the labor market. That gap is not a feature of the work itself. It is a failure of record-keeping and legal classification, and it is fixable.
[P1.2] My case rests on one claim: standardized earnings records make platform work countable, and anything countable should count. Unemployment insurance and social-insurance eligibility were designed around the assumption that a worker has one employer who files one wage report. Platform work breaks that assumption — many clients, fragmented payments, irregular hours — but it does not break the underlying fact of contribution. When a worker earns income and payroll-style contributions are collected on that income, the state is holding money and then telling the worker their record is empty.
[P1.3] That is indefensible.
[P1.4] The mechanism is straightforward. Platforms already process payments at scale and hold transaction-level data on what each worker earned and when. A standardized earnings record — the same schema used for conventional wage reporting, submitted periodically, reconciled against tax filings — converts that data into a portable contribution history. This is an administrative problem with administrative solutions: shared data standards, mandatory platform filing, periodic reconciliation, and pro-rated eligibility formulas for intermittent work.
[P1.5] Countries already adapt contribution thresholds for seasonal and part-time workers; there is no reason platform hours require a different universe of policy.

[C1.1] The proposition treats missing paperwork as if it were missing work. It is not. Unemployment insurance and social-insurance eligibility exist to cover a specific risk: involuntary loss of a job from an identifiable employer, under conditions the state can verify. Platform work is not that risk in a different file format.
[C1.2] It is self-scheduled, multi-client, and exit-at-will. Recording its payments as wages does not convert a logged-off afternoon into a layoff.
[C1.3] PRO’s core move is that anything countable should count. That is the mistake. Royalties, one-off invoices, and rental income are countable too. Eligibility is not a receipt.
[C1.4] It is a test of attachment: who owed the labor, whether the worker was separated, and whether the next period of work is something the state can require. A driver who skips Friday because surge pay is thin has not suffered a plant closure. A standardized schema cannot tell those cases apart, because the difference is not in the ledger.
[C1.5] Force the records anyway and three harms follow. First, moral hazard. Platforms do not discharge workers the way a warehouse does; they change prices and rankings. Treat a thin week as unemployment and claims arrive whenever logging in is less attractive, not when work disappears.
[C1.6] Work-search rules then become theater: the claimant can still open the app.
[C1.7] Second, cost shifting. Mandated filings and pro-rated credits require contributions. Those costs show up in fees, fares, and fewer gigs. The precarious workers this proposal claims to rescue then face a thinner market, not a thicker safety net.
[C1.8] Third, dilution. Insurance is a pool. Intermittent multi-platform earnings that mint eligibility without an employer-style contribution base and a real separation test pull money toward less insurable risk. Traditional workers then finance a benefit redesigned around people who never lost an employer.
[C1.9] Seasonal and part-time rules still rest on employers, hours, and separations. Platform work often has none of those. The coverage gap is real. This cure mismeasures the disease.
