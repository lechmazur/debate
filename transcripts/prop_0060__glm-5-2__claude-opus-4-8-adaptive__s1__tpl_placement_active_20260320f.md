[P1.1] The case for remote work isn't about comfort. It's about removing the structural inefficiencies baked into office attendance itself.
[P1.2] Start with the commute. The average knowledge worker spends roughly an hour per day—often more—transiting to and from an office. That is dead time. It produces nothing for the employer, nothing for the worker, and nothing for the client.
[P1.3] When someone works from home, that hour doesn't vanish into a train car; it gets redirected into actual work, additional sleep, or exercise that makes the workday sharper. The commute is a productivity tax, and remote work eliminates it.
[P1.4] Then there's the office itself. Open floor plans, drive-by questions, overhearing half a dozen conversations, the ambient pressure to look busy—these are not features of collaboration. They are friction. Knowledge work requires sustained focus.
[P1.5] Writing, coding, analyzing, drafting—none of these happen well in fifteen-minute fragments between interruptions. The home environment lets workers protect the blocks of uninterrupted time that deep work demands. That's not preference; it's cognitive architecture.
[P1.6] Collaboration doesn't disappear remotely. It shifts to channels where it can be documented, searched, and referenced later. A Slack thread or a shared document preserves institutional memory in ways a hallway chat never does. The objection that offices are necessary for spontaneity assumes spontaneity is the primary engine of output.
[P1.7] For most knowledge workers, it isn't. The engine is focused execution.
[P1.8] Remote work also forces a healthier management culture. When you can't evaluate employees by whether they're at their desk, you evaluate them by what they actually produce. That shift—from presence to output—is itself a productivity gain. It strips away performative busyness and rewards results.
[P1.9] The proposition says "on net" and "for many." It doesn't claim remote work is universally perfect. It claims that for a large class of knowledge workers, removing commute friction, reducing interruption cost, and shifting evaluation toward output produces more real work. [P1.10] The office was the default. The burden is on that default to justify itself—and it can't.
