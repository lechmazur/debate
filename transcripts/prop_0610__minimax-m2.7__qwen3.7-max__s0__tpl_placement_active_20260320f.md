[P1.1] The European Union should impose a carbon border adjustment on imported industrial goods, and it should do so even when trading partners retaliate.
[P1.2] Here is why this matters: Without a carbon border adjustment, EU industries face real carbon costs while their competitors abroad do not. This creates two catastrophic outcomes. First, EU factories that invest in clean technology are systematically undercut by imports made with cheaper, dirtier methods.
[P1.3] Second, European industries facing carbon pricing simply relocate to countries with lax environmental rules—a phenomenon called carbon leakage—leaving the planet no better off and European workers considerably worse.
[P1.4] A carbon border adjustment solves both problems. It levels the playing field by ensuring that imported steel, cement, aluminum, and chemicals bear a carbon cost comparable to what EU producers pay. This protects the green investments European industries have already made and removes the perverse incentive to relocate. It also creates a powerful diplomatic lever: countries that want duty-free access to the European market now have a financial reason to price their own carbon.
[P1.5] The proposition's critical clause—"even if major trading partners retaliate"—is not a bug but the point. Yes, there will be friction. China, India, and the United States may impose countermeasures on European exports. But consider the counterfactual.
[P1.6] The EU produces roughly 8 percent of global emissions. Without a border adjustment, that remaining 92 percent faces no external pressure to decarbonize. The EU's internal climate policies alone cannot stabilize the atmosphere. Either the EU uses its economic weight to export climate ambition, or it accepts irrelevance.
[P1.7] Retaliation is not an existential threat to the European economy. The EU market of 450 million consumers is enormous, and trading partners depend on access to it far more than Europeans depend on any single export market. The cost of retaliation is manageable trade disruption. The cost of inaction is irreversible climate damage.
[P1.8] Imposing this adjustment is not reckless. It is the only serious path forward.
