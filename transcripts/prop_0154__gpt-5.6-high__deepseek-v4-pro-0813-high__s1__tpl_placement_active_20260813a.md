[P1.1] Foundation-model training creates a classic mass-licensing problem: a useful model may learn from millions of books, articles, photographs, and illustrations, while no developer can realistically locate and negotiate with every owner. Yet declaring all training free places the economic burden on the people whose work supplies the material. Copyright should reject that false choice. A compulsory license converts unavoidable large-scale use into lawful use with compensation.
[P1.2] The scheme should be straightforward: developers report covered training datasets, submit to audits, and pay standardized, periodically reviewed rates into collecting mechanisms that distribute revenue to identified rightsholders. Rates can distinguish commercial from nonprofit use and account for the amount and type of material used. Small or experimental projects can receive thresholds or reduced rates. The license governs ingestion for training, not infringing outputs; ordinary copyright remedies still apply when a model reproduces protected expression.
[P1.3] This is superior to permission-by-permission licensing. Individual vetoes let a few owners block socially valuable models and favor giant firms able to assemble exclusive catalogs. Negotiating millions of contracts wastes resources without producing another poem, image, or safer model. A common license gives startups the same legal on-ramp as incumbents, makes costs predictable, and allows competition on model quality rather than legal war chests.
[P1.4] Rightsholders, including freelancers too weak to bargain alone, gain payment they are unlikely to extract through scattered lawsuits.
[P1.5] No royalty system measures contribution perfectly, but imperfection is not a defense of uncompensated appropriation or paralyzing transaction costs. Copyright already uses standardized and collective licensing where individualized bargaining fails. The relevant comparison is institutional: free training externalizes costs; universal consent creates holdouts and concentration; endless litigation supplies neither certainty nor broad payment. Compulsory licensing uniquely preserves access, competition, and innovation while recognizing that creative labor is not free raw material.
[P1.6] When society wants both foundation models and a sustainable culture that feeds them, payment without veto is the durable bargain.

[C1.1] The proposal’s “payment without veto” is not a bargain; it is a forced sale. Compulsory licensing changes copyright from an exclusive right into a liability rule: once a developer pays the posted price, it can ingest the work regardless of the creator’s wishes. For many rightsholders the objection is not money. A photographer may not want personal photographs in a generative model; a novelist may oppose training systems that later imitate her voice; an artist may have privacy or moral objections.
[C1.2] A license that cannot be refused is not a licensing scheme—it is expropriation dressed in a payment mechanism.
[C1.3] The promised compensation is largely symbolic. Training value emerges from aggregation, filtering, and model design, not from any identifiable contribution. No royalty formula can say why one image among billions mattered more than another. Distribution will favor collectives, stock platforms, and large publishers that maintain metadata, while independent writers and visual artists—the very people the proposal claims to help—will receive tiny or no payments after administrative, audit, and legal costs.
[C1.4] The scheme will not sustain creative labor; it will legitimize low-cost appropriation.
[C1.5] The regime is not the pro-startup alternative it is presented as. Compulsory licensing requires developers to report datasets, submit to audits, track inputs across versions, and resolve disputes over rates. Those costs are easiest for large model firms to absorb and manipulate through rate-setting influence. Smaller entrants will face the same bureaucracy without the bargaining power.
[C1.6] Worse, the proposal legalizes the wrong act. It gives broad immunity for training while leaving the most harmful consequences—outputs that mimic style, flood markets, and replace commissions—to ordinary infringement litigation. Creators would be paid a flat fee to help build the systems eroding their future income. That is a poor trade and should not be made by legal compulsion.
[C1.7] If training is to be lawful, the better path is enforcement of existing rights, voluntary collective licensing where rightsholders choose to participate, and genuine opt-in or opt-out control. Copyright should not conscript creative work into model training.

[P2.1] Calling compulsory licensing “expropriation” simply assumes that copyright must always confer an absolute veto. It does not. Copyright is a statutory economic right with limits designed to reconcile creation with public access. Here, the limitation is especially justified because individual permission at foundation-model scale is functionally impossible, while uncompensated use is unfair.
[P2.2] Payment without veto is not a perfect bargain for every owner; it is the workable legal settlement between two otherwise incompatible absolutes.
[P2.3] The photographer’s privacy and the novelist’s moral objection do not rescue CON. Privacy, confidentiality, and unlawful reproduction can remain separately prohibited. Copyright should not become a generalized power to suppress technologies because an owner dislikes their downstream uses. Nor does an author ordinarily own an abstract “voice” or an artist an entire style.
[P2.4] The relevant protected interest is use of copyrighted expression, and the license pays for precisely that use.
[P2.5] CON says payments may be small because no single work’s contribution can be measured. But that proves aggregation, not worthlessness. Tiny individual contributions can collectively create enormous value; the answer is standardized allocation, claims processes, and auditable records—not letting the aggregator retain all value. Voluntary collectives cannot solve the problem because incomplete participation recreates the same holdouts, orphan works, and legal uncertainty.
[P2.6] The startup objection reverses the comparison. Dataset reporting and a predictable rate are burdens, but millions of negotiations and unpredictable lawsuits are vastly larger burdens. Incumbents can buy exclusive catalogs and sustain litigation; startups cannot. Thresholds and reduced rates further preserve experimentation.
[P2.7] Finally, separating training from outputs is a strength, not a loophole. A training license should legalize training, not authorize infringing reproductions. If outputs copy protected expression, ordinary remedies remain. If they merely compete, copyright has never guaranteed creators immunity from new competition.
[P2.8] CON offers only unstable extremes: universal permission that cannot scale, or litigation that pays few creators. A compulsory license uniquely turns mass use into lawful access, broad compensation, and contestable innovation.

[P4.1] A1: A non-arbitrary rate need not pretend to calculate each work’s causal contribution. It can price the legally covered act using observable factors: the number and type of works ingested, commercial scale and purpose, repeated uses, and market conditions for comparable licensed corpora. A public, periodically reviewed schedule, with creator and developer participation and judicial review, is more disciplined than either a zero price imposed by developers or prices driven by catalog owners’ holdout power. “Actual compensation” means a real charge for actual ingestion, not metaphysical precision about which token changed which parameter.
[P4.2] A2: Developers—not isolated creators—should bear the recordkeeping burden. Dataset manifests, audits, and claims processes let owners establish inclusion without reverse-engineering a model. Royalties for unresolved works can be escrowed for later claims, with resources devoted to identifying owners; they should not revert to the developer. We should not count an unclaimed share as completed individual compensation.
[P4.3] But the scheme creates an enforceable entitlement and evidence trail where CON creates neither. Identifiable owners receive payment, and previously unknown owners can claim it later. Failure to reach literally everyone does not justify paying no one.
[P4.4] These answers reveal CON’s impossible standard. Individual negotiation cannot measure causal contribution either: its price reflects bargaining power, exclusivity, and the threat of litigation. It is less accurate and vastly less accessible, especially for freelancers. Standardization therefore does not create the valuation problem; it manages a problem every licensing system already faces while preventing dominant catalog owners from capturing all returns.
[P4.5] That is the decisive comparison. CON protects a formal veto that millions cannot practically exercise, blocks lawful access for startups, and leaves compensation dependent on metadata, bargaining leverage, or lawsuits anyway. A compulsory license instead makes developers disclose mass use, pay for it, and preserve output liability where protected expression is copied. Imperfect allocation with audits, escrow, and real payments is plainly superior to CON’s model of perfect consent in theory and exclusion or nonpayment in practice.
