[C1.1] Mandating system-level ad and tracker blocking by law is a paternalistic overreach that substitutes bureaucratic control for user agency and market-driven innovation.
[C1.2] First, forcing a specific technical architecture creates a dangerous security monoculture. If the mandated blocking mechanism contains a vulnerability, it becomes a universal attack vector affecting every user. We cannot hard-code security solutions into legislation; we must allow the competitive market to innovate on privacy tools. A legal mandate freezes technology in place, guaranteeing that we will be stuck with today’s "best" idea while attackers find new ways around it.
[C1.3] Second, the "opt-out" default is a deceptive framing. Defaults are destiny. Forcing blocking by default doesn't just "protect" users; it systematically defunds the independent web. Small publishers and creators rely on ad revenue to provide free content.
[C1.4] If the vast majority of users never opt back in—as behavioral data on default settings suggests—we are effectively creating a two-tier internet: one for the wealthy who can afford subscriptions, and one dominated by low-quality content or malware because legitimate revenue streams were legally strangled.
[C1.5] Third, "robust blocking" is a technical chimera that invites widespread breakage and censorship. System-level filters are notoriously aggressive; they frequently break site functionality, block essential scripts, and often misidentify legitimate services as trackers. If the law requires this, we are legislating a broken web. Furthermore, whoever controls the "blocklist" controls the internet.
[C1.6] Entrusting that power to a government-mandated standard or a private entity is an open invitation to censorship and abuse.
[C1.7] Finally, the proposition ignores the basic economic contract of the web. Users routinely trade data for services they value, from free email to real-time maps. A legal mandate to block that exchange doesn't enhance privacy; it destroys the economic engine of the open web, ultimately harming the very users it claims to protect.
