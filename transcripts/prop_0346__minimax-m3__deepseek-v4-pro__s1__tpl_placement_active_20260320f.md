[P1.1] The case for evaluating remote and hybrid workers on outputs, not presence, is really a case about what we actually want from work.
[P1.2] We want results: problems solved, code shipped, customers served, reports delivered, deals closed. That is the entire purpose of employment. Activity monitoring pretends we want something else - that we want to see a body in a chair, a cursor moving, a status indicator flipping green. It substitutes the appearance of work for the substance of work, and then charges everyone for the friction.
[P1.3] Consider two engineers on the same team. One is on camera, status always green. The other walks the dog midday, writes code in the afternoon, ships a clean module by Thursday. Output-based evaluation rewards the second.
[P1.4] Activity-based evaluation rewards the first - and may even penalize the second, because the monitoring system cannot see outcomes. It measures theatre.
[P1.5] Three reasons this matters beyond individual cases.
[P1.6] First, monitoring is gameable. If presence is the metric, presence is what you get - including from people wiggling a mouse while doing something else. The metric does not align with what we actually care about, so it fails on its own terms.
[P1.7] Second, monitoring carries real costs output evaluation avoids: privacy concerns, legal exposure under emerging workplace surveillance rules, and quiet damage to morale and retention. People who feel watched tend to do the minimum the watcher can detect, not the maximum they could do.
[P1.8] Third, knowledge work is intrinsically hard to monitor. A writer staring at a paragraph is thinking. A designer stepping away is solving. A researcher on a walk is debugging.
[P1.9] Presence signals destroy the conditions under which this work happens.
[P1.10] Output-based evaluation is not anti-management. It is the most honest management. Define what good looks like, measure it, and let people figure out how to deliver it. Activity monitoring defines a behavior and hopes it produces the outcome.
[P1.11] That is backwards.

In collaborative, creative, and long-cycle work, the difference between a good quarter and a bad one rarely lives inside a single person’s “output packet.” It lives in how people discuss a design, spot a risk, unblock a junior colleague, or choose not to ship something broken. None of that shows up in a dashboard of finished deliverables.
[C1.2] When you make output the main lens, you change behaviour. People ship what is countable, not what is valuable. A product manager closes tickets quickly instead of resolving the underlying friction. An engineer pushes commits that look productive but leave technical debt for later.
[C1.3] A support agent resolves chats fast by deflecting rather than solving. You reward volume, surface finish, and premature completion while punishing the quiet, connective work that prevents disasters. That is not fairness; it is incentive corruption wearing a mask of objectivity.
[C1.4] Output measurement also burdens managers unequally. Defining “what good looks like” for a single salesperson is trivial; for a cross-functional pod navigating shifting priorities, it veers into guesswork. Managers end up either picking crude proxies – lines of code, words written, calls taken – or grading holistically under the cover of “outputs.” At that point the system ceases to be output-based in any honest sense and becomes ad-hoc judgment dressed in metrics. When you strip all that away and stare only at the finish line, you lose early warning of burnout, over-reliance on one person, or drift from shared priorities.

[C2.3] By calling everything an output, PRO makes evaluation indistinguishable from post-hoc justification. [C2.4] Dismissing presence as “theatre” because a green dot can be faked misses the broader picture. They offer early warning of disengagement or burnout long before a missed deadline, a lead time output evaluation cannot match. The PRO alternative of “conversation, peer review, deliverable quality” either piggybacks on those same presence signals (you notice who to review by seeing who interacts) or is a lagging indicator.
[C2.5] Finally, the manager-burden point is not a concession; it is a trap. Defining anticipatory output metrics for every collaborative contingency is not hard but impossible. Faced with that impossibility, managers default to exactly the crude metrics—tickets closed, lines written—PRO concedes are bad. Activity awareness, handled humanely, lets judgment rest on a richer, real-time picture of how work actually happens, avoiding both the theatre of raw surveillance and the distortion of forced output counting.
