[P1.1] When someone uploads DNA to a genealogy service, they consent to find relatives and build a family tree. They do not consent to having their entire genetic network searched by police without a warrant, without individualized suspicion, and without their knowledge.
[P1.2] That distinction is the heart of this debate. The Golden State Killer case is the celebrated proof of concept. Look closely at how it actually worked: one distant relative uploaded DNA, and through that single act, an entire family tree became accessible to law enforcement. His second cousin never consented.
[P1.3] His aunt never consented. None of the millions of Americans whose genetic data flows through these databases agreed to become permanent genetic informants against their own families.
[P1.4] This is not a privacy problem for the uploader alone. It is structural. Every search implicates dozens, sometimes hundreds, of non-consenting third parties whose DNA was never submitted to anyone. A case-by-case warrant framework cannot fix this, because the database itself is the search.
[P1.5] Investigators are not searching one home with probable cause; they are fishing across millions of family trees simultaneously, identifying genetic cousins and triangulating through innocent relatives to reach a suspect.
[P1.6] Only a categorical prohibition protects those non-consenting relatives from involuntary inclusion in criminal investigations. Anything less leaves the door open for the practice to expand by precedent and by administrative discretion.
[P1.7] The chilling effects compound the case. If genetic testing means becoming a permanent investigative resource for law enforcement, participation drops. Medical research loses subjects. Adopted people lose reunification tools.
[P1.8] Victims who submitted kits for identification become prosecutorial resources they never agreed to.
[P1.9] Finally, mission creep is not hypothetical. Today: cold-case serial predators. Tomorrow: any violent crime. A decade out: identifying political protesters, undocumented immigrants, anyone an agency wants to find.
[P1.10] Once the legal infrastructure normalizes genetic surveillance, expanding it is trivial.
[P1.11] Solving cold cases matters. So does the constitutional protection against general searches. The proposition protects both.
