[P1.3] Ordinary parenting reflects this: we don't let ten-year-olds sign contracts, vote, or consent to medical treatment, because we know their judgment is incomplete. A legal system that calls the same child fully responsible the moment they commit a crime isn't applying a principle. It's applying a double standard driven by anger.
[P1.4] Second, outcomes. Early contact with courts and custody tends to deepen offending rather than end it. A child labelled a criminal is pulled out of school, placed among older offenders, and saddled with a record that follows them into adulthood. The children who end up before courts at ten are overwhelmingly the abused, neglected, excluded, and disabled.
[P1.5] Prosecution treats the symptom while feeding the cause. If CON's goal is fewer future victims, criminalising young children works against it.
[P1.6] Third, and this is where CON's case will struggle: raising the age does not mean doing nothing. Welfare and child-protection systems can still intervene forcefully, through family support, therapeutic placements, and secure accommodation where a child is genuinely dangerous. Scotland moved its minimum age from eight to twelve, and international child-rights bodies have urged at least fourteen. Neither shift abandons public safety.
[P1.7] They move serious cases into systems designed to change children rather than merely sentence them.
[P1.8] So the burden on CON is precise. They must show what a criminal conviction gives a ten-year-old's victims, or society, that a robust welfare response cannot, and show it is worth the damage a conviction does. I don't think they can.
