[P1.1] The eighteen-year-old voting age is not a principle; it is an accident of history, inherited mostly from military draft ages. So the real question is simple: what actually justifies excluding a citizen from the franchise? The only defensible answers are lack of capacity or lack of stake. Sixteen-year-olds fail neither test.
[P1.2] Start with capacity. Voting is what psychologists call a "cold cognition" task — unhurried, deliberative, done privately with time to reflect. The reasoning skills that kind of decision requires are essentially mature by mid-adolescence. The brain systems that keep developing into the twenties govern impulse control in hot, peer-pressured, split-second situations — relevant to drag racing, irrelevant to a ballot booth.
[P1.3] If we disqualified people for still-developing impulse control, we would have to disenfranchise twenty-three-year-olds too. Nobody proposes that, because the standard is capacity to reason about public questions, and sixteen-year-olds meet it.
[P1.4] Now stake. Sixteen-year-olds work legally, pay taxes on their wages, are subject to criminal law, and in many countries can drive, marry, or leave school. More importantly, they hold the longest stake in the decisions elections settle: climate policy, public debt, pension design, education. An electorate that skews ever older, as ours do, systematically discounts the future.
[P1.5] Enfranchising sixteen-year-olds is a modest structural correction to that bias.
[P1.6] Finally, the practical payoff: habit. Voting is habit-forming, and the first vote matters most. At sixteen, most people live at home, attend school, and are embedded in institutions that can support that first vote. At eighteen, they are mid-move — new city, new job, unregistered.
[P1.7] Where the change has been tried, in Austria and in Scotland's referendum, sixteen-year-olds turned out at healthy rates and voted no less competently than adults, by the ordinary measures of matching choices to stated preferences.
[P1.8] Capable citizens with the largest stake in the outcome should not be silenced by an arbitrary line. Lower it.

At eighteen you can sign a binding contract, be sued on it, sit on a jury, serve in the army, be tried and sentenced as an adult, and be told by your parents to leave the house. The franchise belongs in that bundle because voting is the one power that binds everyone else.
[C1.2] The people who choose the lawmakers should be the people the law holds fully to account.
[C1.3] Notice what the pro case never does: it never names a principle that stops at sixteen. If "cold cognition" is mature by mid-adolescence, it is mature at fifteen. If having "the longest stake" earns a ballot, twelve-year-olds have a longer one still. Every argument offered against eighteen works just as well against sixteen.
[C1.4] So the real choice is between one line that tracks a genuine legal boundary and another line that tracks nothing at all.
[C1.5] Then there is independence. A sixteen-year-old is, by law, a dependent minor. Their parents control their housing, their money, often their phone. They sit in compulsory schooling under adults who grade them.
[C1.6] Picture a family where the father makes clear how the household votes, or a civics teacher who makes clear which answer is the enlightened one. We protect the secret ballot precisely so that no one votes under the eye of someone with power over them. Sixteen-year-olds live under exactly that eye.
[C1.7] Finally, the motion says all elections. The evidence is far thinner than that. Austria's experience is mixed on whether early turnout persists, and Scotland's referendum was a once-in-a-generation event whose enthusiasm has not straightforwardly carried into ordinary elections. A high-salience referendum tells us little about a routine council election.
[C1.8] Keep the vote where full adult responsibility begins. That line is coherent, protects voter independence, and asks no one to gamble the franchise on an unfinished experiment.
