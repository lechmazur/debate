[P1.1] The question Beijing asks before launching an invasion is not "how much will this cost our economy?" but "can we physically get an army across the Taiwan Strait?" That is deterrence by denial, and it is the most reliable form of deterrence we know.
[P1.2] Economic deterrence alone fails on three counts. First, credibility: sanctions threats against a $17 trillion economy deeply entangled with our own are threats we visibly hesitate to execute, and Beijing knows it. [P1.3] Putin invaded anyway, adapted, and is still fighting. If Xi believes he can seize Taiwan quickly and present a fait accompli, delayed economic costs become a price he pays for a historic prize, not a reason to abstain.
[P1.4] Now consider the escalation objection preemptively: anti-ship and air defense systems are inherently defensive. They cannot strike Shanghai; they can only sink invasion barges and down attacking aircraft. That makes them uniquely stabilizing—they raise the cost of aggression without threatening the Chinese homeland, avoiding the security-spiral dynamics that genuinely offensive deployments might trigger.
[P1.5] The comparison, then, is stark: denial capabilities make the invasion itself look like failure; economic threats merely make success expensive. A rational aggressor abandons plans that fail. He rarely abandons plans that merely cost. That asymmetry decides this debate.
