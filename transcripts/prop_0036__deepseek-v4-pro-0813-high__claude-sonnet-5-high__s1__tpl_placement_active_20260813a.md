[P1.1] Consumer wearables are not neutral fitness trackers; they are continuous surveillance devices worn on the body. They record sleep, heart rhythm, movement, and sometimes location. From those streams, insurers can infer stress, depression risk, medication disruption, pregnancy-related changes, or cognitive decline. Health and life coverage decisions should not be built on a hidden behavioral score derived from data originally collected for step challenges.
[P1.2] Start with consent. The proposition does not ban wearable use; it requires opt-in under strict limits. That baseline is necessary because without it, “opt-in” becomes a pricing trap. An insurer offers a lower premium for sharing data; the customer who refuses pays the full rate.
[P1.3] Over time, the discounted rate becomes the real rate, and refusal is penalized. That is not voluntary participation; it is coerced surveillance by installment. Strict limits must mean unbundled pricing, easy revocation, data minimization, and no denial of coverage for refusal.
[P1.4] Then consider discrimination. Wearable benchmarks favor people with time to exercise, flexible schedules, newer devices, and no disability. Shift workers, chronic pain patients, parents, older adults, and low-income people often cannot produce the same metrics. If those signals enter pricing, risk pools stop sharing broadly and start ranking people by how closely their bodies fit an algorithmic ideal.
[P1.5] Reliability matters too. A wrist sensor is not a medical instrument. Movement data can be thrown off by illness, device error, shared devices, or physically demanding jobs. Letting those imperfect signals into underwriting creates false precision with real financial consequences.
[P1.6] Opt-in with strict limits is the middle path: consumers who genuinely want to share may do so, but nobody is defaulted into a health surveillance economy. The alternative is insurance that watches you before it covers you.
