[P1.1] A book can sit neglected in a basement for a century and still be read. Software cannot. It needs a runtime, a license check, a server someone is still paying for. That asymmetry decides this motion: we now make culture that dies the moment a company stops caring, and legal deposit is the only instrument that works on the timescale of centuries rather than quarterly earnings.
[P1.2] We already ran this experiment. Most silent film is gone — not because anyone judged it worthless, but because no one was obliged to keep it. Deposit laws closed that gap for print and cinema. Software has reopened it, and the losses are not hypothetical: delisted games, shuttered online worlds, a decade of Flash-era web art that survived only because volunteers raced a shutdown notice.
[P1.3] Voluntary preservation also fails in a specific, fatal way. Enthusiasts can save what they can reach: the installer, the exported video, the screenshot. They cannot reach the server code, the build toolchain, the asset pipeline. So what survives is often the corpse rather than the work — a game you can download but never play.
[P1.4] Only compulsion gets the unpublished components out of a company's hands before the company dissolves.
[P1.5] Deposit is not publication. National libraries already hold embargoed, restricted and rights-encumbered material under controlled access; that machinery transfers directly. Every piracy or trade-secret worry is an argument about access rules, not about whether the artifact enters the archive at all.
[P1.6] The burden is clear: name a mechanism that reliably delivers software's hidden parts, at national scale, without obligation. Enthusiasm has neither the reach nor the legal standing. If no such mechanism exists, the alternative to this mandate is not freedom — it is losing the native art form of our century and calling the scraps an archive.

[C1.1] A book is finished when it is printed. Software is not; it is patched, re-versioned, and tethered to servers and accounts. The motion assumes software is an artifact that can be deposited like a codex. It is not — it is a performance that happens on hardware the archive will never possess.
[C1.2] Deposit laws for print and film succeeded because print and film are stable. Software's culture lives in the running system: the multiplayer world, the live community, the updated app. A vault of installers preserves the corpse, exactly what my opponent attributes only to volunteers [P1.3], but this time by statute.
[C1.3] Take the motion's own best example. Flash-era web art survived because volunteers built emulators — Ruffle and similar projects — not because any deposit law reached Adobe's source code. That is the decisive comparison: preservation was won by engineering, not accession. A mandate delivers bits; only emulation, documentation, and migration deliver works.
[C1.4] Archives that cannot run a 1995 CD in 2050 will not run a 2025 always-online game either.
[C1.5] The mandate also fails on its own terms. Who adjudicates "culturally significant" among millions of apps, daily patches, and endless versions? Deposit creates a bureaucratic gate with censorship risk, version confusion, and no natural edition to file. Faced with compulsion, firms will deposit encrypted builds and sanitized dumps — legally complete, curatorially worthless.
[C1.6] Meanwhile the demand for source, engines, and pipelines [P1.3] forces disclosure of trade secrets that controlled access cannot protect against foreign competitors or leaks, chilling the very creation the motion wants to save.
[C1.7] My opponent asks for a mechanism [P1.6]. Museums acquire, developers open-source, copyright expires, emulation communities win races against shutdowns. Fund preservation capacity, reform copyright, and let those work. Compulsion buys the form of an archive without the substance of preservation.
