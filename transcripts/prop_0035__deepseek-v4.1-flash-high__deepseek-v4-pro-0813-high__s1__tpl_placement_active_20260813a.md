[P1.1] Indoor air is the environment we are most forced to share. A child in a crowded classroom, a cashier in a store, an office worker in a sealed tower: none can inspect the ventilation, negotiate a filtration upgrade, or step outside every time CO2 climbs. When the hazard is invisible and the occupants are captive, voluntary goodwill is not a policy. It is a hope.
[P1.2] Governments already set enforceable standards for shared spaces: fire exits, occupancy limits, sanitation, water safety, lead paint, food handling. Indoor air is the glaring exception. That exception has a health cost and a productivity cost. Poor ventilation concentrates respiratory viruses, allergens, mold spores, and CO2.
[P1.3] Better ventilation and filtration reduce transmission and symptoms and improve concentration and attendance. The mechanism is simple: dilute and clean the air people breathe.
[P1.4] Enforceable standards need not be one-size-fits-all. They can set performance targets—air changes, filtration level, CO2 thresholds—with monitoring, maintenance, transparency, and phased deadlines. That design handles diverse buildings while ensuring nobody can quietly warehouse people in stagnant air. It also corrects a market failure: building owners and employers capture the savings from skimping on ventilation, while occupants bear the health costs.
[P1.5] Children and low-wage workers have the least power to complain.
[P1.6] The cost objection fails as a reason to prefer no standard. Retrofits, filtration, and monitoring are often modest compared with absenteeism, outbreaks, and chronic illness; governments can pair mandates with school funding and compliance timelines. The real choice is not between cost and no cost. It is between visible, accountable investment in breathable buildings and hidden costs dumped on the people inside them.
[P1.7] If indoor air were food or water, we would not accept “voluntary best efforts” in public buildings. Air deserves the same enforceable floor because it is shared, involuntary, and measurable. Set the standard, monitor it, enforce it—and stop treating ventilation as a private virtue rather than public infrastructure.

[C1.1] Indoor air quality matters, but this motion is not a referendum on ventilation; it is a plan to impose uniform, legally enforceable standards on millions of heterogeneous buildings. That remedies a real problem with a blunt instrument.
[C1.2] A single numeric requirement cannot fit a century-old brick school, a modern sealed office, a humid coastal clinic, and a cold-climate community hall. Standards that are safe for one may be unattainable or wasteful for another. Enforceable mandates turn that mismatch into fines, closure orders, and litigation, not cleaner air.
[C1.3] Cost is the issue because enforceability makes it unavoidable. Retrofitting ventilation in older public buildings is often a major capital project, not a filter purchase. Under an enforceable rule, a district may have to choose between meeting a CO2 threshold and retaining staff, repairing roofs, or keeping small libraries open. Workplaces will pass compliance costs through to tenants and workers.
[C1.4] The broad phrase “other public buildings” sweeps in volunteer-run halls and small community facilities least able to bear the burden.
[C1.5] The measurement problem magnifies this. CO2 is a proxy, not a pathogen count. Occupancy swings, outdoor pollution, humidity, and building use all change what “acceptable” air means. A monitor can show compliance while air remains stale, or show a violation during an ordinary crowded event.
[C1.6] The likely result is box-ticking, sensor placement games, and adversarial inspections rather than genuinely better air.
[C1.7] The better policy is differentiated: mandatory ventilation requirements in new construction, clear public reporting, help for high-occupancy settings, and targeted enforcement where conditions are demonstrably dangerous. Existing health, safety, and building codes already reach gross hazards. A blanket new enforcement regime adds legal and financial burden without solving the underlying diversity.
[C1.8] The test is not whether clean air is good. It is whether this enforceable standard improves real buildings at acceptable cost. For the many vulnerable, underfunded sites this motion claims to protect, it would punish scarcity instead of fixing air.
