[P1.1] Emergency powers are a loan against liberty, not a gift. The state may need extraordinary tools—curfews, requisitions, surveillance, spending shifts—but the moment those tools become ordinary, democracy has already lost ground. The proposition requires only this: when an executive claims broad emergency powers, the claim must return to the people’s representatives after a short fixed period for a hearing and a renewal vote.
[P1.2] That rule accepts reality. It does not stop the executive from acting first. In a genuine crisis—terror attack, epidemic, invasion, financial panic—the government can move in hours. But it denies the dangerous fiction that an emergency can last indefinitely without renewed consent.
[P1.3] History offers the warning: emergency regimes tend to outlive the emergencies that created them. Pandemic restrictions, anti-terror decrees, economic emergency orders, and security proclamations all create tempting shortcuts around legislatures, courts, and budgets. Once the executive tastes unilateral rule, the incentive is to extend it, broaden it, or relabel the crisis.
[P1.4] Automatic sunset hearings solve this without demanding impossible legislative foresight. They create a forcing mechanism. If the emergency continues, the executive explains why, the legislature scrutinizes the evidence, and renewal can be granted—narrowly, conditionally, and temporarily. If the emergency has faded, the powers lapse.
[P1.5] If the emergency has changed, the mandate can be revised. Each renewal vote makes accountability visible: citizens know who voted to keep extraordinary authority alive.
[P1.6] The objection about speed fails because the proposal targets continuation, not initiation. The objection about secrecy can be met with closed hearings and classified briefings. The real choice is between two risks: occasionally forcing a legislature to reauthorize emergency action, or allowing executives to convert crisis into permanent prerogative. Democracies should choose the risk that preserves self-government.
[P1.7] Sunset hearings do not weaken emergency power; they keep it emergency.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds modest, but it misdiagnoses the problem. The danger in emergencies is not that executives temporarily hold power — it is that the response itself can be gutted at the worst possible moment by a legislature running on a calendar instead of on conditions.
[C1.2] Sunset renewal votes create a cliff edge. An epidemic does not respect a 30-day timetable. A terror threat does not pause for a committee hearing. Real emergencies move in phases: a second wave, a follow-on attack, a financial aftershock.
[C1.3] An automatic expiration forces the legislature to vote on whether to keep powers that may still be needed — and that vote becomes a target, not an accounting. Opposition parties have every incentive to block, filibuster, or condition renewal to extract concessions unrelated to the emergency itself. The result is a manufactured crisis layered on top of the real one. The executive must negotiate its authority while simultaneously fighting fires.
[C1.4] The PRO side frames every renewal vote as clean accountability. But governing majorities will rubber-stamp renewal and opposition minorities will weaponize the vote. In neither case does anyone actually assess the emergency. The hearing becomes performance; the vote becomes leverage.
[C1.5] We already see this pattern in debt-ceiling standoffs and shutdown politics — legislatures routinely take must-pass mechanisms hostage for unrelated demands. Now imagine that dynamic inside a pandemic or a security crisis, where the cost of delay is measured in lives, not just paychecks.
[C1.6] And this mechanism is not free just because the legislature can renew. The constant threat of lapse changes executive behavior. Agencies hedge, delay long-term planning, and avoid commitments that might be disrupted mid-execution. You get a more timid, more fragmented response precisely when you need coherence.
[C1.7] Existing systems already provide checks: courts review emergency measures, legislatures can override or defund them, and elections remain the ultimate correction. What the proposition adds is a rigid procedural trip wire. Democracies should not gamble their capacity to respond on a calendar-driven vote that conflates oversight with obstruction.
