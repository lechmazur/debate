[P1.1] DOGE-style efficiency is not careful auditing, but a blunt hunt for visible cuts: hiring freezes, buyouts, cancelled contracts, and the assumption that overlap equals waste. This is the bloated-firm fallacy. Public capacity is a capital stock: trained staff, legal routines, data, procurement skill, and field relationships that let the state deliver services and respond when normal channels fail.
[P1.2] The savings are narrow. Some duplication is waste; much apparent overlap is resilience: legal separation, local adaptation, or backup. Different databases may satisfy different statutes; a field office may look costly until a flood, cyber outage, or claims surge makes it the only working channel. A fast, centralized team sees the invoice, not the dependency behind it, so it cuts what is easiest to count: staff, training, maintenance, support.
[P1.3] The losses are broad and compounding. Remove experienced procurement officers and the state does not merely save salaries; it writes worse specifications, monitors vendors poorly, and invites overbilling. Remove senior claims processors, and errors, appeals, overtime, and backlogs rise. Chaotic freezes push out the most mobile employees first, taking tacit knowledge with them.
[P1.4] Agencies then rehire costly contractors, litigate removals, and rebuild capacity for years. The spreadsheet records a cut; citizens experience a queue.
[P1.5] That is the decisive comparison. Waste savings are one-off, bounded, and often shifted into hidden costs. Capacity damage lowers the quality of every service the unit performs, persists, and destroys surge capacity for the next emergency. A delayed passport, disability payment, veterans claim, or disaster grant is not offset by an abstract reduction in duplication; it is state failure.
[P1.6] Failure feeds back: more calls, incomplete paperwork, workaround contractors, appeals, and lower trust make government slower still.
[P1.7] CON must prove net gains after transition costs and lost institutional memory; gross savings cannot. Careful audits can trim waste; DOGE-style campaigns gut the capacity needed to tell waste from muscle.

In ordinary government, waste and duplication are not harmless surplus; they consume money, attention, and staff time that could be used for service delivery.
[C1.2] State capacity is not measured by headcount or office count. It is the ability to deliver benefits, enforce laws, and respond to citizens. A department can have many employees and still fail because they are trapped in redundant reporting, incompatible legacy systems, overlapping compliance rules, and failed contracts. Efficiency drives that cut duplication can increase usable capacity by redirecting scarce talent from low-value administration to front-line work.
[C1.3] Consider common cases. When agencies run separate intake portals for the same citizen need, consolidation removes confusion and repeated paperwork. When back-office HR, payroll, or procurement functions are fragmented, shared services can standardize procedures and reduce errors. When a bloated IT contract absorbs the budget, ending it can free funds for systems that actually process claims.
[C1.4] Even blunt pressure can force agencies to separate legally required work from accumulated habit.
[C1.5] PRO’s model assumes every overlap is backup and every cut is permanent loss. That ignores the damage of doing nothing: entrenched redundancy, vendor capture, obsolete rules, and budget consumed by yesterday’s programs. Waste crowds out capacity just as surely as a layoff. A citizen delayed because two offices blame each other suffers state failure too.
[C1.6] The motion asks whether the category does more damage than it saves. If DOGE-style pressure targets administrative friction, failed contracts, and duplicated processes, the net effect can be stronger service. The decisive standard is outcomes, not staffing comfort. On that standard, efficiency is often repair of state capacity, not destruction.
