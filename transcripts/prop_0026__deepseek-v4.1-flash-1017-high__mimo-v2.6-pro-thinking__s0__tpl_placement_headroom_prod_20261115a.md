[P1.1] When a competent adult and a dignity-based objection collide at the bedside, the adult should decide. That is the core of this motion. Bodily autonomy carries more moral weight because it treats the patient as the author of a life only they must live, while appeals to dignity too often become a proxy for other people’s discomfort, aesthetics, or moral preferences.
[P1.2] Consider a patient with a progressive illness who wants to stop dialysis or decline a risky operation. One person says continued treatment is “dignified”; the patient says the loss of control is unbearable. Dignity here is not a neutral fact. It is a contested evaluation imposed from outside.
[P1.3] Autonomy supplies a workable moral rule: if the patient is informed and competent, their own judgment about what makes life bearable governs. The consequences—pain, dependence, death—fall on them, not on the observer who invokes dignity.
[P1.4] This does not make autonomy absolute. It must be exercised with information and without coercion, and it does not license harming others. But those limits are internal to autonomy, not a victory for dignity. Indeed, much of what dignity properly protects—being treated as a person, not being humiliated or instrumentalized—is secured by real consent and the ability to refuse.
[P1.5] Dignity language adds little when it agrees with autonomy and becomes dangerous when it overrides it.
[P1.6] The decisive comparison is this: autonomy respects the patient’s own evaluation of their embodied life; dignity appeals often substitute someone else’s evaluation. In personal medical choices, the person who bears the body and the burden should have the stronger claim.

[C1.2] PRO says dignity is "imposed from outside," but that is a caricature. My dignity is mine — the interest in not being degraded, instrumentalized, or reduced to a body to be managed. When a starving person "freely" sells a kidney, when a patient drowning in internalized stigma requests to be treated as worthless, the form of consent is intact while the substance of human standing is being traded away. Autonomy registers the signature; dignity asks whether the person signing is being honored.
[C1.3] Notice what PRO concedes in [P1.4]: autonomy is not absolute, must be informed, uncoerced, and non-instrumentalizing. Those are not internal mechanical checks — they are dignity concepts, imported wholesale. Informed consent means treating the patient as someone owed reasons and truth. Non-coercion and non-instrumentalization mean not using people as means.
[C1.4] Strip dignity out and "autonomy" collapses into preference-satisfaction: whoever wants what, so long as they say so. PRO's own limits prove my case — dignity is doing the governing work, autonomy is the procedure dignity authorizes.
[C1.5] The scope argument is decisive too. Personal medical choices include the premature infant, the patient with advanced dementia, the person in a coma. Autonomy has nothing to say about them; their claims on us are dignity claims alone. A principle that cannot protect half the bedside cannot be the weightier one.
[C1.6] Finally, turn PRO's rhetoric around: if dignity language can be a proxy for observers' comfort, autonomy language is a proxy for institutional convenience. Honoring refusals ends expensive treatment; deferring to "choice" lets families and systems off the hook. The right to choose protects; dignity tells us what the person is worth protecting *for*.
[C1.7] Dignity is the foundation autonomy stands on. When they conflict, the foundation governs.
