A speculator can buy a transit-adjacent walk-up, model the units, assemble financing, and proceed without negotiating with the neighbors who bear the cost. [P1.5] offers protections written into rules, but the motion guarantees only speed, not those protections.
[C1.3] Transit investment raises land values before any unit is occupied, so timing matters. A “build now, worry later” rule makes displacement more likely, and the households displaced are least able to return.
[C1.4] PRO frames the choice as veto versus homes. That is false. Cities can approve larger envelopes near transit while preserving conditionality: tie the by-right bonus to anti-displacement performance—no net loss of rent-regulated units, tenant right to return, relocation paid before demolition, enforceable habitability and anti-harassment standards, and affordable set-asides. Projects that cannot meet those terms should face discretionary review, where the city can say no or negotiate.
[C1.5] The decisive question is not whether housing near transit is valuable. It is whether a city should pre-commit to allowing development when it knows some residents will be harmed and has no enforceable plan to prevent that harm. It should not. [C1.6] Uncertainty is not the enemy; unaccountable displacement is.
