[P1.1] The decisive question is who has moral authority when a person’s considered medical choice conflicts with someone else’s account of a dignified life. Bodily autonomy should carry more weight because the patient bears the pain, the risk, and the consequences. Overriding that choice requires more than declaring that another way of living—or dying—would better honor humanity.
[P1.2] Autonomy is not simply getting whatever you want. It is authority over what happens to your body, exercised through informed, voluntary decisions. That authority protects people whose convictions differ from those of their doctors, families, or governments. A competent patient may refuse another exhausting course of chemotherapy even when relatives believe dignity requires continuing the fight.
[P1.3] Conversely, a patient may choose continued treatment despite dependence that others regard as undignified. Autonomy protects both; a prescribed ideal of dignity can silence either.
[P1.4] Appeals to dignity face a fundamental dilemma. If dignity means every person has equal worth, respecting that person’s agency is a central expression of it. If dignity instead prescribes which bodily conditions or choices are worthy, it becomes a contested moral ideal. Why should that ideal authorize control over another person’s body?
[P1.5] Calling a choice degrading does not establish that coercively preventing it is respectful.
[P1.6] The comparison also favors autonomy institutionally. Capacity, understanding, and freedom from coercion can be examined through concrete questions. [P1.7] Serious risks warrant careful deliberation, not an automatic transfer of authority away from the patient.
[P1.8] Autonomy need not create an entitlement to every requested intervention, nor erase other people’s rights. But dignity alone should not defeat an informed personal choice.
