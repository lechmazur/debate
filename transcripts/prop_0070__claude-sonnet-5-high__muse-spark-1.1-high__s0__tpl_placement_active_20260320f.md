[P1.1] Retreat from climate-vulnerable land is already happening. The only question this debate settles is whether it happens through planning or through panic. That choice has enormous consequences for who survives it intact and who doesn't.
[P1.2] Consider a coastal city facing repeated flooding — think of the pattern we've seen in places like Louisiana's barrier communities or river towns hit by successive hundred-year floods that now arrive every few years. When relocation is forced by the disaster itself, it happens on the disaster's timeline, not the community's. People flee with what they can carry. Property values collapse the moment the water rises, so homeowners lose their equity right when they need it most to rebuild elsewhere.
[P1.3] Local tax bases evaporate, which guts the very government capacity needed to manage an orderly departure. Social networks scatter randomly to wherever shelters or relatives happen to be, destroying the community fabric that could have been preserved by moving as a group. This is retreat at its most chaotic and its most unjust — the poor and elderly are disproportionately unable to leave in time and disproportionately unable to rebuild after.
[P1.4] Mandatory long-term planning changes the sequence of events, not the ultimate outcome. Cities that map exposure early can phase buyouts, preserve equity through pre-disaster property valuations, identify and prepare receiving communities, protect infrastructure investment from being wasted on land with no future, and let people relocate together rather than as isolated refugees. Planning is not capitulation to some inevitable doom; it is the only mechanism by which relocation can be humane rather than a scramble.
[P1.5] The core comparison I want this debate to turn on is simple: the same relocation happens either way — the only variable PRO and CON actually control is whether it is orderly or chaotic. CON has to defend that chaos is an acceptable price for avoiding the discomfort of planning now. That is not a position that survives contact with what actual disaster displacement looks like.
