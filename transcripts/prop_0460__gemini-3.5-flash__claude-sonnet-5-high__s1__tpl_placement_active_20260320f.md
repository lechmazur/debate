[P1.1] The single greatest obstacle to achieving a clean, reliable energy grid is not technology or capital; it is our outdated permitting system. [P1.2] However, under the status quo, a single local municipality can veto a multi-state transmission project, stalling critical infrastructure for decades. Prioritizing faster transmission buildout by curtailing local veto power is a moral, environmental, and economic necessity.

If nine jurisdictions along a route approve a project, the tenth should not have the unilateral power to hold the entire region hostage. [P1.4] Second, the climate crisis operates on a strict timeline. We cannot meet essential decarbonization targets if a single transmission line takes fifteen years to navigate a patchwork of local vetoes. Streamlining the process and centralizing siting authority at the federal or regional level is the only way to deploy infrastructure at the speed the crisis demands.
[P1.5] This reform does not silence local communities; they can still negotiate for fair compensation and mitigation. But it eliminates the absolute veto that paralyzes national progress. When local preferences clash with the energy security and climate survival of the entire nation, the broader public interest must prevail. We must prioritize transmission buildout now.

[C1.1] The claim that stripping local veto power is the fastest path to a clean grid gets the causality backwards. Local input isn't the obstacle to speed; it's the shock absorber that keeps projects from ending up in court, in the press, or in the next election as a live grievance. Take away that absorber and you don't get speed—you get resistance rerouted into harder-to-manage channels: lawsuits, state-level retaliation, and multi-decade litigation that federal preemption cannot simply legislate away.
[C1.2] Consider what "local veto" actually protects. Along a transmission corridor, it's often farmers and rural landowners facing eminent domain condemnation by a private developer they never elected and can't vote out. The local hearing, the county commission, the state siting board—these are the only points where an ordinary property owner gets to say anything before a 345kV line crosses their land. Remove that leverage in the name of climate urgency, and you haven't solved a permitting problem; you've handed private transmission developers a blank check to condemn land with minimal accountability.
[C1.3] That is not a neutral efficiency gain—it is a transfer of power from citizens to capital, dressed up as climate necessity.
[C1.4] And it won't even buy the speed PRO promises. Big infrastructure imposed over sustained local objection tends to generate exactly the multi-decade fights PRO wants to avoid—only now fought in federal court instead of a zoning hearing, with injunctions, sabotage risk, and political reversal every time administrations change. A project built through negotiated consent gets built once. A project built through preemption gets re-litigated indefinitely.
[C1.5] The real choice isn't "local veto versus fast transmission." It's between two permitting-reform strategies: compress timelines, create binding deadlines, fund serious compensation and mitigation, and keep local voice as a negotiating chip that buys durable consent—or strip that voice and gamble that federal muscle alone can outrun the backlash. CON's case is that the first strategy is both more just and, over any realistic time horizon, actually faster.
