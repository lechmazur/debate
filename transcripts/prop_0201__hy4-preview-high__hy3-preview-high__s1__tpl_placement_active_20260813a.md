[P1.1] When a state wages aggressive war, it incurs two debts: to the people it shattered and to the international order it defied. Freezing assets acknowledges the second; using them for reconstruction pays the first. Courts should permit that use because frozen sovereign wealth is the most direct, court-supervised way to honor an injured state's right to reparation.
[P1.2] The general principle is old: a state responsible for an internationally wrongful act must make full reparation. Aggression is not a trade dispute; it is the supreme breach of the international community's most basic rules. Sovereign immunity shields lawful public functions, not a financial reserve an aggressor would otherwise repatriate to fund further violence. If immunity is absolute here, the doctrine becomes an incentive: destroy, keep the treasury intact, and leave victims and their partners to rebuild through aid or debt.
[P1.3] Critics fear confiscation. But this motion asks courts to permit, not executives to seize. Courts can demand proof that the asset belongs to the aggressor state, separate it from private or diplomatic property, apply proportionality, and earmark funds to reconstruction rather than general punishment. That is not lawless seizure; it is judging: identifying responsibility, tracing assets, and ordering a remedy.
[P1.4] Picture a bombed power plant or apartment block. Rebuilding costs billions. Today the victim borrows or begs while an aggressor's frozen reserves—often counted in the hundreds of billions—are untouchable. That is a double injury: the victim suffers the strike and then pays the bill.
[P1.5] Court-permitted use reverses it: the responsible state repairs what it destroyed.
[P1.6] Permitting reconstruction use also changes the aggressor's calculus. Freezing alone merely pauses the money; using it for repair ensures aggression carries a restorative price. The choice is not between order and confiscation. It is between making victims pay twice and letting courts enforce the oldest remedy in state responsibility: repair the harm you caused.

Permitting courts to pierce it for reconstruction converts every domestic bench into a reparations tribunal.
[C1.2] Determining "aggressor state" is a political and collective security act, not a routine judicial finding. The PRO leans on the "old principle" of state responsibility, but that principle is executed through treaties and negotiations, not unilateral court orders against frozen accounts. If a national court can label a foreign state an aggressor and redirect its assets, it usurps the roles of the UN and the ICJ, breeding conflicting rulings and diplomatic chaos.

[C1.4] Worst, the precedent is a boomerang. Today it targets a condemned aggressor; tomorrow it empowers any judiciary to raid foreign reserves over contested interventions. Consider a regional court in a powerful state declaring a smaller rival an “aggressor” in a disputed border clash and diverting its frozen reserves to rebuild its own towns. The label may be partisan, the funds may mix with protected holdings, and allies will retaliate.
[C1.5] The [P1.4] power-plant image ignores that the tool rebuilds one state by unraveling the trust that keeps all states’ assets safe.
[C1.6] Reconstruction after aggression is a moral imperative, but the correct path is legislative and diplomatic—not courts bypassing immunity. Empowering judges to fund rebuilds trades short-term satisfaction for a weaker, more volatile legal order.
