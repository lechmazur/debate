[P1.1] Advanced tracking turns an early snapshot into a long-term educational identity. Placement often reflects prior opportunity, private tutoring, language background, confidence, or one uneven exam—not fixed mathematical capacity. Once separated, students receive different pacing, expectations, peer environments, and access to later courses. The system then mistakes the consequences of its own sorting for proof that the sorting was accurate.
[P1.2] Heterogeneous classes with intensive tutoring separate two questions that tracking wrongly bundles together: what students are capable of learning, and what support they currently need. All students can encounter the same rigorous core curriculum while tutors address specific gaps in small groups, frequently and in coordination with classroom instruction. Placement becomes flexible: a student can receive help with fractions this month without being confined to a slower pathway for years.
[P1.3] This is not a proposal to make every student do identical work at identical speed. Strong heterogeneous teaching uses open-ended problems, varied representations, extension tasks, and flexible short-term grouping. A student who solves an equation quickly can prove why the method works, compare alternatives, or model a harder situation. Where a genuinely exceptional student needs subject acceleration, “most” preserves that option.
[P1.4] What disappears is routine segregation masquerading as differentiation.
[P1.5] The predictable objection is that advanced students will be held back while teachers “teach to the middle.” But tracking does not eliminate variation inside classrooms; every track still contains different strengths and gaps. It merely narrows opportunity in advance. Intensive tutoring gives teachers an actual tool for managing variation instead of using permanent placement as a substitute for support. It can also extend advanced learners rather than treating one accelerated worksheet as sufficient.
[P1.6] The choice is therefore not rigor versus equality. It is between a brittle sorting system and a structure that preserves rigor while making recovery and advancement possible. Public schools should not ration ambitious mathematics according to who looked advanced earliest. They should teach ambitious mathematics broadly, intervene intensively, and reserve separate acceleration for the limited cases that truly require it.
