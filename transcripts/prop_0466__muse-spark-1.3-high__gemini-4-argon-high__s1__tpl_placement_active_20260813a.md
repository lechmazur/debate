[P1.1] Coastal communities that live by fishing die by empty seas. Protecting at least 30% of exclusive economic zones as no-take reserves is not charity toward nature; it is life insurance for fishermen themselves.
[P1.2] Fish do not respect paperwork, but they do respond to safety. Inside a true reserve, fish live longer, grow larger, and a large female can produce many times more eggs than several small ones combined. Those eggs drift, larvae settle, and adult fish wander outward. That spillover means the 70% that remains open becomes more productive, not less.
[P1.3] Closing grounds to raise catches sounds paradoxical until you watch depleted reefs rebound and surrounding catches stabilize.
[P1.4] Half-measures do not do this. Quotas, gear rules and seasonal limits are constantly bargained down, misreported and poorly enforced, especially where monitoring is weak. A no-take area with a clear boundary is radically simpler: if you fish there, you are breaking the law. Size matters too.
[P1.5] Small scattered patches cannot protect migratory species, coral spawning networks, or seagrass nurseries. Roughly a third of waters gives protection across habitats while leaving most of the sea open, and preserves the older age structure that best survives warming and shocks.
[P1.6] The alternative is not continued income but collapse. When an inshore stock crashes, it is not the distant industrial fleet that starves; it is the village with no other employer, no savings, and no fish to eat. A temporary loss from closure can be bridged with aid and adjustment; a dead fishery cannot. Villages from Mexico to the Philippines that once fought reserves now defend them because returning groupers, snappers and tourist divers pay better than a final scramble for juveniles.
[P1.7] Dependence is the strongest reason to act, not to wait. You cannot eat jobs tomorrow from fish killed today.
