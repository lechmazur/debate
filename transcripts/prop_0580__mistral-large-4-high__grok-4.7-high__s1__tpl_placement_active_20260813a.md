[P1.1] Cash reaches children faster and more reliably than any subsidy built around a specific service. When a family receives a monthly child tax credit, that money lands directly in their account and can be spent on rent, groceries, or school supplies—whatever closes the gap that month. It does not depend on whether a daycare slot exists nearby, whether the parent works a standard schedule, or whether a provider accepts the voucher. Poverty is fundamentally a shortage of liquid resources, and the most cost-effective response is to put cash directly into the hands of those experiencing that shortage.
[P1.2] Because tax infrastructure already exists, monthly delivery requires minimal new bureaucracy.
[P1.3] Childcare subsidies, by contrast, funnel taxpayer dollars through a constrained market with significant leakage. They only help families who can actually secure care, systematically excluding parents working non-traditional hours, families in childcare deserts, and those needing infant care where supply is scarcest. Subsidies often inflate provider prices rather than lowering family costs, meaning a substantial share of the spending disappears into administrative overhead and market markup rather than poverty reduction.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, a monthly credit reaches every eligible child regardless of employment status or local supply—including families caring for children at home or relying on informal care networks.
[P1.5] Consider a parent in a rural county with one overcrowded daycare center. A childcare subsidy may be worthless if no slot opens for eighteen months. The same dollars delivered as a monthly credit would immediately reduce that family's food insecurity and housing instability. Monthly payments also smooth income volatility, preventing the spikes and crashes that drive evictions and utility shutoffs.
[P1.6] Cost-effectiveness is measured by children lifted above the poverty line per dollar spent, not by vouchers redeemed. Unrestricted cash achieves that with fewer gatekeepers, less leakage, and broader coverage. When you give families money, they spend it on what their children actually need.

[P2.2] The opponent claims cash "leaks" into rent. That is precisely the point. Housing instability is the single greatest disruptor of employment and child development. When a family uses a credit to pay rent, they avoid eviction, keep children in stable schools, and preserve the foundation necessary to work.
[P2.3] Childcare subsidies, meanwhile, leak into administrative overhead, waitlists, and provider markups—money that never reaches families who cannot find slots, work non-standard hours, or face barriers to voucher acceptance. The subsidy model assumes a functioning market; the credit assumes only that families know their own needs.
[P2.4] On targeting, the CON argues subsidies reach only the poor while credits go to the non-poor. But childcare subsidies suffer from notoriously low take-up among the poorest families—the very ones lacking time, information, or nearby providers to navigate the system. Existing tax infrastructure delivers monthly credits with minimal bureaucracy and far broader reach among eligible families. Per dollar, cash lifts more poor children than a subsidy program requiring willing providers, standard schedules, and complex paperwork.
[P2.5] The CON wants to build childcare supply—a years-long project requiring massive capital investment, new facilities, and workforce development—while children remain in poverty today. Cash is the cost-effective bridge. It reduces poverty immediately, universally, and without the market distortions and administrative waste that plague service subsidies. When the metric is children lifted per dollar, the policy that delivers certainty now beats the one that promises capacity later.
