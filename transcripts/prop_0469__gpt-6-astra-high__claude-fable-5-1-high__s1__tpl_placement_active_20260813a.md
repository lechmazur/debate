[P1.1] A government should not be able to license catastrophic environmental destruction and thereby place its architects beyond meaningful accountability. When a river system is poisoned or an ecosystem destroyed for generations, the harm cannot reliably be repaired with compensation. Countries should recognize ecocide as an international crime because the gravest ecological destruction demands accountability that does not depend entirely on the state permitting it.
[P1.2] The offense should be narrow: unlawful or wanton acts committed with knowledge of a substantial likelihood of severe environmental damage that is widespread or long-lasting. Liability should attach to responsible decision-makers, not automatically to workers, consumers, or everyone involved in a polluting industry. This is not criminalizing every environmental mistake. It is identifying a threshold beyond which knowingly destructive conduct becomes an international concern.
[P1.3] The central problem is an accountability gap. Domestic regulation works least reliably when powerful companies influence enforcement or governments themselves sponsor destructive projects. Civil damages may arrive after irreversible loss, while corporate fines can be treated as operating costs. An international offense would require participating countries to establish criminal accountability, with international prosecution as a backstop where jurisdiction exists and national authorities cannot or will not genuinely act.
[P1.4] That changes incentives before destruction occurs. An executive authorizing the knowing poisoning of an entire watershed should have to consider personal criminal exposure, not merely whether the company can afford a penalty. Enforcement will be imperfect, but imperfect deterrence is not zero deterrence—especially when responsibility reaches those making the decisions.
[P1.5] The strongest objection is uncertainty: could this obstruct legitimate development? Clear damage thresholds, demanding knowledge requirements, and proof of individual responsibility answer that concern better than leaving catastrophic conduct outside international criminal law. Communities need infrastructure and livelihoods; they also need functioning land and water.
[P1.6] The choice is not development versus nature. It is whether the most destructive abuses remain negotiable business risks—or become crimes for which their architects can answer.
