[P1.1] Adult friendship looks private until you notice how thoroughly public life has been arranged against it. After school, most friendships survive only if people keep colliding in the same place, at the same hour, without a formal reason. Work has lengthened, fragmented, and followed people home. Commutes have turned evenings into recovery.
[P1.2] Housing has packed adults into smaller units farther from the people they already know. Third places have thinned or been priced out. Screens offer contact without the repeated, unplanned proximity that actually keeps a bond alive. This is not a sudden moral failure.
[P1.3] It is a coordination failure that private effort cannot reliably solve.
[P1.4] That failure already leaks into domains we treat as public. Isolated adults get sicker, use more emergency care, withdraw from civic life, and become worse colleagues and neighbors. Friendship is how people share last-minute childcare, hear about jobs, check on a parent, or show up after a layoff. When those informal systems collapse, the bill does not stay private.
[P1.5] It lands on clinics, welfare offices, HR departments, and city hall. A society that plans for roads, schools, and labor supply cannot pretend the social glue holding those systems together is nobody's business.
[P1.6] Workplaces, cities, and public institutions already decide whether friendship is possible. They set hours, layouts, housing density, park lighting, library hours, transit frequency, and which groups get a room. Calling friendship purely private merely licenses those decisions to ignore a basic human need. Treating it as social policy does not mean assigning friends or policing lunch tables.
[P1.7] It means designing for what friendship actually requires: leftover time, nearby common space, and repeated low-stakes contact.
[P1.8] If we can plan streets for cars and calendars for productivity, we can plan a little room for people to remain each other's people. The alternative is telling adults to try harder inside structures built to isolate them.

[C1.1] The PRO side treats friendship like a public good that failed to get built. I treat it as a private tie whose value depends precisely on institutions not running it.
[C1.2] Three reasons.
[C1.3] First, the moment a workplace is tasked with producing my friendships, it acquires an interest in what those friendships look like. HR doesn't fund the friend who warns you about the boss, helps you leave, or consoles you after a layoff. It funds the friend who shows up to the wellness retreat. That's not a quirk of bad programs.
[C1.4] It's what happens when a relationship becomes a deliverable: the institution optimizes for the version that serves the institution. Calling this "designing for what friendship requires" obscures who the design serves.
[C1.5] Second, the externalities argument proves too much. Loneliness correlates with poor health. So does single parenthood, unemployment, bad sleep, not owning a dog. If every private hardship with measurable public cost becomes a policy domain, policy becomes everything and explains nothing.
[C1.6] The PRO side has not offered a limiting principle. That absence is the point.
[C1.7] Third, the planner's eye misses how ties actually form. Durable friendship grows from repeated, low-stakes, often accidental contact — the kind public institutions can support by building spare conditions like walkable streets, parks, and reasonable hours, but cannot manufacture by announcing friendship as a goal. The track record of programs that try to produce connection — corporate belonging initiatives, mandated team socials, community-center "friendship" programming — is a record of brittle, performative sociability rather than lasting bonds.
[C1.8] The PRO side concedes institutions shape the soil. The question is whether naming friendship as a policy target makes it flourish or wilt. Voluntary ties depend on a protected sphere nothing is officially in charge of. The moment something is in charge, it manages.
[C1.9] And managed friendship is a quieter kind of loneliness.
