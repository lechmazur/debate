[P1.1] The right to control one’s own identity, voice, and intimate personal traits does not end at death, and the unregulated proliferation of AI grief chatbots poses two clear, urgent harms that a prior consent requirement directly addresses.
[P1.2] First, it prevents egregious exploitation of the deceased and their grieving loved ones. Right now, developers can scrape private social media posts, old voicemails, personal text threads, and other intimate digital footprints to train chatbots that mimic a dead person’s speech, mannerisms, and personality with no input from the person who died or their estate. Documented incidents of tech firms launching commercial AI chatbots of deceased people without notifying their families—profiting off a loved one’s likeness while grieving relatives have no say in the tool’s existence or content—make the risk concrete.
[P1.3] A prior consent rule would block this predatory misuse entirely, while still allowing families to create memorial chatbots if the deceased opted in beforehand, or their estate grants permission after death.
[P1.4] Second, it protects grieving people from harm caused by unvetted synthetic recreations. A chatbot trained only on curated public content, rather than the full, complicated reality of a person’s life, can present a distorted, sanitized version of the deceased that derails healthy grieving. Worse, bad actors could program these tools to say things the deceased never would have, inflicting unnecessary emotional pain on already vulnerable loved ones. Requiring consent from the deceased or their estate ensures any memorial chatbot aligns with the wishes of the person being memorialized, and that their closest family has oversight over how their identity is used.

[C1.2] First, this requirement creates a devastating "sudden death trap." The vast majority of people—especially the young or those taken by sudden accidents—never write wills, let alone sign AI consent forms. By requiring prior consent, we effectively outlaw this technology for those who suffer the most abrupt and painful losses. We would deny a grieving mother the comfort of hearing her child’s voice simulated from old videos, simply because a teenager did not fill out a digital afterlife registry before a fatal accident.
[C1.3] Second, it violates the rights of the living. When a loved one sends us texts, emails, or voicemails, those digital artifacts belong to us. If a grieving partner wishes to input their own private correspondence into an AI to simulate a conversation, they are using their own legal property to process their own grief. Demanding external consent to use one's own private memories and data is an unprecedented infringement on personal cognitive liberty.
[C1.4] Finally, relying on the estate as a backup is a recipe for weaponized family conflict. Estates are bureaucratic entities often controlled by corporate executors, estranged relatives, or distant administrators. Giving an estate veto power over a memorial chatbot allows a hostile relative or a bank trustee to block a surviving spouse or child from finding comfort.
[C1.5] We do not require a writer to get estate consent to write a biography, nor an artist to paint a portrait. We must not allow the state to police the private, therapeutic ways the living choose to remember those they have lost.
