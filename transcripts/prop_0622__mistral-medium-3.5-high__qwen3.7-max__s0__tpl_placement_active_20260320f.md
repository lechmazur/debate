[P1.1] Carbon pricing with per-capita dividends beats green industrial subsidies on efficiency, fairness, and political durability. A uniform carbon price puts the same cost on every ton of CO2, creating an economy-wide incentive to cut emissions wherever it is cheapest. Subsidies cannot match this: they only support selected technologies, leaving most emissions unpriced and distorting markets by picking winners.
[P1.2] Revenue recycling as equal dividends makes the policy progressive and politically robust. In Canada, the majority of households receive more in rebates than they pay in carbon costs, with the lowest-income benefiting most. British Columbia’s revenue-neutral carbon tax cut emissions relative to business-as-usual while its economy outpaced the rest of Canada. Subsidies invert this logic: they transfer public funds to private interests, often regressive.
[P1.3] In the US, federal EV tax credits flow overwhelmingly to high-income households, while all taxpayers share the burden.
[P1.4] Dividends also avoid the corruption of subsidy politics. Subsidy programs invite constant lobbying to expand, redirect, or protect specific beneficiaries, creating dependencies and market distortions. A carbon price with dividends is transparent and resistant to manipulation—everyone pays the price and everyone gets the same dividend. The administrative simplicity is another advantage: dividends require no complex eligibility rules or industry-specific negotiations.
[P1.5] The choice is stark: a policy that cuts emissions efficiently, benefits most citizens, and withstands political pressures, or one that is narrow, regressive, and vulnerable to capture. Carbon dividends deliver where subsidies fail.
