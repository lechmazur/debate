[P1.1] A commodity windfall has three features that define this debate: it is volatile, it is huge relative to the local economy, and it runs out. The right test is which tool turns a depleting asset underground into lasting wealth above ground. Sovereign wealth funds are built for that problem, and cash distribution is not.
[P1.2] Start with volatility. Oil and copper prices can halve in a year. If the windfall goes straight to households, household incomes halve with it. The poorest citizens are the least able to hedge, and they end up carrying the full swing of the commodity cycle.
[P1.3] A fund saves in the boom and releases in the bust. Chile's copper savings let it spend through downturns while its neighbours cut. Mongolia promised mining cash handouts in the boom, then found them unaffordable when prices turned.
[P1.4] Second is absorption. Pouring foreign-exchange earnings into a small economy all at once drives up prices and the exchange rate, which hollows out the farming and manufacturing that employ most people. That is Dutch disease, and mass cash transfers deliver it directly: the nominal cheque arrives and local inflation eats its value. A fund holds the wealth offshore and releases it at a pace the economy can actually use.
[P1.5] Third is time. The oil belongs to the citizens of 2060 as much as to today's. Distribution liquidates a national inheritance into present consumption. A fund converts it into a permanent endowment, as Norway, Botswana and Timor-Leste have each tried to do, at very different income levels.
[P1.6] The predictable reply is that governments steal and so the money should go to the people. But universal distribution is not an escape from the state. It needs an honest registry, a payments system and a treasury that passes the money on, so it depends on the same institutions. A rules-based fund with published deposit and withdrawal rules is at least auditable.

A sovereign fund bets on discretionary state virtue.
[C1.2] A universal dividend turns the windfall into millions of visible ownership claims.
[C1.3] Cash here is not a one-off splurge. It is a rule-bound per-capita payment: a fixed share of realized revenue, paid through banks or mobile money, rising and falling with prices. That answers volatility better than a withdrawal rule. Mongolia’s lesson [P1.3] is not “never pay citizens,” but never promise a fixed handout unrelated to revenue.
[C1.4] Households can save, clear debt, buy food, or stock a shop; those are small hedges. A fund is one balance sheet whose rules can be rewritten in the boom.
[C1.5] Nor is Dutch disease an argument for funds [P1.4]. [C1.6] That objection [P1.6] is backwards. Cash needs a registry and payment rail; a fund needs those plus custodians, asset managers, valuations, committees, and procurement. Fund rules are auditable by specialists; a missing deposit is visible to voters. Alaska [P1.7] proves the point: citizens defend the dividend, not the balance sheet.
[C1.7] The fund is plumbing; the cheque is the ownership rule. Future citizens are today's children; a raidable portfolio is weaker than a fed child and a voter who knows the oil is theirs. For weak states, that is the better inheritance.
