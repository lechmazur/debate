[P1.1] Broad emergency executive powers exist for good reason: when a fast-moving crisis hits, legislatures cannot deliberate at the speed of a natural disaster, terrorist attack, or novel public health threat, and executives need clear authority to act fast to prevent widespread harm. But that narrow, time-sensitive purpose collides with a hard, proven reality of power: once granted, emergency authority almost never sunsets on its own. Powers written for acute, existential threats get repurposed for routine, low-stakes law enforcement decades later.
[P1.2] Public health orders written for the first, deadliest phase of a pandemic stay on the books long after case rates fall to negligible levels, because repealing them requires legislative majorities to clear procedural hurdles, fight executive pushback, and cut through public apathy. Even well-meaning executives face little incentive to voluntarily surrender authority that gives them flexibility to act without legislative negotiation, making that drift inevitable without a structural check. Automatic sunset hearings and renewal votes fix that mismatch without crippling initial crisis response.
[P1.3] The initial emergency grant remains fully available the moment a legitimate crisis strikes; no delayed vote blocks urgent, life-saving action. What the requirement changes is the dangerous default of permanent emergency. Instead of forcing legislatures to assemble a hard-won majority to claw power back from an executive that has already grown comfortable wielding it, the rule places the burden where it belongs: on the executive to make a public, on-the-record case, at regular, predictable intervals, that the power remains necessary, remains bounded to its original purpose, and is not being misused for unrelated policy ends.
[P1.4] These recorded votes force transparent accountability, let legislators narrow overbroad powers before they become entrenched, and ensure temporary emergency rule never slips into permanent, unaccountable governance by public inattention.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like modest housekeeping. It is actually a dangerous wager: that scheduled political votes will discipline emergency power without ever strangling emergency response. That wager fails on both ends.
[C1.2] Start with the default. PRO's own framing admits the stakes: in a genuine crisis, speed saves lives. But automatic renewal votes flip the default from "authority continues" to "authority dies unless a legislature affirmatively renews it." Legislatures are not reliable renewal machines.
[C1.3] They gridlock, they recess, they attach riders, they grandstand. When a renewal vote stalls—not because the emergency ended, but because a minority smelled leverage—the power lapses at the exact moment it is needed. The United States government has repeatedly lurched toward funding cliffs and default deadlines precisely because scheduled expiration was used as a hostage-taking instrument. PRO hands that weapon to every faction willing to gamble with a crisis.
[C1.4] Second, notice what sunset votes do to the executive's incentives. If authority evaporates on a calendar regardless of conditions, the rational executive grabs everything imaginable immediately, issues sweeping orders before the first hearing, and treats each renewal as a use-it-or-lose-it clearance sale. That produces broader, faster overreach, not narrower power.
[C1.5] Third, the premise that democracies lack checks is false. Legislatures can already repeal emergency statutes, courts already review emergency orders, and voters already judge executives at the next election. The famous historical problems came from legislatures lacking the will to use existing tools. A new procedural ritual does not manufacture will; it manufactures theater, then cliff-edge failure.
[C1.6] The real problem PRO identifies—apathy and entrenchment—is solved by accountability, calendars and elections, not by building expiry dates into the machinery that keeps people alive.
