[P1.1] Producer subsidies are a bet on specific companies; demand-side policy is a bet on the technology itself. When governments write checks to battery plants and solar factories, they are gambling taxpayer money on which firms will survive. History shows this picks losers. Solyndra absorbed half a billion dollars before collapsing.
[P1.2] Other subsidized ventures built capacity that sat idle when demand failed to materialize. The state cannot outguess the market, and it rarely claws back money when the bet fails.
[P1.3] Demand-side policy works differently. Consumer tax credits, point-of-sale rebates, and clean procurement mandates put purchasing power directly in the hands of households and businesses. A family buying an electric vehicle with an instant rebate adopts clean tech immediately; the manufacturer must compete for that customer’s dollar. This creates a virtuous cycle: real demand pulls supply, forcing innovation and cost reduction.
[P1.4] The incentive is tied to actual deployment, not nameplate capacity. When a government subsidizes production, the factory gets paid whether it sells a single unit or not. When it subsidizes demand, the support vanishes if nobody buys.
[P1.5] Fiscal discipline favors the demand approach too. Consumer credits can be capped, phased down as adoption grows, and automatically expire when the market matures. Production subsidies, by contrast, become permanent entitlements—sunk costs that protect incumbents and crowd out new entrants. They also invite trade retaliation under WTO rules as prohibited export subsidies, whereas demand-side measures generally comply with international trade law.
[P1.6] The metric that matters is tons of carbon avoided per dollar spent, not factory ribbon-cuttings. Demand-side policy pays for outcomes—vehicles on roads, panels on roofs, batteries in grids. Producer subsidies pay for promises. The choice is not between action and inaction, but between paying for activity and paying for results.
[P1.7] Countries serious about climate and competitiveness should put money in consumers’ pockets and let the market deliver the transition.
