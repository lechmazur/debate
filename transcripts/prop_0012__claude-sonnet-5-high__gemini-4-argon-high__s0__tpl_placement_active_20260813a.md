[P1.1] Human remains are not specimens that happen to have once been people. They are the literal bodies of someone's ancestors, taken in overwhelming majority through violence, grave robbery, or coercion that the dead never consented to. The Smithsonian, Harvard's Peabody Museum, countless European institutions - these collections exist because colonial-era collectors dug up burial grounds, bought corpses from battlefield scavengers, or simply took bodies from communities with no power to refuse. That origin story doesn't expire.
[P1.2] A stolen object doesn't become legitimately owned just because the thief's institution held onto it long enough to publish papers about it.
[P1.3] My case rests on three claims. First, ownership and consent matter more than utility. We don't let any other field of science override the basic principle that you need permission to use a person's body - we require consent for autopsies, for organ donation, for medical research on living subjects. Scientific value has never been considered sufficient justification to override bodily autonomy; there is no reason that logic dissolves simply because the person is dead and Indigenous.
[P1.4] Second, "promptly" is doing real work in this resolution, and time itself is a harm. Every year a museum keeps an ancestor's skeleton in a drawer is another year a community cannot complete burial rites, another year of the original theft continuing in slow motion. Descendant communities have testified for generations that this isn't an abstract grievance - it's an ongoing wound. Research "value" is never finished; there will always be a new technique, a new question.
[P1.5] If indefinite deferral is permitted whenever researchers can gesture at future scientific value, repatriation becomes permanently postponable, and the promise of return becomes hollow.
[P1.6] Third, consider the asymmetry of harms. If remains are returned and later science advances, researchers lose a research subject - a real but recoverable loss, often mitigated by data already collected, casts, or genomic samples taken with consent. If remains are kept, a community loses the ability to bury its dead, a loss that compounds with every passing year and can never be restored [clipped]
