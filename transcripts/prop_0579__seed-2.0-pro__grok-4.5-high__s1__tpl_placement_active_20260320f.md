[P1.1] Right now the world runs on dozens of fragmented, unaccountable identity systems. Every bank, benefits office, rental app, and vaccine rollout runs their own checks. None talk to each other, almost none are properly secured. This is the status quo almost all critics of digital ID ignore.
[P1.2] Every year, millions of people get locked out of unemployment, housing aid, or emergency medical care because they cannot produce a printed birth certificate from 40 years prior, or a utility bill with exactly the right address. At the same time, identity fraud costs ordinary people and governments tens of billions annually, nearly all exploiting gaps in these fragmented bad systems.
[P1.3] An optional national digital ID solves both. It is one verified root that the user controls, not fifty unregulated copies held by random private companies. No one is forced to use it. But when you do, you can prove who you are without handing over your full drivers license, social security number, and home address to every bar that just needs to check you are over 18.
[P1.4] Surveillance and breach risks are real—but they are already present, on a much larger scale. Right now police regularly buy identity and location data from ad brokers, credit bureaus, and grocery loyalty apps. A properly run national ID puts strict audit logs, use limits, and citizen oversight on *one* system, instead of a hundred ungovernable ones no one can effectively monitor.
[P1.5] Breach risk? The current system has already leaked the full identity of roughly half the adult population via payroll, retail, and hospital breaches. This is not a debate about perfect safety. It is a debate about which option delivers less overall harm, and more net good.
[P1.6] That is the standard this motion passes on.
