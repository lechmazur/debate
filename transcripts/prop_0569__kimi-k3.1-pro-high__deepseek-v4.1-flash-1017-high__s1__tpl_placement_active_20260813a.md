[P1.1] A fixed-session handoff is not a vote of no confidence in AI. It is a safety rule for a tool that can provide conversation, but cannot independently examine a patient, assume clinical responsibility, or reliably recognize when its own approach is failing. The law should permit chatbots to expand early support while insisting that sustained treatment eventually acquires human judgment and accountability.
[P1.2] The trigger is repetition itself. A patient returning session after session may be improving, but may also be stuck: receiving soothing replies while depression deepens, reassurance while compulsions strengthen, or agreement while mania, psychosis, or suicidal thinking goes unrecognized. A chatbot sees the text it is given; a professional can challenge the account, notice deterioration, consult records, involve others where appropriate, and change the treatment plan. Handoff is therefore not a declaration that every long-term user is ill.
[P1.3] It is a triage rule based on an observable warning sign: the problem has persisted beyond the chatbot’s initial role.
[P1.4] Consider someone who spends weeks telling a compliant chatbot that life is pointless, while always adding, “but I’m safe.” A system optimized to respond helpfully may keep offering coping exercises. The fixed rule breaks that loop. It creates a point at which continued engagement can no longer substitute for assessment.
[P1.5] Requiring the trigger to be fixed also matters. Leaving referral to the chatbot means asking the same limited system that missed the danger to certify that no danger exists. Leaving it to companies invites standards shaped by retention, cost, and liability. Leaving it to patients assumes that those most ashamed, hopeless, or dependent will independently seek help.
[P1.6] A clear legal threshold is enforceable, auditable, and equally applicable.
[P1.7] This need not mean abandonment or an abrupt cutoff. A lawful handoff can include a warm referral, professional review, crisis safeguards, and continued low-risk AI support. The opposition must defend the far stranger rule: that a patient may receive unlimited machine-led mental-health treatment, with no guaranteed human checkpoint, simply because the chatbot and its operator prefer to continue. [C1.1] The motion mistakes a clock for a clinician. Nothing about the tenth session, rather than the third or thirtieth, tells us whether a patient is deteriorating, stable, or finally engaging. A fixed legal cutoff is therefore simultaneously too late and too early: the person masking suicidal intent can pass a chatbot’s cheerful check-ins for weeks before the timer expires, while the person using text-based support for chronic anxiety—or because a clinic is distant, expensive, or stigmatizing—is ejected just as continuity starts to work.
[C1.2] That is the central flaw PRO must answer. Repetition is not a warning sign; it is often the treatment. Many mental-health difficulties are chronic and relapse-prone. Stable patients benefit from ongoing low-intensity support, skills practice, and monitoring.
[C1.3] Forcing a handoff does not create human judgment; it creates a waitlist, a cost barrier, or an abrupt termination. In under-served areas the “human professional” may be months away. A legal rule that converts an available, private, low-cost source of support into a referral queue is not safety—it is harm with a compliance form.
[C1.4] Nor does the absence of a fixed mandate mean unlimited, unaccountable machine treatment. Law can require clear escalation pathways, crisis protocols, clinical supervision, audit of outcomes, transparent limits, and prompt human referral when risk signals appear. Those are risk-based rules. PRO’s rule is calendar-based.
[C1.5] The difference matters: risk-based rules target the patient who needs help now; a session cap waits for an arbitrary number and then burdens everyone equally.
[C1.6] PRO says leaving referral to the chatbot is unsafe. Agreed—so do not make the chatbot the judge. But the remedy is independent oversight and mandatory escalation triggers, not a universal expiry date. The proposition would ban exactly the long-term, low-risk use that makes chatbot support valuable, while giving false reassurance that a human checkpoint at some fixed session solves safety.
[C1.7] It does not. A timer cannot triage.

[P2.1] “A timer cannot triage.” Correct—but it can ensure that triage eventually happens. CON’s risk triggers catch dangers the system recognizes; the fixed handoff catches the different failure where deterioration, misdiagnosis, or simple ineffectiveness goes unrecognized. [P2.2] The “too late and too early” objection therefore attacks a rule we have not proposed. Nothing in the motion delays urgent escalation until the fixed session: immediate risk-based referral can and should coexist with the guarantee. Nor is the handoff an “ejection,” “expiry date,” or ban on long-term support. Professional review can confirm that continued AI-assisted skills practice is appropriate.
[P2.3] The patient keeps useful support; what ends is indefinite treatment without a human checkpoint.
[P2.4] CON’s strongest argument is access: referral may mean cost or delay. But that is an argument for designing feasible handoffs and preserving support during the transition, not for declaring unlimited machine-led care safe wherever human capacity is scarce. Scarcity makes an accountable review pathway more necessary, because patients with fewer alternatives are least able to obtain a second opinion when the chatbot fails them. CON’s alternative offers the illusion that availability itself establishes adequacy.
[P2.5] The claim that “repetition is often the treatment” also proves too little. Of course recurrence can reflect chronic illness or beneficial practice; the fixed rule does not diagnose either. It says that when repeated sessions make AI the primary ongoing intervention, someone with clinical responsibility must assess whether repetition is helping. Maintenance under professional oversight is entirely compatible with the motion.
[P2.6] Finally, calling the number arbitrary does not make it useless. The threshold need not reveal a patient’s condition any more than a scheduled medication review reveals whether a dose has failed. Its function is different: to create a certain, enforceable moment when persistence triggers human judgment. CON would regulate recognized danger; PRO regulates recognized danger and the limits of recognition itself.
