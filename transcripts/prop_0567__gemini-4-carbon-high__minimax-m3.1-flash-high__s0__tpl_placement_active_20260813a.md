[P1.1] Justice conducted out of sight invites abuse, which is why modern legal systems treat the public trial as a cornerstone of legitimacy. Yet banning cameras reduces public access to a few dozen wooden benches on a Tuesday morning. For working citizens, caretakers, and anyone living far from the courthouse, barring cameras effectively bars the public. Permitting cameras in most trials does not invent a new right; it rescues open justice from becoming a polite fiction.
[P1.2] Secondhand substitutes cannot replace direct observation. A cold transcript flattens human reality: it misses a witness’s long hesitation, a prosecutor’s mocking tone, or a judge berating an indigent defendant. Nor does banning cameras keep major trials out of the media. Instead, a ban hands a monopoly over public perception to partisan pundits, selective reporting, and spin on the courthouse steps.
[P1.3] Video gives citizens the primary record, grounding public trust or criticism in what actually happened under oath.
[P1.4] Visibility also improves justice inside the room. Judges, prosecutors, and testifying officers wield immense state power, often in routine trials that no reporter covers. Knowing proceedings can be seen deters judicial bullying, prosecutorial overreach, and careless lawyering far better than rare appeals years later. Sunlight disciplines courtroom power in real time, before an unfair verdict is reached.
[P1.5] Crucially, permitting cameras in most proceedings sets a transparent default, not a reckless absolute. Modern coverage requires no circus of cables or flashbulbs, only a single, silent, fixed camera under strict judicial rules. Jurors remain completely off screen, sidebars stay muted, and judges retain full authority to bar cameras for juvenile cases, sexual offenses, undercover officers, or witnesses facing credible intimidation. Once targeted exceptions shield the truly vulnerable, a default ban protects only the comfort of legal insiders.
[P1.6] Courts exercise power in the public’s name; in most trials, the public deserves to see how that power is used.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes visibility for justice. Cameras can make a trial easier to watch, but filming most proceedings does not necessarily improve adjudication or public confidence. More images are not automatically more truth, especially in an adversarial system where every participant has incentives to perform for an imagined audience.
[C1.2] Camera access changes courtroom behavior. A silent, fixed camera is not a neutral observer when witnesses may address it, counsel may adjust their delivery, and judges know their expressions will be preserved. The result can be a televised performance: rhetorical flourishes replace careful argument, eye contact with the jury becomes eye contact with the public, and disputes are framed for later clips. The PRO side cannot simultaneously argue that cameras deter misconduct and claim that filming changes nothing about how participants conduct themselves.
[C1.3] Nor does a courthouse camera guarantee public access. Someone must decide whether the signal leaves the building, which broadcaster receives it, and how the recording is edited. The public may receive a selected version rather than a complete proceeding. That leaves the same selective mediation the PRO side condemns when it criticizes partisan pundits—only now the selected material is framed by camera angles and broadcast choices.
[C1.4] Transcripts, court records, and targeted access can provide scrutiny without making every routine hearing a media event.
[C1.5] The word “most” is especially dangerous. Routine matters often involve less coercive state power and less need for a permanent visual deterrent. Yet filming them captures litigants’ faces, witnesses’ identities, and family information, creating lasting records in cases where the public-interest benefit is slight. Sensitive proceedings will require exceptions, revealing that “most” cannot mean “regardless of individual harm.”
[C1.6] Judges already have procedural powers, sanctions, appellate review, and a duty to control their courts. The cure for judicial bullying is meaningful correction, not an audience for every dispute. Cameras should be considered case by case, with privacy and fair-trial protections paramount. A default of filming most trials turns transparency into spectacle and exposure; that is not a sound rule for justice.
