[P1.1] Funding colleges by enrollment alone pays for promises, not results. You get money for filling a seat in September, even if that student drops out by December with debt and no degree. That is the current bargain, and it is failing both students and taxpayers.
[P1.2] An enrollment-only formula tells a rational administrator to spend on recruitment, not retention; on marketing, not mentoring. Why hire more advisors, expand tutoring, or overhaul a broken gateway math course? The student is already counted. Under that logic, a campus that enrolls 1,000 freshmen and graduates 300 is funded the same as one that enrolls 1,000 and graduates 700.
[P1.3] The public pays the same for failure as for success.
[P1.4] Tying funding to demonstrated improvement in graduation rates and post-graduation earnings fixes the incentive. If future dollars depend on moving more students across the finish line and into decent livelihoods, universities will invest in what actually helps: early alert systems, intrusive advising, mental health, work-based learning, curricula that align with regional labor needs. Money follows the outcome we actually want.
[P1.5] This is what public investment should demand. Higher education is subsidized because it is supposed to create mobility and economic capacity, not just enrollments. Enrollment is input. Graduation plus earning is output.
[P1.6] And improvement-based funding, properly risk-adjusted for Pell share and starting completion levels, rewards the hard work. It gives the biggest bonus to the regional public and the community college that lifts completion from 35% to 45%, not the selective flagship coasting at 90%. It doesn't kill the humanities; it kills exploitative programs that leave students worse off.
[P1.7] Pay for enrollment and you get enrollment. Pay for success and you get success.
