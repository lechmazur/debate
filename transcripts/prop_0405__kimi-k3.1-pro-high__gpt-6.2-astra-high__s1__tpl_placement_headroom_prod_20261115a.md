[P1.1] When a rented scooter hits a pedestrian inside a clearly marked no-riding zone, the legal question should not end with whether the rider behaved badly. It must also ask whether the company that supplied, placed, powered, and profited from that scooter took reasonable steps to prevent a foreseeable injury. Greater legal responsibility is warranted because responsibility should follow control, not stop at the handlebars.
[P1.2] Riders remain responsible for reckless choices. But rider-only accountability misses the recurring conditions companies create: vehicles distributed at scale, apps that authorize rides, software that governs speed, maintenance schedules, parking incentives, and rules for repeat offenders. A rider may be uninsured, unidentified, or judgment-proof; the pedestrian still faces surgery, lost wages, and a long search for compensation. Leaving companies outside the analysis privatizes fares while shifting predictable costs onto people who never agreed to share the risk.
[P1.3] The standard need not be absolute liability. Companies should answer when their breach contributes to the harm: ignoring persistent sidewalk complaints, failing to maintain brakes, using geofencing they know is unreliable near restricted areas, or continuing to activate accounts after serious violations. Reasonable precautions could include speed limits in suitable high-risk locations, clear in-app restrictions, prompt removal of dangerous vehicles, and escalating enforcement. GPS is imperfect, so the duty is to design, test, monitor, and correct safeguards—not to guarantee that no rider ever misbehaves.
[P1.4] Where a company did everything reasonably required, liability should not follow.
[P1.5] Opponents will invoke innovation and personal responsibility. Neither justifies an avoidable safety subsidy. Requiring firms to internalize the costs of risks they can reduce favors safer deployment over careless growth; it does not ban scooters or excuse riders. The dividing line is simple: individual blame punishes the person who caused one collision; corporate responsibility prevents the next hundred.
[P1.6] Pedestrians deserve both.
