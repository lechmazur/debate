[P1.1] Cities should stop writing blank checks for stadiums. A new ballpark can be a civic asset, but only if the public that pays for it actually shares in the upside or is protected from the downside. Without enforceable revenue sharing or neighborhood-benefit requirements, a subsidy is just a transfer from taxpayers to franchise owners.
[P1.2] The economics are familiar. Stadiums rarely create new spending; they reshuffle it from other restaurants and venues. Construction jobs are temporary. The lasting windfall—ticket revenue, sponsorships, media money, and soaring franchise values—lands in private hands.
[P1.3] Cities absorb the risk: cost overruns, lost property-tax base, and the bill for roads and security. When owners threaten to leave, politicians fold. That is not partnership. It is leverage.
[P1.4] Conditions change the deal. Revenue sharing means if the building prints money, the city that financed it gets a cut—enough to recoup the subsidy or fund services. Neighborhood requirements mean the project cannot wall itself off from the people who live next to it: local hiring, set-asides for small businesses, anti-displacement funds, parks that stay public. Both must be enforceable—contracts with clawbacks, not press-release promises that dissolve after the ribbon-cutting.
[P1.5] Nothing here bans stadiums. Teams that can finance privately should. Teams that want public capital should accept public terms. Cities that refuse those terms are not anti-sports; they are refusing to socialize the cost and privatize the profit.
[P1.6] The alternative is the familiar cycle: another stadium, another public hole, another neighborhood that gets noise and traffic while owners bank the gain.
[P1.7] That is the choice. Subsidize only when the public is a real partner, or do not subsidize at all.

[C1.3] We've seen this with tax increment financing districts across the country—the promised returns evaporate under creative structuring, and taxpayers are left holding the bag. Calling a provision "enforceable" doesn't make it so.
[C1.4] Now consider neighborhood-benefit requirements. They sound community-minded, but in practice they become checklists satisfied through token gestures. A local-hiring provision gets met with a handful of entry-level positions. A small-business set-aside accounts for a tiny fraction of concession revenue.
[C1.5] These requirements let politicians claim they've protected the neighborhood while the fundamental transfer—from public risk to private gain—proceeds unchanged.
[C1.6] Here's the deeper problem: the proposition creates a perverse incentive to subsidize. A city council that might otherwise reject a stadium deal can now point to its conditions and say, "We're being responsible." [C1.7] Instead of stopping the cycle of public giveaways, we're legitimizing it with paperwork.
[C1.8] What actually works is different. Require public referenda for any subsidy above a threshold. Mandate full deal transparency—every provision, every contingency, available to taxpayers before a vote. Build in automatic clawbacks tied to specific, verifiable triggers like relocation or sustained revenue misses.
[C1.9] These are targeted mechanisms, not broad condition categories that invite gaming.
[C1.10] The proposition mistakes process for protection. Better to have fewer subsidized stadiums with genuine accountability than more stadiums wrapped in conditions that dissolve the moment the ribbon is cut.
