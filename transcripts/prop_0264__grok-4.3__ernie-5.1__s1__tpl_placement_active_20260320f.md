[P1.1] Requiring officers to carry personal professional liability insurance places the direct financial risk of misconduct on the individuals who generate it. Cities currently absorb nearly all settlement costs through taxpayer-funded budgets, leaving officers with no personal stake in the outcomes of their decisions. An insurance mandate reverses that insulation by forcing officers to pay annual premiums that rise or fall according to their claims history, exactly as occurs with physicians and other licensed professionals.
[P1.2] This structure produces deterrence at the point of action. An officer who faces higher premiums after repeated complaints or payouts must weigh that ongoing cost against tactical choices in the field. Departments that continue to employ high-risk officers would indirectly subsidize those premiums through wages, giving administrators a concrete reason to remove repeat problems rather than absorb further liability.
[P1.3] Taxpayers lose under the status quo because they finance both the misconduct and its remedies without any mechanism to discipline the source. Insurance shifts the marginal dollar to the officer, so the same revenue that once covered judgments now supports prevention through selective retention and behavioral adjustment. Departments retain flexibility to hire and train while the market price of coverage reveals which officers impose unacceptable costs.
[P1.4] The requirement also clarifies the employment relationship. Officers already accept licensing standards and use-of-force rules; adding financial responsibility simply extends the same logic to the civil consequences of violating those rules. Cities continue to indemnify for legitimate line-of-duty judgments, but personal policies handle the excess and the misconduct component, keeping public budgets from serving as a default backstop.

[C1.1] Requiring every officer to buy personal liability insurance sounds tidy, but it collapses under the weight of reality. First, the numbers don’t work. The median patrol officer earns roughly $60,000 a year. Market‑rate professional liability policies for comparable risk—think physicians, lawyers—run several thousand dollars annually, and that is for professionals with far higher incomes and far more predictable loss histories.
[C1.2] Police work is uniquely volatile: use‑of‑force claims, civil rights suits, and excessive‑force settlements can swing wildly from year to year. Insurers would have to price that volatility with a huge risk loading, pushing premiums into the $8,000‑$12,000 range for a typical officer. That is a 15‑20 % hit on take‑home pay, a burden most officers simply cannot absorb. The result is predictable: either the force loses experienced officers to better‑paying private‑sector jobs, or it is forced to subsidize premiums, which brings us right back to taxpayer money.
[C1.3] Second, shifting cost to individuals does not change the underlying incentive structure. Most misconduct stems from departmental policies, training failures, and systemic bias—not from an officer’s personal negligence. Penalizing the individual with a premium does nothing to fix those root causes, yet it creates a perverse incentive: officers may avoid legitimate risk‑taking, undermining public safety, or they may rely on the insurer’s deep pockets and become less careful—a classic moral‑hazard problem.
[C1.4] Third, the insurance market itself is ill‑suited to police risk. Unlike doctors, there is no long‑standing actuarial database for officer claims, so insurers will either overprice or refuse coverage, leaving municipalities to pick up the tab anyway. In practice, cities already indemnify officers for line‑of‑duty actions; personal policies would merely duplicate that safety net while adding administrative overhead and legal battles over coverage scope.
[C1.5] In short, the proposal trades a clear, collective funding mechanism for a fragmented, inequitable scheme that harms recruitment, does little to deter misconduct, and ultimately still costs taxpayers. The real solution lies in reforming departmental oversight, not imposing a personal insurance tax on the people who keep our streets safe.

[P2.1] The claim that premiums of $8,000–$12,000 would simply bankrupt recruitment ignores how risk-based pricing already functions elsewhere. High-risk officers would face steeper costs and either improve, leave the profession, or be let go; low-risk officers would pay far less. Departments already adjust compensation and discipline to retain talent. Adding an insurance line to that calculus simply makes the true cost of retaining problem officers visible on the balance sheet rather than hidden in annual settlement budgets paid by residents.
[P2.2] The deeper flaw in the systemic argument is that it treats every misconduct payout as the inevitable result of training manuals or bias audits. In practice, most settlements arise from discretionary choices by individual officers—decisions made in moments when no supervisor is present. Removing any personal financial consequence for those choices leaves the officer rationally indifferent to the frequency or severity of complaints. Placing even a portion of that cost on the officer through premiums restores a direct link between conduct and consequence without requiring perfect departmental foresight.
[P2.3] Moral hazard runs in the opposite direction from the one described. When officers know the city will cover judgments regardless of their record, the marginal incentive is to treat liability as someone else’s problem. An insurance requirement reverses that by making repeated payouts raise the officer’s own ongoing expense, creating the same selective pressure that already operates for doctors facing malpractice carriers. Cities retain the ability to indemnify for policy-level judgments; the personal policy covers only the excess and the preventable component.
[P2.4] The market objection assumes police risk is uniquely uninsurable. Yet private carriers already write policies for security firms and off-duty officers, and several cities have tested limited individual-coverage pilots without collapse. The absence of perfect data is not a reason to preserve a system in which taxpayers absorb every loss while officers face zero price signal.
