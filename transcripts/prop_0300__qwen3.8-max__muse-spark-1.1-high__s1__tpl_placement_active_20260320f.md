[P1.1] The core question is whether a state should let the fear of paying more money veto its duty to acknowledge mass wrongdoing. We should favor a presumption toward apology because the moral, political, and even legal benefits of truth-telling outweigh the incremental risk that a claim becomes easier to press.
[P1.2] Formal apology is not a careless admission of unlimited liability. It can be precise: acknowledge facts, express moral responsibility, name the victims, and separate historical truth from the legal questions that follow. Legal advisers often treat every statement as a litigation weapon, but that adversarial posture is exactly what deepens grievance. When a state remains silent, victims and descendants infer that the injury is still denied, which fuels distrust, radicalizes memory politics, and often makes settlement harder.
[P1.3] History supports this: official acknowledgment of the Holocaust by postwar Germany, and apologies for Japanese American internment, did not end disputes, but moved them from denial toward structured redress.
[P1.4] Apology also changes the conditions for justice. An official acknowledgment creates a public record, reduces the evidentiary and emotional burden on victims, and can open negotiated remedies rather than forcing endless case-by-case lawsuits. In many historical atrocities, the harm is already known; the missing element is institutional responsibility. Withholding it to save money treats suffering as a balance-sheet item and teaches citizens that the state cares more about its treasury than its principles.
[P1.5] The proposition says “more willing,” not “without thought.” States can apologize, establish truth commissions, define remedial frameworks, and limit or channel compensation through democratic processes. But the starting point must be acknowledgment, not evasion. If legal risk is real, the answer is to manage that risk openly, not to hide behind silence. A democracy that apologizes only when it is cheap teaches nothing; one that apologizes despite cost affirms that human dignity constrains state power even across generations.
