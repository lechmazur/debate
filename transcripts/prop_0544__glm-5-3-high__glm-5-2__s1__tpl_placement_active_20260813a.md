[P1.1] The question here is a comparison, and the comparison is not close. Raising the purchase age to 21 saves more lives than any offsetting costs it creates, and I'll give three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the ages 18 to 20 are the highest-risk years for both firearm homicide and suicide, and the least capable of impulsive judgment. Neuroscience and behavioral data converge on this: impulse control and long-term planning capacities are still maturing into the mid-twenties. Suicide especially is an impulsive act — a large share of people who survive a suicide attempt never re-attempt, and firearms convert impulse into death with roughly 85 to 90 percent lethality, versus single digits for pills or cutting.
[P1.3] Delay access by three years and you skip the deadliest window for impulsive acts entirely. Substitution fails here not because 20-year-olds can't find other methods, but because other methods rarely kill.
[P1.4] Second, homicide works the same way. Young men aged 18 to 20 have firearm homicide rates far above the population average, and the killings are overwhelmingly impulsive — disputes, escalation, weapons at hand. Federal law already recognizes this logic: handgun purchases from licensed dealers are restricted to 21, and states that extended similar rules to long guns saw measurable drops in youth gun suicides and school shootings. When the federal assault weapons ban expired, military-style rifles became purchasable at 18 — and 18-to-20-year-olds became the default young mass shooters' acquisition route.
[P1.5] Third, the substitution and illegal-acquisition objections overstate how easily they scale. Illegal purchases cost more, carry real legal risk, and require connections most troubled 19-year-olds lack. Yes, the determined will find a way; the marginal buyer — the depressed kid, the angry one with a grudge — is exactly the person deterred. Laws work on margins, and the margins here are human lives.
[P1.6] The proposition asks whether benefits outweigh costs. Deterred suicides and homicides don't come back; delayed purchases usually never happen at all. That asymmetry decides this debate.

The proposition's asymmetry is backwards — the costs compound while the benefits evaporate into substitution and black-market channels.
