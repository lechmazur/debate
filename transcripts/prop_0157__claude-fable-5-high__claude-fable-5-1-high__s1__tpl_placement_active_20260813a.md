[P1.1] A virus does not check passports. That single fact decides this debate, because every argument against sharing vaccine technology assumes rich countries can wall themselves off from a pandemic. They cannot. When billions go unvaccinated, the virus keeps replicating, keeps mutating, and sends new variants straight back into wealthy nations.
[P1.2] Delta and Omicron emerged in undervaccinated regions and battered London and New York alike. A legal requirement to transfer manufacturing technology is therefore not charity—it is the cheapest self-defense policy available.
[P1.3] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, voluntary sharing demonstrably failed. During COVID, wealthy nations promised donations through COVAX and delivered late, short, and often near-expiry. Meanwhile, mRNA producers declined to cooperate with the WHO-backed technology hub in South Africa, forcing scientists to reverse-engineer the vaccine from scratch—losing precious years. When the incentive structure rewards hoarding, only law changes behavior.
[P1.5] We already accept this logic domestically: compulsory licensing, eminent domain, and emergency powers all exist because voluntary goodwill fails in crises.
[P1.6] Second, the proposal preserves innovation incentives. A fixed window after emergency authorization means firms still capture first-mover profits, government advance-purchase contracts, and rich-country markets—the revenue that actually drives R&D. What they lose is only the ability to leave the Global South waiting years for doses it can manufacture itself. Compensation and licensing fees can be built in.
[P1.7] Note too that COVID vaccines were massively publicly funded; taxpayers already bought a stake in this knowledge.
[P1.8] Third, distributed manufacturing is resilience. Concentrating production in a handful of rich countries created the export bans and supply chokepoints of 2021. Regional manufacturing in India, Senegal, or Brazil means the next pandemic ends faster everywhere.
[P1.9] The comparison for this debate is simple: predictable legal obligation versus discretionary corporate mercy. We tried mercy. It cost, by credible estimates, over a million preventable deaths. Law is how civilized societies stop repeating that mistake.
