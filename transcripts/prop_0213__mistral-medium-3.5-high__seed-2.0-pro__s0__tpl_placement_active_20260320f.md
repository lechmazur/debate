[P1.1] Post-authoritarian societies face a stark choice: bury the past or confront it. Broad amnesties choose the first path, trading victims’ silence for a stability that is often temporary and fragile. When atrocities go unacknowledged, survivors remain invisible, perpetrators retain impunity, and the conditions enabling abuse stay intact, ready to spark new violence. Truth commissions with public testimony take the harder but more durable route of confrontation.
[P1.2] They give victims a platform, forcing entire societies to confront the scale of suffering and the systems that caused it. This is not just catharsis; it is a necessary step to break cycles of impunity. South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission exposed the depth of apartheid’s brutality through public hearings, making denial harder and creating pressure for institutional reform. In Peru, the commission’s report spurred educational reforms to prevent future atrocities by teaching new generations about the dangers of unchecked power.
[P1.3] In Rwanda, the gacaca courts demonstrated that public accountability can coexist with national healing. Amnesties, by contrast, often fail to deliver even short-term stability. In El Salvador, the 1993 amnesty law did not prevent renewed violence; instead, it entrenched impunity, undermining the peace process. In Cambodia, the absence of a robust truth process allowed former Khmer Rouge leaders to maintain political influence for years, delaying reconciliation.
[P1.4] The stability that amnesties promise is frequently a mirage, vulnerable to collapse when suppressed truths resurface. Truth commissions address root causes directly by documenting abuses, validating survivors, and creating a public record to support reparations and reforms. Justice deferred through amnesty is not stability secured—it is instability postponed. The path of truth may be more difficult, but it offers the surer foundation for lasting peace.
