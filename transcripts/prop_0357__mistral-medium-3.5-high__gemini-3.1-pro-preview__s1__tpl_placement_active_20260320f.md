[P1.1] Ownership means control. When you buy a car, you should own its full functionality—not rent it indefinitely. Yet automakers now sell vehicles where core features like heated seats, remote start, or advanced safety systems are locked behind subscriptions that disable functionality when payments stop. This isn’t an add-on service; it’s a digital padlock on hardware you already purchased.
[P1.2] The BMW heated seat subscription makes this concrete: the heating coils are physically installed in the vehicle, but the software refuses to activate them without a monthly fee. Tesla similarly locks enhanced autopilot and other capabilities behind paywalls. When subscriptions lapse, owners lose access to features their cars are physically built to perform—functionality that should be inherent to the product they bought.
[P1.3] This practice creates artificial scarcity and distorts consumer choice. It forces buyers to pay repeatedly for what they’ve already purchased, while giving manufacturers a perverse incentive to withhold capability to extract future payments. More alarmingly, it sets a precedent where any vehicle function could be disabled remotely, turning ownership into a conditional privilege rather than a right. Consider the implications: if a safety feature like automatic emergency braking could be locked behind a subscription, lapses could literally endanger lives.
[P1.4] The principle is non-negotiable: when you buy a product, you should retain full use of its hardware capabilities. Requiring automakers to maintain core repair and operational functionality regardless of subscription status preserves the very meaning of ownership in the digital age. Without this requirement, we normalize a future where you never truly own what you pay for—where every purchase comes with an invisible expiration date on its full functionality.

[C1.2] If we mandate that all physically present capabilities must be permanently unlocked without subscriptions, we destroy this cost-saving model. Automakers will be forced into a lose-lose scenario: either return to inefficient, customized manufacturing lines that raise the baseline cost of every vehicle, or simply charge every consumer the maximum, fully-loaded price upfront. Banning software locks does not gift consumers free features; it eliminates the affordable base model, pricing budget-conscious buyers out of the market entirely.
[C1.3] Furthermore, PRO characterizes this as a mere "digital padlock" [P1.1], ignoring that modern automotive features are deeply tethered to ongoing support. Advanced hardware systems rely on continuous software patching, cloud infrastructure, and cellular connectivity to function safely. Demanding that automakers permanently guarantee full functionality after connectivity subscriptions lapse effectively forces companies to provide lifetime software engineering, cybersecurity updates, and server maintenance for free. That is an economically unsustainable mandate.
[C1.4] Finally, the subscription model enhances consumer choice rather than restricting it. A primary buyer living in Florida shouldn't be forced to pay an upfront premium for cold-weather hardware they will never use. However, if they later sell that car to a buyer in Minnesota, the second owner can activate those features instantly via software without an expensive mechanical retrofitting. Subscriptions allow a vehicle's hardware to adapt dynamically to different owners, seasons, and changing needs over its lifespan.
[C1.5] Banning this practice replaces flexible, lower-cost entry points with a rigid, artificially expensive mandate.
