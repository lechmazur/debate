[C1.1] A few cents sounds trivial until it is every carton, every week, for a family choosing between eggs, milk, and rent. This proposal would impose that cost by law — not to stop abuse, which should already be illegal, but to ban specific housing systems regardless of how well animals in them are actually faring. That is blunt, regressive, and often counterproductive.
[C1.2] First, price rises fall hardest on those least able to absorb them. Eggs and pork are not luxuries; they are among the cheapest proteins and staples in food banks, school lunches, and institutional meals. When supply must be rebuilt — new barns, more land, more labor, lower density — costs do not stay theoretical. Producers pass them on, smaller farmers who cannot finance rebuilding exit, and production concentrates in larger firms that can.
[C1.3] Government thus raises a permanent price floor, borne disproportionately by low-income shoppers with no substitute offering the same nutrition per dollar.
[C1.4] Second, banning restrictive systems does not guarantee better welfare. Cage-free hens can suffer higher rates of pecking, cannibalism, disease spread, and keel-bone fractures as they move and collide in large barns; group-housed sows can be injured by aggression that crates prevented. Well-managed conventional and enriched systems can deliver low mortality and good health even while limiting movement. Welfare is multi-dimensional — movement, health, injury, stress — and trading one dimension for worse outcomes on others is not progress.
[C1.5] If welfare is the goal, regulate measurable outcomes and enforce against neglect, rather than outlawing equipment by name.
[C1.6] Third, a phase-out often just moves production elsewhere. Demand for affordable eggs and pork does not vanish; imports fill the gap from places with weaker rules, longer transport, and less oversight. Domestic farmers lose, consumers pay, and animals are no better off — sometimes worse.
[C1.7] Governments should enforce humane care, punish cruelty, and support better systems, not mandate bans that tax the poor, risk worse animal health, and export the practice they claim to end.
