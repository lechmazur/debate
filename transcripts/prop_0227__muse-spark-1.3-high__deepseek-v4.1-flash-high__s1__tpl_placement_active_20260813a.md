[P1.1] Ransomware persists for one reason: it pays. Every payment that recovers one hospital's files finances the attack on ten more hospitals, schools, and pipelines. Allowing victims to pay feels compassionate in the moment but guarantees a larger pool of future victims. Only a government ban can break that cycle, because no individual victim can afford to take the hit for everyone else.
[P1.2] Criminal ransomware groups operate like businesses. They run help desks, negotiate prices, rent their malware to affiliates, and choose targets based on expected return. When payment is likely, attacks multiply; when payment dries up, the model collapses. Shipping firms did not defeat piracy by paying pirates more reliably, but by making hijacking unprofitable through coordinated refusal and defense.
[P1.3] A legal prohibition turns an isolated moral dilemma into a collective commitment that attackers can count on.
[P1.4] A ban without help would be cruel. That is why the second half of the policy is essential: fund victim support. Governments should pay for what ransoms pretend to buy — rapid incident response teams, secure offline backups, rebuilt systems, temporary operations support for critical services, and shared decryption tools. A ransom buys back access at the price of funding crime; public support buys resilience without rewarding criminals.
[P1.5] It shifts money from attackers to defenders.
[P1.6] A ban also gives firms cover to do the right thing. Today executives who refuse to pay face lawsuits and blame if recovery is slow, while those who quietly pay fuel crime with little consequence. Prohibition flips the incentives: refusal becomes legal compliance, payment becomes liability, and insurers stop reimbursing payments that sustain the market.
[P1.7] Pay-or-be-hacked is not freedom. It is extortion subsidized by the law's silence. Starve the attackers, sustain the victims, and attacks will fall.

They target firms in countries that do not ban, or they take payment through intermediaries, “recovery consultants,” or future promises.

Governments can fund support, and they should.

[C1.6] And the piracy analogy cuts against PRO. Pirates were not defeated mainly by merchants refusing ransom; they were defeated by naval power, courts, and jurisdiction. Ransomware havens require enforcement, sanctions, and defense—not a unilateral payment ban that exports the problem.

[P4.1] A1: The test is destination plus bargain, not label. Paying a legitimate responder an hourly fee to rebuild, paying a software vendor for clean licenses, or settling a lawful commercial claim is legal because the money goes to a defender or lawful claimant for work done, not to a criminal for a criminal promise.
[P4.2] That's already how sanctions enforcement works: follow the beneficiary and the quid pro quo, with intent as the trigger, so ordinary recovery is untouched and disguised payments are caught.
[P4.3] A2: No law can teleport an IT team in an hour, but permission to pay can't either — keys often don't come and data leaks anyway. Prohibition protects that rural hospital before the deadline ever arrives: pre-funded offline backups, retainers, and regional surge teams funded by the policy cut the need for last-minute rescue, and attackers who know hospitals in banning states cannot pay stop hunting them. And the law should punish the business decision, not jail caregivers — fines and loss of coverage for executives and insurers who fund attackers, with a narrow life-safety review for true edge cases and immunity for reporting, not prosecution of a nurse under duress.
[P4.4] Those answers collapse CON's case. Their objection is that perfect enforcement is hard and perfect rescue is impossible, so keep paying forever. That is a permanent tax to gangs that guarantees more rural hospitals face the same deadline.
