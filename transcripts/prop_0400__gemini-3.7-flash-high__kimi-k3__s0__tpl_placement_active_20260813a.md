[P1.1] When the state exercises its most coercive powers—stripping a person of their liberty, deporting an asylum seeker, or terminating the disability benefits keeping a family housed—it is not solving a mathematical optimization problem. It is executing a profound moral judgment. Entrusting these life-altering determinations to automated algorithms reduces citizens to statistical inputs and replaces constitutional accountability with unfeeling computation. A legal right to a human decision-maker is an indispensable safeguard for free societies.
[P1.2] First, human dignity and due process require an accountable actor. When the government deprives an individual of fundamental rights or vital support, that person is owed a reasoned justification from an identifiable public official bound by law, ethical duties, and judicial oversight. Automated systems cannot bear moral responsibility for catastrophic errors. They obscure state reasoning behind opaque code, proxy variables, and probabilistic models, leaving citizens alienated and unable to meaningfully contest the logic of their denial.
[P1.3] Second, automated systems are fundamentally incapable of context, nuance, and equitable discretion. Algorithms operate entirely by matching inputs to past statistical patterns. Yet the most critical government decisions—evaluating the credibility of a refugee fleeing persecution, assessing mitigating circumstances in criminal sentencing, or recognizing an atypical medical crisis—turn entirely on human context that historical data ignores. Algorithms cannot comprehend nuance, detect subtle injustices, or grant discretionary mercy.
[P1.4] They merely codify historical biases and crush edge cases under rigid standardization.
[P1.5] Administrative efficiency is a bureaucratic convenience; fundamental justice is a non-negotiable right. While technology may assist in organizing data, the final authority to determine a person’s fate must remain with a human being capable of empathy, moral reasoning, and democratic accountability. We cannot allow the state to outsource its conscience to an algorithm.

[C1.4] Second, the right's real price is delay, and delay is not neutral. Governments make millions of these determinations. [C1.5] PRO's right is, in practice, a right to wait.

[C1.7] The protections that actually guard dignity are rights to reasons, to evidence, to appeal, and to audit — standards binding human and machine alike. We should regulate the quality of the decision, not the species of the decider. A wrong stamped by a human hand is still wrong.
