[P1.1] The average American wedding now runs on the order of thirty thousand dollars—roughly a year of college tuition—and many couples borrow to pay for it. A custom that demands two people begin married life poorer, stressed, and indebted has inverted its purpose. Obligation is earned, not inherited: traditions deserve our compliance when they serve us, not when we serve them.
[P1.2] Start with the money. Every dollar spent on chair covers, a plated dinner for two hundred acquaintances, and a venue that charges extra for cake-cutting is a dollar not spent on a house deposit, debt repayment, or the marriage itself. The industry sells "the most important day of your life" as moral duty, and couples finance a party at credit-card rates while postponing the life the party was meant to launch. That is not romance; it is a wealth transfer from newlyweds to vendors, laundered through expectation.
[P1.3] Then the performance. Social media has turned a communal ritual into content production. The pressure is no longer just a mother's guest list; it is an invisible audience. Couples stage the first look, the sparkler exit, the choreographed dance—moments performed at guests rather than shared with them.
[P1.4] Obligation to "how it's done" has become obligation to an aesthetic nobody in the room actually chose.
[P1.5] The motion is modest: little obligation, not no weddings. A couple that loves a church ceremony, a packed dance floor, a white dress—wonderful, as a choice rather than a tax. A ritual freely chosen carries meaning; the identical ritual performed to satisfy relatives, vendors, and Instagram is theater. Tradition is strongest when it wins on the merits, not when it leans on guilt.
[P1.6] The clash is simple: why should the two people being celebrated have the least say in their own celebration? Any answer must justify the price tag and the performance—not merely say the word "tradition."

[C1.1] The motion treats vendor bloat as if it were the rite, then uses the price tag to cancel obligation. That is a category error. Traditional expectation asks for a public promise, recognized families, and a ceremony a community can understand. It does not ask for cake-cutting fees or a year of tuition spent on chair covers.
[C1.2] Those are market add-ons. You can refuse them and still keep faith with the form. A modest church wedding, a courthouse followed by dinner, a hall and a potluck: all remain traditional, and none require debt. If couples borrow for plated dinners, that is status competition, not the duty.
[C1.3] A wedding recruits other people. Relatives rearrange their lives; two households become one kin group; friends are asked to treat a private attachment as a public fact. That is why some expectations are inherited rather than invoiced. The couple already chooses the spouse, the date, and the scale.
[C1.4] What they should not feel free to discard is the social work the day is for. If only their taste counts, there is no reason this has to be a wedding rather than a party with better lighting.
[C1.5] PRO's test is whether the custom serves the couple's bank account and brand. That is how you get more performance, not less. When inherited form is treated as a tax, the vacuum is filled by the industry and the feed, the very forces the motion complains about. Tradition is the cheapest defense against that: vows, witnesses, a meal, a dress or suit if you want one, a guest list built from actual relationships.
[C1.6] "Little obligation" sounds modest. It is not. It tells the people being celebrated that continuity, kin, and public recognition are optional extras. They are the point.
[C1.7] Spend less. Post less. Still show up for the parts that make a marriage more than a private contract.
