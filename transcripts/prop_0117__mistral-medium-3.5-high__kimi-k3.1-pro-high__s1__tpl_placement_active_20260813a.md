[P1.1] The death penalty fails on both moral and practical grounds. First, it is irreversible. Wrongful convictions are an inevitable feature of any criminal justice system. In the United States, over 190 people sentenced to death have been exonerated since 1973, often through DNA testing, new witness testimony, or exposure of prosecutorial misconduct.
[P1.2] These cases prove that even carefully administered systems make fatal errors. Each exoneration represents a life saved; each execution before such revelations would have been an irreversible injustice. The risk of executing an innocent person is not theoretical—it is a recurring reality that no safeguard can eliminate. This alone should disqualify the death penalty.
[P1.3] Second, capital punishment fails as a deterrent. Extensive research comparing murder rates in US states with and without the death penalty has found no consistent evidence that execution prevents more murders than life imprisonment. Terrorists, often motivated by ideology or a desire for martyrdom, are particularly undeterred by the threat of execution. The practical justification for the death penalty evaporates when its supposed benefits cannot be empirically demonstrated.
[P1.4] Morally, the death penalty is incoherent. A state that criminalizes murder cannot logically justify its own premeditated killing. This contradiction undermines the moral authority of the justice system. Furthermore, the death penalty is applied discriminatorily, with numerous studies documenting racial and socioeconomic disparities in its imposition.
[P1.5] Abolition does not mean impunity. Life imprisonment without parole achieves both punishment and public protection. The global trend toward abolition is unmistakable, with over two-thirds of countries having ended capital punishment in law or practice. These nations recognize that state-sanctioned killing serves no purpose that cannot be achieved through other means, while carrying the unique and unacceptable risk of irreversible error.
[P1.6] Even for the most heinous offenses, execution offers no benefits that life imprisonment cannot provide. Countries should abolish the death penalty and affirm that no government should possess the power to take a human life.

[C1.1] The proposition demands abolition in every country, even for terrorism and mass murder. That categorical rule cannot rest merely on showing that executions carry risks or that imprisonment usually suffices. CON does not require frequent executions; it requires preserving a narrowly governed option for crimes whose scale and continuing danger may make lesser punishment inadequate.
[C1.2] Irreversibility is a reason for exceptional safeguards, not automatic abolition. Exonerations demonstrate fallibility, but they also show why capital cases require heightened proof, full disclosure, independent counsel, and exhaustive review. A wrongful life sentence can also consume decades and end with the prisoner dying behind bars. The relevant question is whether an execution is justified in a particular case, not whether any institution is infallible.
[C1.3] Otherwise, irreversible consequences would disqualify much of criminal punishment.
[C1.4] Nor does disputed deterrence settle the matter. Punishment also expresses proportionality and society’s condemnation of the crime. A state can consistently prohibit murder—the unlawful, private taking of life—while authorizing punishment after public law and due process, just as it prohibits kidnapping while permitting lawful imprisonment. For deliberately planned mass murder or genocide, execution may be the only punishment that adequately recognizes the enormity of the harm.
[C1.5] Calling that “vengeance” simply assumes away retributive justice rather than answering it.
[C1.6] Life imprisonment is highly protective, but not invariably equivalent. An imprisoned terrorist leader may still direct followers, inspire attacks, exploit hostage-taking, or benefit from escape, successful legal challenges, or later political changes. Ordinary murder statistics cannot prove that execution never adds protection in these exceptional circumstances. Likewise, discrimination in sentencing demands equal treatment and procedural correction; it does not establish that every extreme crime must receive the same maximum penalty as lesser ones.
[C1.7] Countries should therefore be able to restrict capital punishment to the clearest, gravest cases, with rigorous review—or choose not to use it at all. A universal prohibition, even where an exceptionally dangerous offender can justly receive no lesser punishment, removes that judgment without proving that imprisonment always delivers equal justice or security.
