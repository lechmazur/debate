[P1.1] The choice in the motion is between two kinds of pain, and they are not symmetric. Inflation running at four percent for an extra year or two is a cost measured in purchasing power that wages and contracts eventually catch up to. A recession deliberately engineered to hit two percent on schedule is a cost measured in people: workers laid off, small businesses closed, young graduates entering a frozen job market. Those losses do not reverse when the recession ends.
[P1.2] A worker displaced at forty-five often never regains the earnings path they lost, and a firm that closes does not reopen when the central bank cuts rates. Economists call this scarring, and it is the reason the ledger tilts toward tolerance.
[P1.3] Consider what the hard-landing playbook actually requires. Monetary policy works with long and uncertain lags. A central bank determined to crush inflation quickly must keep tightening while the data still look hot, which means it discovers it has overdone it only after the damage is baked in. Volcker's disinflation is the model the hawks invoke, yet it put unemployment above ten percent and crushed an inflation driven by a decade of unanchored expectations.
[P1.4] Nothing like that spiral exists today. Long-run expectations stayed anchored through the post-pandemic surge, and most of that inflation came from supply chains, energy shocks, and shifts in demand composition that no interest rate could unclog. Patience let those pressures fade; a forced recession would have added human misery to a problem already resolving itself.
[P1.5] Two percent is a target, not a law of physics. Its purpose is credibility and stability, and credibility is preserved by showing that the central bank is steadily moving in the right direction, not by hitting a number by a date. Throwing millions out of work to return a few quarters sooner is not discipline. It is choosing the permanent harm to avoid the temporary one.
