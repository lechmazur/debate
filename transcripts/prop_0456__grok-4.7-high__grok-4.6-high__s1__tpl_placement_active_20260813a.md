[P1.1] Generative training is not a reprint. It is statistical learning over millions of books, tracks, and frames whose owners are often dead, split across borders, or unknown. Work-by-work permission does not protect those creators. It protects whoever already trained, whoever can buy a catalog, and whoever will train where enforcement cannot follow.
[P1.2] So the permission has to be conditional. Publishers, labels, and studios may train on copyrighted works without individual permission only if they pay into a compulsory licensing scheme. The "only if" is the case. Permissionless training is a paid privilege.
[P1.3] No payment, no privilege.
[P1.4] Unpaid scraping fails the other way. Model owners take the commercial value of other people's catalogs while writers, session players, and illustrators get nothing they can trace. That is a transfer, not a principle. Compulsory licensing reverses it: access is collective, the rate is mandatory, and money can reach independents who will never be offered a bilateral deal.
[P1.5] Copyright already runs markets that could not survive case-by-case consent. A mechanical license let covers be made without a personal yes from every composer. Blanket licenses let radio play songs that no station could clear in advance. Training has the same structure: real social value, catastrophic holdout, no honest price for each input as its own contract.
[P1.6] Collective payment is how the law scales when transaction costs explode.
[P1.7] Voluntary deals should remain for exclusive data and uses a statute does not cover. Large rightsholders will license large labs and call it consent; everyone else is locked out or unpaid. A license of right is harder on free riders than a fair-use shrug, and fairer than a veto only incumbents can survive. The studio that pays may train.
[P1.8] The studio that does not may not.
[P1.9] If a use cannot be cleared one work at a time and should not be free, the law's job is a compulsory license. That is the permission this proposition grants, and the condition it will not drop.

[C1.1] Compulsory licensing is how a legislature pretends consent happened after it has already been taken. The proposition lets publishers, labels, and studios train on other people's work without a yes, so long as they feed a pot. That is not a condition on permission. It is the abolition of permission, with a fee attached.
[C1.2] Copyright is an exclusive right. The author can license, refuse, or wait. The right to say no is not a transaction cost; it is the product. A novelist can decline a film option.
[C1.3] A musician can decline a sample. An illustrator can decline a style clone. The motion treats that refusal as a bug because catalogs are large and owners are scattered. Size does not convert a taking into a license.
[C1.4] The mechanical-license and radio-blanket analogies collapse on contact. A cover is a known song, in a known market, at a known rate. Radio plays the recording. Training does not play the work.
[C1.5] It dissolves millions of works into a machine that can compete with every one of them, indefinitely, across every medium the studio later ships. There is no honest statutory rate for a catalog used as fuel for an infinite substitute. A board will guess, incumbents will lobby, and the number will be wrong forever.
[C1.6] Payment does not rescue the people the motion claims to help. Collecting societies already leak, delay, and overweight registered majors. Unknown, foreign, and split owners—the holdouts used to justify compulsion—are last to be found. The named beneficiaries are the intermediaries sitting on catalogs.
[C1.7] They pay a levy, then train on rivals, estates, and independents who never would have sold.
[C1.8] Voluntary deals already price scarcity. A compulsory floor becomes a ceiling: firms stop negotiating, and creators who would have withheld cannot. If training needs the work, buy the work. Do not mint a paid privilege to ignore the owner.
[C1.9] The studio that wants the corpus should get a yes, not a receipt.
