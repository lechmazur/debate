[P1.1] States should expand vouchers because the conceded risks are not decisive. Mixed test-score results show vouchers are not a magic curriculum; they do not show that a child should be locked into the school a housing map assigns. Public-school systems may lose revenue, but a school system's budget is not the public good. Children are.
[P1.2] The real comparison is an imperfect additional option versus compulsion for families who cannot buy their way out. Affluent parents already exit through housing, tutoring, and private tuition. Low-income parents are told to wait for systemwide improvement. But a child bullied this semester, a dyslexic student missing structured reading help, or a student in an unsafe school does not experience "average effects."
[P1.3] A lost year is not refunded.
[P1.4] So mixed evidence cuts both ways. It bars inflated promises, but also the claim that government may deny choice until every study shows uniform gains. We never demand that assigned district schools prove they are best for each child. Education is about fit as well as scores: safety, stability, values, special-needs support, and being taken seriously when parents warn that a placement is failing.
[P1.5] An average can hide exactly the children for whom exit matters most.
[P1.6] The resource objection is misnamed. When a student leaves, funding still serves a public purpose; it follows the child instead of protecting an enrollment count. Fair guardrails are possible: phase-ins, transparency, nondiscrimination, and basic reporting. But fixed costs, empty seats, or bureaucratic habit are not claims on a child's attendance.
[P1.7] Public education can be funded without giving districts a monopoly on providers.
[P1.8] Exit also disciplines the remaining system. A school that must persuade families to stay has reason to improve safety, communication, and responsiveness. District defenders call that resource loss; families call it accountability. Under uncertainty, the humane error is to preserve an option, not force the least powerful families to absorb the risk.

[C1.2] First, look at who expanded vouchers actually reach. PRO's case is built on the trapped low-income child. But in the broad recent expansions, a large share of recipients, by many reports most, were already in private school. That is not exit for the powerless.
[C1.3] It is a tuition subsidy for families who had already left, paid for from the same pot that funds everyone else.
[C1.4] Second, the choice belongs to the school, not the family. Private schools can turn applicants away, charge more than the voucher covers, and counsel students out. PRO's dyslexic student [P1.2] is the child most likely to be rejected, and in many programs accepting a voucher means giving up the legal special-education protections public schools must honor. "Fit" works for the children schools want.
[C1.5] Third, "funding follows the child" [P1.6] ignores how schools work. If two students leave a class of twenty-five, the teacher, the bus route, the building, and the counselor cost the same. The twenty-three who remain absorb the loss, and they are disproportionately the poorest, the rural, and the disabled. PRO calls that discipline [P1.8].
[C1.6] A school that has to cut its reading specialist does not become more responsive.
[C1.7] And "mixed" understates the evidence. Evaluations of several large statewide programs found meaningful test-score declines for voucher users. PRO says a lost year is not refunded [P1.3], which is exactly why states should not pay to risk one.
[C1.8] Nor is the alternative compulsion. Open enrollment, magnets, charters, and targeted transfers give families a way out while keeping admissions open and results visible. PRO's own guardrails of transparency, nondiscrimination, and reporting [P1.6] concede that vouchers lack these by default.
[C1.9] Under uncertainty, the humane error is not to gamble the many children's school on a subsidy that mostly reaches families who never needed rescuing.
