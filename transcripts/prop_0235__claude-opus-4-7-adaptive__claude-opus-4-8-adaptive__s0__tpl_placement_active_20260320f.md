[P1.1] Seawalls fail in three ways, and managed retreat answers all three.
[P1.2] First, they fail physically. A wall built for today's storms is the wrong wall for 2070. Sea level rise is accelerating, storms are intensifying, and groundwater is salting fields and basements behind defenses that look intact from the outside. Hard infrastructure locks a coastline into a fixed design assumption at the exact moment those assumptions are becoming unstable.
[P1.3] Every dollar poured into concrete is a bet that the climate will sit still. It won't.
[P1.4] Second, they fail economically. [P1.5] A planned, funded buyout — like the ones New York and New Jersey ran after Sandy — ends that cycle. Pay once, move people to higher ground, restore the marsh, and stop paying forever.
[P1.6] Third, they fail morally and ecologically. Hard defenses protect the wealthiest waterfront parcels while displacing flood energy onto poorer neighborhoods down-current. They drown wetlands through "coastal squeeze," eliminating the very ecosystems that buffer storms for free. Managed retreat, done by wealthy countries with the fiscal room to compensate residents generously, is the option that treats vulnerable people as citizens to be relocated with dignity rather than as collateral for someone's view.
[P1.7] The proposition does not say abandon every harbor or bulldoze Manhattan. It says, on balance, prioritize retreat over hard defense. The places where walls genuinely make sense — dense urban cores, critical ports — are the narrow exception. For the long tail of suburban beachfront, barrier islands, and repeatedly-flooded neighborhoods, the honest, cheaper, safer answer is to move.
