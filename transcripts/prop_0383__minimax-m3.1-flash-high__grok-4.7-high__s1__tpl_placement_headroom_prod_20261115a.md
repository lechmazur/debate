[P1.1] Cities should not ban every form of takeaway packaging, but they should retire the items whose single use creates the least benefit relative to their environmental cost. Polystyrene containers, disposable bags, plastic cutlery, excess food containers, and similar packaging are used for minutes or hours, yet can persist for decades. When they are littered or incorrectly processed, they block drains, harm wildlife, and burden waste systems. Their true cost is therefore not captured by the low price printed on them.
[P1.2] That hidden cost decides the case. A disposable cup may appear inexpensive to the buyer, but its price excludes the materials used to produce it, the energy required to transport and refrigerate it, the emissions from disposal, and the cleanup work imposed on municipalities and taxpayers. Those burdens are spread across the public rather than appearing at the checkout. Governments routinely correct such distortions through packaging rules, deposits, and extended producer responsibility; excluding clearly wasteful items would be arbitrary.
[P1.3] The proposition anticipates inconvenience, and that concession strengthens rather than weakens it. Phasing out selected products allows time for businesses to adopt alternatives, install washing systems, or change delivery practices. Lead periods, small-business grants, and clear exemptions for hygiene-sensitive or accessibility-related uses can prevent a blunt ban from becoming unfair. Consumers may occasionally carry a container or wait for reusable ware, but that is a modest, visible cost compared with allowing waste generation to continue indefinitely.
[P1.4] Reuse systems also create opportunities that disposable packaging suppresses. Cafés can gain repeat customers, delivery platforms can reduce packaging volume, and standardized containers can make reverse logistics profitable. Compostable packaging should be used carefully rather than treated as a magic solution: it still requires collection, suitable conditions, and substantial energy in some production pathways. A targeted phaseout can direct money toward genuinely durable or materially better substitutes.
[P1.5] The central comparison is simple: temporary, manageable adjustment on one side versus recurring waste, cleanup, and ecological damage on the other. A society need not eliminate all inconvenience to recognize when convenience is purchased with permanent environmental debt. [clipped]

[C1.1] Retiring so-called wasteful takeaway packaging sounds precise and functions as prohibition by category. Waste is not a property of the cup. It depends on litter rates, replacements, travel distance, and whether a city can wash, collect, or burn what comes next. A clamshell used for twenty minutes can still be the lower-impact choice for a vendor with no washer, a customer with no sink, and a district where return pilots die with the subsidy.
[C1.2] The motion files that context under manageable inconvenience. Context is the comparison that must be won first.
[C1.3] Unpriced cleanup does not justify a ban. If litter and disposal impose public costs, charge them and improve collection. Reserve hard limits for items that stay harmful after the price is honest. Fees leave night-shift meals, hospital visits, and hygiene-sensitive orders available.
[C1.4] A phaseout deletes the option; exemptions and lead times do not restore it. Grants do not put a washer in a two-person stall or give back the lunch-rush minutes. Street vendors absorb the capital cost and the customers who will not carry kits. That is a transfer onto thin margins, not a shared civic inconvenience.
[C1.5] Reuse is not a refund of environmental debt. It pays only after repeated return, transport, and hot washing. Water-stressed cities trade plastic for scarcity. Heavier paper and compostables often use more material, contaminate recycling, or need industrial conditions most cities do not run.
[C1.6] Countrywide rules enlarge the miss: one statute cannot tell a littered coast from an inland city limited by water or landfill design.
[C1.7] The proposition treats that relocation as acceptable because inconvenience was conceded in advance. Hidden costs argue for prices and infrastructure, not for deleting functional packaging before the substitute has cut net harm. A cleaner conscience with a dirtier ledger is not a reason to decide.
