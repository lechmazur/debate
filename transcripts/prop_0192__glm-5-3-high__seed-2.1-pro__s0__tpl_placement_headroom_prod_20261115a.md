[P1.1] Legal personhood has never been a biological badge. Corporations are persons. Rivers have been declared persons in several legal systems. A ship can sue and be sued.
[P1.2] Personhood is the law's tool for identifying entities whose interests demand direct protection, and it has always tracked moral and functional criteria, not species membership. The question before us is simply whether great apes, cetaceans, and elephants meet those criteria — and they do, to a degree that makes continued captivity and exploitation indefensible.
[P1.3] These animals possess self-awareness documented through mirror recognition, tool use with cultural transmission across generations, and in apes, the ability to learn symbolic language and use it to express desires, memories, and even deception. Dolphins have signature whistles that function as names, calling their own and others'. Elephants mourn their dead, return to the bones of relatives, and cooperate in ways requiring understanding of another's perspective. Orcas live in matrilineal pods with dialects that persist for decades.
[P1.4] These are not conditioned reflexes; they are the substrates of a life that matters to the one living it.
[P1.5] The stakes of getting this wrong are concrete. An orca in a tank is a creature adapted to ranges of a hundred kilometers a day, held in a pool it could cross in seconds, dorsal fin collapsed, performing tricks for fish. A chimpanzee used in invasive research experiences terror and pain with the same neural machinery we have. If suffering is the reason we forbid torturing humans, that reason applies here with full force.
[P1.6] The burden on the other side is to name a morally relevant difference — not an arbitrary line like DNA or species membership, which history has repeatedly discredited in debates over who counts. Absent that difference, the law's refusal to recognize these beings is not caution; it is the same convenient blindness that every expansion of the moral circle has had to overcome. Basic rights against captivity and exploitation are the minimal, principled correction.

[C1.1] Legal personhood is not an honorific awarded for passing a handful of cognitive tests, and PRO’s case rests on a fundamental category error between moral consideration and legal status. Personhood in every existing legal system is tied to reciprocal accountability: a corporation can be sued for breach of contract, a ship can be held liable for damages, even personhood status for rivers is administered by accountable human trustees weighing competing claims. Non-human animals cannot meet that reciprocal standard. An orca cannot be held responsible when it kills a trainer; an elephant cannot compensate a smallholder whose family’s food supply it tramples; a chimpanzee cannot answer for injuring a child that strays too close.
[C1.2] Every conflict, every tradeoff, every claim about the animal’s best interest will be decided by self-appointed advocates with no reliable way to verify what the animal wants. The proposed blanket right against captivity and exploitation does not merely end cruel orca shows and invasive research. It bans accredited sanctuary care for orphaned elephants and apes that cannot survive reintroduction to the wild. It blocks vetted conservation breeding programs that are the last buffer against extinction for subspecies decimated by poaching and habitat loss.
[C1.3] It even prohibits the low-stress, voluntary cognitive research that produced most of the evidence PRO cites for these animals’ intelligence, since any human-managed interaction counts as exploitation under the motion’s broad wording. Worst of all, the line the motion draws is no less arbitrary than the species boundary PRO decries. If self-awareness and cultural transmission earn personhood, there is no principled reason to exclude highly intelligent, self-aware octopuses, African grey parrots, or pack-hunting canids. The proposal trades tangible, targeted animal welfare protections for symbolic, unworkable legal status that erodes legal coherence and undermines the very conservation goals both sides claim to support.
