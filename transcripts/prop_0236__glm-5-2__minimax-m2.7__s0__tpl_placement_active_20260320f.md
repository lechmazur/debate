[P1.1] Bans on generative AI in schools have already failed—not because enforcement is imperfect, but because enforcement is structurally impossible. A student can run a draft through a tool in seconds and submit polished work with no trace. Detection tools produce false positives that disproportionately flag non-native English speakers, while missing sophisticated users. Schools that ban AI by default are building a regime that cannot work, and the cost of pretending otherwise falls directly on the teacher-student relationship.
[P1.2] The proposition offers a better framework: permit AI use by default, but require citation and process transparency. This aligns with how students will actually work for the rest of their lives. Every professional field is integrating these tools. A student who graduates never having used AI critically is not more prepared—they are less prepared.
[P1.3] The question is not whether students will use these tools, but whether schools will teach them to use them honestly.
[P1.4] Transparency requirements do something bans cannot: they make AI use visible. When a student must disclose which tool they used, what they asked, and how they revised the output, the teacher can evaluate the thinking, not just the product. This is the same principle behind showing your work in math or citing sources in a research paper. We do not ban calculators because they can do arithmetic; we require students to demonstrate understanding.
[P1.5] Bans also create an equity problem. Students with private tutors and paid AI subscriptions will use these tools regardless of policy. A permission-by-default framework with transparency brings use into the open, where teachers can guide it. A ban enforced unevenly punishes the student who lacks resources to work around it.
[P1.6] The proposition is not permissive; it is rigorous. It demands more of students than a ban does, because it requires engagement with a tool they will use for decades—rather than pretending it does not exist.
