[P1.1] The motion challenges a doctrine, not a neutral policy choice. Treating money in politics as ordinary speech has produced a system where political access is effectively auctioned to the highest bidder. We propose a structural alternative: prohibit corporate political donations and cap large individual contributions at far lower levels. That is not radical; it is the basic restoration of democratic equality.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. When a corporation donates a million dollars, that money was extracted from customers through markups, from workers through wages, and from shareholders through returns. None of those groups consented to its use for political advocacy, and most would actively oppose it if asked. Corporate "speech" is therefore not the speech of any individual at all.
[P1.3] It is aggregated economic power translated into political influence without democratic accountability, and it almost always flows toward the policy preferences of the largest owners.
[P1.4] The individual-donor side is structurally identical, only smaller in scale. A citizen who can write a fifty-thousand-dollar check does not have fifty thousand times the speech of one who cannot; what they have is a louder microphone, immediate access, and the implicit promise of future access. Treating that as "ordinary speech" mistakes volume for voice. The wealthy donor's preference is more likely to be implemented not because it is better argued but because it is more costly to ignore.
[P1.5] Lowering the cap and banning corporate donations does not silence anyone. It removes the structural subsidy that converts wealth into political power. After reform, citizens compete on arguments and turnout rather than on bank balances. That is what treating political participation as citizenship rather than consumption actually looks like.
[P1.6] The opponent will defend the existing framework as principled. But the principle in question protects the economic power of large actors while denying working citizens any comparable lever. Formal equality of speech collapses into dramatic inequality of influence, and the policy results follow that imbalance: regulatory capture, revolving doors, and legislation that the median voter would never approve. The motion simply asks whether democracies should keep designing themselves [clipped]

[C1.1] Banning corporate donations and slashing individual caps does not equalise democracy. It decides who can be heard effectively, and hands the advantage to incumbents, celebrities, and self-funding millionaires who need no donors at all.
[C1.2] A donation is not buying a vote; it is how citizens who lack time, fame, or a platform pool resources to amplify an argument. Money is not the speech, any more than a printing press is, but without it speech reaches almost no one. A corporation is not a mysterious person. It is a legal form through which people associate — a family business, a trade group, a non-profit campaign organisation, as well as a large firm.
[C1.3] Shareholders choose directors and can sell; workers and customers can exit. Imperfect consent is still association, not theft. Banning that form while wealthy individuals and media owners speak freely discriminates by organisational shape, not equality.
[C1.4] Speech inequality is unavoidable and not itself corruption. Some citizens are more eloquent, have more free time, own newspapers, or command millions of followers. Democracy answers with one person, one vote, and voters free to reject a well-funded message. A state that levels volume must decide how much speech is enough — a power far more dangerous than a donor backing a candidate voters may still defeat.
[C1.5] Practically, bans do not remove money; they reroute it to less accountable places. Direct, disclosed donations to a candidate who must answer for them become independent campaigns, opaque groups, and personal spending by rich candidates whose own spending cannot sensibly be capped on the same terms.
[C1.6] Meanwhile lower caps cripple challengers. An incumbent has name recognition, staff, and constant coverage. A newcomer needs early, larger commitments to hire staff, travel, and be taken seriously. Without them, only the already famous or already rich compete.
[C1.7] Target bribery, enforce disclosure, punish quid pro quo. Do not treat participation as corruption and freeze politics in place.

We do not treat any spending that "amplifies speech" as immune from democratic rules. The "one person, one vote" rebuttal concedes the point. If voters reject the message only because it remains visible to them, then Con accepts that volume distorts the choice, which is precisely the corruption the motion targets.
[P2.4] On rerouting. Con argues bans push money into dark channels. That proves too much. The same logic would defeat every anti-bribery rule, every lobbying-disclosure law, every foreign-agent registry.
[P2.5] The rebuttal ignores that disclosure, enforced under the same regime, keeps direct donations visible while banning only the corporate treasury contribution. A disclosed capped donation to a candidate who must answer for it is more accountable, not less, than an opaque outside group.
[P2.6] On incumbents. This is the most serious objection, and it answers itself. Incumbents already enjoy name recognition, free media coverage, and established fundraising networks. The motion invites the standard remedy: small-donor matching funds, which historically help challengers most because they reward new, small contributions over established networks.
[P2.7] Freezing politics in place is what the current unlimited-corporate regime already does.
[P2.8] The clash now is whether democratic equality or incumbent entrenchment is the greater value. The motion wins that choice, because an incumbent protected by wealth barriers is no less a captured office than a regulatory agency captured by the industry it oversees.
