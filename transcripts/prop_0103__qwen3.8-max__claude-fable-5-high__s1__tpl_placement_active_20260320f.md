[P1.1] The strongest reason for a federal gun license is that point-of-sale screening is too narrow. Even when a background check occurs, it tests one moment: whether the buyer is currently prohibited. It does not require basic competence, safe handling, or knowledge of lawful use, and it does not create a durable mechanism to reassess eligibility after the sale. A license system makes the person, not just the transaction, the unit of regulation.
[P1.2] It would require identity verification, training, and eligibility before purchase, and it would give authorities a uniform standard for tracing guns when they are lost, stolen, or used in crimes. If an owner later becomes prohibited, the license can be denied, suspended, or revoked, instead of leaving the earlier sale as the only check.
[P1.3] Training matters because gun safety is not intuitive. Requiring basic instruction before ownership reduces accidental discharges, unsafe storage, and the kind of impulsive access that turns a household crisis into a tragedy. A license also creates a recurring opportunity to remind owners of safe storage and lawful use, something a single cashier moment cannot do.
[P1.4] The proposition does not ban firearms; it conditions purchase on the same kind of responsibility we already require for other dangerous instruments. We license drivers, require permits for concealed carry in many states, and demand safety training for activities far less lethal than owning a gun. The Second Amendment has never been treated as absolute, and a carefully administered federal license is a reasonable way to protect public safety while preserving lawful access.
[P1.5] Opponents will say licensing is bureaucratic or burdensome. But the real burden is the status quo: a patchwork where prohibited persons exploit loopholes, traffickers use straw purchasers, and families pay for preventable violence. A federal license with training and background checks is the minimum standard that point-of-sale screening alone cannot provide.

[C1.3] That is not a safety policy; it is a compliance tax on the innocent.
[C1.4] Second, conditioning an enumerated right on government permission has real victims. Licensing means fees, mandated courses, paperwork, and—crucially—waiting on a federal bureaucracy. Picture a woman leaving an abusive partner who needs protection this week, not after a multi-week federal processing queue. [C1.5] We do not require a licensed course before you may publish, worship, or refuse a search. The driver's-license analogy fails: driving on public roads is a privilege; keeping arms is a textual right, and courts have grown increasingly skeptical of exactly this kind of gatekeeping.
[C1.6] Third, PRO's genuine concerns have surgical answers. Post-sale changes in eligibility? Red-flag laws and improved prohibited-persons reporting to NICS handle that without licensing every hunter in Montana. Straw purchasing?
[C1.7] Prosecute it—it is already a felony. Safe storage? Incentivize training with tax credits rather than mandating it as a precondition of a right.
[C1.8] The comparison judges should hold: PRO must show licensing meaningfully reduces criminal access. If crime guns bypass the system, all that remains is cost, delay, and exclusion borne entirely by lawful citizens.
