[P1.1] Infertility is not a lifestyle preference or a disappointment to be endured privately. It is a medical condition in which a core bodily system does not function — ovaries do not release eggs, sperm are absent or damaged, fallopian tubes are blocked by disease, endometriosis destroys tissue. We diagnose its causes with blood tests, scans, and surgery, and we treat it with medicine and procedures. That is healthcare by any honest definition.
[P1.2] Basic healthcare has never meant only care that keeps you alive until tomorrow. Health systems routinely fund treatment for conditions that cause pain, disability, loss of function, and psychological harm: joint replacement for arthritis, treatment for depression, surgery for cataracts, care for sexual dysfunction. Infertility fits that pattern exactly, and often combines all of those harms. Excluding it does not reflect a medical distinction; it reflects an outdated judgment that reproduction is optional for the patient even when the body is diseased.
[P1.3] That exclusion creates a brutal inequity. When IVF is left to the private market, the same diagnosis leads to a baby for a wealthy couple and to debt, delay, or childlessness for a poorer one. Age makes delay decisive, so those who must save for years lose their best chance while they wait. A condition’s treatability should not depend on income, postcode, or an employer’s insurance plan.
[P1.4] Treating infertility as basic healthcare simply removes reproduction from the list of functions we restore only for those who can pay.
[P1.5] This does not require unlimited, on-demand IVF for every circumstance. Basic care everywhere is prioritized by clinical need, age, prognosis, and number of attempts. Countries can fund assessment and proven treatment, set transparent limits, and require single-embryo transfer to reduce costly multiple pregnancies. That is responsible medicine, not a blank check.
[P1.6] A health system that repairs hearts, knees, eyes, and minds but tells patients with diseased reproductive systems to buy their own cure has drawn a line morality cannot defend and medicine does not support.

[C1.1] Basic healthcare is a promise a country makes to everyone who qualifies, paid from shared resources and ranked ahead of care it will refuse. That promise belongs to treatment that prevents death, limits serious disability, or restores ordinary function at a cost the system can sustain. IVF does not qualify.
[C1.2] Some causes of infertility deserve ordinary medicine: infection, hormonal disease, painful endometriosis. IVF is not that treatment. It is a laboratory bypass — eggs retrieved, fertilized outside the body, embryos transferred in hope of pregnancy. The reproductive system is not repaired.
[C1.3] Success is uncertain and often needs repeated cycles. The aim is a child, not the patient's recovery, and pregnancy itself adds medical risk. A legitimate service is not the same as a basic entitlement.
[C1.4] Calling this the equal of joint replacement or cataract surgery confuses a wished-for outcome with restored health. A person with blocked tubes or poor sperm quality can live a full life, work, and stay well. What is missing is a particular life project, not a functioning body in the sense medicine is obliged to repair. Hips, eyes, and depression treatment return capacity to the patient, usually with reliable benefit.
[C1.5] IVF buys a chance that someone else will exist.
[C1.6] Budgets are finite. Every publicly funded cycle is a bed, a nurse, or a drug budget taken from a patient whose need is more urgent. Age caps and attempt limits do not erase that trade-off; they only select which hopeful patients consume the scarce capacity.
[C1.7] Unequal private access is real. It is not a reason to redefine basic care. States already decline to socialize many valued goods. The harsher inequity is the one created when primary care, emergency treatment, or chronic-disease management is thinned to subsidize an expensive, low-yield route to parenthood.
[C1.8] Fund diagnosis and disease treatment. Do not treat the creation of children as the same public obligation.

[P2.1] Their definition collapses under the examples used to support it. Basic care is not limited to treatment that repairs an organ or prevents death. A coronary bypass does not unblock the original artery; dialysis does not heal kidneys; insulin does not cure the pancreas; a prosthetic hip replaces rather than restores tissue. Medicine constantly bypasses or compensates for failed function.
[P2.2] IVF does the same for failed ovaries, tubes, or sperm production. Calling it a laboratory bypass describes how it works, not why it is not healthcare.
[P2.3] The claim that infertile patients can live and work proves too much. So can people with cataracts, arthritis, hearing loss, and many forms of depression — all routinely treated because health is more than survival and employability. Reproduction is an ordinary bodily function, and its loss causes distress and relationship strain no less real for being private. If a full life is possible without treatment, that argues against half the basic-care catalogue, not a principled line around fertility.
[P2.4] Nor does aiming at pregnancy place IVF outside patient care. The patient is stimulated, monitored, and given the capacity her body lacks to conceive. Obstetric care is publicly funded even though pregnancy is not a disease and its benefits include the child. Medicine values treatment by the functioning it makes possible, not by whether tissue looks repaired afterward.
[P2.5] Uncertainty and cost require prioritisation, not exclusion. Chemotherapy and antidepressants can also fail or need repetition. Eligibility based on age, prognosis, and limited cycles directs funding toward reasonable chances and controls multiple pregnancy through single-embryo transfer. Comparing IVF only with emergency care evades the real question: whether it belongs alongside other funded, non-emergency treatments restoring function and relieving harm.
[P2.6] By those criteria, it does.
[P2.7] Finally, funding diagnosis while refusing the only effective treatment for many causes is rationing by wealth with a medical label. It tells a poorer patient exactly why she cannot conceive, then reserves the remedy for a richer patient with the same disease.

[C2.1] Their bypass examples cut the wrong way. A bypass graft, dialysis, insulin, or a prosthetic hip substitutes for a function the patient keeps exercising. The beneficiary is that patient. IVF is a medical procedure, but it does not leave anyone able to conceive.
[C2.2] Blocked tubes stay blocked; sperm quality is unchanged. The cycle has worked only if another person comes to exist. That is not restored function. It is an attempt to produce a child.
[C2.3] Obstetric funding does not bridge the gap. We treat a pregnancy that already exists because a patient now needs care. Managing pregnancy is not a warrant to manufacture it.
[C2.4] The full-life reply fails too. Cataracts, arthritis, hearing loss, and depression impair the patient's own capacity to see, move, hear, or think. Disease in the reproductive system can do that, and that disease belongs in basic care. Once pain and pathology are treated, IVF does not return a capacity the patient will use.
[C2.5] It answers grief over an unmet life project, not impaired function.
[C2.6] Chemotherapy and antidepressants do not rescue the analogy. They target illness in the patient; repetition is justified because the illness remains. IVF is repeated because a child has not appeared. Caps and cycle limits do not change that aim.
[C2.7] They ration an expensive hope while still taking staff, theatre time, and drugs from patients whose own health is at stake. Placing IVF beside other non-emergency care, as [P2.5] urges, hides the ranking basic care requires.
[C2.8] Diagnosis without a funded cycle is the line between treating disease and socializing parenthood, not rationing by wealth in disguise. Infection, hormonal failure, and painful endometriosis stay ordinary medicine. Where the remaining desire is a genetically related child, unequal private access is inequality in a valued good. A system can explain the diagnosis, treat the disease, and still refuse to promise every qualifying citizen a publicly funded child.

[P4.1] A1: No. A successful cycle usually does not let her conceive unaided next month, and it need not. Dialysis does not let kidneys work between sessions, insulin does not let the pancreas work between doses, and a bypass graft does not reopen the artery it routes around. Those are basic because they supply a missing bodily function when needed, not because they permanently cure its cause. IVF does the same at the decisive step: eggs and sperm meet because medicine compensates for tubes, ovaries, or sperm that cannot achieve that, and pregnancy then occurs in the patient.
[P4.2] It has worked when her body achieves conception and gestation with assistance, just as dialysis works while performing filtration. The child is the result of that reproductive function, not a reason to redefine it away.
[P4.3] A2: It obliges a country to include assessment and a reasonable, limited number of funded cycles for patients with diagnosed infertility and a reasonable chance of success, allocated by transparent clinical criteria rather than wealth. Basic care has never meant identical, unlimited provision for every diagnosis. Transplants depend on suitability and supply, joint replacements on fitness and severity, and many drugs on likely response. Age, prognosis, and attempt limits are how a universal service stays fair and sustainable; categorical exclusion is how ability to pay becomes the real eligibility test.
[P4.4] Those answers expose why the opposing line fails. They accept treating endometriosis, infection, or hormonal failure even when the point is fertility, yet call treatment non-medical when IVF is the effective route. The impairment has not changed; only the technique and price have. Calling the harm mere grief over a life project also understates a diagnosed loss of bodily function, and ignores that health systems treat many non-lethal losses because they deeply restrict lives.
[P4.5] Scarcity justifies limits, not redefinition. Fund IVF as basic care: bounded, clinically governed, and available on need — not sold to those who can reach it before time runs out.
