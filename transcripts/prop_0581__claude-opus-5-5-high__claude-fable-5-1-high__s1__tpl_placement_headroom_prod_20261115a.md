[P1.1] Every merger comes with a promise: bigger means cheaper, faster, more efficient. The question is who actually collects those gains. Decades of American experience answer it: shareholders and executives collect, consumers pay. Stricter enforcement against horizontal and vertical deals fixes that, on three grounds.
[P1.2] First, prices. Hospital consolidation is the clearest case. Retrospective research has repeatedly found that hospital mergers in concentrated local markets raise prices, often substantially, without measurable quality improvement. Those costs flow straight into insurance premiums and wages.
[P1.3] Airlines tell the same story: a wave of legacy-carrier mergers left a handful of airlines controlling most domestic traffic, and routes that lose a competitor tend to see higher fares and fewer seats. When rivals disappear, the discipline on pricing disappears with them.
[P1.4] Second, innovation. Competition is what forces firms to invent; monopoly lets them coast. Economists have documented "killer acquisitions," where pharmaceutical incumbents buy startups with overlapping drug candidates and quietly shelve them. In tech, dominant platforms have absorbed nascent rivals like Instagram before they could mature into challengers.
[P1.5] Vertical deals add another danger: a firm that owns a bottleneck, whether a distribution channel or a key input, can degrade access for rivals and starve the independent innovators who would otherwise displace it.
[P1.6] Third, the claimed efficiencies are routinely overstated. Many mergers fail to deliver promised synergies even by their own investors' standards. More importantly, strict enforcement does not ban scale; it bans buying scale by eliminating competitors. Firms can still grow internally, sign supply contracts, or form joint ventures.
[P1.7] And the deals stricter enforcement actually catches sit in already concentrated markets, where scale economies are largely exhausted.
[P1.8] Finally, weigh the asymmetry. Wrongly blocking a merger costs some delay while the firm grows another way. Wrongly approving one is nearly permanent, because a merged company is almost impossible to unscramble. When errors are this lopsided, caution protects consumers.

Vertical integration typically lowers prices by eliminating double marginalization: two separate firms each marking up, versus one firm pricing once. [C1.4] Now innovation. The overwhelming majority of startup exits are acquisitions, not IPOs. Venture capital funds a drug candidate or a software startup precisely because an incumbent might buy it. [C1.5] Killer acquisitions are a small minority of deals even in pharma, and policing them requires targeted scrutiny, not a blanket presumption against scale.
[C1.6] On productivity: when a better-managed firm acquires plants, the acquired plants tend to become more productive. That is the mechanism by which best practices spread across an economy. "Grow internally instead" ignores that building a second factory from scratch wastes capital that acquisition would have redeployed.
[C1.7] Finally, the asymmetry argument cuts the other way. Wrongly blocked deals are also permanent: the failing firm closes, the drug candidate dies in a startup that can't fund Phase III. And markets do unscramble dominance; Instagram's supposed lock-in didn't stop TikTok. Error costs run both directions, and strict enforcement guarantees the one we can see.
