Convenience is not a technical specification. It shifts with queues, disability, weather, and dead phones. No operator can know whether a regulator or court will later call its card lane insufficiently convenient.
[C1.2] That is a permanent legal cloud over every biometric deployment, including optional fast lanes. PRO says the motion bans nothing; in practice it chills adoption through compliance risk.
[C1.3] Biometrics are not just a toy for gadget lovers. They can be an access route for someone whose card was stolen, whose phone died, who has no bank account, who finds PIN pads difficult, or who is a visitor without the local payment app. A well-designed palm or face system can reduce dependence on carrying anything. Demanding duplicate “always convenient” infrastructure at every site raises costs and liability, so fewer operators will offer the option at all.
[C1.4] The losers are the people who benefit from a second way to pay.
[C1.5] The permanence argument is real but does not support this motion. Biometric data is sensitive; it should be encrypted, stored locally where possible, deletable, audited, and backed by strict liability for misuse. We do not respond to card fraud by requiring every card reader to have a permanently staffed cash lane. The proposition singles out one technology while ignoring the failures of cards, phones, and cash.
[C1.6] The real wrong is coercion, and it can be prohibited directly. No essential transport or public service should deny access to someone who refuses biometrics. But a stadium offering face entry as a faster lane alongside ticket scanning is choice, even if the other lane is slower. The “always convenient” test cannot tell the difference.
[C1.7] Targeted anti-coercion rules and security standards protect consumers without freezing out a method that can broaden access. That is why the proposition should be rejected.
