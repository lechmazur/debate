[P1.1] Single-family-only zoning is a government mandate of scarcity. It tells most residential land in many cities that no matter how severe the housing shortage, only one detached house per lot is allowed. That drives up land costs, pushes workers farther from jobs, enforces car dependence, and locks in a pattern rooted in racial and class exclusion. You cannot solve a shortage while the default law forbids more homes where homes already exist.
[P1.2] Incremental density bonuses sound reasonable but preserve the problem. They keep exclusion as the rule and treat extra homes as a favor to be earned through lotteries, owner-occupancy requirements, extra parking, design review, or fees. Uptake stays tiny, concentrated among affluent homeowners who can navigate the paperwork for an accessory unit, while renters get almost nothing. Builders still face uncertainty parcel by parcel, so financing stays risky and small apartment buildings that used to house teachers and mechanics remain illegal on most blocks.
[P1.3] An outright ban on new single-family-only districts flips the default. Every residential area allows at least small multi-unit homes — duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes — by right. No one is forced to build them, neighborhoods are not bulldozed overnight, but anyone can. That broad legalization is what lowers the per-unit cost of land, lets many small builders add homes incrementally, and spreads growth instead of shoving all apartments onto noisy arterials.
[P1.4] That is also a fairness issue. When only wealthy subdivisions keep exclusive rules while lower-income corridors absorb all density, that is not preserving character, it is hoarding opportunity. Freedom to build modest density everywhere will produce more homes quickly and quietly in a few years than a decade of narrow carve-outs. Ending single-family-only zoning ends that two-tier system, cuts commuting emissions, and restores a basic property right: to house more than one family on your land.

[C1.1] The choice on the table is not "unlimited housing vs. exclusionary zoning." Both sides agree single-family-only zoning is bad. The real question is which reform actually survives contact with politics and delivers housing without wrecking the coalition needed to keep building. That question favors CON.
[C1.2] First, blanket bans are politically fragile. A citywide mandate that abolishes single-family zoning overnight hands opponents a single, simple target: repeal it, and the whole reform dies at once. We have already seen this pattern — sweeping upzonings get challenged in court, put to referendum, or rolled back by the next council after a backlash election. Incremental density bonuses, by contrast, are hard to repeal piecemeal because they are built parcel by parcel into existing permitting systems.
[C1.3] A policy that survives ten years of small wins beats a policy that gets reversed after one bad election cycle.
[C1.4] Second, bans ignore capacity. Sewer lines, school enrollment, street parking, and fire access were sized for one household per lot. A blanket legalization triggers duplexes and fourplexes everywhere at once with no mechanism to pace infrastructure investment or extract funding for it. Density bonus programs routinely tie extra units to impact fees or affordable-unit set-asides — they capture some of the land-value windfall created by upzoning and put it toward the very services new residents need.
[C1.5] A pure ban gives that windfall away for nothing.
[C1.6] Third, "by right everywhere" is not the same as "built everywhere." Cities that have tried near-total legalization still see modest uptake in the first years, because construction is limited by materials, labor, and financing, not just zoning code. If uptake is going to be gradual either way, the design question is whether gradual growth comes with public benefits attached or without them. Density bonuses answer that question correctly.
[C1.7] CON's case is simple: durable, funded, locally calibrated reform beats a sweeping mandate that risks backlash, repeal, and infrastructure strain — and still produces housing at roughly the same real-world pace.

[P2.1] Calling a clean ban fragile gets politics exactly backwards.
[P2.2] A single rule — no neighborhood may forbid two to four homes — is visible, simple to defend, and quickly creates thousands of owners whose property gained flexibility. A bonus program is a maze of conditions: design review, extra parking, owner-occupancy, discretionary approval. Opponents never need a dramatic repeal; they just tighten one screw until uptake collapses to zero. That is why so-called durable incrementalism so often durably produces almost nothing, while leaving exclusion as the legal norm.
[P2.3] The infrastructure warning also misreads scale. Ending single-family-only does not order towers everywhere; it lets a duplex or fourplex fit where one house stood, using the same street frontage and the same pipes, and only when an owner already chooses to rebuild. Turnover is naturally paced because just a few percent of lots redevelop each year. There is no everywhere-at-once surge to outrun sewers or schools.
[P2.4] Bonus schemes do not solve capacity either; they load the same bedrooms with extra fees that kill small projects, blocking the new taxpayers who would fund upgrades.
[P2.5] Nor does a ban give away a windfall for nothing. The largest windfall today flows to owners of artificially scarce single-family lots. Broad permission dilutes that scarcity premium and cuts land cost per door, which is how a teacher or mechanic gets housed without waiting for a subsidized lottery unit. Capturing value by taxing the few homes that survive a bonus gauntlet leaves the city with neither homes nor revenue.
[P2.6] Finally, the claim that building will be gradual either way concedes the argument. If crews and loans limit pace, eligibility decides output. Fifty lots using a bonus cannot compete with fifty thousand lots where small builders are simply allowed to work. Same labor, vastly more shots on goal, spread across neighborhoods instead of dumped on loud arterials.
[P2.7] That is why broad legalization beats narrow permission: it makes modest density normal, not begged for.
