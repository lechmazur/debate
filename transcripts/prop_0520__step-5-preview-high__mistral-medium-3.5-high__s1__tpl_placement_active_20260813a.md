[P1.1] The motion asks what immigration policy should do first: recognize professional credentials quickly, or add another layer of labor-market testing. PRO says recognition. Not because border flows don’t matter, but because the current bottleneck isn’t the number of entrants; it’s the waste. Thousands of foreign-trained nurses, engineers, accountants, and teachers arrive with skills their new country urgently needs, then spend months or years proving what their transcripts, licenses, and experience already show.
[P1.2] Tighter labor-market testing sounds protective, but it is a blunt, time-lagged instrument. It asks employers to navigate advertisements, prevailing-wage calculations, and delay, while the candidate waits. The result is predictable: skilled migrants exit into sectors where their credentials are irrelevant—driving, cleaning, food service—or they don’t come at all.
[P1.3] Meanwhile hospitals in underserved regions post vacancies, and construction firms delay projects. The economy loses twice: the productive output the migrant could have generated, and the service quality local residents go without.
[P1.4] Faster credential recognition changes the default. It makes competence the entry ticket, not paperwork endurance. When a jurisdiction can verify a license or accredited degree through a transparent, expedited assessment, it can place that person in the role the labor market is actually signaling—through shortages, regional postings, and wage pressure—without demanding a redundant, months-long bureaucratic ritual. The migrant’s first contribution becomes tax revenue and patient care rather than subsidized underemployment.
[P1.5] The counterargument says testing protects domestic workers. But labor-market testing rarely distinguishes who is truly harmed; it mostly distinguishes who has time. A domestic applicant with equal credentials competes best when the process is fast, transparent, and merit-based. Delay and opacity favor incumbency, not fairness.
[P1.6] Recognition isn’t a loophole; it is a signal. It says: if you have the training, we will let you use it. That is the priority this policy should set.

[C1.3] Far from being blunt, modern labor-market testing can precisely target regional shortages, wage benchmarks, and specific skill gaps. [C1.4] The PRO frames underemployment of skilled migrants as the core waste, but this ignores the larger risk: domestic professionals displaced by foreign competition, and sectors distorted by mismatched supply.
