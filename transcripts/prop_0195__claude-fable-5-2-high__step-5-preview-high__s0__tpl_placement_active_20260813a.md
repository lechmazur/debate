[P1.1] Every regulatory decision can fail in two ways: by approving something harmful, or by blocking something beneficial. The precautionary principle, as commonly applied, counts only the first. It demands that innovators prove safety before proceeding, treats uncertainty itself as grounds for prohibition, and never asks what the delay costs. That one-sided ledger is why it blocks more good than it prevents harm.
[P1.2] The asymmetry is structural. When an approved product hurts people, there are victims, headlines and hearings. [C1.1] The proposition asks us to weigh foregone benefits against prevented harms as if both were settled facts. They are not. The precautionary principle does not ban innovation; it forces uncertainty into the open before irreversible risks are externalized onto the public. The real question is not whether delay has costs—it does—but whether the record shows that this uncertainty review is the primary reason beneficial technologies vanish, or whether it is the reason catastrophic harms never reach the market.
[C1.2] Precaution is a screening mechanism, not a prohibition. When the pharmaceutical industry must prove safety before release, that does not block more cures than it prevents poisonings; it prevents thalidomide-scale tragedies and keeps public trust in medicine intact. When chemicals are tested before dispersal, we avoid the slow accumulation of persistent pollutants that no later remedy can reverse. When nuclear and biological materials are controlled, we avoid contamination that no cleanup fund can fully repair.
[C1.3] These harms are invisible in the historical record precisely because precaution stopped them before they materialized. The proposition mistakes this invisibility for absence.
[C1.4] The alleged blocked innovations often collapse under scrutiny. Golden Rice faced not only biosafety review but intellectual-property, agricultural, and trade barriers; nuclear power struggles with waste and cost questions that no amount of regulatory liberalization solves. GM crop restrictions in Africa reflected trade alignment with Europe more than pure bioscientific precaution. These are complex policy failures, not proof that precaution per se blocks more good than harm.
[C1.5] Indeed, when a technology is genuinely safe and beneficial, it typically clears precautionary review once the evidence matures—what precaution delays, it rarely destroys.
[C1.6] If we abandon precaution, the innovation that survives is the innovation that externalizes risk fastest. That is not beneficial innovation; it is a race to the bottom. The ledger favors precaution because it protects the conditions—public trust, environmental stability, human health—under which any innovation can flourish. Count the real harms, not the ghosts of inventions we imagine we lost.
