[P1.1] Religious exemptions from anti-discrimination law make sense only when they protect actual worship and doctrine, not commercial privilege. The proposition draws the right line: exemptions should be tightly limited to core religious functions.
[P1.2] Anti-discrimination rules exist so people can work, buy goods, receive services, and join public life without exclusion for race, sex, religion, disability, or sexual orientation. Market participation is not a private sacrament. A church choosing its pastor exercises religion. A religious hospital refusing a qualified nurse, or a faith-based university denying student housing, because of identity is commercial conduct in religious dress.
[P1.3] Core functions are identifiable: selecting ministers, conducting worship, teaching doctrine to adherents, and administering sacraments. These define a religious community. A ministerial exception already tracks that boundary. Extending the same shield to janitors, accountants, retail clerks, patients, customers, and tenants turns conscience protection into a license to exclude.
[P1.4] Once an institution sells services, competes for customers, or employs people who do not preach the faith, it has entered the market. Market rules include equal treatment.
[P1.5] Broad exemptions let organizations take public money, tax advantages, and paying clients while rejecting the duty not to discriminate. That is not neutrality. It subordinates employees and customers to a preference the religion does not need in order to exist. Tight exemptions keep what is genuinely religious and refuse what is merely advantageous.
[P1.6] Wide commercial carve-outs protect market power, not belief. Religious liberty stays intact when a congregation governs its pulpit. It should not expand so that the same congregation's bookstore, clinic, or catering arm may refuse service every comparable business must provide. The line is administrable and necessary.
[P1.7] Exemptions belong where religion is practiced, not where it is branded onto commerce.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes religion stops at the sanctuary door. For many faiths, it begins there. Feeding the hungry, healing the sick, teaching children, and placing orphans are not commercial add-ons to worship; they are worship in action. A Catholic hospital, a Jewish school, or a Muslim relief agency does not become a secular business because it charges fees, hires staff, or serves strangers.
[C1.2] Its commercial form is how a ministry survives.
[C1.3] “Tightly tied to core functions” therefore asks the state to do theology. Is a faith-based school’s math teacher core if she leads prayer? Is a nurse core if care is offered as witness? Is adoption core if a faith teaches family formation?
[C1.4] Judges and regulators would have to rank doctrines, decide which activities are really religious, and reward traditions that privatize belief while punishing those that serve outwardly. That is not a neutral line; it is government preference for a narrow, interior style of religion.
[C1.5] Broad institutional protection is also what preserves pluralism for everyone else. Religious providers run schools, clinics, shelters, and charities because their convictions differ. Force them to hire against their mission, provide services that contradict it, or house and celebrate arrangements it rejects, and many will not secularize — they will close, withdraw from public partnerships, or stop serving the general public. The losers are not abstract institutions.
[C1.6] They are patients with fewer clinics, parents with fewer schools, and homeless people with fewer beds, replaced by thinner, state-approved uniformity.
[C1.7] Equal access does not require identical rules for every employer and seller. Employees and customers are not trapped; they can work for and patronize countless secular and differently religious organizations, while a believer has only institutions that actually embody her faith. Anti-discrimination law should stop invidious exclusion in the general market, not conscript religious communities as its enforcers inside their own missions. Exemption should follow sincere religious identity and mission, not survive only when a bureaucrat labels an act a sacrament.

[C2.2] A school does not deliver mathematics plus optional prayer; it forms children through adults trusted to model the faith. A hospital's nurses counsel families and enact commitments about life and suffering. Replace that community with staff required to be indifferent or hostile to its mission, and the ministry has not remained intact. Only the service counter has.

Must a judge decide whether a teacher who leads chapel personifies faith, but one who mentors and prays with students does not? [C2.4] PRO asks courts to rank which religious roles count, and draw the theology themselves.
[C2.5] Nor does entering the market surrender that identity. [P2.3] treats fees, payroll, and public partnerships as a waiver, but religious charities have long served outsiders because their faith commands outward service. Serving the public is not evidence the work stopped being religious; often it is the reason it is religious.
[C2.6] Finally, [P2.6]'s local-provider concern does not justify a core-only rule. If the fear is no alternative care or schooling, address that scarcity directly; do not redefine every religious employer everywhere. Closures are not a 'veto' — they are the predictable result of telling an institution its character is unlawful. The public then loses the provider, worse than a plural system where distinct institutions serve people in distinct ways.

[C4.5] PRO protects religion only when it looks like clergy and worship. CON protects it also when it feeds, teaches, and heals as a community — while leaving the vast secular market, and non-mission discrimination, fully regulated. That is pluralism without government ranking of ministries.
