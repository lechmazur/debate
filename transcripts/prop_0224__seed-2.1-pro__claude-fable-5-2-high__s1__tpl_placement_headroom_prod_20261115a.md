[P1.1] The 2026–2036 window is a tight decade where emission reductions delivered late barely count, and public dollars wasted on stranded, delayed, or incomplete infrastructure deliver zero climate benefit. Life extension for existing nuclear plants is the single fastest, lowest-cost zero-emission resource available per public dollar: these plants already connect to the grid, employ trained local workforces, and run at 90%+ capacity factors around the clock, no weather backup required. A typical license extension and targeted equipment upgrade delivers firm zero-emission power for less than a quarter of the total system cost of matching that output with new wind, solar, and batteries, once you account for required redundant overbuild of variable generation, multi-hour battery storage, and thousands of miles of new long-distance transmission that routinely takes a decade or more to permit and build, eating straight into the critical 10-year window.
[P1.2] Public dollars for new nuclear builds already under construction or in late permitting will also deliver firm, dispatchable zero-emission power within the window, without the hidden system costs that erode the emission value of wind and solar. By contrast, public spending on wind, solar, and battery scale-up routinely gets stuck in multi-year interconnection queues, faces local land-use pushback, and relies on battery supply chains that will remain constrained by mineral and manufacturing bottlenecks well into the 2030s. Grid operators across major markets have found that even record wind and solar build rates leave persistent fossil generation gaps during extended calm, cloudy periods that current short-duration batteries cannot cover.
[P1.3] Every public dollar tied up in those delays is a dollar that does not cut emissions during the window, while every dollar spent extending existing nuclear, or completing already permitted new nuclear, avoids those displacement gaps that wind and solar cannot fill fast enough at equivalent public cost.
