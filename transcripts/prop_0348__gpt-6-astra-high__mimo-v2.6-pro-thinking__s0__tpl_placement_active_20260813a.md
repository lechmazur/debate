[P1.1] Patients already make triage decisions before a clinician sees them: whether to wait, search online, call a clinic, or seek urgent help. The choice is not AI versus an immediately available doctor. It is an approved, integrated route into care versus the delays and guesswork patients already face. Health systems should make that route available, not discourage it.
[P1.2] The key distinction is a front door, not a gatekeeper. Patients should be able to use a tool assessed for symptom-checking and triage, with a direct route to human help and no requirement to accept its recommendation. Its job is not to deliver a definitive diagnosis. It is to gather relevant information, identify possible urgency, and connect the patient to an appropriate next step.
[P1.3] That can improve access in practical ways. Someone developing symptoms after office hours can receive structured guidance rather than navigate scattered search results. A patient who struggles to explain their concerns can arrive with an organized symptom history. Where scheduling is integrated, advice can lead directly to an appointment rather than another phone queue.
[P1.4] These are useful functions even when a clinician must make the eventual medical judgment.
[P1.5] Safety is the strongest reason to integrate these tools responsibly rather than push patients away from them. Approval is not a guarantee of perfection: systems should require clear limits, conservative handling of uncertainty, human escalation, and monitoring for missed urgency, unnecessary referrals, and unequal performance. A tool that performs poorly should be restricted or withdrawn. Discouragement does not remove patients’ need for guidance; it can leave that need to less accountable alternatives.
[P1.6] The opposing case must therefore show more than that AI can make mistakes. It must show that discouraging approved tools produces a safer, more accessible pathway than offering them with oversight and preserved human access. We should judge the whole patient journey, not demand perfection from one doorway while overlooking the obstacles outside it.
