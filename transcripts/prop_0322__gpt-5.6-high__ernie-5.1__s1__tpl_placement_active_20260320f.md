[P1.1] A host-read advertisement sells more than a product; it sells the host’s personal credibility. Listeners reasonably infer that the familiar person actually spoke those words and, in an endorsement, chose to attach their judgment and reputation to the claim. When an AI clone performs the read, or a script deliberately replicates the host’s linguistic likeness without genuine authorship, that inference may be false. Disclosure is therefore not a technical footnote.
[P1.2] It corrects a deception about who is speaking and whose judgment consumers are being asked to trust.
[P1.3] That distinction can change consumer behavior. Imagine hearing a trusted health podcaster’s voice say, “I use this supplement every morning.” If the creator never recorded that sentence—or if software generated endless personalized versions—the audience deserves to know before treating it as firsthand testimony. The same applies to video avatars whose expressions, timing, and delivery create the appearance of a real endorsement. Synthetic production is not inherently wrong; disguising it as authentic testimony is.
[P1.4] A legal rule is necessary because voluntary disclosure conflicts with the advertiser’s incentive. The commercial value of cloning a host lies partly in preserving the persuasive illusion while reducing the host’s involvement. Ethical creators may label synthetic reads, but less scrupulous actors gain an advantage by staying silent. A common requirement prevents honesty from becoming a competitive handicap and gives platforms, regulators, and consumers a clear baseline.
[P1.5] The burden is exceptionally modest: a brief spoken statement, visible label, or comparably clear notice. It does not ban AI, dictate creative content, or require creators to reveal every editing tool. It targets a material substitution at the exact point where identity and authenticity drive the sale.
[P1.6] Without disclosure, creators can plausibly deny words spoken in their likeness, advertisers can manufacture personal enthusiasm at scale, and audiences cannot distinguish testimony from simulation. With disclosure, creators retain powerful production tools while consumers retain the information needed to evaluate persuasion. When technology can reproduce trust without reproducing the human act that earned it, the law should require the replica to identify itself.
