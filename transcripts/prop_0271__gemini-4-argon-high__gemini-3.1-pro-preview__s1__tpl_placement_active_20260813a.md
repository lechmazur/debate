[P1.1] Right now, if a parent wants their thirteen-year-old to watch a math tutorial or message soccer teammates on a major platform, they face a coercive choice: ban the app entirely and isolate the child, or accept the behavioral slot machine bundled inside it. App makers offer superficial parental controls—like screen-time timers or profanity filters—while guarding the lever that actually drives compulsive use: the algorithmic recommendation engine.
[P1.2] Parents need the power to unbundle utility from manipulation. Today, even inside family-supervision modes, platforms routinely treat algorithmic discovery as an inseparable core feature rather than an optional setting. True parental power means a genuine toggle to kill autoplay, block personalized "For You" feeds, and restrict apps to search-only or chronological updates from real-world friends.
[P1.3] We cannot leave these options to app makers’ discretion because their revenue model is structurally hostile to parental restraint. Recommendation engines are built to maximize adolescent time-on-device, not well-being. Left to design their own safety dashboards, platforms predictably offer PR-friendly placebos while keeping the engagement engine locked on. Keyword filters may catch explicit language, but they do not stop an algorithm from noticing a vulnerable preteen lingering on videos about extreme dieting or despair, and then flooding their screen with similar content to capture attention.
[P1.4] Moreover, control must align with responsibility and local knowledge. When an algorithmic feed wrecks a child’s sleep, fractures their focus, or triggers an anxiety spiral, software executives do not pay the therapy bills or comfort that child at two in the morning—parents do. Parents also understand context that no platform possesses: they know whether their child has ADHD, struggles with body image, or simply is not developmentally ready for endless-scroll persuasion.
[P1.5] Empowering parents to delay or disable algorithmic feeds does not cut children off from the modern world. Instead, it strips away predatory design so technology remains a tool kids use intentionally, rather than a system that uses them.

Handing parents a sledgehammer to smash algorithmic feeds does not create a safe digital environment; it breaks core functionality and incentivizes dangerous workarounds.
[C1.2] First, algorithms are not merely "behavioral slot machines"—they are the essential infrastructure of discovery. A purely search-based or chronological web assumes users already know exactly what they are looking for. For a teenager developing new interests, algorithms surface educational creators, niche hobbies, and supportive communities they would never know to type into a search bar. By forcing apps into a stripped-down, search-only mode, parents do not just block the harmful content PRO cites; they block the serendipitous discovery of science channels, art tutorials, and vital support networks for marginalized youth who rely on algorithmic curation to find peers.
[C1.3] Second, granting parents the power to drastically degrade an app’s core functionality breeds an adversarial dynamic. When parents unilaterally cripple how platforms operate, teens do not cheerfully accept this neutered "utility." Instead, they borrow friends' devices, create unmonitored burner accounts, and move their digital lives entirely into the shadows. True parental supervision requires visibility and dialogue.
[C1.4] Expanding granular restriction tools simply drives adolescent online activity underground, precisely where parents cannot help them navigate what they encounter.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO’s approach lets tech companies off the hook by privatizing a systemic problem. Framing digital safety as a matter of "parental power" shifts the immense burden of algorithmic moderation onto already exhausted parents. Instead of forcing platforms to adopt systemic, age-appropriate design codes that make algorithms safe by default for *all* children, PRO’s model demands every parent become a full-time systems administrator. We need algorithms that are fundamentally safe for minors at the platform level, not a dizzying dashboard of toggles that provides an illusion of control for tech-savvy parents while leaving everyone else's children behind.
