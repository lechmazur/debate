[P1.1] Irregular migration is, at its core, a labor market operating without a license. Farms, construction sites, slaughterhouses and hotel kitchens need workers; workers abroad need wages. When the law offers no door, that match still happens, just through the desert and through a smuggler. The question is which policy actually changes that behavior, and the answer is the one that changes the incentive rather than the obstacle course.
[P1.2] Start with mechanism. Enforcement raises the price of crossing, but it does not touch the reason for crossing. A worker facing a wage many times higher than at home will pay a higher smuggler fee, try a second time, or take a more dangerous route. A visa changes the calculation itself: why pay thousands and risk your life when you can board a bus with papers, work the harvest, and go home?
[P1.3] History bears this out. When the United States massively expanded Bracero visas in the 1950s, border apprehensions collapsed within a few years. When that program ended in the mid-1960s, with demand for labor unchanged, unauthorized crossings climbed for decades. More recently, as seasonal visas for Mexican workers grew substantially, irregular Mexican labor migration fell to a fraction of its earlier scale.
[P1.4] Now look at enforcement's record. From the 1990s onward, the United States multiplied its border budget and agent numbers many times over. The undocumented population did not shrink; it roughly tripled. Why?
[P1.5] Because a harder border made leaving riskier than staying. Men who once came for a season and returned home stopped circulating and settled, then brought their families. Enforcement converted temporary workers into permanent undocumented residents and handed smugglers a lucrative monopoly.
[P1.6] One tool drains the demand for irregular entry; the other makes it costlier while locking people in. Walls redirect flows. Visas replace them.
