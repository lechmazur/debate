[P1.1] Flaring is not energy production; it is energy destruction. The gas burned at a wellhead is gas that never reaches a turbine or a stove. So the trade-off at the heart of this motion is largely a mirage: a strict cap does not order operators to stop producing oil, it orders them to stop throwing away the gas that comes up with it. Capture it, pipe it, reinject it, or burn it for power on site — each option adds supply instead of subtracting it.
[P1.2] Leakage is the same story in slower motion. Methane escaping a compressor seal or an open thief hatch is inventory walking out the door. Fixing it costs hardware and crew hours, not barrels. The industry's own abatement estimates have long found that a large share of oil and gas methane fixes pay for themselves, because the gas you stop losing is gas you can sell.
[P1.3] What blocks those repairs is not physics; it is that wasting gas is free and fixing it is not.
[P1.4] That externality is enormous. Methane traps roughly eighty times more heat than carbon dioxide over twenty years, which makes it the fastest available brake on near-term warming. Voluntary pledges and intensity targets have had their turn, and satellites keep finding enormous plumes over the Permian, Turkmenistan, and the Niger Delta. Self-policing failed; enforceable thresholds are the correction.
[P1.5] My opponent will invoke winter prices and struggling households. Then notice who actually pays. Without a ban, the public absorbs the heat, the asthma, the crop losses, and the gas burned instead of sold. With one, a profitable industry absorbs a capital cost on a phase-in schedule, with exemptions for genuine emergency flaring.
[P1.6] A rule that forces waste to stop is the rare climate policy that pays for itself.
