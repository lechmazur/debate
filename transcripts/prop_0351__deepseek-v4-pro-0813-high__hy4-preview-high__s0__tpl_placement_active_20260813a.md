[P1.1] Remote work has quietly converted junior roles from apprenticeships into task execution. The harm is not immediate output; it is the slower accumulation of judgment, relationships, and visibility that early careers depend on. Employers and workers admit some social costs but still understate the career damage because today’s deliverables look acceptable while tomorrow’s promotion pipeline does not.
[P1.2] First, apprenticeship breaks. Juniors learn by overhearing how seniors negotiate, frame bad news, and edit. In an office, those lessons are ambient and constant. Remotely, they become scheduled and usually absent.
[P1.3] A junior doesn’t know to ask “how should I have handled that call?” until no one is around. The result is narrower tacit knowledge: better at completing tickets or slides, worse at reading a room and exercising judgment. The missing element is not information; it is the chance to watch judgment being formed.
[P1.4] Second, feedback becomes slower and blunter. Office feedback is immediate, low-stakes, and side-by-side. Remote feedback often arrives only after the project, in a formal review that rewards output but rarely repairs process. Early mistakes compound.
[P1.5] By the time formal feedback lands, the junior has repeated the same mistake on the next assignment. The junior looks productive while the developmental loop thins.
[P1.6] Third, visibility and sponsorship shrink. Promotion decisions run on trust, not just track record. Juniors need to be seen handling pressure, volunteering, and forming relationships with senior colleagues. Remote work makes junior output visible but junior potential much less visible.
[P1.7] That especially hurts people with less inherited professional capital: safer from awkward office politics, but absent from the informal conversations where advocates are made.
[P1.8] The understatement comes from timing. The cost appears years later, in weaker mid-level candidates and unexplained attrition. Managers see current productivity. Workers see autonomy.
[P1.9] Neither sees the missing feedback loop while it is still cheap to fix. That lag is why the usual optimism is too confident.

[C1.2] We should not confuse a change in the medium of learning with a loss of its substance.
