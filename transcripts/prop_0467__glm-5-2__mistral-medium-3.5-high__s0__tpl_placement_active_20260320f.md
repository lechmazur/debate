[P1.1] A cyberattack that cuts power to a hospital's ICU kills the patient just as dead as a missile striking the generator. International law should not categorize these as different acts simply because one arrives through code and the other through a warhead. The law of armed conflict has always been concerned with effects, not delivery mechanisms. A blockade that starves civilians is an armed attack.
[P1.2] Poisoning a city's water supply through digital sabotage is functionally identical. The proposition does not invent a new legal category—it extends an existing one to a method that achieves the same result.
[P1.3] The current gap creates a dangerous deterrent asymmetry. If a state knows that attacking another nation's power grid with malware will be treated as criminal nuisance rather than armed aggression, it faces far less restraint. The absence of a credible military response option makes critical infrastructure a low-cost, high-impact target. Classifying these attacks as equivalent to armed attacks closes that loophole and raises the cost of launching them.
[P1.4] This is not a radical expansion. International law has repeatedly adapted to new means of causing mass harm—chemical weapons, aerial bombardment, and nuclear capabilities each required frameworks that recognized their destructive potential without waiting for a traditional battlefield. Cyber operations that achieve kinetic-equivalent effects occupy that same space.
[P1.5] The proposition includes proportional response—this is not blank-check escalation. It subjects cyberattacks on civilian infrastructure to the same necessity and proportionality constraints governing any use of force in self-defense. What changes is that the door to legitimate self-defense is not locked shut because the weapon was digital.
[P1.6] The real question is not whether cyberattacks look like traditional warfare. It is whether they cause the harm that warfare causes. When they do, the law must respond accordingly.
