[P1.1] Judge this motion on the ledger, not on a snapshot. The question is not whether pre-K vocabulary test scores stay higher in third grade—they often do not—but whether a universal year of quality early schooling changes a child’s trajectory enough to pay back its cost. It does.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Children who gain language, self-regulation, and classroom routines enter kindergarten ready to follow instructions and manage frustration. That changes how teachers see them and how schools spend money. A child who is not misread as disruptive or delayed avoids pull-out remediation, grade retention, and special-education placements that cost far more over a decade than a year of pre-K.
[P1.3] Upstream prevention is cheaper than downstream repair; one avoided special-education placement can offset years of pre-K.
[P1.4] The durable gains are real, and not only in boutique programs. Intensive early programs like Perry and Abecedarian cut later arrest and welfare use and raised earnings. Universal systems scale part of that mechanism: large-scale systems in Oklahoma and Georgia leave children better prepared and less likely to be held back years later, and studies tracking Tulsa and Boston cohorts still find stronger engagement and fewer disciplinary problems. Even where a narrow test-score gap fades, the social gains—less crime, higher graduation, more parental employment and tax contribution—persist.
[P1.5] Universality is not waste; it is design. Targeting often misses hidden disadvantage, stigmatizes families, and loses peer-group benefits that raise quality for everyone. Universal pre-K builds broad coalitions and stable funding, and it catches children who need help but would not qualify for a poverty-tested slot.
[P1.6] The motion asks whether the gains are large enough to justify public cost. On the full ledger—graduation, earnings, reduced remediation, reduced crime, adult independence—the benefits are not marginal. They are the compounding returns that early investment uniquely produces. That is the standard for judging the spend.

[C1.1] The motion has three load-bearing words: universal, lasting, and cost. The strongest evidence for early education fails on the first, the strongest evidence for universal programs fails on the second, and the arithmetic fails on the third.
[C1.2] Take universal. Perry and Abecedarian were tiny, intensive, decades-old programs aimed at profoundly disadvantaged children, with home visiting and staff ratios no state can afford at scale. Citing them for a program that enrolls every four-year-old is like citing a chemotherapy trial to justify prescribing it to the healthy. The children who drive those famous returns are precisely the ones a universal program dilutes, because most of its seats and dollars go to children who were already in some preschool or in perfectly adequate home care.
[C1.3] You pay full price for a marginal effect near zero.
[C1.4] Take lasting. When researchers have actually randomized admission to real, scaled public programs, the picture is grim. Tennessee's statewide pre-K lottery study found early gains that vanished within a couple of years and turned negative by middle school: lower test scores, more disciplinary referrals, more special-education placement, not less. The national Head Start randomized evaluation showed fade-out by early elementary.
[C1.5] Quebec's universal childcare expansion was followed by worse behavior and anxiety outcomes that persisted into adolescence. Those are the studies with real control groups. Oklahoma and Georgia comparisons are observational, and their "less retention" findings sit alongside flat achievement.
[C1.6] Take cost. Quality pre-K runs on the order of ten thousand dollars a child a year, meaning tens of billions annually nationwide. That money has rivals: intensive targeted programs for the children who actually benefit, tutoring in grades K–3 where fade-out happens, or direct child allowances. Every dollar spent subsidizing a preschool a middle-class family would have bought anyway is a dollar not spent on the child the mechanism was built for.
[C1.7] Prevention beats repair only when the prevention works. At scale, for everyone, it hasn't.
