[P1.1] The proposition does not ban targeted advertising. It puts the burden where it belongs: anyone harvesting personal data at scale to profile people for ads must first obtain explicit opt-in consent. Without it, courts and regulators should presume the practice unlawful. The rule reaches large-scale harvesting and profiling, not a first-party login the user actively chooses.
[P1.2] That default is necessary because today's consent is mostly fiction. A visitor meets dozens of trackers on one page, buried notices, pre-ticked boxes, and accept-all walls. Data then flows through brokers, analytics firms, and ad exchanges the user never heard of. You cannot meaningfully consent to a chain you cannot see, especially when refusal means losing access to information, work, or social life.
[P1.3] Opt-out is not consent; it transfers labor onto the individual.
[P1.4] Explicit opt-in also blocks the standard evasion—renaming personal data as "audience segments" or "inferences." The unit of regulation is the profiling, not the label.
[P1.5] The harm is not annoyance. Ordinary clicks can reveal illness, pregnancy, debt, sexuality, religion, or political fear. Those inferences can shape prices, job ads, insurance offers, and manipulative messaging. Once the profile is built and leaked, no delete request restores the status quo.
[P1.6] This is mass surveillance with an advertising business model.
[P1.7] Presumptive unlawfulness is proportionate and workable. It is rebuttable: a firm can seek genuine consent, use contextual advertising, or justify a narrowly tailored practice. If the value is real, the firm can prove it; the public should not have to prove harm after the fact. The opposite rule—collect first, answer questions later—lets the most opaque actors write the terms.
[P1.8] The decisive question is who bears the burden of proof. The other side must defend a system where silence equals permission. A free society should require an affirmative yes before strangers turn private life into a product.

[C1.1] Treating an entire economy as presumptively criminal is not privacy protection, it is regulatory panic. It reverses the basic principle that lawful commerce is permitted until proven harmful, replacing evidence with suspicion.
[C1.2] Targeted advertising is not an exotic abuse; it is how free search, free maps, free video, free news, and millions of small sellers survive. A local bookstore cannot buy a national TV spot, but it can afford to show its ad to readers nearby who actually buy books. Presuming that unlawful until the bookstore's ad network extracts an explicit yes from every profiled stranger does not empower users, it bankrupts the publishers and small firms who depend on efficient reach while entrenching giants that already hold direct relationships and first-party logins.
[C1.3] Explicit opt-in also fails on its own terms. We have already run this experiment with cookie banners. Users confronted with endless permission screens do not become informed sovereigns; they click accept to make the box disappear. Mandating that fatigue at population scale, backed by a legal presumption of guilt, produces more mindless clicking, less genuine understanding, and a compliance bonanza for lawyers rather than control for ordinary people.
[C1.4] The surveillance analogy collapses the crucial difference. Governments can arrest, tax, and conscript; advertisers can show a different shoe ad. Existing law already treats health, precise location, children's data, and other sensitive categories with special care. Stretching that emergency logic to all large-scale ad profiling treats inference that someone likes hiking as if it were wiretapping.
[C1.5] Courts should judge deception, broken promises, security failures, and misuse of truly sensitive information, not presume that building an audience is illegal. A targeted, risk-based regime with clear disclosure, workable opt-outs, data minimization, and harsh penalties for violators disciplines bad actors without declaring the open internet guilty until proven innocent.
