[P1.1] The decisive fact is that U.S. overdose deaths are now a poisoning crisis, not a simple drug-use crisis. Fentanyl is already inside the drug supply, and most victims do not know their dose. The fastest way to save lives is therefore to change what happens when an exposed person uses, not to attempt the impossible task of removing every illicit shipment from a continent-sized border. Medication-assisted treatment does exactly that: methadone and buprenorphine reduce craving and withdrawal, keep people in care, and sharply reduce fatal overdose risk by reducing dangerous use itself.
[P1.2] Harm reduction works even faster. Naloxone reverses an overdose that is already happening; fentanyl test strips and syringe programs give people immediate information and contact with services. These interventions lower the death rate per exposure now.
[P1.3] Interdiction and harsher penalties operate on the wrong part of the supply chain. Fentanyl is synthetic, extremely potent, and cheap to move in tiny quantities. Seizing larger amounts does not eliminate demand, and suppliers have repeatedly adapted by switching precursors, routes, and production methods. The result is an endless whack-a-mole: a disrupted network is replaced, and the replacement product may arrive with even more unpredictable potency.
[P1.4] Penalties also fail because the most dangerous traffickers are not the ones most easily arrested, while threatening low-level sellers and users discourages overdose witnesses from calling 911. Past supply-side escalation has not stopped fentanyl from saturating the market.
[P1.5] The next five years sharpen the contrast. Expanding MAT through primary care, jails, and community clinics can be done within existing health infrastructure. Naloxone distribution and harm-reduction outreach can scale within months. Interdiction and penalty escalation require long investigations, legal battles, and international coordination, and even successful enforcement cannot reliably reduce deaths because it does not reduce the fatality risk of the drugs already present.
[P1.6] A seized shipment is replaced; a person started on buprenorphine or revived with naloxone may not die tomorrow. On the actual metric that matters—overdose deaths—the demand-side path wins.
