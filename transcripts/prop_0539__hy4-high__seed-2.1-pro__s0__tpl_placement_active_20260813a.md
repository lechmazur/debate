[P1.1] Cross-border commercial surrogacy should remain legal only as a regulated exception, not a border-shopping market. “Only when” is the decisive phrase: where agencies and clinics meet enforceable anti-exploitation, parentage, and health-protection standards, law can permit a path to parenthood; where they cannot, law should withhold that profit. That distinction protects the surrogate, the child, and intended parents when disaster exposes a paper contract.
[P1.2] Without anti-exploitation rules, poverty becomes a recruitment tool. Brokers control translation, housing, medical schedules, and payment; a signature inside that structure can be formally voluntary and practically trapped. Licensing, independent advice, transparent compensation, bans on debt bondage and deceptive recruiting, grievance procedures, and audits do not make inequality vanish; they make it answerable. Agencies and clinics must carry the duty because they design and profit from the transaction; private contracts leave the weakest party to sue across borders after harm.
[P1.3] Parentage and health are not optional add-ons. A cross-border arrangement can involve a surrogate in one state, parents in another, and a birth in a third; without verified parentage, citizenship and exit permissions, and plans for disability, dispute, or abandonment, the child bears the legal chaos. Without screening, limits on risky embryo transfers, obstetric care, insurance, postpartum treatment, and records, the surrogate bears the medical chaos.
[P1.4] Opponents must defend one of two worse worlds. A blanket ban sounds clean but pushes demand underground; it protects no one, merely removing the forum where consent, payment, care, and parentage can be inspected. Unregulated legality rewards the cheapest jurisdiction and treats bodies and babies as supply-chain risks. If standards are called unworkable, high-risk medicine is generally conditioned on licensing, records, and sanctions.
[P1.5] Border complexity is a reason for enforceability, not an excuse for fatalism. The line is simple: legal only when protections are real; illegal when profit rests on coercion, parentage limbo, or untreated harm.

[C1.1] The proposition’s neat “only when” guardrail is a dangerous legal fiction. Cross-border commercial surrogacy by definition spans multiple sovereign jurisdictions with conflicting family, contract, and human rights rules, and no transnational authority exists to actually enforce the promised anti-exploitation, parentage, and health standards across borders. A clinic can pass a paper audit to secure legal approval today, but if a surrogate suffers permanent obstetric injury, or intended parents abandon a disabled newborn, there is no reliable mechanism to hold operators liable when they rebrand under a new name, move operations across a nearby border, or rely on local courts that refuse to honor foreign claims or defer to well-connected commercial operators.
[C1.2] That veneer of certified legitimacy does not reduce exploitation—it supercharges it. For-profit brokers will market “ethically compliant” surrogacy to reassure wealthy intended parents, while carving out routine workarounds just below audit thresholds: recruiting surrogates from marginalized, linguistically isolated border communities who fall outside formal oversight, structuring payments as “expense stipends” to evade fair compensation rules, and routing parentage paperwork through shell jurisdictions to avoid scrutiny. This is not hypothetical: the same model of third-party “ethical certification” for cross-border adoption failed repeatedly to stop child laundering, because the profit incentive to cut corners on a single transaction worth tens of thousands of dollars is far stronger than any intermittent audit regime.
[C1.3] The false choice between this unworkable regulated model and an unregulated free-for-all collapses immediately. A clear ban on for-profit cross-border commercial surrogacy, paired with expanded domestic altruistic surrogacy access, family support, and streamlined adoption pathways, does not push vulnerable parties into more danger than a system that licenses exploitation under a seal of approval it can never actually back up across conflicting national borders. Unlike the hollow cross-border certification regime, domestic rules apply under a single legal system, with clear penalties and accessible remedies, without creating a profit-driven global market that preys on poor women to serve wealthy foreign clients.
