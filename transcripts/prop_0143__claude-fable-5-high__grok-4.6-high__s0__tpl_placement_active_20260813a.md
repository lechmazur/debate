[P1.1] A person's voice, mannerisms, and intimate way of speaking are not raw material that becomes free for the taking the moment they die. They are the most personal thing anyone possesses — the residue of a whole life. The proposition asks only this: before a company builds a chatbot that impersonates your dead mother's affection, someone with the right to speak for her must say yes. That is a floor, not a ceiling, and here is why it must exist.
[P1.2] First, dignity does not expire at death. We already accept this everywhere else in law and custom: wills bind us, organ donation requires consent, defamation of the dead is taboo, and publicity rights in many jurisdictions survive the grave precisely because a person's identity retains moral weight. A simulation of someone's intimate personality is a far deeper appropriation than using their photo in an ad. If the shallow use requires permission, the deep use cannot require less.
[P1.3] Second, without a consent requirement, the incentives are grotesque. Grief-tech firms profit most from the bereaved at their most vulnerable, and the cheapest product is one built from scraped voicemails and social media posts — no permission asked, no family consulted. A widow could discover a subscription service selling conversations with a puppet of her husband, trained on data he never agreed to share for that purpose. Consent is the only checkpoint that stops identity from becoming inventory.
[P1.4] Third, consent protects the living, too. Grief researchers have long warned that avoidance of loss can prolong and complicate mourning. A consent gate ensures at least one deliberate human decision — by the deceased in life or by their estate — stands between raw grief and a commercial simulation, rather than letting an app store decide.
[P1.5] The counter-position must defend this: that anyone, for any motive, may simulate anyone's dead loved one without asking. Requiring a "yes" first is the minimum a decent society demands.
