[P1.1] The modern airline ticket has become a labyrinth of deception. When a passenger books a flight, they see one price, only to discover that bringing a standard rolling bag—the kind that fits in any overhead bin—costs an extra $35 to $60 each way. This is not pricing; it is a shell game that renders honest comparison shopping mathematically impossible. A traveler comparing a $200 fare that includes a bag against a $180 fare that excludes it is not comparing flights; they are comparing apples to oranges, with the true cost hidden in fine print.
[P1.2] A carry-on bag is not a luxury amenity. For the vast majority of travelers, it contains medications, laptops, and essentials needed during the flight itself. Treating this basic mobility tool as an optional add-on is like a train company charging separately for seats or a restaurant charging for utensils. The current system forces consumers to pay twice for the core service: transportation from A to B with their person and their immediate belongings.
[P1.3] It is a bait-and-switch model that exploits the sunk cost of booking to extract fees for what should be standard service.
[P1.4] This fragmentation serves no genuine economic efficiency. Airlines already enforce strict size and weight limits for safety reasons. Once a bag meets those objective, universally understood standards, charging extra for it is pure rent-seeking. It obscures true prices, punishes transparency, and shifts the complexity of revenue management onto bewildered passengers at the gate.
[P1.5] A reasonable baseline—one personal item plus one standard carry-on included in the base fare—would restore honesty to the market without preventing airlines from charging for genuinely excessive baggage.
[P1.6] The proposition does not ban fees for oversized luggage or multiple bags. It simply requires that the baseline of normal travel be honored in the ticket price. That is not heavy-handed regulation; it is basic consumer protection against a pricing model designed to confuse.
