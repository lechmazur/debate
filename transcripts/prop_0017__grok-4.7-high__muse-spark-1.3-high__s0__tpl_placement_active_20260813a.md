[P1.1] Anti-encampment laws are not illegitimate in principle. Streets are not housing, and cities owe people fire safety, sanitation, and passage. The question is when enforcement is justified rather than used to exile the poor from view. The answer is exact: only when the person told to leave has somewhere they can actually go, a way to keep what they need to survive, and help that leads out rather than around the block.
[P1.2] Criminalizing sleep, sitting, or a tent when shelter is full, closed, or out of reach punishes status. Someone who cannot obey except by vanishing has not been regulated. They have been made an offender for being poor in public. That is not order.
[P1.3] It is police power spent on relocating a visible problem without reducing it.
[P1.4] “Genuinely available” is the distinction, not a slogan. A bed thirty miles away that bars partners, pets, or anyone who fails a nighttime sobriety screen is not available to the person in front of the officer. Storage is not a courtesy. Sweeps that seize IDs, medications, phones, and work clothes do not clear a hazard; they destroy the tools of exit and guarantee return.
[P1.5] Services matter for the same reason. A citation does not treat illness, replace a lost prescription, or produce a lease. Without those three conditions, enforcement is a loop—displace, confiscate, cite, repeat—paid for twice, once in suffering and once in overtime.
[P1.6] When the conditions are met, justification returns. A city that can offer a real bed tonight, a place for belongings, and a path into housing or treatment may clear a camp without making poverty itself the crime. Public health and individual liberty then point the same way. Until that offer is real, anti-encampment law does not solve homelessness.
[P1.7] It criminalizes the evidence of it. The “only when” clause is therefore the measure of legitimacy, not a loophole around it.

[C1.1] Cities do not need to solve homelessness before they can stop a sidewalk from becoming a campsite. An encampment law is justified when the encampment itself creates dangers no city can tolerate, regardless of how many shelter beds are open that night.
[C1.2] A tent village under a freeway overpass with open flames, stacked propane, human waste in storm drains, and needles near a school playground is not visible poverty. It is a firetrap, a health hazard, and a seizure of space everyone else loses: the wheelchair user forced into traffic, the shopkeeper closing early, the child who cannot use the park. That harm does not disappear because the shelter is full or forbids dogs.
[C1.3] The proposed test pretends to be compassionate, but “genuinely available” is a veto disguised as a standard. [P1.4] lists barred partners, pets, sobriety screens, distance as disqualifiers. In real life someone will always have a reason — understandable, human — to refuse what exists. If each refusal makes enforcement unjustified, the most entrenched camps become the most immune, and the law applies only to those willing to leave anyway.
[C1.4] That is not legitimacy; it is surrender of shared space to whoever claims it first.
[C1.5] Poverty is not a crime, but conduct in public is regulable. A poor person sleeping on a friend’s couch breaks no encampment ordinance. Building a semi-permanent structure, storing shopping carts of belongings, blocking passage, and refusing to move do. The difference is not wealth but appropriation.
[C1.6] Worse, waiting for ideal services abandons the people inside. Unregulated camps concentrate assault, trafficking, overdose, and infection far from help. Clearing them, even imperfectly, allows cleanup, welfare checks, and dispersal from predators. Shelter, storage, and treatment are good goals, but they are policy aspirations, not a permission slip for public danger.
[C1.7] Order protects the homeless too.
