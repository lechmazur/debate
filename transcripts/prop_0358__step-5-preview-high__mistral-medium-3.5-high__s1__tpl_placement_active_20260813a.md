[P1.1] Traffic deaths are made of physics, not merely of lawbreaking. A city that wants fewer deaths must lower the energy in everyday movement and make mistakes survivable. That is why lower default urban speed limits and street redesign do more than harsher penalties after a crash.
[P1.2] The causal chain is direct. Impact speed determines whether a crash is a near miss, an injury, or a funeral. A pedestrian struck at 20 mph has a meaningful chance to survive; at 40 mph, death becomes likely. As speeds rise from urban street levels to arterial levels, the risk that a struck pedestrian or cyclist dies rises steeply; design can shorten crossings, narrow lanes, add refuge islands, daylight corners, calm approaches, and separate people from fast vehicles.
[P1.3] Those changes do not require every driver to behave perfectly. They engineer forgiveness into the system.
[P1.4] Post-crash penalties, by contrast, work at the end of the chain. They cannot unbend the vehicle's trajectory, restore reaction time, or reconstruct the pedestrian's body. Their deterrent effect is also diffuse: drivers overestimate their control, crash risk feels remote, and punishment is delayed, rare, and applied to the surviving driver rather than the dead victim. A longer sentence may express public anger, but it rarely alters the speed choice that turned a mistake into a fatality.
[P1.5] Street redesign has further advantages. It treats non-drivers as legitimate users, not obstacles; it can protect children, older residents, and cyclists; and it is politically visible as infrastructure, not punishment. Lower default speeds set the expectation for every trip, every visitor, and every street, rather than relying on exceptional enforcement after bad outcomes.
[P1.6] PRO is not arguing against all accountability. It is arguing about priority: when the goal is reducing deaths, prevention beats retribution. Cities should spend their political capital where crashes are actually prevented and survivability is engineered into the street.
