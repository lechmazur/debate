[P1.1] Broad pronouncements about "culture" have become a blank check for large employers to demand office attendance regardless of whether a role actually benefits from it. When a company invokes culture as a sweeping justification, it inevitably means different things to different people—often serving as a catch-all that deflects scrutiny rather than providing a genuine business rationale. Requiring role-specific reasons changes the conversation from vague appeals to concrete necessity. If an employer must explain why a particular data analyst or software developer needs to be at a desk in a specific building to perform their duties, the justification either holds up or it doesn't.
[P1.2] That discipline protects both the employer and the employees from decisions driven by inertia, real estate commitments, or a desire for visibility rather than productivity.
[P1.3] Role-specific justification also acknowledges a fundamental truth: not all work is the same. A nurse, a warehouse associate, and a videoconference-based account manager have vastly different relationships to physical presence. Treating them all as interchangeable cogs in a "cultural" machine ignores the actual mechanics of their work. When justifications are tied to specific roles, employers can still mandate returns where there's a real case—say, for lab-based researchers or in-person client service teams—while sparing those whose output is location-independent.
[P1.4] This targeted approach is more likely to generate genuine cultural benefits anyway, because it avoids alienating employees whose only experience of "culture" under a broad mandate is a pointless commute.
[P1.5] Finally, this requirement creates a healthy accountability mechanism without paralyzing business decision-making. Employers remain free to set their own policies; they simply need to be able to articulate why a given role requires physical presence. That's a low bar for any legitimate business need, but a meaningful safeguard against mandates that are arbitrary or purely symbolic. It replaces the ambiguity of culture-talk with the clarity of operational reasoning, making it possible for employees and the public to distinguish between necessary workplace coordination and blanket policies that serve no purpose beyond control.
[P1.6] That distinction is exactly what this proposition is designed to enforce.
