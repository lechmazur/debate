[P1.1] Affordability is a problem of arithmetic before it is a problem of policy. When there are more households than homes, someone gets priced out—full stop. The only durable fix is to change that ratio, and the fastest way to change it is to let people build. That is why streamlining permitting and zoning beats rent control: one attacks the shortage, the other merely rations it.
[P1.2] Consider a city where restrictive zoning bans anything but single-family homes on most of its land, and where a single apartment project waits years for approvals, environmental review, and neighborhood appeals. Every one of those delays is a tax on new housing that gets baked into rents. Cut the delay, allow more units per lot, and supply rises where demand is highest. When Minneapolis and parts of Auckland loosened zoning, permitting and construction rose and rent growth notably lagged their regions.
[P1.3] Tokyo, which permits housing liberally at the national level, kept rents remarkably flat for decades while its population grew. Supply works.
[P1.4] Now look at what rent control actually does. It helps the specific tenant who already holds a controlled unit—and I won't pretend otherwise. But it does nothing to create a single new home. Worse, by capping returns it discourages construction and maintenance, pushes owners to convert units to condos or short-term rentals, and shrinks the very stock newcomers, young people, and the poor need.
[P1.5] Economists across the political spectrum rarely agree on much; they agree that hard rent control reduces available supply. It relabels scarcity as fairness while quietly making scarcity worse.
[P1.6] Here is the clean comparison the whole debate turns on. Both sides claim to help renters. Only one side makes more homes exist. Tenant protections and rent control redistribute a fixed pie among insiders; supply expansion bakes a bigger pie so more people can eat.
[P1.7] In advanced economies where the binding constraint is chronic under-building in high-demand cities, the policy that increases the number of homes is the policy that increases affordability.
[P1.8] Build more, and prices follow.

[C1.2] Start with the gap PRO never closes: streamlining grants permission, not production. A city can upzone every parcel and still get nothing built, because building is decided by developers facing interest rates, labor shortages, materials costs and their own incentive not to flood a market they're selling into. What upzoning reliably does immediately is raise the value of the land, because the right to build more is capitalized into the lot price. The first beneficiary of a rezoning is the person who already owns the dirt.
[C1.3] That's why so many liberalizations produce a spike in land values and a trickle of cranes.
[C1.4] Second, there's a floor under construction cost. In an advanced economy, delivering a new urban apartment costs what it costs — land, concrete, labor, financing. Even with zero permitting friction, that unit rents near the top of the market. Affordability at the bottom then depends on "filtering," which operates over decades and gets swamped in exactly the high-demand cities PRO cares about, because those cities attract new residents faster than the filtering works.
[C1.5] Tell a family facing a thirty percent increase this year that relief arrives through the second-hand market in 2045.
[C1.6] Third, PRO is attacking a caricature. Contemporary rent regulation is anti-gouging: caps at inflation plus several points, with new construction exempted for a decade or more. Those designs cannot deter building — the builder is exempt — yet they immediately stop the spikes that drive eviction, doubling-up and homelessness. Tenant protections like just-cause eviction and real notice periods convert a housing unit into a stable home at near-zero fiscal and supply cost.
[C1.7] And notice: PRO's own showcases don't isolate supply. Tokyo pairs liberal building with strong tenant security; Vienna's famous affordability rests on regulated and social rents. The successful cases regulate.
