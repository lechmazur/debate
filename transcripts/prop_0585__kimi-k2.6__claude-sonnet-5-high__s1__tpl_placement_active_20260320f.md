[P1.1] When the wind dies in Kansas and the sun fades in Arizona, batteries eventually go dark. Transmission lines do not. The question before us is whether we should primarily bet on technology that does not yet exist at scale, or build the infrastructure to share power across regions using assets we already have. Transmission expansion and regional market integration win on reliability, decarbonization speed, and economic certainty.
[P1.2] Reliability demands diversity, not just inventory. A grid integrated across broad geographic regions smooths renewable variability through the simple mathematics of distance and different weather patterns. When the West has excess solar and the Midwest has roaring wind, integrated markets move electrons instantly. Long-duration storage faces a harder constraint: it must be charged before it can discharge.
[P1.3] During extended weather patterns—week-long heat domes or winter doldrums—batteries drain. Wires tapping distant, active resources do not.
[P1.4] Decarbonization urgency makes the timeline matter. Remote renewables in the Great Plains and offshore Atlantic waters are already the cheapest new generation, but they are stranded without high-capacity lines. Subsidizing long-duration storage bets on breakthroughs in manufacturing, chemistry, and mineral supply chains that remain speculative and years away. Transmission builds on proven engineering and unlocks gigawatts of clean power immediately.
[P1.5] The economic logic is equally stark. Regional integration captures the portfolio effect of mixed weather systems and staggered demand peaks, flattening prices without guessing which technology wins. Storage subsidies, by contrast, pour public money into competing technologies that may obsolete one another—an expensive tournament where taxpayers bear the risk. Infrastructure is patient capital; storage subsidies are venture bets.
[P1.6] If the goal is a reliable, decarbonized grid this decade, we should build the network first and store only what the network cannot share.
