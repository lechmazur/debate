[P1.1] A drug is not proven because it works in the easiest people to recruit. It is proven when it works in the people who will actually take it. Proportionate representation across age, sex, and ethnicity must be a condition of approval, not an aspiration after it.
[P1.2] First, biology is not interchangeable. Older bodies clear drugs more slowly, carry more disease and other medicines, raising risks of falls and kidney injury. Sex affects body composition, hormones, and cardiac risk. Ancestry-linked variation can change metabolism and adverse reactions.
[P1.3] When trials skew young, male, and overwhelmingly from one ethnic background, they miss the right dose, safety profile, and sometimes whether the drug works at all.
[P1.4] That gap turns approval into extrapolation. The treatment reaches the 78-year-old woman on three other prescriptions and the patient from an underrepresented group whose disease progresses differently — neither adequately studied. Doctors guess. Patients become the real experiment, without trial consent or monitoring.
[P1.5] Some get too little and fail treatment. Some get too much and are harmed. Others lose trust in medicine that was never tested on people like them.
[P1.6] Second, speed without representativeness is illusory. A fast approval followed by safety warnings, restricted labels, or withdrawal is not faster access to effective medicine; it is faster uncertainty. Representative enrollment yields usable labels, trusted dosing, and known benefit in groups who will receive it. That avoids treating millions, discovering harm, and starting over.
[P1.7] Proportionate does not mean rigid quotas for every rare subgroup in every small trial. It means enrollment tracking the population bearing the disease, enforced before approval. If a condition mainly strikes older adults, they cannot be a token minority. If women bear half the burden, they cannot be sidelined for convenience.
[P1.8] Slower approval is a real cost. But an untested-for-you drug is not a cure for delay. It is delay disguised as progress, paid later by the patients least likely to have been studied.

[P2.1] The patient dying while waiting is a powerful image, but it does not distinguish the sides. A drug approved quickly for someone else's body can also let disease progress, because the dose is too low, an interaction was never tested, or efficacy differs in older, female, or minority patients. [P2.2] CON warns underrepresented patients are last to receive new therapies, so delay hurts them most. After a skewed approval, they are first harmed by extrapolation and last to benefit from reliable evidence. Wealthier patients can seek specialists and second opinions when a label fails. Those with least access cannot buy their way out of missing data.
[P2.3] A pre-approval gate protects people who cannot compensate afterward.
[P2.4] A requirement does fix site location, exclusions, travel burden, and mistrust — by giving sponsors a financial reason to fix them. Voluntary encouragement left those barriers intact because convenient recruitment is cheaper. Tie approval to enrollment and companies fund community sites, broaden eligibility for older and comorbid patients, pay for transport, and work with trusted clinicians. Barriers persist while avoiding them is profitable.
[P2.5] Proportionate means proportionate to people with the disease, not a census replica. A sickle-cell trial should overwhelmingly enroll patients of African ancestry; a prostate trial cannot enroll women for balance. That is not a hedge. It is the only scientifically coherent standard, set in the protocol before recruitment begins.
[P2.6] That standard answers the rare-disease objection. Proportionate to a small population does not require an enormous trial; it forbids studying a disease of older adults almost entirely in the young, or one affecting women mainly in men, because that is easier. Planned from day one, representative enrollment is front-loaded, not endlessly delayed.
[P2.7] If better voluntary tools already worked, trials would not still exclude the patients who will swallow the pills. Tools without consequences are advice. Approval is the consequence.

[P4.1] A1: The reference population is set before recruitment, when regulators approve the protocol. Using routine epidemiology on who develops the disease, the sponsor must state expected enrollment shares by age, sex, and ethnicity and justify deviations in advance. If a rare disease is concentrated in elderly women of one ancestry, proportionate means a trial composed mainly of those women; it does not demand impossible numbers of young men or unaffected groups. The enforceable rule is fidelity to that disease-specific plan at a size the population can support, with exceptions only for groups genuinely rare in the disease, never for convenience.
[P4.2] A2: I would not abandon the gate at an arbitrary calendar deadline, because sponsors would simply plan representative recruitment last and wait out the clock. That does not make it endless. Targets and timelines are fixed upfront, scaled to urgency, alternatives, and prevalence; for a fast-progressing cancer, that means expedited review, parallel community sites, and oversampling high-burden groups from day one. Dropping the gate does not prevent deaths.
[P4.3] It shifts risk to elderly, female, and minority patients who will receive a drug whose dose and benefit were never established in them.
[P4.4] Those answers expose the asymmetry CON keeps reversing. They call delay certain and extrapolation merely correctable, as if a revised label can undo a fatal interaction, kidney injury, or months of ineffective cancer treatment. Post-launch detection is weakest precisely where trials were thinnest, because underrepresented patients have less specialist access, less follow-up, and less power to trigger a label change.
[P4.5] Nor is a numerical target inert. Sponsors cannot conjure eligible older or minority participants at the final site visit; to meet a pre-set plan they must open sites where those patients are, relax exclusions that screen them out, and fund travel and follow-up. CON prefers encouragement without that consequence — the approach that produced the skew.
[P4.6] A usable drug, tested in its real users, is access. A faster label built on somebody else’s body is not.
