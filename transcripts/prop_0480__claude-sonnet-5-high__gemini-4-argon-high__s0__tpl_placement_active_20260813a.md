[P1.1] Every dollar spent on cash and every dollar spent on services eventually competes for the same pool of public money, so the real question is which spending buys more welfare per dollar. That comparison favors universal basic services, and the case rests on three mechanisms: market failure, risk-pooling, and the fragility of cash in the face of unequal bargaining power.
[P1.2] Start with market failure. Childcare, transit, broadband, and healthcare are not ordinary consumer goods. They have huge fixed costs, strong network effects, and massive social spillovers. A bus line is only valuable if it runs regularly and connects to other lines; a single family handed cash cannot buy "a tenth of a bus route."
[P1.3] Internet infrastructure in a rural town isn't built because one household can now afford a subscription — it gets built when government aggregates demand and commits to the line itself. Cash transfers assume a functioning market is sitting there ready to sell you what you need at a fair price. In health, childcare, and transit, that market often doesn't exist or is monopolistic, so the cash just gets captured in higher prices rather than more access. Universal provision solves the supply problem that cash cannot touch.
[P1.4] Second, risk-pooling. Healthcare needs are wildly unequal and unpredictable — one family needs $500 a year, another needs $50,000 for a cancer treatment. Universal public health coverage spreads that risk across everyone, which is dramatically more efficient than handing everyone the same flat cash sum and hoping the unlucky ones saved enough. Insurance pooling by its nature beats individual cash buffers.
[P1.5] Third, bargaining power. Inside households, cash transfers are not always controlled by the person with the greatest need — a child needing care, a partner needing autonomy. Direct provision of childcare or healthcare bypasses that internal power imbalance and delivers the service straight to the person who needs it, no negotiation required.
[P1.6] Put simply: cash trusts broken markets to fix themselves. Universal services fix the market directly. That is the throughline I'll defend across this debate.
