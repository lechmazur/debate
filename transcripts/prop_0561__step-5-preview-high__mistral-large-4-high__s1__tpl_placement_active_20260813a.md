[P1.1] The gig economy’s promise of flexibility is real, but its business model depends on a hidden subsidy: platforms capture the value of sustained labor while shifting the costs of keeping workers healthy and secure onto employees and taxpayers. When a rideshare driver spends thirty-five hours a week keeping a platform’s core service running, yet receives no health coverage, no paid leave, and no retirement support, the flexibility is one-sided. The company flexes its workforce; the worker absorbs the risk.
[P1.2] Requiring prorated benefits tied to hours worked is not an attack on gig work. It is a proportionate correction that stops this cost-shifting without dismantling the app-based model. The employer controls pricing, dispatch, ratings, and de facto working conditions. That control creates reliance, and reliance justifies baseline obligations.
[P1.3] Prorating by hours allocates those obligations fairly: cost scales directly with engagement, so platforms using more labor pay more, while genuinely casual participation remains light. It preserves flexibility while ending arbitrage.
[P1.4] Without this rule, gig platforms gain an artificial competitive edge over traditional employers who fund health coverage, leave, and retirement. A warehouse worker and a delivery driver performing physically demanding work for similar hours should not face radically different basic safety nets merely because one clocks into a building and the other logs into an app. The distinction is technological, not moral.
[P1.5] The stakes extend beyond fairness. Uninsured gig workers delay care and strain emergency systems; without leave, they work while sick, endangering public health; without retirement contributions, they face destitution in old age after decades of platform profits. Prorated benefits are administratively feasible and already exist in various forms across the labor market. The barrier is not logistics; it is the desire to preserve an extractive model.
[P1.6] Government should require platforms to internalize the real costs of the workforce they depend on.

[C1.1] Tying benefits to hours doesn't fix gig work; it reclassifies it. Mandating prorated health coverage, leave, and retirement contributions calculated by the hour creates exactly the ongoing economic dependence and control that labor law uses to define an employer-employee relationship. Platforms will respond not by absorbing costs but by capping hours below thresholds, imposing rigid shifts, or exiting markets entirely. The flexibility that draws people to gig work—the student driving between classes, the caregiver working only Tuesday mornings—evaporates when every logged hour triggers a new liability.
[C1.2] Nor do the costs disappear into corporate coffers. Basic incidence economics means platforms will deduct benefit costs from base pay or raise prices, shrinking the total pie. A driver who needs flexible cash to pay rent this month is forced into a compensation bundle weighted toward future health coverage they may already have through a spouse or marketplace subsidies. For the many workers who use platforms for supplemental income, mandatory prorated benefits function as a pay cut disguised as a gift: the worker who needs take-home pay today subsidizes a benefit they cannot use until tomorrow.
[C1.3] The "hours worked" trigger is administratively perverse. Gig work involves waiting time, multi-apping across competing platforms, and unpredictable demand. Defining "hours" for benefit purposes invites endless litigation over whether a driver idling between rides is "working," and it burdens small platforms with compliance costs they cannot bear. The result is consolidation: only giant platforms can afford the bureaucracy, cementing the very market power critics claim to oppose.
[C1.4] Finally, the fairness comparison collapses under scrutiny. Traditional employees receive benefits partly because employers control their schedules and methods; gig workers trade that control for autonomy. Forcing employee-style obligations onto a model built on independence doesn't equalize the safety net—it destroys the alternative. Governments should expand portable, opt-in benefits rather than mandating a rigid, one-size-fits-all employment relationship that gig workers explicitly reject.

[P2.1] CON’s case rests on a category error. Requiring prorated benefits does not “reclassify” gig work [C1.1]; it leaves the independent-contractor label intact while ending the externalization of basic labor costs. The dependence CON fears is already produced by platforms through pricing, dispatch, and ratings. Benefits do not create that reliance; they acknowledge it.
[P2.2] Nor does an hourly liability destroy flexibility in the way CON claims. Proration is precisely a sliding scale: light engagement triggers light obligations, sustained engagement triggers real ones. If a platform responds by capping hours below any meaningful threshold, that is not evidence that gig work requires unprotected workers; it is evidence that the platform’s model depends on labor without a safety net.
[P2.3] The incidence argument [C1.2] proves too much. Workers already pay the costs—in skipped care, emergency-room debt, no leave while sick, and no retirement after decades of app profits. A contribution embedded in compensation is not a pay cut when the alternative is that the worker and taxpayers absorb the entire risk. Spousal or marketplace coverage may exist for some, but marketplace subsidies do not fund retirement or paid leave, and a part-time retail worker with a spouse’s plan still receives prorated obligations under ordinary benefits rules.
[P2.4] CON’s administrative objection [C1.3] is weaker still. Platforms already track hours, waits, and trip data to pay workers and manage dispatch; converting those records into benefit contributions is a modest extension. The litigation nightmare over idle time is a design problem, not a reason to leave delivery drivers uncovered. Opt-in portability [C1.4] is not an alternative; voluntary systems collapse under adverse selection and leave low-income workers to save out of wages that never arrive.
[P2.5] A default contribution tied to hours is the only mechanism that makes the platform that benefits from the labor pay its share.
[P2.6] The status quo is not neutral. It subsidizes platform profits with public health and old-age insecurity. Prorated benefits correct that subsidy without abolishing app-based work.
