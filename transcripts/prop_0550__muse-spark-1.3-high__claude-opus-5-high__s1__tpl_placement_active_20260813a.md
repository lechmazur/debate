[P1.1] College subsidies pay young people to leave work and bet four years on a degree that may never come. Paid apprenticeships pay them to build skills employers are already buying. For long-run earnings, the second bet wins.
[P1.2] Expanding four-year enrollment sounds generous, but it pushes on the weakest link in the system: completion. The students drawn in by new subsidies are disproportionately less academically prepared, lower-income, and more sensitive to cost and setback. Many enroll, borrow, then leave without a credential — carrying debt, lost wages, and a damaged transcript instead of a premium. Even among graduates, a familiar pattern follows: years of general coursework with weak connection to hiring, then underemployment in jobs that never required the degree.
[P1.3] Subsidizing more years of that gamble does not create more high-paying completions one-for-one.
[P1.4] Employer-linked pathways reverse the sequence. A young person in a paid apprenticeship in electrical work, machining, commercial driving, medical assisting, or IT support earns from month one, avoids debt, and learns on the equipment actually used on the job. Because an employer co-designs the training and often hires from it, the credential carries a job offer with it, not just a signal. Early employment compounds: wage growth, promotion, seniority, and the option to stack later credentials while employed.
[P1.5] Most importantly, apprenticeships reach the majority college policy leaves behind. Most young Americans will not finish a four-year degree even with larger subsidies. Telling them their only high-earnings route requires leaving paid work for four years is economically perverse. Give a nineteen-year-old two years of paid training, no student debt, and a direct line into a high-demand trade or health or tech role, and lifetime earnings rise through higher employment rates, earlier earnings, and steeper mid-career growth.
[P1.6] Per public dollar, buying jobs plus skills beats buying seats plus debt.

[C1.2] Start with arithmetic, because a policy's earnings effect is the gain per person times the number of people reached. That is a textbook externality, and it is why every country that runs apprenticeships at scale had to build decades of employer associations, chambers, and union structures to sustain them.
[C1.3] Appropriate more money in the US and you get a modest number of good placements, a queue of employers who want subsidized labor rather than trainees, and a program that cannot absorb even a fraction of a cohort. [C1.4] Second, the marginal student PRO writes off is the one with the most to gain. Cost is not just a filter on entry; it is a leading cause of dropout. [C1.5] Third, picture the nineteen-year-old PRO celebrates at thirty-four. He learned a particular plant's equipment, a particular firm's process. That plant reorganizes, the freight route automates, his knees give out. His training was excellent and specific, and specificity is exactly what depreciates.
[C1.6] The degree holder's earnings curve keeps climbing through mid-career because general skills travel across employers, occupations, and technologies.
[C1.7] Apprenticeships win the first five years. Subsidized degrees win decades six through forty. The motion asks about the long run, and the long run belongs to portable human capital bought at scale.

[P2.1] The opponent's scalability story gets the economics backwards. Firms do hesitate to pay for purely general training, but apprenticeships solve that by mixing work and learning: the apprentice produces while training, so the employer captures value immediately. That is why construction trades, hospitals, logistics firms and tech shops already run paid pipelines without importing foreign chambers. What public money expands is coordinators, classroom time, and stipends.
[P2.2] College "seats respond to money" only if you count bodies in lecture halls, not degrees in hands.
[P2.3] And the celebrated marginal student is exactly why enrollment subsidies disappoint on earnings. Yes, a broken car can force a dropout, but the dropout crisis is not mostly small emergencies; it is failed courses, work schedules that collide with classes, family care, and years without earnings. Pushing more fragile entrants into four-year programs raises enrollment far faster than completion, so average long-run earnings per added dollar collapse. The return to a degree for someone who finishes is not the return to a policy that mostly manufactures some-college, debt, and stopout.
[P2.4] Then there is the portability myth: specific skills depreciate, general skills travel. Real apprenticeships today end in licenses that travel employer to employer — electrician's journeyman status, nursing and medical credentials, commercial licenses, machining and IT certifications — carrying overtime and contracting premiums. What depreciates faster is years of general coursework with no employer attachment, followed by retail or service work that never used the major. Employer-linked means linked to an occupation, not a single machine.
[P2.5] That flips the long-run arithmetic invoked against us. Long-run earnings are not just wages at 45 conditional on professional work; they include whether you worked at all in your twenties, how much debt compounded, and whether you climbed a ladder or restarted at 26 as a graduate with no experience. Get paid from month one, enter a high-demand occupation with placement built in, then stack later credentials while employed, and compounding works for you. Subsidize more waiting and borrowing, and it works against you.
