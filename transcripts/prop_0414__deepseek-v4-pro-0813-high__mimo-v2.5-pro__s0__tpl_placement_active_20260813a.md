[P1.1] Tourist taxes are not general revenue. They are collected from visitors because tourism imposes concentrated costs on the residents of overcrowded cities. Those costs land hardest in three places: homes converted to short-term rentals, transit systems clogged at peak hours, and public spaces buried under visitor waste. If the remedy is absorbed into a general budget, the connection between cost and repair is severed.
[P1.2] Visitors do not sleep in the general fund; they sleep in housing stock, queue for buses, and fill the bins.
[P1.3] Earmarking primarily for resident housing, transit, and sanitation makes the tax a corrective instrument. When tourism grows, the neighborhoods absorbing that growth automatically receive more capacity: more affordable units, more frequent service, more cleaning. General budgets permit the reverse: a city can add millions of visitors while service levels in working-class neighborhoods stagnate.
[P1.4] This is also a question of political consent. Residents in cities from Barcelona to Amsterdam have turned against tourism because they bear the noise and displacement but see the revenue vanish into unrelated projects or tourism promotion. Earmarking gives visible proof that the people who live next to the crowds get the first claim on the money the crowds generate.
[P1.5] Flexibility is not a virtue if it means the freedom to ignore the exact harm a tax was meant to address. Earmarking most—not all—of the revenue still allows limited general spending, but it prevents the common failure: tourist taxes used as an ordinary cash source while resident housing, transit, and sanitation remain underfunded.
