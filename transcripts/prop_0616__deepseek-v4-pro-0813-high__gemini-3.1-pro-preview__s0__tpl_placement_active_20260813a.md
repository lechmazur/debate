[P1.1] Advanced military AI is a hardware problem. Frontier AI systems run on leading-edge accelerators; those accelerators are made in fabs; those fabs depend on a small set of advanced lithography, deposition, etch, and metrology tools. That is the point of control. Unlike finished chips, these machines are not easy to smuggle or hide.
[P1.2] They are large, serialized, software-linked, and require calibrated installation, spare parts, maintenance, and updates from the producer. Restricting them attacks an adversary’s production capacity, not just one year’s chip inventory.
[P1.3] Delay matters strategically. AI-enabled targeting, autonomous swarms, real-time intelligence fusion, electronic warfare, and cyber operations are force multipliers. A hostile state that can fabricate frontier accelerators at scale can close the capability gap suddenly, producing a military breakout. Slowing that process is not a guarantee of permanent superiority, but it buys time for defensive adaptation, deterrence, and alliance coordination.
[P1.4] That is how arms control works: not perfect denial, but costly, slower access.
[P1.5] Commercial cost is genuine, but it is the wrong test. If a government fails to restrict the tools, firms may reap short-term export revenue while the state loses a long-term strategic lever. Once an adversary builds an indigenous leading-edge fab, it no longer needs foreign permission for military AI hardware. Exporters can be compensated, production can shift to trusted markets, and the restriction can be limited to the most advanced, militarily relevant tools.
[P1.6] But a lost technological edge cannot be bought back at any price.
[P1.7] The reply that adversaries could use older nodes or workarounds actually concedes the point. Older nodes produce less capable, less efficient systems for the same military tasks; workarounds consume years, scarce engineers, and money. The proposition only promises to slow adversarial military AI, not erase it. At the margin where wars are won, that slowdown is worth major commercial cost.
