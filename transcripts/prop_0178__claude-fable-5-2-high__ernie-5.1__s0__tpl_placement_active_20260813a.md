[C1.1] The proposition assumes a single lever can out‑perform every labeling or education effort, but the evidence tells a different story. Reformulation mandates are blunt instruments that force manufacturers to hit numeric targets while ignoring what those targets actually achieve. When sodium caps are imposed, companies often replace salt with sugar or potassium salts that can be harmful to people with kidney disease. Fat reductions frequently lead to added refined carbohydrates to preserve mouthfeel, driving caloric intake upward.
[C1.2] The result is a reformulated product that looks healthier on a label but may be worse for the very metabolic outcomes we care about.
[C1.3] Labeling and education, by contrast, work on the demand side. Front‑of‑pack warnings in Mexico and Chile have cut purchases of sugary drinks and high‑salt snacks by measurable percentages, and they do so without forcing a one‑size‑fits‑all product redesign. Education campaigns that teach cooking skills and reading nutrition facts create lasting changes that persist even when the food supply shifts. These approaches also avoid the political backlash that routinely weakens mandates: the British sugar levy was repeatedly diluted after industry lobbying, and the promised population‑wide blood‑pressure fall was modest compared with the sustained drops seen in countries that invested in school‑based nutrition curricula.
[C1.4] Mandates also create perverse incentives. Manufacturers may split their lines, keeping a “premium” version above the cap for wealthier consumers while the reformulated, lower‑quality version floods low‑income shelves, reinforcing the very inequality the proposition claims to solve. Education empowers the same low‑income shoppers to choose better options when they exist, rather than assuming they will accept whatever the factory decides is acceptable.
[C1.5] Finally, the health gain from a mandate is limited to the nutrients it caps. It does nothing for dietary diversity, food access, or the cultural drivers of over‑consumption. A comprehensive labeling and education strategy addresses all of those dimensions simultaneously, making it the more powerful, more adaptable tool for improving population health.
