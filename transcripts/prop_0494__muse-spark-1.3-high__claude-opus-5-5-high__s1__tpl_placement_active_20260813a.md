[P1.1] Modern peer review runs on invisible unpaid labor, and it shows. Papers wait months for two or three anonymous paragraphs that can kill years of work with no reasons given and no appeal. Good reviewers toil for nothing while lazy ones dash off a dismissal, biased ones block rivals, and no one outside the editor ever knows. Anonymity without compensation does not protect rigor; it protects irresponsibility.
[P1.2] Paying reviewers turns a favor into a professional obligation. A modest fee tied to deadlines and quality standards would buy speed and care: reviewers could justify the hours, editors could demand thoroughness and enforce turnaround, journals could recruit beyond the overtaxed few. Unpaid review pretends prestige is payment, but prestige flows to authors and publishers, not reviewers. Paying is simple fairness, and fairness produces reliability.
[P1.3] When work is compensated, shirking has consequences.
[P1.4] Publishing reports alongside papers cures the secrecy disease. When a review is signed and public, cruelty has a cost, vague rejection must become argument, and hidden biases — against young labs, foreign names, heterodox ideas — face daylight. Readers gain enormously: they see what reviewers challenged, what authors conceded, where uncertainty remains. That context is more valuable than a binary accept stamp.
[P1.5] And publication gives reviewers the one currency academia respects: visible credit for intellectual work.
[P1.6] Together the reforms reinforce each other. Pay without transparency risks purchased rubber stamps; transparency without pay asks even more free labor under scrutiny. Paid, public review creates a traceable record of who judged what and why, rewarding careful critics and exposing sloppy or self-serving ones. The present system asks us to trust science while hiding how its gatekeeping works.
[P1.7] Open, compensated review would be faster, fairer, and more useful to readers — which is what working better means.

PRO names bias against "young labs" and "foreign names," but disclosure hands power to the established: senior figures can criticize freely, while juniors must calculate.
