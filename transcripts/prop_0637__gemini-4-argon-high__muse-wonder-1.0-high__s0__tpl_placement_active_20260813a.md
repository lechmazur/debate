[P1.1] Freezing a transit neighborhood in amber does not protect its low-income residents; it guarantees their eventual removal. When a city invests heavily in rail or rapid bus lines, nearby land becomes highly desirable. If the municipality restricts new housing there, affluent newcomers do not vanish.
[P1.2] Instead, they bid up the existing, older housing stock, converting duplexes into single-family homes and outbidding working-class tenants for the exact same apartments. The choice is never between densification with displacement risk and stability with zero displacement. The choice is between absorbing demand in new, vertical housing or forcing that demand to cannibalize existing affordable units.
[P1.3] Allowing densification by-right—meaning compliant projects proceed automatically without discretionary political battles—is the only way to break this scarcity trap. Case-by-case discretionary review empowers a small, unrepresentative faction of incumbent homeowners to delay or shrink projects at public hearings. Those delays add massive financing costs, ensuring that only high-margin luxury developments survive the gauntlet. By-right zoning removes the neighborhood veto, slashes permitting risk, and unlocks the volume of construction needed to stabilize regional rents.
[P1.4] Even when redeveloping parcels near a station creates localized, short-term displacement risk, the trade-off overwhelmingly favors by-right density. First, a single mid-rise apartment building near transit replaces a handful of structures with dozens of homes, acting as a sponge that absorbs high-income demand and relieves price pressure across the broader community.
[P1.5] Second, locking working families out of transit corridors forces them into car-dependent sprawl, saddling them with thousands of dollars in annual vehicle expenses and worsening climate emissions. Finally, zoning scarcity is the crudest, most destructive tool for tenant protection. Municipalities can address short-term transition pains directly by using the surging property tax revenues from new dense buildings to fund tenant relocation assistance, legal aid, or right-to-return guarantees. We should not starve an entire city of transit-accessible housing tomorrow to avoid localized friction we can directly mitigate today.

[C1.1] By-right densification near transit asks the residents most vulnerable to a new station to surrender the only leverage that could keep them there, in exchange for a speculative promise that expensive new homes will eventually help someone else.
[C1.2] When development is automatic if it meets form and height rules, a municipality cannot condition approval on replacing demolished low-rent units one-for-one, keeping rents within reach of displaced tenants, phasing construction, or guaranteeing a right to return at a comparable rent. That is not a technical detail. Near transit, the cheapest housing is usually the older duplexes, garden apartments, and small rentals most attractive to tear down. By-right makes them targets first, while leaving whether anything affordable returns to developer profit.
[C1.3] That loss is not short-term friction. A tenant priced out of a transit neighborhood rarely waits nearby for filtering to work. She moves farther from work, loses childcare and community ties, takes on a car payment, and seldom returns to a new building leasing at transit-premium rents. The new mid-rise may contain more units, but more units at double the rent does not preserve access; it changes who access is for.
[C1.4] Rising nearby values can lift taxes, insurance, and landlord expectations on surrounding buildings, spreading pressure beyond the redeveloped lot.
[C1.5] Nor does removing discretionary review make the market neutral or abundant. It hands certainty to landowners and developers, whose land values jump the moment upzoning is announced, long before any rent relief could appear. They can build luxury product, land-bank the entitlement, or sell at the higher value, with no ability to bargain for affordability. Hearings can be abused, but the answer is clear rules with mandatory inclusion, replacement, and tenant protections enforced as conditions—not unconditional entitlement.
[C1.6] Transit investment should expand opportunity for existing residents first. If a policy pushes them out to create room for wealthier newcomers, calling the harm temporary does not make the trade justified.

If that promise were binding, approval would no longer proceed “even if” displacement rises; it would be conditional. The motion deliberately chooses entitlement over guarantee.
[C5.3] Once that order is set, the loss is not short-term. A demolished low-rent home, a broken lease, and a move away from school, work, and care do not reappear when a market-rate building opens years later at double the rent [C1.3]. The promised benefit, by contrast, remains speculative: that luxury supply will absorb rather than attract demand, discipline nearby landlords, and eventually filter back to the people removed.
[C5.4] Conditional densification reverses that order without freezing transit neighborhoods: protect sitting tenants, require comparable replacement, and build first where no one must be erased. A city should not make the residents who made a transit neighborhood livable pay upfront for access they may never regain.
