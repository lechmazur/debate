[P1.1] A host-read ad sells one thing: the belief that the person you've spent hundreds of hours with actually said these words, in this moment, about this product. Advertisers pay a premium for host-reads over programmatic spots precisely because that voice carries personal credibility. When a cloned voice delivers the pitch, the advertiser is still buying that credibility—but the host never extended it. The listener is paying for a vouch that didn't happen.
[P1.2] Endorsement law already rests on a simple principle: an endorsement must reflect the honest, actual experience of the endorser. A synthetic read severs that link. The host may never have seen the script. The host may not even know the ad ran.
[P1.3] Yet the listener hears "I've been using this mattress for months" in a voice they trust like a friend's. That's not a gray area; it's a false statement of who is speaking.
[P1.4] Voluntary disclosure cannot fix this, because the incentives run the wrong way. The whole commercial value of the clone is that it's indistinguishable. Every creator who honestly labels a synthetic read makes it less valuable; every creator who stays silent collects the full premium. Without a legal floor, honesty is a competitive disadvantage, and the honest creators get undercut by the silent ones.
[P1.5] Markets with hidden quality differences don't self-correct—they degrade.
[P1.6] The remedy is almost trivially light. One spoken sentence: "This ad uses an AI version of my voice." Creators already announce "this episode is sponsored by." Nobody argues that line chills creativity.
[P1.7] The requirement touches only ads and endorsements, not storytelling, satire, or art.
[P1.8] Picture a mid-sized podcaster whose contract lets the network run cloned reads after the show ends. Listeners keep hearing her recommend financial products for years. She's gone; her trust is still being spent. Disclosure is the minimum that protects both the audience and the host whose reputation is being rented out without their voice.

[C1.1] This mandate is a clumsy, overbroad solution hunting for a problem existing law already solves, and it will do more to erode trust in advertising disclosures than to protect listeners. First, there is no coherent, enforceable bright line between the AI vocal tools the rule targets and the routine production tricks creators have used for decades without disclosure: punch-and-roll editing to fix flubs, vocal tuning to smooth a hoarse delivery, digital stitching of dozens of takes into one seamless read, even software that removes coughs, ums, and background noise.
[C1.2] Modern AI voice tools sit on a continuous spectrum with those edits, often used to patch a misread line, record a last-minute sponsor copy tweak when the host has a cold, or clean up audio recorded in a home studio. Forcing legal disclosure every time AI touches a vocal track, even when the host wrote the script, tested the product, and signed off on the final ad, labels perfectly ordinary production choices as deceptive, when no reasonable listener cares if a single line was patched instead of re-recorded in a full session.
[C1.3] Worse, flooding listeners with trivial, irrelevant disclosures for every minor AI edit trains audiences to tune out all ad warnings, making it harder, not easier, to spot genuinely bad acts. Existing false advertising and right of publicity laws already penalize the actual harm PRO cites: running ads a host never approved, attaching a host’s reputation to products they never endorsed, or making false claims about their personal experience. Those laws target real deception without imposing one-size-fits-all compliance costs that fall heaviest on small, independent creators who lack legal teams to parse vague, shifting rules about how much AI use counts as enough to trigger disclosure, turning routine post-production into a legal liability for creators who can least afford it.
