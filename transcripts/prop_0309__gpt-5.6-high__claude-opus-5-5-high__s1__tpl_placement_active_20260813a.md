[P1.1] Medical access is not an ordinary consumer good. A market can tolerate the nearest cinema or supermarket moving an hour away; it cannot treat an hour of additional travel during a stroke, hemorrhage, or complicated birth as harmless inefficiency. The proper test is therefore not whether every rural facility maximizes revenue, but whether residents retain reliable access to essential care within clinically meaningful time.
[P1.2] Rural hospitals face a structural market failure. They must maintain staff, equipment, and readiness around the clock despite serving small, dispersed populations. That unused capacity looks wasteful on a balance sheet, yet it is precisely what society needs when emergencies are unpredictable. The benefits also extend beyond paying patients: emergency preparedness, infectious-disease response, maternity coverage, and stabilization after accidents protect entire communities.
[P1.3] Markets underprovide services whose value lies partly in being available before anyone knows who will need them.
[P1.4] Consolidation cannot erase geography. A larger regional center may perform specialized procedures more efficiently, but its excellence is irrelevant to a patient who cannot reach it in time. Longer journeys also burden routine care: older residents postpone appointments, low-income families lose wages arranging transport, and chronic conditions worsen until they become expensive emergencies. Closure can further drive away clinicians, employers, and families, weakening the community and shrinking the tax base.
[P1.5] The apparent saving at one facility is displaced into ambulance systems, referral hospitals, disability, and preventable deterioration.
[P1.6] Subsidy need not mean preserving every building or duplicating every specialty. Governments can fund a minimum-access network: emergency stabilization, primary and maternity care where needed, telemedicine support, rotating specialists, transport links, or smaller rural emergency facilities connected to regional centers. Funding can be conditional on quality, coordination, and demonstrated geographic need. This captures consolidation’s advantages for complex care without abandoning local first-line capacity.
[P1.7] Markets optimize billable volume; governments must also protect equal citizenship, resilience, and survival. When distance itself determines whether illness becomes death or disability, local access is infrastructure—not a luxury that rural people should lose because their postcode is unprofitable.
