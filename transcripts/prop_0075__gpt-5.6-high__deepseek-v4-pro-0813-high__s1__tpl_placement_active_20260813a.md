[P1.1] Religious liberty is strongest when it protects religion as religion—not when it becomes a transferable license to ignore civic rules in ordinary commerce. The proposition draws that necessary line.
[P1.2] Core religious functions require meaningful autonomy. A congregation must be free to select clergy, define doctrine, conduct worship, form members, and decide who speaks in its name. Applying ordinary anti-discrimination law to those decisions could force a faith community to alter its message or leadership. Exemptions there protect freedom of conscience and association rather than mere institutional convenience.
[P1.3] But the justification changes when a religious institution enters broad commercial activity. If it operates a public shop, rental business, or large service enterprise, customers and ordinary employees encounter it as a market actor. They may have no realistic alternative, no role in transmitting doctrine, and no reason to bear exclusion because of the owner’s identity. A blanket exemption would transform religious freedom from a shield against state interference into a sword against equal participation.
[P1.4] This distinction also prevents unfair competition. A religiously affiliated business should not gain powers denied to secular competitors while serving the same public, hiring for the same nonreligious jobs, and earning revenue in the same market. [P1.5] The rule can be administered without judges deciding theology. Institutions may explain their beliefs; courts need only examine the function at issue: Does this person lead worship, teach faith, govern the religious community, or represent its doctrine? [P1.6] If the role is bookkeeping for a commercial subsidiary or selling ordinary goods to the public, apply the ordinary rule.
[P1.7] Neither right should erase the other. Tight, function-based exemptions preserve the decisions a religious community must control while ensuring that public commerce remains governed by equal rules. That is not hostility toward religion.
