[C1.1] Notice what the motion smuggles in. "When crowding, pollution, and housing pressure outweigh revenue gains" sounds like an accounting test, but nobody has ever run it. No city publishes a ledger weighing a resident's lost sleep against a dockworker's wage. In practice the clause is a slogan that any council can declare satisfied the moment a few streets feel full in August.
[C1.2] So the real question is whether, once a city decides tourism has become a burden, a hard cap on ships and coaches is the right instrument. It is the wrong one, for four reasons.
[C1.3] First, caps ration what should be priced. A ship berthing beside homes creates real costs: fumes, noise, a surge of feet on old stone. The honest response is to charge for them — berth fees scaled to emissions, timed landing slots, a per-passenger levy — and spend the money on shore power, cleaning, and resident services. A cap forfeits that revenue and leaves the pollution of the ships still allowed exactly as it was.
[C1.4] Pricing shrinks harm; a cap merely shrinks the count.
[C1.5] Second, caps misdiagnose housing. Day-trippers do not sleep in the city; they cannot convert a flat into a holiday let. Residents are pushed out by short-term rental platforms and permissive zoning. Capping buses to fix housing is like capping umbrellas to fix rain, and it lets councils avoid the fight with landlords that would actually keep families in place.
[C1.6] Third, caps displace rather than solve. Push the ships to an outer port and the same passengers arrive by shuttle, now with added road traffic. Dubrovnik's crowds did not vanish; they rescheduled.
[C1.7] Fourth, caps are regressive. The cruise and the coach are how the elderly, the disabled, families on a budget, and people who cannot afford three nights in a hotel ever see Venice or Bruges. A cap tells those visitors to stay home while the wealthy overnight guest walks in unchallenged. Rationing by cap means rationing by class.
[C1.8] Manage the flow. Charge for the harm. Don't lock the gate.
