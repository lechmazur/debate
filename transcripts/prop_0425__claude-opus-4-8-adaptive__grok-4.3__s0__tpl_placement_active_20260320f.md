[C1.1] Digital platforms reach students that physical infrastructure systematically misses. Commuters, students with disabilities, caregivers, and those on irregular schedules cannot reliably attend weekly club meetings or occupy common spaces. A well-designed engagement app or moderated online community can deliver consistent contact without requiring physical presence, turning isolation into low-friction participation. Prioritizing brick-and-mortar spending first simply leaves these groups waiting longer for support that already exists in digital form.
[C1.2] The claim that in-person contact is cheaper and more durable ignores recurring maintenance, security, and staffing costs that quickly exceed the price of scalable software licenses. Once built, digital tools can be updated instantly across an entire campus population, whereas a new lounge or funded club remains fixed to one location and one narrow slice of users. Universities facing enrollment growth or budget pressure cannot expand physical capacity fast enough to match digital reach.
[C1.3] Belonging is not exclusively physical. Sustained online groups, peer-support channels, and interest-based forums generate repeated interaction and weak ties that many students report as meaningful. When institutions invest in these first, they create baseline engagement that later physical events can build upon rather than replace. Reversing the order risks spending scarce funds on spaces that sit underused while digital needs remain unmet.
[C1.4] PRO's sequencing assumes physical infrastructure automatically generates the demand that justifies later digital layers. In practice, students already live in digital environments; platforms that meet them there convert existing habits into measurable connection instead of hoping empty rooms will fill themselves.
