[P1.1] When a college town hits a severe housing shortage, a public university that keeps recruiting internationally without limit is exporting its growth costs onto renters, workers, and its own students. A temporary cap is the responsible correction.
[P1.2] Public universities have a dual fiduciary duty. They are funded, chartered and subsidized by the local taxpayer to educate residents, not to maximize global enrollment revenue. Their license to operate rests on the community's ability to house them. If that community can no longer house itself, the university cannot ethically keep importing demand.
[P1.3] The demand shock is real and immediate. In markets like Vancouver, Sydney, Toronto or Bristol, vacancy has fallen below 1%. An extra 2,000 international students doesn't just need 2,000 beds; in a tight market they trigger bidding wars, illegal overcrowding - six to a two-bedroom - and push low-income local renters and first-year domestic students out. And international growth is different from domestic access.
[P1.4] Domestic numbers are already managed as part of a public planning mandate; international intake has been left unconstrained precisely because it chases fees. It also leans hardest on the private rental market, because international students rarely get guaranteed dorms.
[P1.5] Building more purpose-built student housing would help, but it takes 3-5 years. Zoning reform takes longer. Enrollment decisions happen in months. The only lever that works on the timeline of the crisis is enrollment.
[P1.6] Third, unconstrained recruitment fails its own students. Recruiting young people from abroad into a city where they will spend 70% of their budget on a precarious room and commute an hour is not opportunity, it is extraction. [C1.1] A cap does not build a bed. That is the whole problem with PRO's case. Every mechanism PRO describes — bidding wars, six to a two-bedroom, vacancy under 1% — is a symptom of cities that have refused to permit housing for decades. Vancouver and Sydney were unaffordable before the recent enrollment surge and will remain unaffordable after any cap.
[C1.2] Turning students away treats the one visible newcomer as the cause of a structural failure, and it lets the actual culprits — councils and zoning boards — off the hook entirely. Once the university has "done its part" by freezing intake, the political pressure to upzone simply evaporates.
[C1.3] Second, a cap removes the money that housing needs. International fees are the cross-subsidy that keeps domestic tuition down, funds departments, and, crucially, finances campus housing. Canada's recent federal cap is the live experiment: colleges announced program closures and layoffs within months, while rents kept rising. PRO's own timeline concedes purpose-built housing takes years — so why cut off the revenue stream that pays for it at precisely the moment it is most needed?
[C1.4] A university flush with fee income can master-lease apartment blocks, build modular residences in under two years, and guarantee beds to every international admit. A capped university can do none of that.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's trigger is a trap. "Hold intake flat until vacancy recovers above 3%" sounds temporary, but many of these cities have not seen 3% vacancy this century. A "temporary" cap indexed to a number the city controls becomes permanent by default, and the city has every incentive to keep it that way.
[C1.6] Finally, notice who bears the cost. PRO frames unconstrained recruitment as "extraction," yet the students still choose to come, and they spend, work, and pay taxes locally. Rationing admission by passport shrinks the pie rather than growing the housing stock. The responsible university scales beds with enrollment, not enrollment with the failures of its planning department.
