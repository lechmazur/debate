[P1.1] The euro is the single greatest structural flaw in the modern European Union, a top-down forced monetary union that bypassed the deep political integration required to make it work. By locking diverse economies into a single currency without a shared fiscal authority, the EU stripped member states of the core adjustment tool they once had: independent exchange rates. When national economies face asymmetric shocks—say, a tourism collapse in Spain, or a manufacturing slump in Italy—they can no longer devalue their own currency to boost exports and stem job losses.
[P1.2] This has turned routine economic downturns into existential crises for the bloc, as seen in the 2010s sovereign debt crashes that required brutal, EU-mandated austerity that cratered local growth and fueled mass unemployment across southern Europe.
[P1.3] Without the euro, the EU would have remained a flexible, voluntary trade and regulatory cooperation bloc, not a half-baked fiscal union. Member states could have tailored monetary policy to their own unique cycles: Greece could have eased its currency during recession, Germany could have tightened during its export booms, avoiding the persistent north-south imbalance that now defines EU politics. The euro’s locked exchange rate also artificially boosted Germany’s export competitiveness while overvaluing southern currencies, deepening regional inequality rather than closing it.
[P1.4] Most damaging, the euro has eroded public trust in the EU itself. The harsh bailout terms, fiscal straitjackets, and never-ending debt crises tied directly to the single currency have fueled the rise of Euroskeptic parties across the continent. Without the euro, the EU would not have had to impose these unpopular, divisive measures, and public support for the project would be far higher today. The bloc’s current fragility, political polarization, and economic stagnation are all direct products of the common currency—none of which would exist if it had never been adopted.
