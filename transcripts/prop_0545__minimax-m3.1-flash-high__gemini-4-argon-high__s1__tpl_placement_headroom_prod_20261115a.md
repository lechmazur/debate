[P1.1] Presumed consent should mean an opt-out system, not permission to override objections. Its central advantage is straightforward: it removes the burden of affirmatively signing up. Patients willing to donate often never enter a registry because they never encounter the form or postpone it. A default authorizing donation unless someone expressly declines converts that lost enrollment into potential donors.
[P1.2] For most patients, this is the decisive mechanism: more eligible organs enter the donation pathway, so deaths that currently yield no donation can generate transplants. The gain is measurable in completed transplants and lives saved, not merely registry sign-ups.
[P1.3] The ethical objection fails if the system includes genuine, timely notice and a simple, no-penalty route to opt out. Consent remains informed and voluntary because silence follows an understandable notification; an objection is respected at every stage. No one is compelled to undergo an operation, and families cannot disregard a patient’s recorded refusal. Conversely, under opt-in, family refusal can frustrate a patient’s previously expressed willingness.
[P1.4] Presumed consent, properly designed, makes the patient’s own choice controlling rather than transferring it to relatives.
[P1.5] Family distress need not rise either. Relatives often experience uncertainty and grief at the moment of death, not because donation itself causes additional grief. Advance information and respectful communication reduce surprise. The relevant comparison is not an imagined world without distress, but two systems providing similar end-of-life care: one requiring action to save lives and the other enabling action by default.
[P1.6] Any coercion, secrecy, or harmful pressure is an implementation failure that safeguards should prevent, not an inherent reason to reject the rule.
[P1.7] Presumed consent therefore improves effectiveness while respecting autonomy. It increases the chance that a willing patient becomes an actual donor, while objections remain decisive. More transplants need not mean less ethical protection or more family distress.

Deprived of certainty that their loved one truly wanted to donate, cautious families frequently say no, leaving actual transplant rates dependent on intensive care infrastructure and family counseling rather than the legal default.
[C1.5] Because presumed consent cannot simultaneously force organs into the supply, preserve genuine consent, and spare grieving families, the proposition fails.
