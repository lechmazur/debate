[P1.1] The case for taxing sugar-sweetened beverages rests on a simple reality: price drives behavior, and behavior is driving a public health crisis that falls hardest on exactly the households opponents claim to protect.
[P1.2] Sugar-sweetened beverages are not an ordinary grocery item. They are the single largest source of added sugar in many diets, clinically linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. These conditions do not strike randomly. They cluster in lower-income communities, where aggressive marketing, limited healthy alternatives, and food environments saturated with cheap sugary drinks make overconsumption the path of least resistance.
[P1.3] The health burden is already regressive. Diabetes is already regressive. Treating the tax as the source of inequity mistakes the instrument for the disease.
[P1.4] A meaningful tax — high enough to actually reduce purchases, not just gesture — disrupts that cycle. When Mexico implemented its soda tax, purchases of taxed beverages declined, with the largest reductions among lower-income households. That is not a failure of equity. It is the point: the households most at risk benefit most from shifting consumption.
[P1.5] The regressivity objection also treats the tax in isolation. Revenue can be recycled — funding healthy food access, school nutrition programs, or direct household rebates. If the tax raises money from a harmful product and redirects it toward the communities bearing the health costs, the net distributional picture shifts substantially. A tax designed well is not a penalty on the poor; it is a reallocation away from products that harm them.
[P1.6] The tobacco precedent is instructive. Cigarette taxes were called regressive too. They also produced the largest health gains among lower-income smokers, contributing to historic declines in smoking-related disease. No serious policy framework would now say we should have kept cigarettes cheap to spare poor families the tax.
[P1.7] The real question is whether governments are willing to act on preventable harm. Half-measures that keep harmful products affordable protect the market, not the household.
