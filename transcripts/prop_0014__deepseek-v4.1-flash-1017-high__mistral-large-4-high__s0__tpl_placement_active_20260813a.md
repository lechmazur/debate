[P1.1] The national debt is not just large; it is large at a moment when the government’s cheapest borrowing days are over. Debt held by the public is around the size of the entire economy and, on current policy, is set to keep climbing as an aging population raises health and retirement costs. That changes the meaning of every new spending commitment. When money was cheap and debt low, a new program might be financed with modest future cost.
[P1.2] Today, each permanent commitment is layered onto a budget already absorbing higher interest payments—payments that now rival major agencies and buy no schools, roads, or defense.
[P1.3] The core of this case is priority, not prohibition. Emergencies and genuinely high-return investments can still be argued. But most new spending commitments should wait until there is a credible path to stabilize the debt. Why?
[P1.4] Because interest compounds. Higher debt raises borrowing costs, crowds out private investment, and shrinks fiscal room exactly when a recession or war arrives. It also makes future adjustment harder: once a program has constituents, it becomes a baseline, not a choice.
[P1.5] The counterargument will be that debt is sustainable and that investment pays for itself. But sustainability is a trajectory, not a mood; the trajectory is worsening. And “investment pays for itself” is a claim about specific projects with measurable returns, not a license for every new commitment. The burden of proof should sit with those proposing new permanent spending, not with those demanding that the books be stabilized.
[P1.6] A government that cannot control its interest bill eventually controls nothing else; it borrows to pay interest and postpones choices until a crisis makes them for it. Fix the leaky roof before adding a wing. Serious deficit reduction first; new commitments after, and only with offsets. That is the responsible priority.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses a balance sheet with a strategy. Yes, debt is large, but treating deficit reduction as the first priority mistakes the symptom for the disease. The debt-to-GDP ratio—the metric that actually matters—falls not when you cut spending, but when you grow the economy faster than you borrow.
[C1.2] Consider the arithmetic. Austerity shrinks the denominator. When governments slash investment during high-debt periods, they reduce future productive capacity, tax revenue, and growth. Europe's post-2010 austerity experiment demonstrated this across the continent: deep cuts produced stagnation, not stabilization, and debt ratios rose despite the pain.
[C1.3] The United States has the unique advantage of issuing debt in its own currency; the binding constraint is not the nominal debt level but real productive capacity and inflation. Cutting infrastructure, research, and education to reduce a number on paper leaves the country poorer and less able to service any debt. That poverty is the true cost of premature austerity.
[C1.4] The claim that interest crowds out private investment reverses causation. U.S. Treasuries remain the world's safest asset; global demand for dollars means the risk is not a sudden spike in rates but underinvestment. Public investment in infrastructure, clean energy, and technology raises productivity, which supports sustainable growth and expands private opportunity.
[C1.5] The alternative—deferring maintenance and innovation—creates larger future liabilities: crumbling bridges, climate damage, and lost competitiveness that dwarf current interest costs.
[C1.6] The "fix the roof before adding a wing" metaphor fails because the wing pays for the roof. Strategic borrowing for high-return public investment grows the tax base, making the debt sustainable. Deficit reduction pursued as an end in itself is self-defeating when it sacrifices the very growth required to outgrow the debt. The priority should be expanding productive capacity, not accounting austerity that immiserates the present to protect a spreadsheet.
[C1.7] A government that underinvests today inherits a weaker economy tomorrow, and a weaker economy cannot pay its debts, no matter how small the deficit.

[P2.3] Issuing debt in our own currency buys time and reduces default risk; it does not repeal real resource constraints or inflation. [P2.4] The “crowding out is reversed” claim also misses the relevant scarcity. Even if private investment is not visibly crowded out today, fiscal space is. [P2.5] This is a priority dispute, not a prohibition. CON’s standard would let every appealing category jump the queue and call it growth.
