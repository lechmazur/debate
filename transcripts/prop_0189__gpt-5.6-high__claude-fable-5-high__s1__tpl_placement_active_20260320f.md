[P1.2] The right comparison is not today’s construction bill against next year’s overflow budget. It is lifecycle cost against lifecycle cost. [P1.3] Separation requires major capital once, but then preserves scarce wastewater-treatment capacity for actual sewage. Cities can phase work by watershed, coordinate it with street and utility renewal, and avoid paying to dig the same corridor several times.
[P1.4] Separation also produces a more resilient system. Combined networks are vulnerable precisely when cities most need them: during intense rainfall, power disruptions, and flooding. [P1.5] Meanwhile, sanitary sewage remains enclosed and treatment plants are less likely to be hydraulically overwhelmed. Separation does not make stormwater harmless; it makes each waste stream visible and manageable with the appropriate treatment instead of mixing both into one emergency.
