[P1.1] Every day on packed transit, women are groped, followed, and intimidated. The harm is not abstract and it is not waiting for a perfect institutional fix. The motion asks only whether high-harassment systems may be allowed to use women-only cars or sections even though they are not a cure. The answer is yes, because a voluntary, reversible safety tool that reduces real assaults during a dangerous gap is justified even if it does not end harassment forever.
[P1.2] Start with the key word: allowed. No one is forced into these cars, and no system is required to adopt them. A ban would take a locally chosen option away from cities and riders who have asked for it. Transit systems in Tokyo, Mexico City, Rio, Cairo and Mumbai experimented with women-only cars because crowded anonymity produced routine groping and because ordinary enforcement had been too slow.
[P1.3] Riders in those places often report feeling safer, and some women resume travel they had abandoned. That renewed access to work, school, and public life is a public good, not a favor to victims.
[P1.4] The opponent will say this only shifts the burden and treats a symptom. But refusing the option also shifts the burden—onto women waiting for reforms that are chronically underfunded. Women-only cars work alongside better staffing, cameras, and policing; they do not replace them. A tourniquet is not criticized for failing to cure the disease.
[P1.5] In a train car where a woman is groped or pressed against her will week after week, reducing that risk by even a meaningful fraction matters.
[P1.6] Nor is this akin to apartheid-style segregation. The car is opt-in, often time-limited to rush hour, and can be defined to include transgender women and others who face the same threat. The objection that it is only a partial solution to public-safety failures concedes the premise: the motion survives precisely because the relief is incomplete. The state's failure to fix everything is a reason to permit immediate mitigation, not to forbid it.
