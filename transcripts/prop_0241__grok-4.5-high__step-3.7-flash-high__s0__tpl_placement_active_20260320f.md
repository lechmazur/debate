[P1.1] Criminal punishment exists to protect people and sustain a livable society. On that measure, rehabilitation is the more important aim because it stops the next crime, while retribution only settles the last one.
[P1.2] Retribution is backward-looking. It asks whether the offender suffered enough for what they already did. Once the sentence is completed, that aim is satisfied—even if the person re-enters society more alienated, more skilled at crime, and more likely to create fresh victims. The moral ledger is balanced; the streets are not safer.
[P1.3] Rehabilitation treats the period of punishment as an opportunity to change the trajectory: addiction treatment, education, vocational skill, and cognitive work that reduce the motives and capacities for reoffending. When it works, the public gains the only durable form of protection—an offender who no longer chooses or needs the old path.
[P1.4] That difference is decisive. Pure payback produces revolving doors. People cycle through prisons, families stay broken, communities stay dangerous, and the state keeps paying for the same failures. Rehabilitation multiplies benefits: fewer assaults, fewer thefts, fewer traumatized families, lower long-term costs.
[P1.5] Even imperfect programs outperform empty desert-satisfaction once the original harm is already done. Proportionality still constrains every sentence—no one is arguing for leniency without desert. But a constraint is not the highest purpose. Between two aims of punishment, the more important one is the one that better fulfills why we have criminal justice at all: reducing future victimization while still doing justice.
[P1.6] Rehabilitation does that work. Retribution alone leaves the future as bloody as the past.
