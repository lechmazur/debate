[P1.1] Parasocial monetization is not ordinary fandom. It is a one-sided intimacy that platforms deliberately convert into revenue: viewers feel personally connected to a creator who cannot possibly reciprocate at scale, and the platform monetizes that asymmetry. The motion does not ban tips, subscriptions, or creators making a living. It asks for stronger user controls over the specific mechanics that turn affection into pressure.
[P1.2] Top-spender rankings are the clearest example. They transform support into a public status competition: who is the biggest fan, who is closest to the creator, who will spend to stay visible. That is not a neutral donation jar; it is a leaderboard engineered to make comparison and escalation feel social. Hiding those rankings costs creators little and gives users a way to opt out of the scoreboard without opting out of the community.
[P1.3] Highly personalized creator upsells are worse. When an offer is tailored to a user’s emotional attachment—using their viewing history, messages, or expressed affection—it stops being advertising and starts mimicking a relationship obligation. “This is for you” hits differently when the “you” is built from intimate signals. A user should be able to disable that channel without abandoning the platform.
[P1.4] The opposing side will say users can simply ignore the rankings or close the app. But that is the same advice as telling someone to look away from a casino floor designed around them. Platforms profit from engineered escalation; individual willpower is not a substitute for a setting. Stronger tools are the modest intervention: let users hide the scoreboard and switch off personalized upsells while leaving voluntary, transparent support intact.
[P1.5] The central question is who controls the boundary between fandom and financial obligation. When the platform controls it, monetization drifts toward exploitation. When users control it, creators can still earn—just without treating emotional attachment as a lever. Vote PRO.
