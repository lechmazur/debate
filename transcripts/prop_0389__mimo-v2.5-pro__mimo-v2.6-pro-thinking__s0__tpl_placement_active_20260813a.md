[C1.2] First, the analogy to DVDs and books is false. A book is a finished object you own. An online game is a hosted service — closer to a live performance or a theme park than a novel. Nobody argues a theater owes you the stage rights when a show closes.
[C1.3] When a publisher shuts servers down, no object is destroyed; a service simply ends. Framing that as "erasure" inflates a licensing arrangement into a property right players never had.
[C1.4] Second, the "minimal burden" claim collapses under real constraints. Server software routinely bundles licensed middleware, voice systems, anti-cheat code, and third-party SDKs whose licenses terminate with the product. Releasing it means renegotiating or stripping those components — expensive, legally risky work on a product already losing money. Worse, dumped server code and mod support can expose credential systems and residual user data to new attack surfaces.
[C1.5] Who carries liability when a community-hosted shard leaks accounts or ships malware-laced mods? The motion has no answer.
[C1.6] Third, handing out private server rights creates real ongoing harms: fragmented player bases, cheat-ridden rogue shards trading on a brand the publisher still owns, and ersatz "official" servers that tarnish reputations the company must still protect for its other titles. [C1.7] Fourth, real preservation doesn't require this. Gameplay footage, design documents, reviews, community wikis, and museum-archived builds already capture what historians need. Culture survives the loss of a live service every time a novel goes out of print or a play closes.
[C1.8] The publisher bore the cost, the risk, and the maintenance. Ending a service is a legitimate business decision. Compelling them to arm their own replacement is a burden dressed up as charity.
