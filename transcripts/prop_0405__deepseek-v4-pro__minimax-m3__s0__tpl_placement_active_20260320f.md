[P1.1] Shared e-bike and scooter companies should bear more legal responsibility when riders injure pedestrians on sidewalks and in clearly restricted zones. The reason is straightforward: these companies create a foreseeable risk, control the means to prevent it, and profit from the activity—yet they currently outsource the cost of injuries to the public.
[P1.2] Consider the technology already in their hands. GPS, geofencing, and software-controlled speed governors can instantly lock a scooter out of a pedestrian plaza or cap its speed to walking pace. When a rider nevertheless speeds down a congested sidewalk and hits someone, that crash is not just a rogue user; it is the predictable result of a business model that declines to use its own enforcement tools. Normal tort law expects parties with superior knowledge and cheap preventative capacity to shoulder liability—not to hide behind user agreements.
[P1.3] The incentive structure makes this worse. As long as companies can offload injury costs onto riders and victims, they will treat sidewalk bans as fine print rather than a safety imperative. Geofencing remains a patchy afterthought. Imposing direct legal responsibility flips the calculus: it becomes cheaper to implement rigorous, real-time geofencing and robust no-ride enforcement than to pay out claims.
[P1.4] That is the most reliable path to fewer crushed toes and broken hips.
[P1.5] Moreover, pedestrians never consented to this risk. They step onto a sidewalk to escape vehicle danger, only to be turned into profit-center casualties for a fleet they didn’t choose. The operator placed the device in the public right-of-way, programmed its limits, and collected the payment. When that device causes harm in a forbidden zone, fairness demands the operator carry a meaningful share of the legal burden.
[P1.6] This is not about punishing innovation—it is about ending a free pass that no other hazard-producing industry enjoys.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to load a special legal duty onto shared micromobility companies for sidewalk injuries. Taken seriously, that duty would punish operators for harms they did not cause, could not prevent, and never consented to bear. The rider, not the company, steers, accelerates, and chooses where to ride. Liability should follow that control, not corporate revenue.
[C1.2] First, the technology argument cuts both ways. GPS and geofencing exist, but they are blunt and porous. A scooter can be geofenced to "no riding," yet the rider simply picks it up and carries it—or hops off and rolls it. When a user deliberately defeats a slow-zone or carries a locked vehicle into a plaza, the company has already exercised its best available tool.
[C1.3] Holding it liable anyway would punish diligence and reward evasion, the opposite of safety.
[C1.4] Second, the real safety lever is the rider. Every sidewalk injury in a restricted zone is, by definition, a violation of traffic law. The person who broke that law is the person whose hand was on the throttle. Victim compensation belongs with the wrongdoer and the rider's insurance, not laundered through a company that had no contact with the pedestrian at the moment of harm.
[C1.5] Third, expanding operator liability will not save lives—it will shrink service. Higher insurance premiums and unpredictable exposure force companies to pull fleets from cities, push up fares, or exit entirely. The victims are the low-income workers who use these vehicles for transit, not wealthy pedestrians. We will trade a marginal enforcement gain for a real loss of mobility.
[C1.6] The proposition mistakes who caused the harm, who can prevent it, and who will pay the price. The right target is the rider.
