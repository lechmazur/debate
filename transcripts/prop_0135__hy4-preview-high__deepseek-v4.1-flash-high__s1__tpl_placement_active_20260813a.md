[P1.1] Start with the condition the motion writes for itself: a cap is required exactly when resident crowding, pollution, and housing pressure already outweigh tourist revenue gains. When that net harm is present, refusing to limit cruise ships and tour buses is not prudence; it is choosing depletion over stewardship.
[P1.2] Counting gross visitor spending instead of net community value rigs the comparison. Cruise and large bus arrivals are volume without value. Thousands disembark at once into fragile, car-free, pedestrian-scale streets; they buy low-margin snacks and souvenirs, then return to ship or coach, generating emissions, wastewater, congestion, and too little lodging tax. Profits leak to cruise lines, foreign operators, and platforms, while locals absorb the noise, dirty air, and infrastructure strain.
[P1.3] If the ledger is already negative, every extra busload makes residents poorer in the ways that matter: space, clean air, and a home.
[P1.4] Experience already answers the fear that caps kill tourism. Cities like Venice, Barcelona, and Dubrovnik have restricted or rerouted large liners, bus parking, and peak cruise calls. They did not let tourism vanish; they curbed the most damaging surges and protected the amenity that actually commands premium visits. A walled medieval street cannot absorb a dozen coaches an hour and remain the place people travel to see.
[P1.5] There, the sidewalk is the monument; buses turn it into a queue. A historic center hollowed out by short-term rentals is not compensated by a day-tripper's espresso.
[P1.6] Capping these arrivals is precise demand management, not a ban on visitors. It targets high-volume, low-dispersal modes that overwhelm an irreplaceable urban fabric and shifts the mix toward fewer, higher-value, longer-staying guests. When the burden already exceeds the benefit, the cap is the conservative act: preserve the city that earns the revenue, and stop letting outsiders extract the gains while residents pay the costs.

Cap ships and buses, and the demand returns as rental vans, rideshare surges, and coach parties from the next port. Same pavement pressure, fewer receipts.
[C1.5] Third — and this should decide the debate — the housing claim indicts the wrong defendant. Cruise passengers sleep on the ship. Bus-tour visitors sleep at home. They are the least housing-intensive visitors a city can host.

[C1.7] Finally, the ledger hides who loses. Port fees, berth charges, pilotage, coach parking, and the café, guide, and museum revenue around the terminal flow to locals and small operators. A cap doesn't transfer that money to residents; it evaporates it, along with the case for investing in the infrastructure that would have made the volume tolerable.
[C1.8] Charge for the peak. Time the slots. Earmark the revenue for transit and housing. That cuts crowding, cuts pollution, funds homes, and keeps the income.
[C1.9] A cap loses on all four counts.
