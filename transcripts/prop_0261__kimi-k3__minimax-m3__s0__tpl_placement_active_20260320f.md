[P1.1] A family clears out a dead father's house. The paperbacks go to his daughter, the records to his son. Then they open his tablet: a decade of ebooks, films, and music bought at full price — and discover that every item is a "non-transferable license" that expired when he did. The seller keeps the money; the family inherits nothing; the library is not resold, it is simply deleted.
[P1.2] That deletion is the status quo CON must defend, and it fails every test of a rational property regime.
[P1.3] First, transaction reality. The button says "Buy now," not "Rent until death." Consumers pay purchase prices for permanent possession, and inheritance law exists precisely to carry accumulated property to the next generation. Fine print that silently converts every purchase into a life-interest rental defeats the expectation the sale itself created.
[P1.4] The law should track the substance of the deal, not the euphemism.
[P1.5] Second, there is no countervailing harm. Non-transferability during life exists to stop copy-multiplication piracy. At death that rationale evaporates: the original owner is gone, death is verifiable, and a single transfer to an heir preserves the one-copy principle the license was meant to protect. The rights holder was already paid in full.
[P1.6] Confiscation at death protects nothing; it merely destroys paid-for value.
[P1.7] Third, administrability is proven, not speculative. Delaware and other jurisdictions already give estate fiduciaries access to digital assets. Germany's highest civil court treated a deceased user's account as inheritable, like letters and diaries. Major platforms now build legacy-contact tools.
[P1.8] The machinery exists; what is missing is the guarantee.
[P1.9] The clash is therefore narrow. CON must argue that death should operate as a corporate windfall — an automatic repossession of paid-for property, triggered by tragedy, justified by clauses nobody reads. Weighed against family security, honest dealing, and zero demonstrated harm to sellers, that position collapses. Purchased assets should behave like what they are: property.
[P1.10] And property outlives its owner.

[C1.2] First, "non-transferable purchased digital assets" isn't a coherent legal category. It sweeps in ebooks, films, games, software, cloud storage, even crypto wallets. Any workable law needs to carve—and every carve weakens the "guarantee" PRO is selling.
[C1.3] The motion offers a slogan, not a rule.
[C1.4] Second, sellers don't own most of what they'd be forced to transfer. Amazon, Apple, and Steam license content from authors, labels, and studios. Guaranteeing transfer without the rights-holders' consent breaks existing royalty and licensing arrangements, and puts platforms in the position of giving away property they never owned. The German decision PRO cites dealt with one platform's own terms; generalising it substitutes judicial imposition for the chain of contracts this market actually runs on.
[C1.5] PRO's harmlessness claim collapses here, because forcing transfer doesn't just confirm a sale already made—it imposes a new obligation on parties who aren't even in the room.
[C1.6] Behind both points sits a privacy problem PRO never engages. Inheriting an ebook is one thing; inheriting an inbox or messenger history is another. Those assets are full of other people's words and images. A blanket inheritance rule would strip post-mortem privacy from third parties who never agreed to become heirs' reading audience—and it would do so by default, regardless of what the deceased actually wanted.
[C1.7] The reasonable alternative—disclosure duties, legacy-contact tools, narrowly defined heir access—delivers family security without breaking licensing chains or exposing private correspondence. The motion asks for more, and the more is exactly what makes it unworkable.
