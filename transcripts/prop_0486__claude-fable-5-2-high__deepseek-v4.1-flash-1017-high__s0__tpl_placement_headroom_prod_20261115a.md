[P1.1] A permanent burial plot is the only public service a city hands out once and never gets back. That is the whole problem. Land-scarce cities have a fixed supply of ground and an unending supply of deaths, and a policy of giving each death a piece of ground forever has only one mathematical destination: the gates close.
[P1.2] When they close, the promise of "public burial" becomes a lottery won by whoever died first. Everyone afterward gets a waiting list, a plot an hour outside the city, or a price only the wealthy can meet. Permanent plots are not a tradition preserved for all; they are a subsidy to the past, paid by everyone who comes after.
[P1.3] The cities that have hit this wall already show where the status quo leads. Singapore limits burial terms to around fifteen years, then exhumes. Hong Kong's public graves are similarly temporary. In scarce cities, permanence is already a fiction — what remains is disruption for grieving families, performed later and less gently.
[P1.4] Composting and alkaline hydrolysis solve the problem honestly. Both return a body to soil or sterile remains in hours or weeks rather than occupying ground for centuries. Both avoid the fuel burning and emissions of flame cremation. And both leave families with something tangible: soil for a memorial grove, remains to scatter or inter in a shared garden.
[P1.5] A cemetery built this way becomes a living park that can receive every generation, not a full ledger.
[P1.6] Note what the motion asks: a transition in what public cemeteries allocate going forward. Existing graves stay. Private and religious grounds remain available.
[P1.7] So the test is simple: which policy can still say yes to a grieving family fifty years from now? Only ours can.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a land-management problem for a mandate to abolish a burial form. Cities can ration land without redefining what a public cemetery is. The real question is not whether composting or alkaline hydrolysis can work — they can — but whether a city should stop offering permanent interment altogether and push every family toward two technically mediated alternatives. That is a coercive answer to a problem that calls for plural provision.
[C1.2] Public cemeteries are the burial option for families who cannot buy private plots or navigate exclusive religious grounds. For many communities, intact burial is not decorative tradition; it is a religious or moral requirement. Remove the public permanent option, and the wealthy still find a grave while the poor and the devout are told to accept compost or dissolution. The proposition says private and religious grounds remain available, but that concedes the equity problem: the public system becomes the second-class system.
[C1.3] Nor is the land argument as binary as it sounds. A city can cap permanent plots, use shared family graves, build terraced cemeteries and columbaria, reclaim marginal land, and reserve a smaller permanent section alongside term burial. The choice is not “infinite graves” versus “no graves.” Permanence is not the same as unlimited expansion; a cemetery can allocate a fixed number of perpetual plots and stop when full, preserving the promise for those admitted rather than breaking it for everyone.
[C1.4] Even composting and hydrolysis need processing sites, transport, and memorial land for soil or remains.
[C1.5] The humane policy is to add these methods as voluntary options, not to make them the price of a public burial. A grieving family should not have to choose between its conscience and its city’s cemetery. Vote CON.
