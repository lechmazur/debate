[P1.1] Judge restrictions at the margin: a dwelling is either a home or tourist inventory. In high-demand city neighborhoods, a landlord renewing a lease competes with nightly bookings, premium dates, and investors who capitalize that revenue. Location is fixed and supply slow, so scattered conversions change the outside option for comparable units: asking rents rise, leases vanish, and small buildings are worth more as de facto hotels than as homes. That is the displacement mechanism CON must answer.
[P1.2] Caps, registration, primary-residence rules, and whole-unit limits attack it directly. They need not erase hospitality; they stop ordinary apartments becoming the highest bidder's short-stay use. Units then return to annual leases, stop being priced on tourist revenue, and lose the speculative premium that justified buying out tenants. The gain recurs each month a family avoids a rent hike, forced move, longer commute, school disruption, and broken care networks.
[P1.3] Stable tenancy is the base of the local economy CON claims to defend.
[P1.4] Host income and visitor spending losses are real, but narrower and more substitutable. Whole-home STR revenue is mainly an asset return to owners and small professional operators, not broad neighborhood wages. A long-term tenant also buys groceries, transit, childcare, and services year-round. Nor does high visitor demand vanish because one district caps entire-home listings: guests shift to hotels, licensed home-sharing, nearby areas, different dates, or longer stays.
[P1.5] Some spending moves; displacement removes year-round customers and workers.
[P1.6] The comparison is a durable fall in housing pressure against a partial loss of optional asset income and relocatable tourism. Sensible restrictions allow owner-occupied rooms and set minimum stays, preserving genuine sharing while targeting the scarce conversion that matters: entire homes removed from the rental stock. Where housing demand already exceeds available homes, that trade is decisive. Keeping residents in place reduces rents and displacement more than the remaining losses in host income and visitor spending hurt the neighborhood.
