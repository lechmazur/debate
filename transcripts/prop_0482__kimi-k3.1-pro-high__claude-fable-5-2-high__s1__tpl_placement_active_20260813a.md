[P1.1] A hospital does not save a child with a patent; it saves the child with a device that is available, appropriate, and working. In low-income health systems, that distinction is decisive. Equipment is often defeated not by a lack of medical knowledge, but by purchase prices, import delays, missing consumables, and a single proprietary component that no local technician may replace.
[P1.2] Open-source hardware attacks those causes of death together. Publishing design files, bills of materials, and testing procedures, with legal permission to manufacture and repair, lets public agencies, universities, nonprofits, and local firms share development costs instead of duplicating them. Cheaper production can mean more devices per budget; distributed supply can mean less dependence on distant vendors; open repair documentation can turn months of downtime into a local maintenance job.
[P1.3] The largest gains need not come from futuristic implants. They include stethoscopes, diagnostic jigs, laboratory equipment, oxygen-delivery components, and rugged monitoring tools—the everyday infrastructure through which enormous numbers of patients pass. Open designs can be adapted for unreliable electricity, heat, dust, and locally available parts, rather than merely donating machines optimized for wealthy hospitals and hoping the environment cooperates.
[P1.4] CON will invoke safety and innovation. But openness is not a waiver of regulation: designs can be independently inspected, version-controlled, validated, and manufactured only by qualified facilities. Hidden failures and repair restrictions are hardly a safety ideal. Patents can help finance frontier research, yet traditional pipelines systematically follow purchasers with money.
[P1.5] Where hospitals cannot pay proprietary margins, the promised innovation either never arrives, arrives late, or arrives without a sustainable service ecosystem.
[P1.6] Judge total patients reached over a device’s working life, not elegance at product launch. Traditional IP concentrates knowledge and then asks low-income markets to fund its recovery. Open-source designs turn one successful, tested solution into reusable public capacity. When affordability multiplies access, repairability multiplies uptime, and adaptation makes devices usable, the result is straightforward: more functioning devices at more bedsides, and therefore more lives saved.

The comparison is between two systems for financing, validating, manufacturing, and maintaining devices. Openness changes who can perform that work, where it happens, and whether a hospital remains dependent on one distant supplier.
[P2.2] “Off-patent” is not “open-source.” The legal freedom to imitate an old principle does not provide production drawings, calibrated test procedures, supplier specifications, repair documentation, or permission to modify service software and consumables. That is why [C1.2] does not answer the motion. A pulse oximeter’s underlying science may be old while the practical knowledge needed to produce and sustain a reliable unit remains dispersed, costly, or inaccessible.
[P2.3] Nor do CON’s downstream costs disappear under proprietary development. Quality manufacturing, validation, training, and surveillance must be funded under either model. The traditional pipeline recovers them through margins on limited sales; an open pipeline lets governments, donors, universities, and qualified manufacturers share validation and testing infrastructure rather than repeatedly purchasing the same restricted package. Qualified manufacture is a safeguard, not an argument for secrecy.
[P2.4] Accountability likewise comes from institutions and enforceable contracts, not closed files. A purchaser can license named manufacturers, require traceable batches, incident reporting, and recalls, while publishing the reference design and test methods. CON’s emergency ventilator example proves only that rushed, high-risk designs are difficult to certify—not that mature open designs for basic equipment cannot support accountable production.
[P2.5] Finally, tiered pricing and donor procurement remain vulnerable to withdrawn subsidies, discontinued product lines, and expired service contracts. Neither model conjures money from nothing. But open development lets the same expenditure leave behind reusable designs, multiple supply options, and repair capacity. A concentrator awaiting a proprietary part treats nobody; returning it safely to service saves patients without waiting for a new product launch.
[P2.6] CON identifies real barriers, but IP protection does not remove them—and often makes the final, life-threatening barrier, downtime, harder to overcome.
