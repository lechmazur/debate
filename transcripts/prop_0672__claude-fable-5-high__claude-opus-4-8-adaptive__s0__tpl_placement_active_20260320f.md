[P1.1] The proposition turns on a simple asymmetry: deployment is measured in years we cannot get back, while manufacturing capacity is a promise that arrives late, partially, or not at all.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Tariffs work by raising prices—that is their entire function. When EVs, solar panels, and chips cost more, fewer get bought and installed. Clean-tech adoption is acutely price-sensitive: solar projects pencil out or die on cents per watt; mass EV adoption hinges on hitting price parity with gasoline cars.
[P1.3] A 25–100% tariff on the cheapest available products directly delays the moment ordinary consumers and utilities switch. BYD sells competent EVs abroad for roughly the price of a used Corolla; walling those out keeps American EV adoption a luxury phenomenon for years longer.
[P1.4] Now the other side of the ledger. The track record of tariff-built manufacturing is thin. The U.S. has imposed solar tariffs in various forms for over a decade, and the result was not a thriving domestic panel industry—it was higher installation costs, repeated project delays, and continued import dependence, just rerouted through Southeast Asia. Factories take years to permit, build, and staff; tariffs deliver the cost increase immediately and the capacity, if ever, on a five-to-ten-year lag.
[P1.5] Meanwhile downstream industries—installers, fleet operators, automakers needing chips—employ far more people than the protected upstream segment, and tariffs squeeze all of them.
[P1.6] Finally, the stakes are not symmetric. Every gas car sold instead of an EV locks in fifteen years of emissions. Every delayed solar farm is a coal or gas plant running longer. Manufacturing capacity, by contrast, can be built later or supported through subsidies and procurement that don't tax deployment.
[P1.7] Carbon lock-in is irreversible; industrial policy timing is not.
[P1.8] So the test is this: tariffs impose a certain, immediate, compounding deployment cost in exchange for a speculative, delayed manufacturing gain. That trade fails on its own terms.
