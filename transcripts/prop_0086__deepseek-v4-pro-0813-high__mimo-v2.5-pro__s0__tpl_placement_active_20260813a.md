[P1.1] Human companionship is not a service slot that an interactive device can fill. It is a reciprocal relation: the older person is known, remembered, missed, teased, forgiven, and seen as a particular human being, not as a monitored body. A robot or chatbot can simulate questions and responses, but it cannot want the person’s good, feel concern when she is absent, or be trusted the way a human can. Replacing most human contact therefore does not cure loneliness; it hides it behind scheduled prompts and recorded voices.
[P1.2] The harm is sharpest for older adults with dementia, depression, hearing loss, or limited mobility. Some may not reliably understand that they are speaking to a machine. A simulated companion can exploit that confusion, create one-sided attachment, or soothe staff into thinking emotional needs are covered. If a provider says companionship was delivered because a device was present, real isolation becomes harder to detect and correct.
[P1.3] Tight staffing does not justify substitution; it exposes priorities. The answer to too few humans is more humans: better pay, safer ratios, trained volunteers, family involvement, and community visiting programs. Replacing most companionship to manage shortages does not solve the shortage; it makes the shortage permanent and lowers the definition of care.
[P1.4] Human presence also catches what algorithms miss: a subtle change in breathing, a bruise, new confusion, the difference between tiredness and despair. Machines can trigger alerts, but they cannot be accountable for failing to care. The real choice is not between robots and nothing. It is between authentic human presence and managed abandonment.
[P1.5] When staffing is tight, that is exactly when older adults most need scarce human companionship, not its replacement.
