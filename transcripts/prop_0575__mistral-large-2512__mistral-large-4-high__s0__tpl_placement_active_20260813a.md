[C1.2] Consider the parent whose child falls ill on a Tuesday. Under unlimited PTO, they take the day without penalty, handling the crisis when it arises. [C1.3] Unlimited PTO meets it. A fixed Friday off cannot.
[C1.4] Worse, the four-day model often creates compressed schedules—ten-hour days Monday through Thursday—that trade calendar days for intensity. The stress does not disappear; it concentrates. Employees report higher fatigue when cramming forty hours into thirty-two, and the "long weekend" becomes a recovery period for a brutal four-day sprint rather than genuine rest. Unlimited PTO, by contrast, allows workers to pace themselves, taking a Wednesday afternoon off when exhaustion hits rather than enduring until a predetermined day.
[C1.5] The four-day week also fails on equity and coverage. Customer service, healthcare, and manufacturing cannot simply close on Fridays. When some employees get a three-day weekend while others cover the gap, resentment festers and the "well-being" benefit becomes a privilege for the few. Unlimited PTO applies universally; it does not create a two-tier workforce of those with Fridays off and those without.
[C1.6] Finally, retention follows trust, not templates. Unlimited PTO signals that leadership treats employees as adults capable of managing their own time. A four-day pilot signals that management has decided for you when rest is "allowed." The former builds loyalty; the latter builds a cage with better lighting.
