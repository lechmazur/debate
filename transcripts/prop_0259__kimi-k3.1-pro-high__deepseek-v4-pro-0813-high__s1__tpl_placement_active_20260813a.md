[P1.1] The strongest case for permanent standard time is simple: stop disrupting sleep twice a year, then choose the fixed clock that best matches human biology. Keeping seasonal changes fails the first test; permanent summer time passes it but fails the second.
[P1.2] The spring transition abruptly removes an hour of sleep from a population already often sleep-deprived. Even the autumn transition does not simply “give back” the damage: changing schedules disturbs routines, complicates work and transport, and creates avoidable errors. These costs fall on everyone, including children, patients, and people whose jobs require early starts. The benefits of changing clocks, by contrast, are neither necessary nor evenly shared.
[P1.3] Once the clock is fixed, morning light matters more than the label suggests. Human circadian rhythms respond strongly to light, and darkness late into the morning makes waking harder and can push social schedules further out of line with the body’s clock. Permanent summer time would impose especially late winter sunrises on many regions. That means more children travelling to school and more workers commuting in darkness, while early-rising workers receive no compensating benefit from an extra hour of evening daylight they cannot use.
[P1.4] Standard time is not perfect at every latitude or season, but it is the safer year-round compromise: it protects winter mornings without forcing a biannual national sleep experiment.
[P1.5] The main objection is that summer time provides lighter evenings for recreation, shopping, and sport. But an activity can begin later without falsifying everyone’s clock. Schools, employers, sports leagues, and shops can adjust seasonal hours where that genuinely helps. The reverse is impossible: an individual cannot create winter morning sunlight.
[P1.6] Policy should preserve the scarce, biologically important light rather than rearrange society around a discretionary preference.
[P1.7] Nor should outdated assumptions about lighting savings settle the issue. Modern energy demand includes heating, cooling, electronics, and varied work patterns; longer evenings do not guarantee system-wide savings. A policy with uncertain benefits cannot justify certain recurring disruption.
[P1.8] The choice is therefore between universal stability plus healthier morning alignment, and convenience purchased through sleep disruption [clipped]

A shop may stay open later; school sports, transit frequency, and family routines will not all follow automatically.

[C2.7] It is a defense of the one system that preserves winter mornings and summer evenings. Since the status quo already provides standard-time winter mornings, the proposition’s added protection is weak and its added cost—removing the most usable light of the year—is not.
