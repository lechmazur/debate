[P1.1] The question is not whether terrorism and mass murder are evil. They are. The question is whether our answer to evil should be the very thing we claim to abhor: the deliberate killing of a captive human being.
[P1.2] Begin with the nature of the threat. A suicide bomber has already chosen death. A mass shooter often expects to die. For these offenders, execution is not a deterrent; it is the fulfillment of their design.
[P1.3] It manufactures a martyr, hands their movement a recruiting symbol, and grants the spectacular end they sought. Life without parole denies that victory. It reduces them to a case number, a silence. If the goal is to protect society and demonstrate moral resolve, we do that more effectively by refusing to sink to their level.
[P1.4] Second, any system built by humans will err. Terrorism cases are prosecuted under maximum pressure, with classified evidence and heightened emotion. When the punishment is death, a mistake is not a miscarriage of justice. It is murder committed in our name.
[P1.5] There is no appeal from the grave. Life imprisonment preserves the possibility of correcting error, a possibility we owe to every accused person.
[P1.6] Third, the death penalty is not necessary for public safety. A person serving life without parole cannot kill again. Whatever legitimate purpose the state has—incapacitation, retribution, denunciation—is fully served by permanent imprisonment. The only thing execution adds is the power of the state to kill.
[P1.7] That power is not justice; it is a temptation to brutality.
[P1.8] A society that kills terrorists does not prove it is better than they are. It proves it is willing to become them. Refusing that bargain, even now, even here, is not softness. It is the only stance that leaves our moral authority intact.
[P1.9] That is why abolition matters most precisely when the crime is worst.

[C1.3] The PRO calls execution a temptation to brutality. But what is the brutality in demanding that the gravest crime meet the gravest consequence? Retributive justice is not vengeance. It is the structured recognition that some acts rupture the social contract so completely that no lesser penalty can express what society owes to the victims.
[C1.4] To abolish the death penalty for these cases is to tell the families of the murdered that the state cannot match the scale of their loss.

[C1.7] And on the PRO's concern about wrongful convictions: every legal system accepts some risk. The question is whether we abolish a tool because it can be misused or refine its use. Reasonable doubt standards, appellate review, and moratoriums on execution address error without surrendering the principle that extreme evil deserves extreme justice.
[C1.8] Abolishing the death penalty for the worst crimes does not elevate us. It lowers the ceiling of what we demand of justice itself.
