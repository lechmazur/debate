[P1.1] Affordability is not a mystery. In every expensive advanced-economy city, rents rose fastest exactly where it became hardest to build: coastal California, London, Sydney, Toronto, Boston. Meanwhile Tokyo, a metro of 37 million with permissive national zoning, has kept rents roughly flat for decades. That is not a coincidence, and it is not explained by tenant law.
[P1.2] Tokyo's renters are not unusually protected; Tokyo's builders are unusually unblocked.
[P1.3] Judge affordability the honest way: what does a typical household pay for a home it can actually get? Not what a lucky incumbent pays for the unit they already hold. That distinction decides this debate. Rent control lowers the posted rent on a subset of occupied units while doing nothing about the number of homes.
[P1.4] The queue behind the door gets longer, not shorter. The nurse moving to the city, the young couple, the immigrant family — they face a smaller pool of available units, higher rents in the uncontrolled segment, and key money, discrimination, and long waits. Research on San Francisco's rent-control expansion found landlords responded by converting and redeveloping to exit the rental market, shrinking rental supply and pushing citywide rents up. Protection for insiders financed by outsiders is not affordability; it is a transfer.
[P1.5] Streamlining permitting has the opposite geometry. Auckland's upzoning was followed by a construction surge and rent growth below the national trend. Minneapolis, which allowed more units and cut parking mandates, saw rents rise markedly slower than comparable metros. Supply also cascades: new market-rate buildings pull higher-income tenants out of older housing, freeing those units down the chain.
[P1.6] That is why the same neighborhood filters cheaper when building is legal and gentrifies violently when it is not.
[P1.7] And note the asymmetry in error costs. Overbuild, and you get somewhat cheaper housing. Overregulate rents, and you get a frozen stock, deferred maintenance, and a generation locked out permanently. Vienna, so often invoked, works because it builds relentlessly — the lesson is the cranes, not the caps.
