[P1.1] Cash transfers make a wonderful soundbite: give people money and let them choose. But the proposition forces a comparison at equal cost, and at equal cost, services win. Here's why.
[P1.2] First, in-kind provision buys more of the thing it targets. A dollar of cash buys a dollar of care. A dollar spent on public childcare, transit, or broadband buys wholesale purchasing power—bulk procurement, capital investment, public infrastructure that no household can access alone. When a city builds a bus line or a municipal fiber network, the per-unit cost of mobility and connectivity falls for everyone who uses it.
[P1.3] Cash can never capture those scale economies; each family faces retail prices alone. Universal services are not charity distributed one wallet at a time—they are collective bargaining against entire markets.
[P1.4] Second, services fix market failures that cash cannot touch. Childcare is not expensive because parents are poor; it's expensive because providing skilled care to small groups of children is inherently costly, and private markets respond with thin supply, quality races to the bottom, or geographic deserts where no one opens a center at any price. Hand a family in such a desert a voucher and they have money for a service that doesn't exist. Build the service and the problem is solved at its root.
[P1.5] The same logic governs transit—no individual purchase creates a bus network—and healthcare, where the sick need care costing vastly more than any flat transfer could cover.
[P1.6] Third, universality builds the political durability that poverty programs never achieve. Programs for "the poor" become political punching bags, means-tests metastasize, and transfers get eroded by inflation and hostile legislatures. Universal systems—public schools, pensions, libraries—survive because everyone has a stake and everyone defends them.
[P1.7] Finally, services produce spillovers cash cannot: a vaccinated population, a workforce freed by reliable transit, children in quality early education whose gains last decades. Cash maximizes individual choice in the moment. Services maximize capabilities over a lifetime. That is what prosperity is actually made of.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to compare two ways of spending the same dollar. PRO's whole case rests on an assumption: that government-run universal services deliver more value per dollar than the people who actually live the need. That assumption is false, and here is the case that will win this debate.
[C1.2] First, needs are not uniform, but universal services are. A single mother in a dense city needs childcare; a single mother in a rural county fifty miles from the nearest center needs a car, or help paying a neighbor. A commuter needs a bus line where they live, not where a planning committee decided to build one. Cash lets each household solve its own equation.
[C1.3] Universal service provision forces one design onto everyone and inevitably over-serves some communities while leaving others with a subsidized service that simply isn't there for them—PRO's own "childcare desert" example. A voucher for a service that doesn't exist is a real problem. But so is a shiny public daycare built in the wrong zip code, funded at the expense of the family in the desert who gets nothing at all.
[C1.4] Second, "buying wholesale" assumes government buys well. In practice, public provision runs into Baumol's cost disease, contractor capture, and slow-moving bureaucracy—look at how much public transit and public healthcare systems spend per unit of service compared to private alternatives, and how routinely infrastructure projects blow past budget and schedule. A dollar funneled through agencies and vendors before it reaches a person is not obviously worth more than a dollar that reaches them directly.
[C1.5] Third, cash respects agency and adapts to local prices automatically—where services are cheap, families buy more of them; where they're expensive or unavailable, families improvise. That flexibility is not a weakness against market failure; it's insurance against government failure, which is at least as common and often worse, since a bad bus route lasts decades but a bad purchase lasts a week.
[C1.6] Equal cost, unequal fit: that is why cash wins.

[P2.6] Third, on heterogeneous needs: universal services are platforms, not straitjackets. [P2.7] One is uniform money; the other is genuinely flexible capacity.
