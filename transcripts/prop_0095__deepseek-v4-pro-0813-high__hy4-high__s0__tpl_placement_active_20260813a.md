[P1.1] A performance is not raw footage. It is a person’s face, timing, breath, and choices. When a studio uses AI de-aging or synthetic voice to materially alter that performance, it is generating new expressive content from the actor’s identity. That should never happen by default; it should require explicit consent and compensation.
[P1.2] The key line is material alteration. Color grading or removing a stray hair does not change what the actor did. But de-aging that smooths micro-expressions, or synthetic voice that changes intonation, emphasis, or words, replaces the actor’s actual performance with a machine-generated version. The audience may believe they are watching the actor, but the actor did not make those choices.
[P1.3] That is a new use of labor and likeness, not a routine edit.
[P1.4] Contracts signed before these tools existed cannot be stretched to cover them. A general clause about using an actor’s image in a film is not consent to have a synthetic version of their voice deliver lines they never spoke, or to have their face altered into a different age for scenes they never performed. Consent must be specific because the harm is specific: the actor’s own body and voice become the raw material for a performance they did not give.
[P1.5] Compensation follows from the same principle. If the studio extracts additional value from an actor’s identity—new dialogue, a younger face, a longer presence in the film—that is additional use of the actor’s craft and market value. Paying only the original day rate lets the studio profit from a performance the actor never delivered. That is unjust enrichment.
[P1.6] This does not ban the technology; it makes permission the default. Studios remain free to negotiate, pay, and use these tools when actors agree. The alternative is a system where a living actor sees their own face and voice rewritten on screen without knowledge or consent, while someone else controls the result. That is expropriation, not innovation.
