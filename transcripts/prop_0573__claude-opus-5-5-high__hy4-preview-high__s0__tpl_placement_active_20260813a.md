[P1.1] Every streaming platform already knows exactly how many times your song played, how long viewers watched your show, and how the pot of money was divided. The only party kept in the dark is the person whose work generated the value. This motion fixes that one asymmetry, and it rests on three arguments.
[P1.2] First, you cannot enforce a contract you cannot see. A musician paid from a pooled royalty system, or a screenwriter owed performance-based residuals, is told what they earned but not how it was calculated. Without the underlying numbers (streams, minutes viewed, the revenue pool, the share formula) an audit is guesswork. When the platform is both the scorekeeper and the payer, trust is not a sufficient safeguard.
[P1.3] Requiring disclosure turns a promise into something verifiable.
[P1.4] Second, standardization is what makes data useful. Scattered, voluntary reports in incompatible formats let platforms decide which metric flatters them. Netflix spent years treating viewership as a secret and now publishes selective engagement figures on its own terms. That is disclosure as public relations, not accountability.
[P1.5] A common format lets an artist compare what one platform pays per engaged listener against another, and lets a small label check its statement as easily as a major can. The 2023 Hollywood strikes put this at the centre: writers and actors fought partly because they could not tell whether a hit show was a hit. Nobody should need to go on strike to learn whether their work succeeded.
[P1.6] Third, bargaining power follows information. Markets work when both sides can price the deal. Right now platforms negotiate with full knowledge of each work's performance while creators negotiate blind. Shared data makes that market more honest.
[P1.7] It does not set rates.
[P1.8] The objections are weak. Cost is modest because platforms already compute these figures in order to pay anyone at all. Trade secrets stay protected because the data goes to the relevant rights holders about their own works, not to competitors. What platforms lose is only the ability to pay people in the dark.

[C1.1] It is not enough to repeat that people cannot enforce what they cannot see, or that "when the platform is both the scorekeeper and the payer, trust is not a sufficient safeguard" [P1.2]. That establishes a problem, not this remedy. The motion would not upgrade transparency; it would substitute mandated, standardized disclosure for real heterogeneous accountability, while loading costs, leakage risks, and competitive harm onto the market it claims to help.
[C1.2] Standardization is the axle, and it breaks on contact with reality. To be comparable across every service, regulators must collapse wildly different activities into one unit: streams, minutes watched, engaged views, completions. That unit then becomes the goal. Platforms tune thumbnails, autoplay, and thirty-second hooks to satisfy the metric; creators optimize length and openings rather than quality.
[C1.3] Worse, no single yardstick fits every model. A pooled, pro-rata music service, an ad-supported short-video app, a subscription streamer with binge releases, and a fan-centric alternative allocate value in fundamentally different ways. A standardized mandate either produces number-shaped noise or silently blesses one business model as the default.
[C1.4] The leakage point is not cured by saying the "data goes to the relevant rights holders about their own works" [P1.8]. Modern works carry layers of rights: labels, publishers, session musicians, sample owners, unions, guilds. Because standardized payout data must be reconciled to revenue pools and allocation formulas, it spills into licensing terms and platform economics. Those figures inevitably shape renewal bargaining and litigation.
[C1.5] The beneficiaries are not obscure bedroom artists but the sophisticated rights holders best able to exploit them; meanwhile platforms facing richer liability prefer airtight, corporate-owned catalogs over risky new uploads.
[C1.6] The real answer to hidden accounting is tailored: audit rights, escrowed verification, minimum statement requirements, competition oversight. Those tools fit contracts and models. Mandating one standardized dataset instead delivers rigid metrics, protects incumbents, and upgrades the leverage of already-dominant rights holders. A fair market needs verifiable accounting, not a one-size-fits-all scoreboard.
