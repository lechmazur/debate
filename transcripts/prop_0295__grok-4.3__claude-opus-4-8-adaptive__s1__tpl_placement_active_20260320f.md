[P1.1] Returning artifacts without accompanying investment in conservation, research, and institutional capacity simply transfers risk from former colonial museums to under-resourced nations. Many source countries inherited fragmented heritage infrastructures shaped by extraction and underdevelopment; shipping objects back into environments lacking climate control, secure storage, or trained staff produces predictable loss through deterioration, theft, or illicit resale. The result is restitution in name only.
[P1.2] Provenance research funded by the returning power directly addresses the legitimacy gap that still surrounds many claims. Without systematic local investigation, objects can re-enter circulation under disputed ownership or be claimed by multiple parties, recreating the very conflicts that restitution was meant to resolve. External support for that work also transfers skills rather than perpetuating dependence on foreign expertise.
[P1.3] Museum capacity funding converts one-time transfers into durable public assets. Countries receiving large returns frequently lack the cataloguing systems, exhibition infrastructure, and revenue models required to maintain collections over decades. Targeted investment allows those institutions to generate tourism income and educational programs that justify continued public protection. Absent such support, returned items risk becoming costly burdens that governments later deprioritize.
[P1.4] Treating shipment as the finish line therefore evades the core obligation created by centuries of removal. The powers that extracted the objects retain the resources to ensure those objects survive and serve their original communities; declining to spend them converts a moral accounting exercise into a logistical one that predictably fails the receiving side.

[C1.1] Strip away the generous language and look at what this motion actually builds: a permanent arrangement where the former colonizer keeps a hand on the object after it goes home. Whoever funds the conservation lab, trains the staff, and bankrolls the provenance research sets the standards, signs the checks, and quietly retains influence over heritage that is supposed to be wholly returned. That is not the end of colonial control. It is its rebranding.
[C1.2] My central claim is simple. Restitution is complete when ownership and authority pass fully to the source nation, and the motion is wrong to demand more. Three reasons.
[C1.3] First, dependency is the opposite of dignity. To insist that the returning power must fund capacity is to declare that the receiving country cannot be trusted to steward its own past. That presumption of incapacity is exactly the colonial logic restitution exists to overturn. A nation that owns the object should decide whether to spend on climate control, a new museum, or its hospitals.
[C1.4] Second, the motion manufactures a delay trap. Once "return plus capacity-building" becomes the moral standard, any museum reluctant to give things back has a respectable excuse: we cannot release these objects until the receiving institution meets our funding-defined readiness. Conditioning return on infrastructure hands foot-draggers a permanent veto. The cleanest engine of actual repatriation is the bright line of completed transfer, not an open-ended donor relationship.
[C1.5] Third, if real obligation exists, earmarked museum money is the worst vehicle for it. Strings-attached funding routed through conservation programs lets the former power choose what gets preserved and how the story is told. Genuine reparation, if owed, is unconditional resources the receiving nation controls absolutely, not a managed grant that keeps foreign experts and foreign priorities embedded inside the gallery.
[C1.6] The dignified outcome is letting go completely. The motion refuses to let go, and calls the lingering grip generosity.

[P2.3] The supposed delay trap is inverted. Museums reluctant to return already cite inadequate receiving conditions as justification. That outcome serves the communities meant to benefit, rather than leaving returned objects as fiscal liabilities that later governments can quietly neglect.
