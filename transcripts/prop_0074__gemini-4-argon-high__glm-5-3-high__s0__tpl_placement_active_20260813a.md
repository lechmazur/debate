The seed money raised by selling enslaved families, relying on unfree campus labor, or liquidating expropriated land did not vanish into history.

[P1.3] Treating historical acknowledgments as sufficient reduces an economic crime to a public-relations exercise. A commissioned report or campus memorial costs an institution virtually nothing while allowing it to claim the moral authority of self-reflection. When a university admits its foundational wealth came from dispossession or bondage, yet insists its ledger is closed by a ceremony, it engages in moral laundering: keeping the compounded financial returns while paying its debt in words. Justice does not allow an institution to diagnose a material injury and prescribe a purely symbolic cure.

Slavery, segregation, and dispossession operated by stripping targeted communities of land, wealth, and educational access, concentrating those exact advantages behind university gates. By converting hoarded historical capital into educational mobility and local economic stability, universities repair the specific generational disparities their rise helped create.
[P1.5] The choice in this debate is straightforward: when an immortal institution still benefits from historic extraction, responsibility requires investing a share of that compounded wealth back into the communities that paid the price, not pretending a plaque settles the account.
