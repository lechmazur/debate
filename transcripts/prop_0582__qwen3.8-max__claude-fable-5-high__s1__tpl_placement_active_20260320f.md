[P1.1] The central question is not whether GLP-1 drugs have a high list price. It is whether, over the next five years, they prevent enough expensive acute care to outweigh that price. In Medicare, the answer is yes, because the program covers the people most likely to have near-term, costly complications.
[P1.2] Obesity in older adults is not a cosmetic issue; it accelerates heart attacks, strokes, heart failure, type 2 diabetes, kidney disease, and mobility-limiting conditions. Those events dominate U.S. health spending. A hospitalized heart-failure exacerbation, cardiac event, or stroke can cost tens of thousands of dollars and trigger post-acute care, readmissions, and long-term disability. GLP-1 therapies do more than reduce weight; in high-risk patients they improve blood pressure, glycemic control, inflammation, and cardiac strain, and randomized trials have shown meaningful reductions in major cardiovascular events among overweight or obese patients with established disease.
[P1.3] Those benefits begin within a few years, not decades.
[P1.4] Broad Medicare coverage matters because the current barrier is not just price. Fragmented coverage pushes effective treatment toward cash-pay patients and away from the older, sicker patients with the greatest absolute risk. If Medicare covers these drugs broadly with clinically sensible criteria, more high-risk beneficiaries can receive them before a preventable hospitalization occurs. That reduces Medicare medical spending and also lowers total U.S. spending by cutting emergency care, uncompensated hospital costs, and downstream disability that ripple through families, Medicaid, and private insurers.
[P1.5] The fiscal test is not perfection. It is whether avoided care exceeds added drug spending. Given the concentration of obesity-related risk in Medicare, the high cost of acute events, and the relatively rapid clinical effects of GLP-1 drugs, even a conservative reduction in heart attacks, strokes, heart-failure admissions, and diabetes complications can pay for a large share of the drugs. Over five years, broad coverage turns a high-cost treatment into a lower-cost substitute for hospital care.
