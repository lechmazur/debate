[P1.1] An actor's voice is not interchangeable sound. It's identity, craft, and livelihood. When AI clones it to perform in another language, it creates a new performance the actor never gave — different inflections, emotions, emphasis that audiences will attribute to them. That should never be taken by fine print.
[P1.2] Broad contract boilerplate cannot do this work. Those clauses are signed at hiring, often before anyone knows which markets, languages, or synthetic tools will be used, buried among pages of standard terms presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis. A day player or supporting actor cannot meaningfully bargain over future voice cloning when they need the job today. Consent obtained that way is fiction.
[P1.3] Opt-in requires the opposite: a clear, separate yes for a specific AI use, with pay and limits stated.
[P1.4] That specificity matters because risks are specific. A dramatic performance can become comic when timing shifts in translation. A line reading can sound angry, leering, or political when the synthetic voice misses context. Once released globally on streaming, that false version follows the performer forever, shaping casting and reputation, yet they were never in the booth.
[P1.5] No reasonable person would call that covered by 'producer may exploit performance by any means now known or hereafter devised.'
[P1.6] The industry can handle a higher standard. Film already gets separate clearances for nudity, stunts, and likeness extensions because some uses are too personal for boilerplate. A short opt-in rider for AI dubbing — which language, which project, whether the voice model is retained — adds little friction compared to the cost of localization itself, and actually builds trust that makes actors more willing to approve international release.
[P1.7] Efficiency does not justify expropriation. If studios value global audiences, they can ask the people whose identities will speak to them.

[C1.1] Start with the counterfactual PRO never states. The alternative to AI dubbing is not the actor's own voice. It is a stranger's voice. Localization has for a century replaced performers entirely with dub artists who invent their own timing, their own emotional shading, their own leer or laugh — attributed to the face on screen, in front of hundreds of millions of viewers.
[C1.2] Nobody has ever demanded that the original actor opt in to that. So [P1.4]'s harm story proves far too much: if a shifted line reading in translation is expropriation, conventional dubbing has been mass expropriation since the 1930s. What AI actually changes is that the voice sounds more like the performer's own, not less. PRO is demanding a special veto for the more faithful option.
[C1.3] Second, opt-in doesn't do what PRO says it does. [P1.2] concedes the day player has no bargaining power — then proposes a remedy that depends entirely on bargaining power. A separate rider signed on the same day, by the same person who needs the job, is the same fiction with an extra signature line. What opt-in reliably delivers is leverage for the people who already have it: the lead with a lawyer.
[C1.4] The background performer gets a checkbox and a shrug.
[C1.5] Third, the veto is a hostage problem. A single film has dozens of speaking roles. Under a genuine opt-in rule, any one holdout — or one unreachable estate, or one agent who wants a renegotiation — blocks localization of the whole title. The predictable outcome isn't respectful dubbing; it's no dubbing into Tagalog, Swahili, Polish.
[C1.6] Marginal-language markets and the audiences who rely on dubbing rather than subtitles lose first.
[C1.7] The right instrument is collective: union-negotiated floors, residuals tied to synthetic use, mandatory disclosure, and hard limits on retaining voice models, applied to everyone including the performer with no leverage. Plus remedies for genuinely defamatory or misleading uses.
[C1.8] Individual consent theater is not protection. Terms are.

[P2.5] And nothing in opt-in forbids union floors; consent is the floor below which residuals cannot fall, not a substitute for them.
[P2.6] Collective terms and after-the-fact suits for defamation don't answer that. Pay for impersonation is still impersonation without permission.
[P2.7] Finally the hostage story. No single holdout blocks Tagalog or Swahili or Polish, because refusal of cloning does not equal refusal of dubbing.
