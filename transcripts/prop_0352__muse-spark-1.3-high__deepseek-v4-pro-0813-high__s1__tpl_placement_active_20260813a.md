[P1.1] Concentration in a handful of superstar cities is not efficiency. It is overload.
[P1.2] When jobs, young people, and capital pile into London, Paris, New York or Tokyo while smaller towns empty, the result is predictable: soaring housing costs, clogged transport, overheated labor markets in the center, and simultaneously wasted skills, empty buildings, and collapsing tax bases elsewhere. That is a misallocation of national resources, and governments that shrug at it are endorsing decline they could prevent.
[P1.3] Second, viable small towns are national insurance. Rural regions grow food, manage water and forests, host energy projects, military installations, and supply chains that cities cannot live without. A country that lets its interior hollow out becomes fragile — dependent on a few crowded corridors for growth, vulnerable to shocks, price spikes, and infrastructure failure. Keeping regional economies alive is not nostalgia; it is resilience.
[P1.4] Wind, solar, advanced manufacturing and remote services now make dispersal more practical than a generation ago.
[P1.5] Third, abandonment compounds. Once a hospital closes, a school shrinks, and the last major employer leaves, families follow, services deteriorate further, and recovery becomes far more expensive. Leaving whole regions behind also fractures politics, turning understandable anger over neglect into lasting division. Modest, early public action — transport links, broadband, regional universities and hospitals, incentives for manufacturing and remote work — can break that spiral at far lower cost than coping with megacity expansion and regional rescue later.
[P1.6] Governments already shape economic geography every day through roads, rail, broadband funding, defense spending, university placement, and tax rules. The choice is never intervention versus nature. It is whether policy blindly subsidizes concentration or deliberately spreads opportunity.
[P1.7] Smaller places do not need to become capitals. They need a fair chance to keep jobs, youth, and dignity. A state that invests to make that possible builds a larger, more stable, more productive country.

[C1.1] The central mistake in this motion is to treat concentration as a problem to be reversed. Dense metropolitan areas are not accidental overcrowding; they are engines of productivity. Firms cluster because they share labor pools, suppliers, and ideas. Workers move because clustering raises wages and opportunity.
[C1.2] When a government tries to fight this, it does not spread prosperity; it spends scarce public money on locations where each dollar returns less. That is a worse allocation, not a correction.
[C1.3] The costs the other side names—housing costs, congestion—are real, but they are symptoms of governments failing to let successful cities build enough homes and transport. The cure is not to push jobs back into shrinking towns. It is to make growing cities more affordable and connected. If a young person stays in a region only because a subsidy keeps a firm alive, they often trade future income and mobility for a fragile job.
[C1.4] Real compassion is helping people move, retrain, and live near opportunity—not trapping them in place.
[C1.5] Rural functions do not require full economic viability. Farming, forestry, water and energy are increasingly capital-intensive; they need fewer workers, not more residents in every village. A country can maintain food and resource security with productive rural hubs and a smaller rural population. But keeping dispersed hospitals, schools, rail lines and broadband promises is expensive per person, and that money competes with higher-return urban investment.
[C1.6] The “break the downward spiral” argument is usually wrong about cost. Place-based subsidies often delay decline, leaving empty industrial parks and abandoned projects when incentives end. Once an area’s underlying comparative advantage disappears, public money can purchase delay but rarely reversal. The correct standard is raising living standards for people, not preserving a fixed map of towns.
[C1.7] Governments should accept and accommodate concentration—while removing the housing and transport barriers that make it painful—rather than subsidize permanence in places where the economic logic has changed.
