[P1.1] A boardroom is where the decisive questions are settled: whether to close a plant, outsource a function, load up with debt, or invest for a decade. Yet in the large public company, the people who live with those consequences—the workforce—have no guaranteed seat at that table. The proposition corrects a structural blind spot. Large listed firms should be legally required to reserve a significant share of board seats for representatives elected by their workers.
[P1.2] This is not worker control; it is shared governance. Worker-elected directors would sit alongside shareholder representatives, with the same fiduciary duty to the company. Their value is information and accountability. Workers see safety failures before regulators do, know when a restructuring plan is fantasy, and bear firm-specific costs that shareholders can diversify away.
[P1.3] A board that hears only executives and investors systematically underestimates operational risk and social cost. Mandatory seats force that knowledge into decisions before they become strikes, scandals, or write-downs.
[P1.4] Voluntarism cannot deliver this. Incumbent boards rarely hand away power; firms that experiment face pressure to revert; and workers cannot coordinate a binding demand across the market. A legal requirement solves the collective-action problem and prevents a race to the bottom. Limiting it to large publicly traded companies keeps the rule targeted: these are the firms with the scale, dispersed ownership, and public accountability to justify shared governance.
[P1.5] A significant percentage matters because token representation is worse than none—it creates the appearance of voice without the capacity to challenge management.
[P1.6] The opponent will warn of paralysis and divided loyalties. But shared governance is not a veto for any constituency; it changes who is in the room when priorities are set. Germany’s co-determination experience shows worker representation can coexist with a competitive industrial economy. The real comparison is between boards accountable only to capital and boards required to hear the people who make the company run.
[P1.7] On that comparison, the mandate wins.

And because they are a minority, they can force deliberation without blocking.
[P2.5] A council or survey [C1.5] is a microphone management can switch off; the board is where the decision is actually made.
[P2.6] [C1.4]’s “workers are not one interest” is an argument for election rules, not against representation. Large firms already aggregate dispersed shareholders through institutions; we can aggregate workers through constituencies. And the comparison is asymmetric: a saver’s portfolio is diversified across firms and asset classes; a worker’s job, skills, and community are not. The mandate puts that undiversifiable risk into the room.
[P2.7] Finally, the exit threat is not a refutation. If a rule is good governance, “delist to avoid workers” is like “leave the jurisdiction to avoid audits”—a race to the bottom, exactly what law exists to stop.
