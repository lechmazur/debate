[P1.1] Language is not a neutral conduit; it is an architecture for thought. When English dominates global science, commerce, and the internet, it does not merely connect humanity—it imposes an epistemic monoculture that systematically marginalizes non-anglophone cultures and knowledge systems.
[P1.2] First, in science, linguistic hegemony produces profound epistemic loss. Crucial localized knowledge—such as traditional ecological management, botanical medicine, and regional epidemiological insights—is sidelined because it cannot be easily codified within the Anglo-American publishing apparatus. Simultaneously, non-anglophone researchers pay an exhausting "linguistic tax," spending finite time and cognitive bandwidth navigating foreign idiom rather than conducting primary research, while international incentives steer them away from urgent local crises toward subjects favored by Western editorial boards.
[P1.3] Second, in business and society, English dominance drives cultural attrition and domestic inequality. When economic survival and social prestige are tied exclusively to English fluency, societies face a coercive trade-off: abandon mother tongues or remain economically disenfranchised. This dynamic creates an insular, bilingual elite detached from its own community, hollows out native investment in local-language education, and accelerates the obsolescence of vernacular languages that encode distinct moral, philosophical, and social frameworks.
[P1.4] Finally, the digital realm hardens this asymmetry. Global internet infrastructure—from search engine indexing to large language models—is disproportionately built upon English data. As a result, the default modes of human knowledge and machine reasoning reflect anglophone cultural norms, flattening global intellectual diversity into a standardized, Western-centric mirror.
[P1.5] The coordination benefits of a lingua franca cannot justify this epistemic erasure. When global progress demands the suppression of indigenous epistemologies and the cultural alienation of billions, the system does not empower the world; it colonizes how humanity thinks. English dominance has ceased to be an open bridge—it has become a bottleneck on human knowledge.

[C1.1] A common language is not a replacement culture; it is an additional layer that lets other cultures travel, trade, and be heard beyond their borders. English dominance harms non-anglophone knowledge only if acquisition meant abandonment, but in most of the world it functions as bilingual addition: people learn English for school, science, or work while continuing to live, worship, joke, and raise children in their own tongue.
[C1.2] That additivity turns into amplification. A Brazilian ecologist, a Japanese materials scientist, or an Indian clinician can publish locally grounded findings in English and have them read, tested, and built upon on every continent within days. Without a shared scientific language, that same knowledge would remain trapped in smaller linguistic silos, dependent on slow, selective translation, or subordinate to another dominant language anyway. Fragmentation does not protect diversity; it makes peripheral knowledge easier to ignore.
[C1.3] The economic effect is similarly emancipatory. English gives a farmer's child in Vietnam, a programmer in Kenya, or a nurse in the Philippines direct access to customers, employers, scholarships, and technical manuals without waiting for intermediaries from an old linguistic elite. Refusing that access in the name of cultural purity would lock opportunity inside wealthier, better-connected families and states, deepening the very inequality critics claim to oppose.
[C1.4] Digitally, English-built infrastructure has become the scaffolding for multilingual expression, not its grave. The same internet once skewed toward English now carries vast ecosystems in Spanish, Hindi, Arabic, Indonesian, and hundreds of smaller languages, while translation tools, subtitles, and multilingual Wikipedia and video platforms let local knowledge be archived and discovered globally. A language can be recorded, taught, and revived far more effectively when its speakers are connected.
[C1.5] The real threat is not a shared bridge language but underinvestment in mother-tongue schools, publishing, and media. That failure is fixable alongside English. Removing the bridge would not save distinct ways of knowing; it would strand them.

The answer to unequal access is to democratize the key, not melt it down while elites keep private copies.
[C2.5] Digitally, English as a pivot has become infrastructure for other languages, not their replacement. Translation between Hindi and Swahili, subtitles, and multilingual platforms work better because a huge shared corpus exists to train and connect them. Remove that hub and low-resource languages do not flourish equally; they become more isolated from each other. PRO catalogues frictions of joining a global system, but never shows non-anglophone cultures would know more, earn more, or speak louder in a fragmented one.
