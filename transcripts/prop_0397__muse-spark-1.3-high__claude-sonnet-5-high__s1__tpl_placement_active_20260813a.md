[C1.2] Student loans are unsecured against a future that hasn't happened yet. A mortgage lender can repossess a house; a student lender has nothing to seize but hope. That's precisely why the law has always treated this debt differently — not out of cruelty, but because the entire model of funding higher education for people with no collateral and no credit history depends on loans being hard to walk away from. Make discharge easy, and you don't punish "reckless lenders" — you make every rational lender, public or private, tighten underwriting.
[C1.3] The first people cut off will be exactly the low-income, first-generation students PRO wants to protect, because they're the ones who look riskiest on paper.
[C1.4] Notice also who actually eats the cost. Federal loans dominate this market. "Much easier discharge" doesn't claw money back from greedy schools — tuition is already paid, spent, gone. It shifts the loss to taxpayers, the same public now asked to fund both the original loan and the write-off.
[C1.5] That's not accountability; it's just relabeling who pays.
[C1.6] And once discharge is easy rather than exceptional, you invite exactly the gaming PRO's own framing tries to exclude. A borrower who *expects* a low-earning field has every incentive to borrow generously and plan around eventual discharge, because unlike a gambler's debt, this "bad bet" was subsidized at origination by the government itself.
[C1.7] The compassionate case for the home health aide is real — but it's already answered by hardship discharge, income-driven repayment, and disability discharge. The question is not whether hardship deserves relief. It's whether we blow up the lending system to get there. It doesn't have to, and shouldn't.
