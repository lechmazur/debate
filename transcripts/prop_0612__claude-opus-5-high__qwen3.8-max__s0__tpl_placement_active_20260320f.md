[P1.1] Start with what money actually buys. When a foreign ministry funds a campus institute and retains the right to appoint its director, approve its staff, and set the boundaries of what may be discussed, the university has not received a gift. It has leased out a piece of its own judgment. That was the recurring pattern with state-run language and culture institutes embedded in Western campuses, and it is the pattern in gift agreements that carry non-disparagement clauses, naming vetoes, or quiet expectations about which visiting scholars get invited and which topics stay unfunded.
[P1.2] The deepest harm here is not a bribe anyone could prosecute. It is anticipation. A doctoral student picks a dissertation topic that keeps a fellowship alive. A department chair declines to host a dissident speaker because next year's renewal is pending.
[P1.3] Nobody is ever told no; they simply learn what not to ask. Independence dies by budget line, not by decree.
[P1.4] Which is why disclosure alone is necessary but insufficient, and why limits are the operative half of this motion. Sunlight tells you a chemistry centre draws forty percent of its funding from one authoritarian state's science ministry; it does not free that centre from the dependency. Caps do. A limit means no single foreign government ever becomes a load-bearing pillar of a department, so the university can still say no without institutional injury.
[P1.5] And universities cannot fix this themselves, because the incentives run the wrong way. The department captures the money; the whole institution absorbs the reputational and epistemic cost. Any single university that refuses tainted funds simply loses them to a competitor. That is a textbook collective action problem, and the standard solution is a rule that binds everyone.
[P1.6] Requirements of this kind also arm administrators: "our law forbids that clause" is a stronger bargaining position than institutional goodwill.
[P1.7] We already accept this logic for elections, defence contracting, and political lobbying — domains where we decided foreign state money warrants transparency and ceilings. Truth-production deserves at least the same protection as campaign advertising.

[C1.1] The proposition takes a real danger—donors seeking control—and answers it with a tool that often weakens the very independence it claims to defend. The question is not whether foreign states can try to influence campuses; they can. It is whether this remedy protects inquiry. Foreign government-linked money is not one thing.
[C1.2] It includes allied research councils funding physics, public health agencies funding cancer trials, cultural programs funding language study. The motion would let states impose limits based on source, not on whether the money buys control. That is a category error.
[C1.3] The abusive cases PRO describes share a feature: contractual leverage. Appointment rights, speech restrictions, topic vetoes. Those are addressable directly. Universities should be required to reveal donor conditions and prohibited from accepting clauses that surrender hiring, curriculum, or speech.
[C1.4] But a blanket limit on funding volume does not remove control; it merely makes lawful research poorer and more precarious. If a centre is overdependent, the remedy is diversification and reserve rules, not a crude cap that cuts collaboration while leaving bad bargains possible below the threshold.
[C1.5] Mandatory limits also invite political abuse. Once governments define which foreign links are dangerous, the list will expand with diplomatic tension. A ministry can choke uncomfortable fields by declaring a funding stream suspect. The more contentious the field, the more lawful scholarship becomes collateral.
[C1.6] That turns academic independence over to the state it supposedly protects. Domestic governments are not neutral arbiters: they have their own foreign-policy, electoral, and industrial interests. Giving them a ceiling-setting role over universities substitutes one patron’s leverage for another’s.
[C1.7] Finally, limits are unnecessary to solve the coordination problem. Disclosure plus standard forbidden-clause rules binds everyone and lets universities refuse abusive terms without losing competitiveness. The real threat is concealed control, not foreign support as such. PRO’s case proves the need for transparency and contract hygiene; it does not prove that countries should cap foreign government-linked funding.
