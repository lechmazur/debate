[P1.1] When police misconduct produces a settlement or judgment, someone must absorb the loss. Too often it is the municipal taxpayer: residents who neither committed nor controlled the abuse watch money leave budgets for schools, sanitation, and public safety. Requiring officers to carry personal professional liability insurance creates a fairer default. Covered claims can draw on the officer’s policy, and municipalities can make that coverage respond before discretionary indemnification, rather than treating every misconduct cost as a public expense.
[P1.2] This does not demand that officers bankrupt themselves over every error. Liability must still be proved, defenses remain available, and insurance manages defined financial risk. Nor need it weaken victims’ recovery. A city can preserve its obligation to compensate victims while using coverage and indemnification rules to allocate the ultimate cost responsibly.
[P1.3] The question is not whether a victim is made whole; it is why taxpayers should repeatedly be the deepest pocket for misconduct they did not commit.
[P1.4] The requirement also supplies an accountability signal. Insurers assess risk through premiums, coverage terms, and renewal decisions. A repeated pattern of covered claims can make an officer more expensive to insure and harder for a department to ignore, prompting supervision, retraining, or removal. This is no substitute for discipline or prosecution.
[P1.5] It stops the municipal budget from converting repeated misconduct into an anonymous public cost.
[P1.6] Expect the objection that cities could simply pay premiums or raise salaries. A city-paid, fully reimbursed policy would deliberately hollow out the reform; the proposition’s purpose requires meaningful personal coverage. A fixed salary adjustment is transparent compensation, not an open-ended misconduct subsidy. Other professions routinely carry liability insurance without collapsing.
[P1.7] Police exercise extraordinary coercive power; a financial backstop is a modest condition of that trust. Taxpayers should fund public safety, not serve as the insurer of last resort for individual wrongdoing.

[C1.1] The proposal names a real injustice — taxpayers bankrolling misconduct — and then builds a mechanism that reaches none of it.
[C1.2] Start with legal reality. When an on-duty officer violates someone's rights, the municipality is liable: vicariously for the act itself, and directly where its own policies, training, or supervision enabled the harm. Victims and courts pursue the pocket the law designates, and no insurance mandate changes who owes a judgment. The taxpayer is the backstop because the law makes the taxpayer the backstop.
[C1.3] Worse, the gravest cases are uninsurable. Policies exclude intentional wrongdoing as standard practice, and many jurisdictions bar insuring punitive damages outright. The headline settlements that animate this debate — deliberate brutality, planted evidence — are precisely the claims a personal policy will not touch. What remains insurable is negligence, the routine claims cities already absorb cheaply.
[C1.4] The motion rebuilds the system to cover the wrong slice of the problem.
[C1.5] And the money never actually leaves the taxpayer. Officers' salaries are public money; a mandatory premium is either reimbursed or recovered through negotiated raises. My opponent calls a salary offset "transparent compensation" — precisely. It is taxpayers funding the premium either way, now laundered through an insurer skimming overhead and profit.
[C1.6] Taxpayers buy the policy, still pay the big settlements, and tip a middleman for the arrangement.
[C1.7] Accountability degrades as well. Claims paid by an insurer are textbook moral hazard; the officer's skin leaves the game. The premium "signal" the other side celebrates prices risk, not culpability — it punishes officers for their precinct and for claims insurers settle to minimize their own losses, regardless of fault. Real deterrence is discipline, decertification, and prosecution.
[C1.8] A renewal notice is a spreadsheet proxy for all of it.
[C1.9] Finally, a wealth-based insurance hurdle screens recruits by creditworthiness rather than character, into a profession already struggling to hire.
[C1.10] The promise is a cost shift. The reality is a cost increase with accountability diluted.
