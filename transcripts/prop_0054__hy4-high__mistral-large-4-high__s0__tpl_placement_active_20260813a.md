[P1.1] A daily cap treats childhood media as one substance measured in minutes. That is the core error. Thirty minutes video-calling a grandparent, making music with a sibling, or getting homework help is not the same event as thirty minutes of autoplay clips, hostile group chats, or frightening content before bed. The clock sees one behavior; the child experiences content, design, purpose, company, and timing.
[P1.2] Advice should train parents to see those differences first.
[P1.3] The harms travel through mechanisms a cap cannot identify. Sleep is disrupted by late, arousing, hard-to-stop use. Language and attention suffer when screens replace conversation, play, and schoolwork, not merely because a screen was on. Mood is shaped by comparison, harassment, and feeds.
[P1.4] Risk rises when a child is alone with strangers or a persuasive feed, and falls when a caregiver co-views, asks questions, sets a purpose, and helps the child stop. Same minutes, different mechanism.
[P1.5] Cap-first advice gives parents a dangerously easy conscience. A family can hit the number while a child is isolated or pulled into status competition; another can exceed it because homework, disability support, distant family contact, or a co-created project requires a screen. The first looks compliant; the second looks irresponsible. That is bookkeeping, not guidance.
[P1.6] It invites children to hide minutes rather than discuss what happened online.
[P1.7] Even duration matters chiefly through displacement: endless use crowds out life. It belongs inside the question: What is the child doing, with whom, under whose design, at what time, and what is it displacing? A cap is an odometer; parents need a steering wheel. Advice should emphasize age-appropriate content, co-use for younger children, device-free sleep and meals, privacy and peer norms, advertising literacy, stopping points, and family conversation.
[P1.8] Those tools handle harmful excess and harmful use that stays under the limit. Defending caps puts parental attention on a simple, portable number instead of the child's actual media life.

[C1.1] The proposition treats minutes and meaning as separable levers and says parents should weight the second more heavily. That is the error. A daily cap is not bookkeeping that distracts from quality; it is the container that makes quality possible. Without a hard boundary on total time, advice about content collapses under platform design engineered to maximize engagement minute-by-minute.
[C1.2] Consider advice that leads with context instead of limits. A parent told to focus on what the child is doing faces an impossible audit: Is this video educational or just well-produced? Is this game social or isolating? Is this chat supportive or grooming?
[C1.3] These judgments must be made in real time against autoplay, infinite scroll, and feeds designed to override parental oversight. The result is exhaustion, and exhaustion defaults to permissiveness. A simple cap, by contrast, is enforceable at 8 p.m. when a twelve-year-old is three hours into a livestream and the parent is tired. It works because it requires no content expertise.
[C1.4] Duration is not a neutral proxy; it is a direct mechanism of harm. Every added hour displaces sleep, play, homework, or conversation, and extends exposure to advertising, comparison loops, and sleep-disrupting light. It entrenches habit loops that make stopping harder. The proposition treats displacement as a qualitative question, but displacement happens through accumulated minutes.
[C1.5] Harmful content can strike in thirty minutes, yes, but compulsive use, feed anxiety, and sleep debt scale with total daily dose. Public health guidance on smoking or sugar does not begin with "focus on the quality of the cigarette"; it begins with a limit because dose is the first-order driver of harm.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition's examples of good use—video-calling grandparents, homework help—are cases where a cap should flex, not disappear. A cap is a budget, not a prison. Families can reallocate minutes toward high-value use, but the budget itself forces the trade-off conversation. Without that anchor, "focus more on quality" becomes nothing enforceable, and the default winner is the platform, not the parent.
