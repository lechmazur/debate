[P1.1] Prohibition is not neutrality; it is a supply squeeze on a life-saving organ. Kidney demand is driven by kidney failure, not price. Because lawful donation falls short, patients wait years and many die on dialysis. That unmet demand becomes a business opportunity for traffickers and brokers.
[P1.2] The black market is not a random evil; it is what prohibition produces when desperate patients and sellers are pushed outside the law.
[P1.3] In the illegal market, donors get the worst deal: pay below the value they create, no reliable screening, no real consent process, no follow-up care, and no remedy if harmed. Recipients face unvetted donors, higher infection and mismatch risk, and no protection if the transplant fails. Brokers capture the profit and hide complications.
[P1.4] A regulated legal market flips those incentives. Compensation can be transparent and tied to medical and psychological screening, informed consent, cooling-off periods, and long-term donor follow-up. Legal providers compete on safety and outcomes, not secrecy. Once a lawful, safer alternative exists, the black market loses customers and price premium, shrinking the harms prohibition entrenches.
[P1.5] The life-years math is direct: more kidneys means fewer people dying on dialysis. Compensating donors expands supply beyond altruism alone; shorter waits allow transplantation before years of dialysis have already damaged the heart and blood vessels. A living-donor kidney generally offers better long-term survival than dialysis or a deceased-donor organ. Regulation also protects donors, so the gain is not bought by sacrificing them.
[P1.6] The real choice is whether vulnerable people face pressure inside a system with rights and follow-up care, or inside a criminal market with none. Regulation saves lives and reduces harm.
