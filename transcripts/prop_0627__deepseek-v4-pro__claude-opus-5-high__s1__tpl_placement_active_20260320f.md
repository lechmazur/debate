[C1.1] The proposition fails on a dilemma that PRO's own opening walks straight into. We are told this right "does not handcuff economic development; it only forbids a race to the bottom." Then either the right is so thin that it duplicates statutes we already have and buys nothing, or it is thick enough to bind—in which case unelected judges are setting the dial on the single most polycentric question in modern governance.
[C1.2] Because "minimally clean and healthy" is not a floor. It is a slider. What counts as an acceptable level of fine particulates depends on what it costs to get there, who pays, and what else that money and political capital could have bought—vaccines, housing, transit. When a Dutch court ordered the state to cut emissions by a specific percentage, it did not discover a constitutional minimum; it picked a number from a policy menu.
[C1.3] A judge can competently decide whether a specific factory poisoned a specific well. That is bilateral, causal, remediable. A judge cannot competently decide the national energy mix, and pretending the two inquiries are alike is where PRO's case breaks.
[C1.4] Worse, the right cuts the wrong way. Environmental litigation rights, in practice, are wielded by whoever can afford lawyers and already has an address. In the United States, environmental review has become the premier instrument for blocking transmission lines, wind farms, lithium mines, and apartment buildings near transit. Constitutionalize a right to a clean environment and you hand every incumbent homeowner and every fossil incumbent a veto dressed in the language of human dignity.
[C1.5] Decarbonization requires building enormous amounts of stuff, quickly. A judicially enforceable environmental right is a machine for delay.
[C1.6] And there is a cost PRO ignores entirely: declared rights that courts cannot deliver teach citizens that constitutional promises are decorative. That corrosion is not free.
[C1.7] The measure of success here is cleaner air breathed by real people—not a paragraph in a constitution. Legislatures, agencies, and ordinary tort law deliver the first. This right mostly delivers the second.
