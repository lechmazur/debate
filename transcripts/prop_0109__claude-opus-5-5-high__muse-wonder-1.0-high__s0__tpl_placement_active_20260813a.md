In a tight market the price reflects too few homes chasing too many households. Ban the fund, and the same home still sells to a higher-income couple, a small landlord buying through an LLC, or a cash-rich downsizer. The nurse still loses.
[C1.2] The real crowd-out is scarcity built over decades: restrictive zoning, slow permitting, and neighborhoods that block duplexes, townhomes, and apartments near jobs. Institutional buyers go where rents and population are already rising; they are mostly chasing that shortage, not causing it. They also own only a small share of the nation's single-family stock, so a purchase ban barely moves market prices. It treats the most visible buyer as if it were the binding constraint.
[C1.3] The ban also hits renters and sellers. Many households cannot buy yet — because of credit, debt, a recent move, or an uncertain job — but still want a yard and a local school. Managed rentals, often renovated from distressed or vacant properties first-time buyers could not finance, serve that demand. Sellers may be older owners, estates, or families needing a quick sale; blocking a ready buyer cuts their equity and can leave hard-to-sell homes empty.
[C1.4] It is also easily evaded and hard to stop there. Define 'large' too narrowly and capital buys through subsidiaries or mid-sized firms that face less scrutiny. Define it broadly and a city deters the very capital and build-to-rent projects that add supply, sends purchases just outside its borders, and invites costly fights over who counts as local. The winners are not first-time buyers without cash; they are the next wealthiest bidders.
[C1.5] Legalize more homes, speed approvals, and target down-payment help. That expands who can win instead of rationing bids on a shortage.
