[P1.1] Ultra-fast online casino products and in-play betting are not just gambling in a new wrapper; they are engineered for a different relationship with the user. A weekly lottery draw, a casino table session, or a pre-match wager usually contains friction: waiting periods, social cues, cash exchange, and a natural pause before the next stake. An online slot or a next-ball betting market removes those brakes. A person can place hundreds of high-intensity bets in an hour, from a phone, with one-touch deposits, autoplay, and instant losses or near-misses that push immediate re-staking.
[P1.2] That speed matters because harm is not only about how much money is risked, but about how quickly behavior can become compulsive. Rapid event cycles shorten the gap between impulse and action, weaken reflection, and make loss-chasing easier. In-play betting is especially dangerous because it hijacks live sport: odds shift constantly, micro-markets invite continuous wagering, and the product feels like skillful participation rather than repeated betting. The result is a harm profile concentrated in intensive, repetitive use, not occasional recreation.
[P1.3] The correct regulatory response is therefore product-specific. Governments already accept that not all gambling is alike: age limits, venue rules, stake caps, and licensing conditions vary by format. If a product is designed to deliver repeated betting opportunities every few seconds, it deserves tighter controls than a product that resolves once per match or once per week. That means aggressive tools: limits on stake size and spin speed, mandatory breaks and cooling-off periods, restrictions on autoplay and one-touch credit, stronger affordability checks, and special curbs on in-play micro-betting and push notifications.
[P1.4] The question is not whether slower gambling can also harm people. It can. The question is whether public policy should match regulation to the mechanism of harm. Because ultra-fast digital products create that harm more directly and continuously, they should be regulated more aggressively.
