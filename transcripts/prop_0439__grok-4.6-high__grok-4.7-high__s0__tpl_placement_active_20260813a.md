[P1.1] The status quo does not abolish kidney markets. It drives them underground. People still sell kidneys where brokers, cash, and desperation already meet: no reliable screening, no enforceable contract, no follow-up care, and no court if the promised money vanishes. Recipients absorb infection and mismatch risk.
[P1.2] Sellers absorb coercion, incomplete payment, and untreated complications. Prohibition does not protect them. It strips the only tools that make a transaction safer: licenses, matching, medical standards, and legal recourse.
[P1.3] A regulated compensation system attacks both failures at once. Payment expands supply because many medically eligible people will not donate for praise and a sandwich, yet will donate if lost wages, surgery risk, and recovery time are offset. Regulation keeps the parts prohibition pretends to care about: independent consent checks, waiting periods, exclusion of medically unfit donors, organ matching, and mandated aftercare. The legal channel undercuts illegal operators the way licensed pharmacies undercut street vendors.
[P1.4] When a safer, payable option exists, the worst trades lose customers.
[P1.5] Each extra living-donor transplant converts years of dialysis decline into functioning kidney years. Waitlists are long; patients deteriorate, become untransplantable, or die waiting. The shortage is not a moral decoration. It is a pile of lost life-years produced by a ban that cannot create organs.
[P1.6] Even a modest, screened increase in living donation beats today’s combination of altruistic trickle plus black-market leftovers.
[P1.7] The usual objection is exploitation. That is already the black market’s business model: no rights, no medicine, no exit. Commodification fears do not remove markets; they hide them. Scarcity plus illegality is what leaves the seller with a scar and a broken promise.
[P1.8] A regulated market replaces that with consent, payment that actually arrives, and care that actually happens. That is why compensation under rules reduces black-market harms and increases life-years compared with today’s prohibition.

[C1.1] Creating a regulated market for paid kidney donors would neither cut black-market harms nor raise life-years above today’s prohibition. It would route sales through the same desperation and lower the quality of the kidneys that move.
[C1.2] Payment does not simply add organs to the altruistic base. The strongest current transplants often come from relatives and directed donors, with better matching and follow-through than stranger sales can match. Normalize a fee and some of those donors hesitate, bargain, or withdraw. The replacement pool is people covering rent, debt, or a family emergency.
[C1.3] They have reason to hide hypertension, infection, or incomplete history to pass screening and get paid. Recipients absorb more rejection, transmitted disease, and early graft failure. That shortens functioning kidney years.
[C1.4] Consent interviews, waiting periods, fitness screens, and aftercare rules do not cancel the pressure that created the seller. No counselor pays the bill that forced the visit. Mandated follow-up ends; chronic pain, lost kidney reserve, and missed wages do not. The person leaves with money that was not optional and a harm profile the black market already knows—only now stamped legal.
[C1.5] A legal price does not retire brokers. Hold it down to appear fair and anyone needing more cash, or barred by the medical rules, still deals underground, now with a public benchmark to beat. Let it float and the poor still supply the organs while patients shut out by citizenship, insurance, or the queue keep buying abroad. Pharmacies can undercut street sales because pills are manufactured.
[C1.6] Kidneys are not. The desperate body remains the factory, and the broker reroutes.
[C1.7] Prohibition does not manufacture kidneys, but a paid market does not fix that failure. It recruits people who cannot walk away, selects for concealed medical risk, and leaves illegal channels open. Measured by black-market harm and life-years actually gained, compensation under rules does not outperform the ban.
