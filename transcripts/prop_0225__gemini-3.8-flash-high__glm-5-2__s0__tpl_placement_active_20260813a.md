[P1.1] Public universities operate on a clear social contract funded by taxpayers. Their purpose is twofold: to deliver genuine socioeconomic mobility for students and to supply the foundational competencies a nation needs to thrive. Uncapped enrollment in programs with chronically poor employment outcomes betrays both promises.
[P1.2] First, open-ended enrollment in low-demand programs exploits the very students public institutions are meant to uplift. Funneling thousands of young people into saturated fields under the generic banner of "degree attainment" leads directly to underemployment and persistent debt. This credential treadmill disproportionately punishes working-class and first-generation students, who take on debt expecting a career payoff that the market cannot support. Public universities have an affirmative duty of care; they should not sell credentials that function as financial dead ends.
[P1.3] Capping enrollment does not eliminate these disciplines; it right-sizes them to genuine demand, preserving academic quality while protecting students from foreseeable distress.
[P1.4] Second, this policy corrects a severe structural distortion. Low-employment programs are often bloated because they are cheap for universities to run—filling lecture halls that maximize tuition revenue with minimal overhead. Meanwhile, vital sectors facing acute national shortages—such as healthcare, electrical engineering, cybersecurity, and green infrastructure—face severe capacity bottlenecks. These high-demand disciplines require capital-intensive investments: modern laboratories, clinical placements, and competitive faculty salaries.
[P1.5] Subsidizing oversized cohorts in low-yield tracks directly starves the fields that maintain public health, technological competitiveness, and basic infrastructure.
[P1.6] Public funding is finite, and higher education policy is fundamentally an act of prioritization. Opponents may defend unrestricted access as intellectual freedom, but freedom without economic viability is an empty promise. It is neither progressive nor rational to underwrite debt traps while hospitals lack nurses, energy grids lack engineers, and critical systems decay. Capping enrollment in low-employment tracks and redirecting public subsidies toward national skills shortages restores higher education to its primary duty: serving the collective public interest through shared prosperity.
