[P1.1] Boards of large public companies currently answer to one constituency: shareholders seeking short-term returns. That structural fact explains a lot of what goes wrong in modern capitalism—mass layoffs timed to prop up quarterly earnings, wage stagnation even as productivity rises, plant closures decided by people who never set foot in the plant. Worker board representation is not a radical experiment; it is a correction to a governance system that has one blind spot built into its design, and that blind spot happens to cover the majority of the people whose labor creates the company's value.
[P1.2] The mechanism is simple. Give workers a meaningful bloc of board seats—not a token seat, but enough to actually shift deliberation, say a third of the board—and decisions get made with information and incentives that are currently absent. Workers know things management doesn't: where safety corners are being cut, where morale is collapsing, where a restructuring plan will blow up in six months. Germany's codetermination system, running for decades across major firms, shows this isn't fantasy—worker-inclusive boards there coexist with some of the most competitive, export-strong companies on earth.
[P1.3] That is the existence proof CON has to explain away, not dismiss.
[P1.4] There's also a fairness argument that stands on its own regardless of efficiency: workers bear catastrophic downside risk from corporate decisions—job loss, relocation, benefit cuts—while having zero formal voice in making them. Shareholders can diversify away a bad bet in one company by holding a hundred others. A worker cannot diversify away the loss of their only job. Governance power should track exposure to consequences, and right now it radically does not.
[P1.5] Finally, notice what the legal requirement actually does: it doesn't ban shareholder control, ban profit, or seize equity. It reallocates a defined share of decision-making seats. That's a modest, targeted fix to a specific, demonstrable imbalance—not the sweeping restructuring of capitalism CON will likely paint it as.

[C1.2] Worker directors answer to a constituency whose dominant interest is often job tenure, plant continuity, and near-term wage claims. Those priorities frequently collide with the need to restructure, exit failing lines, relocate production, or reallocate capital toward higher returns. The result is not balanced deliberation but paralyzed boards forced to serve two incompatible masters. Firms that cannot make hard calls lose ground to those that can, and the eventual damage lands on workers when the firm weakens or fails.
[C1.3] Germany’s codetermination is no clean existence proof. It sits inside a two-tier board structure, different financing patterns, and export specializations the U.S. system does not share. Many high-performing economies thrive without such rules; transplanting them by statute does not magically import only the good parts.
[C1.4] The fairness claim reverses the actual risk positions. Shareholders cannot diversify away jurisdiction-wide value destruction or capital flight once boards become political. Workers already operate with wages, contracts, mobility across employers, unions, and labor law. Residual claimants have none of those protections for the equity they alone supply.
[C1.5] Governance power properly tracks residual risk-bearing, not every form of exposure.
[C1.6] “Significant percentage” is also not modest. It invites capture by activist blocs, perpetual litigation over election rules, and higher capital costs as investors price in divided control. Firms that treat labor well already attract talent and outperform; markets reward that without coercion. This mandate does not correct imbalance—it installs conflict that makes every major decision slower, costlier, and less coherent.
