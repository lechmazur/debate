[P1.1] Coal is not just another energy source. It is the single largest driver of the climate emergency and a factory of lethal air pollution. Unabated coal power—plants that dump carbon straight into the sky—cannot coexist with a livable climate. A global phaseout by 2040 is the latest credible deadline that still gives countries time to rewire their grids while closing the window on catastrophe.
[P1.2] Voluntary pledges have already failed. Plants keep being built, then locked in for decades. Carbon leakage turns one nation’s “phaseout” into another nation’s boom. That is why the commitment must be universal and why it must carry teeth: trade measures on carbon-intensive goods, or finance penalties through multilateral lenders.
[P1.3] If a country continues to sell electricity that destabilizes everyone’s weather, the rest of the world is entitled to stop subsidizing that choice through open markets and cheap capital.
[P1.4] This is not punishment of the poor. It is the opposite. The people who breathe coal smoke and lose harvests to extreme weather are overwhelmingly in developing countries. Cheap solar, wind, and storage already undercut new coal in most markets.
[P1.5] The 2040 line, paired with finance for grids and just transition, turns a race to the bottom into a race to replace. Enforcement prevents free riders from capturing dirty-industry jobs while others pay the climate bill.
[P1.6] Without a hard, penalized deadline, incumbents delay, investors keep pouring money into stranded assets, and the safest climate pathways slip away. Fifteen years is enough to retire remaining plants, cancel new ones, and build replacement capacity; waiting until 2050 simply banks emissions we cannot afford. With a 2040 deadline and real penalties, capital reallocates, clean technology scales, and the atmosphere stops being a free dumping ground. Sovereignty does not include a right to impose climate damages on every coastline and farm.
[P1.7] Countries should commit because delay is not energy security. It is shared ruin, paid first by those least able to adapt.

[C1.1] A 2040 ban on unabated coal, enforced by trade and finance penalties, fails the test that matters: will it retire coal without breaking the grids and the coalition that any real cut depends on?
[C1.2] The coal still doing the work is firm domestic baseload in China, India, Indonesia, South Africa, and Vietnam. Much of that fleet is young. Undercutting a new plant is not the same as retiring that fleet by 2040 — a demand to scrap plants with decades of life left and to finish transmission, storage, and backup that large developing grids have not shown they can build on that clock. Daytime solar does not run a steel mill or a hospital through the night.
[C1.3] Pull firm power first, and the substitute is rationing, diesel, or rushed gas — costlier, and one election from reversal.
[C1.4] Penalties make refusal rational. A carbon border tariff, as Europe is already building, is written by the importer and paid by the exporter — protection dressed as climate policy. Finance penalties do worse: they withdraw the capital grids need to change. The motion specifies sanctions.
[C1.5] It does not bind anyone to grants, cheap debt, or technology access that would make 2040 conceivable. Shut out of markets and multilateral money, governments will not darken factories on schedule. They will finance coal bilaterally, and leakage will change banks rather than stop.
[C1.6] A single deadline dodges equity. Countries that grew rich on coal, and now hold capital and spare capacity, are not situated like countries still electrifying homes. Penalties they write will be enforced downward: green conditionality, not shared burden. Stack standards can cut local coal smoke without a climate sanction grafted onto trade law.
[C1.7] Climate risk does not license a pact that loses the states operating the plants.
[C1.8] Coal ought to shrink. This package will not be why it does. It strands assets, punishes late developers, and splits cooperation. Reject it.
