[P1.1] Start with what the motion actually says: a *presumption*, not a prohibition. The buyer keeps the right to rebut — through divestiture of control, offtake guarantees, golden shares, minority stakes. All the presumption does is shift the burden of proof onto the party that knows the most and shares the least. That is ordinary regulatory design, and it is the right design here for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the asymmetry of errors. Approve a bad deal and you get a lithium or cobalt asset with a thirty-year life, embedded offtake contracts, and a foreign owner you cannot claw back in a crisis without expropriation. Block a good deal and a seller gets a somewhat lower price from the next bidder. One mistake is reversible with money; the other is reversible only with a sovereign fight.
[P1.3] When errors are that lopsided, you tilt the default.
[P1.4] Second, this is not hypothetical risk. Rival states have already treated minerals as leverage — China's rare earth squeeze on Japan during the 2010 Senkaku dispute, and more recently export controls on gallium, germanium, and graphite. We are not guessing at intent; we are extrapolating from behavior. A presumption simply refuses to keep re-learning the same lesson deal by deal.
[P1.5] Third, the market cannot price this. A state-linked buyer bidding for a nickel mine isn't seeking the commercial return; it's buying an option on future coercion, subsidized by a treasury. It will therefore outbid every purely commercial rival, systematically. Neutral, deal-by-deal review compounds the problem, because the reviewer must prove danger against opaque ownership chains, third-country shells, and nominee structures.
[P1.6] Requiring the state to prove hostile intent about a firm whose actual controllers are legally obliged to cooperate with a foreign intelligence service is a standard that guarantees under-enforcement.
[P1.7] This is not autarky. Allied and neutral capital flows freely; the presumption attaches only to firms closely tied to rivals. The question is who bears the burden of uncertainty over an irreplaceable, immovable asset. It should be the buyer, not the public.

[C1.1] Calling this a mere presumption hides what it does. The trigger is not a concrete act of control; it is “closely tied to a rival state,” an elastic category that reaches sovereign funds, minority state stakes, state-directed banks, processors, and joint ventures. Once triggered, the buyer must prove a negative — that its ownership will never become a tool of coercion. Security agencies rarely accept such guarantees, so the “presumption” becomes a blanket rejection.
[C1.2] That is a nationality-based investment ban, not a burden shift.
[C1.3] The cost falls exactly where supply is weakest. New lithium, copper, nickel, and rare-earth projects are already short of capital. Rival-state firms often bring more than money: processing capacity, offtake finance, and technical capability. Excluding them delays projects, tightens supply, raises prices, and leaves consuming countries more dependent on the same rival processors they fear.
[C1.4] Indonesian nickel did not scale because Western capital led; it scaled substantially through Chinese-financed processing. A presumption against that capital would have meant less supply, not more security.
[C1.5] The threat is also misdiagnosed. PRO’s own examples — rare-earth export limits, gallium and germanium controls — show the leverage ran through export licensing and domestic processing, not ownership of a foreign mine. A state that can already restrict processing does not need to buy a mine abroad to coerce. The presumption attacks the wrong node while ignoring stockpiles, recycling, processing diversification, and coordinated export controls.
[C1.6] And the asymmetry of errors is overstated. A bad deal can be made safer through conditions: limits on board control, offtake caps, ring-fencing, local processing, monitoring, or divestiture triggers. Broad supply losses from excluding an entire class of investors are slower and harder to reverse than a board seat. The better policy is calibrated, behavior-based review plus resilience investment, not an unfocused veto.
