Most loot-box items are non-transferable licenses, valuable only inside a game the publisher controls.
[C1.2] If every randomized reward sold to a child becomes gambling, then toy vending machines, booster packs, mystery boxes, and promotional scratch-off stickers become casino equipment. That expansion does not clarify the law; it dilutes it.
[C1.3] The real issue is whether the remedy protects children. Gambling law’s ordinary answer to minors is exclusion, not supervised purchase. If loot boxes are legally gambling, the logical endpoint is banning them for children or licensing publishers like casinos. That pushes play toward grey markets, account sharing, and gift-card workarounds, or triggers cosmetic redesigns that preserve the same spending loops.
[C1.4] Neither makes children safer. A targeted consumer-protection framework can do more: mandatory spend limits, clear odds, refund rights, no dark patterns, parental dashboards, and restrictions on features that pressure repeated spending.
[C1.5] PRO’s best evidence—flashy reveals, near-misses, and third-party resale—supports targeted regulation, not the gambling label. Where items can truly be cashed out, regulate the cash-out. [C1.6] Treating every randomized in-game purchase as gambling collapses a useful distinction into a slogan. It invites over-enforcement, ignores parental responsibility, and gives regulators a blunt instrument when children need precise safeguards.
