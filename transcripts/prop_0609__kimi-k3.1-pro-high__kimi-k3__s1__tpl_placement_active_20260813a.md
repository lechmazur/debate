[P1.1] A climate-vulnerable country can be trapped in a vicious circle: a storm destroys roads, crops, and power systems; reconstruction deepens its debt; debt repayments then crowd out the very defenses that would limit the next disaster. Unconditional relief can ease this year’s budget without breaking that circle. Making relief automatically contingent on climate-resilience investment does.
[P1.2] First, the condition targets the cause of recurring insolvency, not merely its symptoms. Resilience includes flood defenses, resilient power and water systems, early-warning capacity, climate-smart agriculture, and protection for hospitals and schools. These investments reduce future losses, protect revenue, and lower the likelihood that creditors must repeatedly rescue the same country. CON treats debt relief and climate adaptation as competing priorities; in vulnerable countries, adaptation is part of restoring solvency.
[P1.3] Second, contingency makes scarce relief accountable. Debt-service savings are fungible: without a condition, they can disappear into debt refinancing, poorly governed projects, or expenditure that leaves exposure untouched. This does not require assuming every government is wasteful. It recognizes that relief is justified not simply by transferring fiscal space, but by producing durable benefits for citizens and creditors.
[P1.4] A transparent resilience requirement also gives domestic taxpayers and donor publics confidence that relief will not become a recurring subsidy for inaction.
[P1.5] Third, automaticity answers the strongest objection to conditionality: donor discretion. Relief should follow published, proportionate criteria and verified milestones, not case-by-case political bargaining. Countries should choose investments through their own resilience plans, with independent but streamlined verification. Once the agreed standard is met, relief follows automatically.
[P1.6] That is more predictable than today’s discretionary negotiations, not less sovereign.
[P1.7] CON may invoke urgency or neo-colonial conditions. But delaying resilience leaves schools, clinics, and livelihoods exposed to the next shock, while unpredictable relief leaves finance ministries unable to plan. The fairest compact is not “money now, vulnerability later”; it is assured relief for nationally chosen protection. Automatic climate-resilience conditionality turns cancellation from a temporary breathing space into an escape route from the debt-climate trap.

[C1.1] Automatic contingency sounds tidy: relief flows when resilience is delivered. It fails for three reasons, all rooted in one flaw — the motion welds an urgent liquidity instrument to a slow capital-planning one, then makes the urgent wait on the slow.
[C1.2] First, timing. Debt relief matters most in the weeks after a shock, at the edge of default. Resilience investment is slow: plans, procurement, construction, verification. [P1.1] proves it — the storm destroys the roads, then reconstruction deepens the debt.
[C1.3] Under this motion, that country's relief now waits on a verified flood-defense program. When Hurricane Maria gutted Dominica in 2017, the island needed liquidity within weeks; relief conditioned on completed resilience milestones would have arrived in years. Automaticity doesn't remove delay; it hard-codes it.
[C1.4] Second, conditionality has a record, and it is grim. Structural-adjustment conditions bred resentment, compliance theater, and programs that collapsed when governments couldn't comply — relief stayed stuck anyway. Climate conditionality adds a problem adjustment never faced: there is no agreed metric for "resilience." An automatic trigger demands a precise standard; a precise standard invites gaming, and whoever writes and verifies it still holds the power.
[C1.5] [P1.5] promises nationally chosen plans, but a plan that must satisfy the creditor's checklist to unlock relief is chosen the way a hostage chooses. Automatic is not apolitical; it is donor power disguised as a formula.
[C1.6] Third, the regressive edge. The most indebted, most climate-vulnerable states are typically least able to plan, procure, and document qualifying projects. Automatic contingency means the weakest fail the test and get nothing, while better-administered states collect relief. A policy aimed at "vulnerable countries" systematically excludes the most vulnerable.
[C1.7] None of this chooses relief over resilience. Disaster-triggered payment suspensions, concessional adaptation grants, voluntary debt-for-climate swaps: these pair the goals without holding either hostage. The motion forbids relief whenever compliance lags — including pure humanitarian emergencies and plain insolvencies no seawall can fix. That is not a compact; it is a trap.
