[P1.1] The core of this debate is simple: if a platform controls the price, assigns the work, monitors performance, and can deactivate a worker through ratings, it is exercising employer-like power. It should therefore carry employer-like duties. Reclassifying most gig workers as employees by default is the only rule that matches economic reality.
[P1.2] Today’s misclassification shifts risk from firms to individuals. A delivery rider injured on the road, a driver waiting unpaid between fares, or a domestic worker dependent on one app for income may have no sick pay, no minimum earnings floor, no workers’ compensation, and no clear route to collective bargaining. When those gaps appear, the cost does not vanish; it moves to hospitals, families, local governments, and taxpayers. Platforms gain the benefits of a managed labor force while avoiding the responsibilities of employment.
[P1.3] The proposition does not ban freelancing. It creates a default, with narrow exemptions for genuinely independent contractors: people who set their own rates, negotiate terms, serve multiple clients, and can build a business beyond a single platform. A default rule also reverses the burden: instead of isolated contractors litigating against well-resourced platforms, regulators determine status from the facts of control. That distinction is workable.
[P1.4] A consultant selling custom services is different from a rider whose pay per delivery is fixed by an algorithm and whose future access depends on platform scores.
[P1.5] For Europe and Asia, a default employee rule also reduces regulatory arbitrage and creates a level playing field. Responsible firms should not be undercut by competitors treating labor as disposable input. Employee status can still preserve flexible scheduling; the issue is not forcing fixed shifts, but ensuring that flexibility does not become a device to evade basic protection. The winning path is clear: protect workers where control exists, and reserve independence for those who actually possess it.
