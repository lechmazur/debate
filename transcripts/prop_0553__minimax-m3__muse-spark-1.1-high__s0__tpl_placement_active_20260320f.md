[P1.1] India stands at a crossroads. With over a billion mobile users and a consumer electronics market expanding rapidly, the question is not whether Indians will repair their devices, but who controls that repair: the consumer, or the manufacturer. A national right-to-repair law is the difference between ownership and permanent leasing.
[P1.2] The core case is straightforward. When a phone screen cracks or a laptop battery fails, Indians face a rigged choice: pay inflated prices to authorized service centers, or risk voided warranties and blacklisted devices for choosing an independent technician. The current system transfers wealth from Indian households to foreign manufacturers while denying livelihood to local repair ecosystems. A right-to-repair law breaks this arrangement by mandating access to spare parts, diagnostic tools, schematics, and repair documentation on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory terms.
[P1.3] The economic argument is equally compelling. India’s informal repair sector employs millions of technicians. Without parts access, this workforce collapses. Meanwhile, the formal sector benefits from manufactured scarcity.
[P1.4] Right-to-repair redistributes economic power to Indian consumers and small businesses rather than preserving it in the hands of a handful of global OEMs.
[P1.5] The environmental case is undeniable. Planned obsolescence and repair restrictions generate millions of tonnes of e-waste annually. India already struggles with one of the world’s largest e-waste burdens. Extending product lifespan through repair directly reduces toxic waste, conserves the rare earth minerals required for new devices, and lowers carbon emissions from manufacturing.
[P1.6] The United Nations has recognized repair as a climate intervention; India should codify it.
[P1.7] The security concern raised by critics, that parts access threatens cybersecurity, is solvable and overblown. Software locks and component authentication can coexist with repair rights. The EU’s Cyber Resilience Act and India’s own cybersecurity framework already provide templates for balancing both objectives.
[P1.8] Finally, the sovereignty argument matters. India cannot build a self-reliant electronics ecosystem while remaining dependent on foreign companies for every screw, screen, and schematic. Right-to-repair is industrial policy, consumer protection, and environmental strategy rolled into one. The motion should be affirmed.
