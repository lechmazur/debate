[P1.1] Market access is a sovereign privilege, not a corporate right. When a pharmaceutical company seeks approval to sell a drug within a country's borders, the state is not obligated to grant that access unconditionally. The proposition recognizes a simple truth: regulatory approval is a negotiation, and transparency about a drug's full safety and efficacy profile should be the non-negotiable price of admission.
[P1.2] Today, the clinical trial ecosystem suffers from a profound distortion. Positive results are published, promoted, and used to secure market access, while negative or inconclusive trials languish in file drawers. This selective visibility creates a dangerous information asymmetry. Regulators, physicians, and patients make decisions based on an artificially optimistic evidence base.
[P1.3] When a drug causes harm after approval—when the hidden trials would have warned of it—the cost is measured in preventable suffering and wasted healthcare expenditure.
[P1.4] Requiring public disclosure of negative results as a condition of market access fixes this at the precise moment of maximum leverage. Before a company gains access to millions of patients and the revenue that follows, it must account for the complete truth of its product. This is not censorship; it is informed consent at the level of public health policy. It ensures that the evidence base reflects reality rather than marketing strategy.
[P1.5] Furthermore, this requirement aligns incentives correctly. Companies will design better trials and report honestly if they know that hiding failures will block market entry. The alternative—allowing access while concealing negative data—rewards gaming the system and punishes scientific integrity.
[P1.6] Countries already exercise this power through pricing negotiations and labeling requirements. Adding disclosure of negative trial results is a natural extension of regulatory sovereignty. The question is not whether states have the authority to demand transparency, but whether they have the will to use it. The answer must be yes.

[C1.5] Unfiltered release creates a second harm. Unsuccessful studies, stripped of the weighting regulators applied, travel easily into headlines and advocacy. [C1.6] Countries already impose labeling rules, safety monitoring, and post-approval obligations. They can tighten registry reporting without turning unfavorable findings into a gate on sale. Concealing material data from regulators is already a violation in functioning approval systems. [C1.7] It taxes the failures that teach the field and withholds vetted treatments from patients.

[P2.6] The public health cost of a second company repeating a failed, harmful trial dwarfs any competitive disadvantage and redirects research toward genuinely promising avenues.
[P2.7] Finally, the definition objection is a red herring. Trial registries already mandate results reporting, including negative findings, with established frameworks for classifying endpoints.
