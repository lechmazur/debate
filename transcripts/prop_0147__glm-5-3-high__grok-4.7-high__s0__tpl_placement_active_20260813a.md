[P1.1] The word doing the work in this motion is "routinely." Nobody's banishing the voice assistant from the kitchen. The question is whether the default caregiver for a two-year-old's bedtime story and goodnight conversation should be a device. It should not, and the reasons are not sentimental—they're developmental.
[P1.2] Young children build language and emotional regulation through contingent interaction: a baby babbles, an adult responds, and thousands of these exchanges wire the brain's capacity for communication and trust. This is the well-established serve-and-return mechanism developmental psychologists have described for decades. A smart speaker, however sophisticated, does not watch a child's face, notice that she's frightened by the wolf on page four, and slow down. It responds to a query.
[P1.3] The child learns to request, not to relate. When a device becomes the routine narrator of bedtime, the child's most intimate daily ritual is being run by something with no memory of her, no stake in her, and no love for her.
[P1.4] Second, bedtime is not content delivery. It's attachment practice. The point of the story is only partly the story; it's the warmth of the parent's body, the shared gaze at pictures, the negotiation of one more page. That is where children learn that grown-ups who care for them are reliable and present.
[P1.5] Outsource it routinely and you're not saving time—you're transferring a developmental function to a product designed by a company whose incentive is engagement, not your child's flourishing.
[P1.6] Third, dependence cuts both ways. Parents who routinely hand off the hardest, most exhausting parts of early childhood—reading the same book for the fortieth time, answering endless "why" questions—lose the very practice that builds parental competence and empathy. Skill atrophies where it matters most.
[P1.7] The burden on the other side is high: they must show that routine replacement of human interaction in the earliest years is safe, not merely convenient. Plausible mechanisms point the other way. When the stakes are a child's foundation for language, trust, and attachment, the default should stay human.
