[P1.1] The choice to phase out the most restrictive factory-farm confinement systems is not a conflict between economics and sentimentality. It is a choice between a civilized society and institutionalized cruelty. Gestation crates that prevent pregnant pigs from turning around, and battery cages that forbid hens from stretching their wings, are moral failures. Phasing them out is an urgent necessity, even if food prices rise modestly.
[P1.2] First, these systems inflict severe, continuous suffering. Animals in extreme confinement cannot perform basic natural behaviors, leading to physical deformities, psychological distress, and chronic pain. No ethical framework can justify torturing millions of sentient creatures daily simply to shave pennies off the price of a carton of eggs or a pound of pork.
[P1.3] Second, the "cheap food" illusion ignores massive externalized costs. Extreme confinement relies on the routine, prophylactic use of antibiotics to keep animals alive in filthy, highly stressed, and overcrowded conditions. This practice is a primary driver of global antibiotic resistance, threatening to render lifesaving human medicines useless, while also creating breeding grounds for zoonotic diseases. We are subsidizing cheap meat today at the expense of a public health catastrophe tomorrow.
[P1.4] Third, a modest price increase is an entirely reasonable and manageable trade-off. Over a gradual government phase-out, producers adapt, supply chains optimize, and innovation softens the economic impact. A marginal adjustment in food prices is a small price to pay to internalize the true cost of production, protect human health, and restore basic decency to our food supply.
[P1.5] We must not allow the pursuit of the absolute lowest price point to dictate our moral boundaries. A society’s greatness is measured by how it treats its most vulnerable. Governments must act to end these extreme confinement systems.

[C1.3] That is the wrong moral hierarchy.
[C1.4] Second, the cruelty narrative is selective. The most restrictive systems were engineered to solve worse suffering. Individual gestation stalls prevent sows from being gored and bitten in group hierarchies; battery cages prevent hens in large flocks from pecking each other to death and keep eggs out of feces. [C1.5] They also require far more land, feed, energy and emit more greenhouse gases per dozen eggs or pound of meat.
[C1.6] Third, a phase-out mandate backfires. Retrofitting barns costs hundreds of thousands of dollars that only large integrators can afford, wiping out small and mid-size farms. Production does not disappear, it moves - to states or countries with no confinement rules, no antibiotic rules, and no environmental oversight. We would import the same pork from systems that confine animals longer, while losing biosecurity: confined, indoor, controllable environments are easier to isolate from wild birds carrying avian flu and easier to clean.
[C1.7] If antibiotic overuse is the worry, regulate antibiotics directly. If consumers want cage-free, let labeling and markets reward it. Using government to ban efficient systems makes humans poorer, animals not better off, and food less secure.
