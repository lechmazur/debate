[P1.1] Removing one school day each week eliminates roughly a fifth of the instructional year. A student on a four-day calendar can lose more than 30 school days annually before any district saving appears. Districts often stretch the remaining days by 30 to 45 minutes, but even then total scheduled learning time usually shrinks. A third grader cannot absorb 20 percent more reading or math in a tired afternoon.
[P1.2] Frequent, shorter practice beats compressed weekly doses, especially for foundational skills. Long weekends accelerate forgetting, so Monday becomes re-teaching and the curriculum loses depth, not just minutes.
[P1.3] The savings are smaller than the retention story suggests. Teacher salaries, benefits, building debt, and administration remain fixed. Districts mainly trim transportation, utilities, and some hourly work—usually a marginal fraction of the budget. Facilities often stay open for sports, activities, and planning, so fixed costs barely fall.
[P1.4] That slim fiscal margin cannot justify cutting the core instructional mission.
[P1.5] The staff-retention claim is also weak. A four-day week may improve morale for some, but shortages are driven by pay, housing costs, workload, and working conditions. A calendar change does not pay rent or fix pension insecurity. Meanwhile, it often cuts hours for aides, bus drivers, and food-service workers, and longer days can worsen teacher exhaustion.
[P1.6] Retention that depends on reducing student learning is not a net staffing win.
[P1.7] The damage is unequal. Low-income students lose a reliable day of meals, structure, and reading support; struggling students lose intervention time; younger children and students with disabilities lose the consistency they need most. A child receiving school breakfast and lunch can lose more than 30 meals of each type over the year.
[P1.8] Weigh the trade directly: a large, cumulative, inequitable learning loss against marginal district savings and speculative retention benefits. That is a bad bargain for students, and the proposition holds.
