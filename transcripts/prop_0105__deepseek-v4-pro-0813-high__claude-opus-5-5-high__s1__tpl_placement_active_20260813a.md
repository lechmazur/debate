[P1.1] The central question is whether a court may act on credible evidence of imminent danger, or must wait until someone has already been convicted—by which point the threatened harm may be irreversible. Red-flag laws choose the only workable answer: a temporary, judicially supervised removal of firearms while risk is assessed.
[P1.2] This is not punishment and it is not permanent. An emergency order typically lasts days or weeks, followed by a full hearing where the gun owner can contest the evidence, cross-examine witnesses, and regain the firearms if the state fails to meet its burden. The standard is not rumor or dislike; it is credible evidence of imminent risk, the same kind of dangerousness inquiry courts already use for domestic violence protective orders, civil commitment, and pretrial detention. If a judge can order a person held in jail before trial because they pose a danger, a far more intrusive deprivation, then temporarily removing firearms is plainly within constitutional bounds.
[P1.3] The opponent’s insistence on a criminal conviction fails exactly when the risk is most acute. A suicidal person does not get convicted before pulling the trigger; a domestic abuser often escalates in the weeks before trial; a mass shooter may have no disqualifying record but has told others of violent plans. Waiting for conviction means waiting for the body count. A temporary removal can be reversed; a death cannot.
[P1.4] That asymmetry should decide the case.
[P1.5] The process is not a blank check. Red-flag orders require sworn statements, judicial findings, and a prompt post-deprivation hearing. Abuses can be corrected and firearms returned. But the cost of error on the other side is final.
[P1.6] Courts should be able to act on credible evidence of imminent risk before a conviction, because the purpose of law is to prevent predictable violence, not merely to punish it after the fact.
