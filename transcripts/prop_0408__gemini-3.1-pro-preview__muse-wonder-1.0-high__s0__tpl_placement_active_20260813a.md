[P1.1] When a student turns eighteen, society immediately grants them the power to sign legally binding contracts, accumulate high-interest credit card debt, and take on tens of thousands of dollars in student loans. Yet, our current education system expects them to navigate these life-altering financial realities through pure osmosis. By leaving financial literacy to elective exposure or family background, we are setting up millions of young adults for avoidable economic hardship. Schools must require a serious financial-literacy course for graduation because managing money is an inescapable, universal reality of adult life.
[P1.2] First, leaving financial education to families actively perpetuates generational poverty and inequality. If a student's parents are unbanked, struggle with debt, or fall victim to predatory lending, that student inherits a deficit of knowledge. Wealthier families can pass down strategies for investing, building credit, and tax planning; marginalized students are left to learn by making costly mistakes. A mandatory financial literacy course acts as a great equalizer, ensuring that every student, regardless of their background, possesses the fundamental tools to build economic stability.
[P1.3] Second, an elective model fails those who need it most. Students who are unaware of the dangers of compound interest or the intricacies of student loan terms are precisely the ones least likely to voluntarily select a financial literacy elective over a study hall. A graduation requirement recognizes that basic financial competence is not a niche interest—it is a core survival skill.
[P1.4] Finally, the justification for any core curriculum is to prepare students for functional participation in society. We mandate algebra, history, and physical education because we deem them essential. It is indefensible to claim that calculating the trajectory of a parabola is a mandatory graduation requirement, while understanding a credit score, a lease agreement, or a W-2 is not. Equipping students with the ability to manage their finances protects them from predatory forces and empowers them to make sound decisions.
[P1.5] We must make financial literacy a guaranteed foundation, not a privilege of circumstance.

[C1.1] Nobody graduates ready for adulthood by learning about compound interest at sixteen and forgetting it by twenty-two. The proposal mistakes an important topic for an effective graduation requirement, and it will pay for that mistake with scarcer instructional time, weaker learning, and a new barrier for the students it claims to help.
[C1.2] First, a serious course is not free. A semester covering debt, taxes, budgeting, credit, investing, and insurance must displace something else — remedial math, literacy, science, computer science, or a career pathway that actually raises earnings. Schools already struggle to get students proficient in reading and math, the very skills needed to read a loan agreement. Forcing a trade against those foundations, or against electives students choose because they connect to jobs and college, makes graduates poorer in the name of teaching them about money.
[C1.3] Second, timing undermines retention and behavior. Most teenagers have no mortgage, no tax complexity beyond a part-time W-2, no retirement account, and no real control over household budgeting. Teach APR formulas years before they sign for a car loan, and the lesson becomes another test to cram and discard. Financial mistakes are rarely caused by never having heard of interest; they are driven by low income, emergencies, marketing, and impulse in the moment.
[C1.4] What changes outcomes is just-in-time help — guidance when completing aid forms, starting a first job, or opening a first account — not a universal course delivered years too early.
[C1.5] Finally, a mandate guarantees compliance, not competence. Under-resourced schools will assign the course to whoever has a free period, buy a canned curriculum, and pass students through worksheets, while affluent schools offer stronger versions. Add it to graduation and the weakest version becomes one more hurdle that can delay diplomas for mobile, working, and struggling students.
[C1.6] Keep strong electives, embed practical numeracy in math, and target help at real decisions. Do not turn good intentions into a compulsory checkbox.
