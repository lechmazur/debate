[P1.1] The proposition is not about reopening every historical wound. It sets a narrow, demanding rule: when public institutions still hold, use, or profit from land historically seized from Indigenous communities, the state must offer land return, long-term revenue sharing, or equivalent restitution.
[P1.2] The decisive issue is ongoing benefit, not ancient grievance. Many public universities, parks, forests, and resource portfolios were built on dispossessed Indigenous land. In the United States, land-grant universities were famously financed through sales of Indigenous territories; public parks and timber or mineral estates often rest on lands from which Indigenous peoples were removed. If a museum still displays stolen art, we do not say the theft is too old to matter.
[P1.3] We require return, compensation, or an agreed equivalent. The same principle should govern public land.
[P1.4] This follows from the state’s own claim to legitimacy. Governments punish theft, enforce contracts, and ask citizens to respect public property. They cannot coherently retain assets acquired through the very kind of dispossession they now condemn. Restitution is not charity; it is the correction of unjust enrichment.
[P1.5] It also gives public institutions a cleaner foundation: they can continue serving education, conservation, or public use without building that service on an unremedied wrong.
[P1.6] The proposition is also practical because it supplies a menu of remedies. Where return is workable, transfer land or restore co-management. Where return would genuinely cripple essential public services, use long-term revenue sharing, trust funds, scholarships, housing land, or alternative land transfers. That flexibility answers cost and disruption concerns without letting the state keep the benefits of seizure.
[P1.7] CON may argue that history is complicated or that budgets are strained. But those objections concern the form of restitution, not whether the obligation exists. The clash is simple: CON must defend the state’s right to keep gains from dispossession. If the public still benefits from taken land, justice requires a remedy.
[P1.8] Vote PRO.

[C1.2] The proposition fails on three distinct grounds: arbitrariness, institutional paralysis, and the historical reality of land claims.
[C1.3] First, tying restitution to whether a public institution currently benefits creates an indefensible moral lottery. Indigenous communities dispossessed by private real estate empires, industrial mining, or urban sprawl receive zero restitution under this rule, while those whose ancestral lands became a state college or park receive perpetual windfalls. True state obligations to Indigenous sovereignty, healthcare, and economic development must be universal and needs-based, funded through democratic taxation—not dictated by the historical accident of where a state campus was built.
[C1.4] Second, the proposed remedies cannibalize public missions. Public universities and conservation systems operate on constrained margins to preserve common access. Forcing them to alienate land or divert operating revenues into perpetual sharing agreements inevitably drives up tuition, invites privatization, or degrades the stewardship of vital ecological reserves. Depleting the commons harms all citizens to deliver targeted remedies.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO treats centuries of complex territorial history as a simple bilateral transaction. In reality, ancestral lands were subject to successive layers of migration, overlapping use, and inter-tribal conquest long before state acquisition. Attempting to assign exclusive property rights or perpetual revenues today invites intractable conflicts over competing tribal claims, turning public land into an administrative battlefield.
[C1.6] The state’s duty to Indigenous nations is real, but it is fulfilled through sovereign self-governance, treaty trust obligations, and broad social equity—not by destabilizing the shared civic institutions that anchor public life.
