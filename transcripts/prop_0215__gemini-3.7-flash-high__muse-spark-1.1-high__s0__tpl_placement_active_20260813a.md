[P1.1] When institutions actively administer systems of profound human abuse, their obligation does not expire with the passage of time, nor does it dissolve into symbolic apologies. Institutional responsibility demands material repair.
[P1.2] Across residential school networks in Canada, boarding schools in the United States, and coercive assimilation institutions globally, religious orders and former state churches were not passive bystanders. They managed daily life, enforced the systematic suppression of Indigenous languages and cultures, and presided over environments rife with severe neglect and abuse. In doing so, these institutions secured state subsidies, land grants, and immense social authority—benefits and institutional continuity that extend directly into the present day.
[P1.3] Exempting these institutions from contributing to reparative funds creates an intolerable moral hazard. It suggests that enduring religious bodies can claim continuity when inheriting vast property portfolios, historical prestige, and legal protections, yet sever that same continuity the moment past liabilities come due. The motion does not demand blanket financial penalties against faith communities broadly; it requires contributions specifically from those bodies that played central, demonstrable roles in administrative abuses. Where institutional power was leveraged to execute state-sanctioned harm, institutional resources must now be leveraged to heal it.
[P1.5] Justice is not merely declarative; it is reparative. When an enduring organization participates centrally in the devastation of entire generations, true reconciliation requires that it contribute tangibly to their restoration.
