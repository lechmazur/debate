[C1.1] The motion gets the sequence wrong. In the years after civil war, a unified "civic account" is rarely a spontaneous consensus; it is the version written by whichever group controls the ministries, the archives, and the public square. That makes the shared memorial a victor's plaque, not common ground. Imposing it while distrust is still raw does not stop symbolic refighting—it moves it underground and gives it a government-funded target.
[C1.2] The motion's deeper flaw is treating separate narratives as division. A community that mourns its dead in its own idioms is not necessarily refusing a common future; it is doing the recognition necessary to enter one. When an official memorial demands that this grief be folded into a single story, many survivors hear erasure, not reconciliation. The result is a second injury: the state again tells one group that its experience, names, and moral account do not fit the public record.
[C1.3] Separate narratives can coexist with minimal legal limits: prohibition of genocide denial, incitement, and celebratory monuments to perpetrators. The real difficulty is not the existence of multiple memories, but the state's refusal to enforce those limits. Northern Ireland's parallel commemorative practices accompanied a durable political settlement; memory remained contested, but political violence declined because disagreement was managed rather than suppressed. Rwanda's centralized memorialization, by contrast, shows how a single official memory can become an instrument of selective mourning and political control.
[C1.4] The relevant test is whether memorials reduce the risk of renewed violence. A forced shared account fails because it asks post-war institutions to arbitrate what they are least trusted to arbitrate. Preserving separate narratives, while denying public honor to denial and atrocity, is the better route. Let the memories compete openly.
[C1.5] The state should not have to pretend they are one.
