[P1.1] A shopper comparing two boxes of cereal can see the headline price instantly, but a reduction from 500 to 450 grams is easy to miss. If the price stays at $5, the cost per kilogram rises by about 11%. Nothing fraudulent need have occurred, yet the shelf presents a stable price while hiding a real price increase. Requiring clear shrinkflation and unit-price-change displays corrects that information gap.
[P1.2] This is not a ban on smaller packages, a price control, or a demand that every redesign be justified. The requirement is narrower: when the old and new sizes create a meaningful unit-price change, show the change prominently beside the product online and on the shelf, using the previous size, new size, and comparable unit price. Current unit prices cannot tell shoppers that the product they habitually buy has quietly become more expensive.
[P1.3] The stakes are practical. Food budgets are managed through repetition, not fresh spreadsheet analysis on every trip. Smaller print, changed package shapes, and scrolling app interfaces make mental comparison especially unreliable. Clear disclosure protects people with tight budgets, limited time, or weaker eyesight without requiring them to memorize last month's weights.
[P1.4] It also improves competition. If firms can raise effective prices less visibly than headline prices, they face a reward for obscurity rather than efficiency. Transparent changes let consumers switch, accept the new value, or choose a genuinely cheaper alternative. Retailers already manage prices, product records, and package data; a targeted notice is a modest extension of systems used to sell the same goods.
[P1.5] Any clutter or administrative cost must be weighed against systematic consumer confusion across millions of routine purchases. Accurate labels are not enough when the commercially decisive fact remains functionally invisible. Visible unit-price changes make the posted price honest in practice, not merely in arithmetic.

[C1.1] PRO's own cereal example defeats PRO. Five dollars for 500 grams is ten dollars a kilo; five dollars for 450 grams is about eleven. The unit price already rises, automatically, on every product, with no shopper memorising anything. That is precisely what unit pricing exists to do: cancel out package size so a tired parent can compare cereals per kilo, today, across the shelf.
[C1.2] The claim that current unit prices leave shrinkflation invisible is wrong.
[C1.3] So the mandate's real content is a history lesson: a flag saying this used to be bigger. That needs a baseline, and baselines are where it collapses. Previous size as of when — last month, last year, before the bonus pack, before the recipe change? Retailers don't choose these weights; manufacturers do, variant by variant, market by market.
[C1.4] Apps list third-party sellers who never stocked the old size. A rule with a fuzzy reference point is an audit trap and an invitation to game: relaunch as a new line, or trim just under the "meaningful" threshold, and the notice disappears. Honest continuity gets punished; churn gets rewarded.
[C1.5] Then the perverse incentive. A smaller pack at a familiar price is often the option shoppers prefer to a headline increase; the mandate turns it into a scarlet letter. Firms will take the headline rise instead, and nobody's grocery bill improves. Meanwhile, in a period of broad cost inflation, these flags land on a large share of shelf edges until shoppers stop seeing them — burying the one clean, comparable number that actually helps.
[C1.6] The marginal benefit is near zero because the decisive number is already posted. The costs — arbitrary baselines, engineered relaunches, louder shelves, dearer headline prices — are not. Make unit prices legible and identical online; don't mandate a second label that manufactures noise.

[P2.3] Where an app seller lacks a predecessor, the rule can distinguish “no verified history” from “no change,” not fabricate one. [P2.4] The “scarlet letter” argument concedes that disclosure changes behaviour. That is the point. Shoppers who genuinely prefer a smaller pack at $5 can still choose it after seeing the change. [P2.5] A firm choosing a visible headline increase instead does not show transparency’s harm; it shows the same effective increase becoming contestable through switching and competition.
[P2.6] Finally, a temporary notice tied to size reductions does not permanently plaster every shelf. Better current unit prices are compatible with this motion and insufficient alone.
