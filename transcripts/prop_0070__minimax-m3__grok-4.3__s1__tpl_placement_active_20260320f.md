[P1.1] The motion is precise: it calls for mandatory *planning*, not mandatory movement. That distinction matters, because opponents will try to blur it. A city can require that long-term retreat be mapped, funded, and sequenced without forcing a single resident out the door tomorrow. What climate-vulnerable cities are currently doing is nothing — while rebuilding schools, roads, and sewage plants in places that will be underwater within their bond maturity.
[P1.2] The case for proactive planning rests on three pillars: cost, chaos, and lock-in.
[P1.3] First, cost. Pre-disaster mitigation is well understood to save several dollars in future recovery for every dollar spent, and relocation planning is the highest-leverage form of mitigation because it ends the cycle of rebuilding doomed assets. A house raised on stilts in a floodplain that floods every three years is not a recovery; it is a deposit on the next disaster. Planned retreat converts recurring losses into a one-time capital cost.
[P1.4] Second, chaos. When retreat is forced by catastrophe, it happens with no housing waiting, no schools transferred, no jobs relocated, and no social networks preserved. We have seen this in unincorporated neighborhoods around Houston after Hurricane Harvey, and on Louisiana's Isle de Jean Charles, where a federally funded managed relocation came only after a decade of compounding displacement and community dissolution. Planning buys the time to do it with dignity.
[P1.5] Third, lock-in. Every paving project, sewer trunk, and 30-year municipal bond issued in a climate-vulnerable zone is a commitment to stay. Without a planning mandate, the default is always rebuild. Mandatory planning breaks that default and forces the honest conversation about which assets are worth defending and which are not.
[P1.6] The alternative — wait until retreat is unavoidable — is not neutrality. It is a choice to let the disaster make the decision, at far higher human and fiscal cost. That is the status quo, and the motion rightly names it: a slow-motion emergency dressed up as local control.

[C1.1] Mandatory planning forces cities to label neighborhoods as future sacrifice zones years ahead of need. That label immediately depresses property values, deters private investment, and accelerates out-migration by residents who still have time and resources to leave on their own terms. The result is a self-reinforcing decline that arrives before any disaster, not after.
[C1.2] Local governments already hold the legal tools to restrict new construction in the most exposed sites through zoning and insurance requirements. A blanket mandate overrides those targeted decisions with one-size-fits-all timelines that ignore differences in topography, economic base, and political will. Once the plan exists on paper, state and federal agencies treat it as a signal to withhold infrastructure funds, further starving the very places the plan claims to manage.
[C1.3] Adaptation measures such as elevated infrastructure, restored wetlands, and updated building codes have repeatedly extended the usable life of vulnerable districts without requiring mass relocation. These steps preserve tax bases and social continuity while actual climate impacts remain uncertain in both timing and severity. Mandatory retreat planning treats every remaining year as wasted rather than potentially productive.
[C1.4] Communities facing repeated flooding still retain the option to negotiate buyouts parcel by parcel when residents are ready. Imposing a city-wide schedule removes that consent and replaces it with administrative deadlines that favor planners over the people whose lives are being sequenced. The motion therefore trades voluntary, incremental adjustment for top-down disruption that creates new costs and conflicts of its own.
