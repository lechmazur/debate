[P1.1] A family tablet sits on the kitchen counter, logged into a parent's account. A nine-year-old opens the app store for a drawing game and sees slots offering free coins, an app promising anonymous chat with strangers, and suggestive thumbnails next to cartoons. Nothing has been downloaded yet. That is the point: today protection means hunting through nested menus, building separate child accounts that collapse on a shared device, and redoing it after updates.
[P1.2] The requirement is modest: app stores must offer a simple, visible, PIN-protected way for the owner to block or hide gambling, pornographic, and high-risk social apps. It is not a ban, not removal from the store, and not a lock on an adult's private choices. An adult can enable it, disable it, or override it. Every app stays available.
[P1.3] What changes is who bears the friction — the child stumbling into an adult storefront, or the store that already tags these apps by age and content.
[P1.4] Those categories pose distinct, foreseeable harms. Gambling apps rehearse loss-chasing with free chips, near-misses, and push offers before children grasp that money is real. Pornographic apps are built for compulsive browsing. High-risk social apps — anonymous, ephemeral, stranger-matching by design — enable grooming and sextortion parents cannot review later because messages vanish.
[P1.5] Shared devices intensify all of it, because the child browses under an adult login with payment methods attached.
[P1.6] Voluntary controls have had years and produced fragmentation: different labels, buried settings, filters that block installs but not listings, search results, or recommendations. Stores profit from discoverability and have little incentive to make profitable categories easy to hide. A uniform simple requirement fixes that incentive without dictating family choices. It standardizes the dashboard, like a clear seatbelt buckle, and leaves parents to decide whether to use it.
[P1.7] Children should not need luck, and parents should not need technical expertise, to make a shared screen safe.

[C1.1] This proposal trades vague, unaccountable government power for a supposed fix that ignores the messy reality of shared family life. First, the categories the state would force app stores to flag—gambling, pornographic, “high-risk social”—are not objective, nor are they static. “High-risk social” could easily be stretched to block apps used by marginalized groups for mutual aid, LGBTQ+ youth support networks, or even labor organizing tools, depending on which partisan officials draw the lines. There is no guardrail in this proposal to stop that censorship creep, and no process for families to appeal misclassified apps.
[C1.2] Second, shared devices are not exclusively used by children. A teen accessing sexual health resources, a survivor of domestic violence using anonymous social apps to connect to support services, or an adult with a responsible recreational gambling habit would all have their access dictated by whoever holds the PIN—often a parent, but in abusive households, a controlling partner. This feature does not just protect kids; it gives abusers a new, state-sanctioned tool to isolate vulnerable people in their own homes, with no oversight to stop misuse.
[C1.3] Third, the claim that this is a “simple” toggle ignores that existing parental controls already offer this functionality for families that want it. The problem is not a lack of tools, but uneven awareness and implementation. A blanket mandate forces every family to conform to a single state-designed standard, removing flexibility for households with different needs, values, and risk tolerances. It also sets a dangerous precedent for the government to dictate what content is acceptable on private platforms, opening the door to far more invasive content mandates down the line, from blocking reproductive health apps to censoring political dissent under the guise of family safety.
