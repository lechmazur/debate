[P1.1] When a hospital chain buys a rival, the headline is higher patient bills. But the quieter damage is the wage squeeze on nurses, technicians, and support staff. In a labor market with few employers, a merger creates monopsony power that depresses wages, cuts hours, and erodes benefits—harm that the current consumer‑price lens simply does not see. Yet wages are the largest component of household income, and when they fall, consumer demand falls, eroding the very market that antitrust is supposed to protect.
[P1.2] Treating labor‑market monopsony as seriously as consumer‑price effects is not a radical addition; it is a correction of a blind spot that has allowed mergers to pass review while silently impoverishing workers. Economic theory shows that monopsony reduces output and welfare just as monopoly does, but with a twist: the loss is borne by workers who are also consumers, amplifying the damage. Empirical work on hospital and retail mergers finds wage drops of several percent—comparable to or exceeding the price increases that trigger scrutiny.
[P1.3] For instance, when a dominant retailer acquires a regional chain, local workers lose bargaining leverage and wages can dip measurably within a year.
[P1.4] If competition policy continues to ignore labor effects, it will systematically underestimate total welfare loss and will legitimize deals that concentrate buying power over labor. The tools already exist: labor‑market concentration metrics, counterfactual wage analysis, and the same Herfindahl‑Hirschman thresholds used for product markets can be adapted. The question is not whether we can measure labor monopsony, but whether we will give it equal weight. Giving it equal weight restores the original consumer‑welfare goal, because a worker who earns less is a consumer who buys less.
[P1.5] To protect competition we must protect the people who supply the labor that makes markets work.

[C1.1] Labor monopsony belongs in merger review; equal footing with consumer prices does not. The proposition mistakes sensible expansion of evidence for a categorical rule that would distort decisions. Consumer-price and labor-market effects are often two sides of the same distortion, not independent harms to count with equal weight.
[C1.2] Consider a merger that lets the combined firm replace an inefficient supplier, raise wages because labor is scarce, and lower its product’s price. Consumers lose through the price increase, but workers gain and output expands. By contrast, a price increase accompanied by lower wages may redistribute surplus rather than reveal an independent harm. What matters is the causal, welfare-weighted change from the merger counterfactual, including employment, quality, productivity, and consumer welfare.
[C1.3] The empirical premise is also less settled than [P1.2] suggests. Studies differ by industry and often find heterogeneous effects, not a stable wage penalty attached to every merger. A firm’s wage share can fall because it hired lower-skilled workers, adopted different technology, or operates where labor supply is unusually elastic. None proves that the merger reduced bargaining power.
[C1.4] Establishing monopsony requires a labor-market counterfactual: geography, occupational switching, substitute employers, and labor-supply effects.
[C1.5] Nor are concentration thresholds portable. A product-market share may be meaningful within a local catchment; an employer can compete nationally for scarce skills while looking dominant locally. A local employer share may also reflect skill specialization rather than monopsony power. Copying product-market HHI thresholds into labor markets would manufacture precision without validity.
[C1.6] Competition policy should consider labor monopsony explicitly and use labor-specific evidence. But it should not give it automatic parity with price effects or import consumer-market metrics unchanged. A merger should be challenged because of its total competitive harm—not because two distinct-looking indicators crossed supposedly equal seriousness lines.

[P2.2] Weighting labor effects equally does not distort decisions—it corrects a distortion that already exists by omission.
[P2.3] On empirical uncertainty, the opponent conflates heterogeneity with irrelevance. Yes, not every merger depresses wages. But the same variability plagues product-market price prediction, and we do not discard consumer-price analysis because results differ across industries. The appropriate response is better data, not inaction.
[P2.4] Several percent wage drops in concentrated labor markets are well-documented patterns, not outlier findings, and dismissing them because some mergers show no effect is like rejecting antitrust scrutiny because some mergers are benign.
[P2.5] The counterfactual objection is a methodological hurdle, not a principled bar. Product-market counterfactuals require defining relevant markets, substitutability, and geographic scope—problems antitrust agencies already navigate. [P2.6] Finally, the claim that local concentration reflects skill specialization rather than power misunderstands how monopsony operates: a hospital system dominating a regional nursing market exercises buying power regardless of whether nurses could theoretically relocate. Treating labor monopsony with parity restores the consumer-welfare standard the opponent claims to protect, because workers are consumers too.

A merger may lower wages because it removes bargaining power, but it may also change staffing, skill mix, scheduling, or productivity. If lower labor costs subsequently produce lower prices, the merger may transfer surplus from workers to consumers rather than create two distinct welfare losses.
[C2.3] [P2.5] therefore answers the wrong objection. Yes, agencies can define labor markets and evaluate employer alternatives. The problem is not categorical methodological difficulty; it is that labor concentration and wage effects often have different causal meanings from product concentration and price effects. [C2.4] Equal treatment could double-count that harm unless the causal chain is disciplined.
[C2.5] Nor does [P2.6] establish monopsony by showing that a hospital dominates a local nursing market. Dominance may reflect specialization, while actual constraints include commuting distance, occupational licensing, childcare, and the limited number of suitable employers. The relevant alternative is not wherever a nurse could theoretically relocate on paper.
[C2.6] Labor monopsony plainly belongs in merger review. But “as seriously as” consumer-price effects imports a false equivalence between a direct market-output measure and a multifaceted input-price outcome. Competition policy should weigh demonstrated causal harm on its merits, not give labor effects automatic parity by analogy.
