[P1.1] A worker’s right to remain should not be one employer’s private property. That is the kafala defect, and portable, worker-held visas are the correction.
[P1.2] Under sponsorship, the employer does not merely hire. He controls legal presence. Quit, and the visa can collapse. Complain, and deportation becomes leverage.
[P1.3] Unpaid wages, confiscated passports, and unsafe housing persist less from regulatory indifference than from blocked exit. A market the weaker party cannot leave is not a labor market. It is a captive one.
[P1.4] Portability reverses the incentive. If a domestic worker, builder, or nurse can change jobs without a no-objection certificate, the firm that underpays loses that worker to one that will not. Competition, not appeals to goodwill, disciplines the worst employers. Notice periods, contract enforcement, and narrow limits in genuine security roles can sit on top of portability.

[P1.8] Partial Gulf reforms already concede the principle: easier exits, job changes after a waiting period or with ministry approval. Those half-measures admit that absolute sponsor control is neither moral nor administrable. They also expose the remaining flaw. [C1.1] The proposition asks you to gamble the governance of the largest guest-worker populations on Earth on a theory. The theory is elegant. The gamble is reckless.
[C1.2] Start with what the kafala system actually does beneath its abuses: it makes migration self-limiting and tied to demand. A worker enters because a specific employer demonstrated a need, pays the costs, and absorbs the risk that the job ends. Portable, worker-held visas sever that link. A worker becomes a free-floating resident whose right to stay no longer depends on any job.
[C1.3] In economies where migrants already vastly outnumber citizens — in Qatar and the UAE, by several times over — that is not a labor-market tweak. It is a quiet rewrite of who the population is, decided by migration flows rather than by any legislature.
[C1.4] Second, the employer's investment is real. Recruitment fees, visas, training, and setup costs are front-loaded and substantial. If a worker can walk to a competitor the week after arrival, rational employers stop investing at all: they poach ready-trained labor and hire no one fresh. The entry pipeline that supplies PRO's cherished mobility dries up, and the workers who most need access to the Gulf — new migrants — lose it.
[C1.5] Third, portability does not abolish exploitation; it relocates it. Where similar reforms have been tried elsewhere, the pathology that follows is visa-selling, ghost sponsorship, and informal middlemen charging workers for the "right" to a portable permit. Workers now carry the fee burden the employer once carried. That is not liberation; it is the recruitment-debt problem PRO claims to be solving, with worse interest.
[C1.6] Finally, the abuses PRO cites — confiscated passports, unpaid wages — are already illegal. They are enforcement failures, and enforcement is what the Gulf states are actually reforming: wage protection systems, exit-right guarantees, ministry-approved transfers. You do not fix enforcement by demolishing the migration-control architecture around it.
[C1.7] The choice is not captive labor versus freedom. It is targeted reform versus an experiment these states cannot run in reverse.
