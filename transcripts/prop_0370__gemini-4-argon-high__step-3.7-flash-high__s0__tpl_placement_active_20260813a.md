[P1.1] A personal smartphone is private property, bought and maintained with a worker’s post-tax wages. When a large employer makes installing corporate messaging or tracking software a routine condition of getting or keeping a job, it annexes private property into corporate infrastructure. If real-time messaging or GPS tracking is genuinely necessary to perform a role, a large corporation has the capital and procurement scale to provide a company device, a dedicated scanner, or an on-site terminal. Shifting hardware wear, battery degradation, and data costs onto employees—especially hourly retail, delivery, or service workers—forces staff to subsidize their employer's baseline operating expenses.
[P1.2] Second, a personal phone holds the entirety of a person’s private life: banking, medical portals, personal photos, and private conversations. Work tracking and enterprise messaging apps routinely require invasive device permissions, background telemetry, or mobile device management profiles. Even when employers promise tracking only occurs on the clock, background processes and permission creep regularly expose off-duty movements and network metadata. Worse, mixing corporate communications with personal hardware exposes a worker’s private device to legal discovery or inspection during company disputes, and often requires granting corporate IT departments remote-wipe authority that can obliterate personal data when an employee leaves.
[P1.3] Finally, mandatory apps on personal phones dissolve the boundary between paid labor and free time. When work channels sit on the same home screen as family messages, off-the-clock pings create constant pressure to read updates, coordinate schedules, and answer quick questions without pay. Notice the exact scope of this proposition: it does not prohibit truly voluntary opt-ins for workers who genuinely prefer carrying a single device, provided a penalty-free, employer-supplied alternative exists. It simply establishes a fair baseline for large enterprises: workers sell their labor, not permanent corporate access to their personal property.
[P1.4] If a digital tether is required to do the job, the employer must supply the hardware.
