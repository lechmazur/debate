[P1.1] Climate targets are not aspirational decorations—they are physical deadlines. The EU and UK have committed to net-zero trajectories that require doubling or tripling renewable generation capacity within a generation. Onshore wind is the cheapest, fastest-to-deploy utility-scale renewable available. Grid expansion is the connective tissue that makes any of it usable.
[P1.2] Both are being strangled by planning regimes that give small local constituencies effective veto power over infrastructure whose benefits are national and whose urgency is existential.
[P1.3] The core issue is democratic accountability, not its absence. National governments set climate targets after broad electoral mandates. They are then unable to meet those targets because a handful of objectors at the local level can delay projects for a decade or kill them outright. This is not local democracy in any meaningful sense—it is a structural veto by the most affected few over the least affected many.
[P1.4] Polling consistently shows strong majority support for onshore wind across Europe. The opposition that actually blocks projects is concentrated, organized, and disproportionately loud. Treating it as the authentic local voice ignores everyone who did not show up to a town hall meeting.
[P1.5] Consider grid infrastructure specifically. A single jurisdiction along a transmission route can block a line serving millions. There is no principled theory of consent under which one municipality should hold that power. The costs of grid congestion—curtailed renewables, reliance on gas peaker plants, higher consumer prices—are paid by people who never had a say in the objection.
[P1.6] Relaxing restrictions does not mean eliminating environmental review or community input. It means setting firm timelines, raising approval thresholds for rejection, and designating infrastructure of strategic importance so that national need is not permanently subordinate to local preference. The alternative is climate policy that everyone votes for and no one can build. That is not a serious position, and it is not one the EU or UK can afford.
