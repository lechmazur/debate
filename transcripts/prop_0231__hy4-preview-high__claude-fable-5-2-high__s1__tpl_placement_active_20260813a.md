[P1.1] Picture a doorway in Barcelona and a stairwell in Mexico City: people injecting or smoking stimulants in public, syringes and glass pipes on the pavement, police moving the group two blocks away before it reassembles. European and Latin American cities should permit regulated supervised consumption sites because they replace that cycle with a licensed room where consumption happens under medical watch. Regulation means trained staff, sterile equipment, oxygen and naloxone, rules against dealing and violence, and on-site referrals to housing and treatment.
[P1.2] This is not endorsement; it is management.
[P1.3] Overdose deaths fall mechanically. On the street, a witnessed overdose often becomes a delay, a panic, or a fear of arrest; in an alley, the user may simply be found dead. Inside a supervised room, the same collapse is met with monitoring, oxygen, and naloxone in minutes, producing survival and a health referral. European cities from Zurich and Frankfurt to Amsterdam, Copenhagen, Barcelona, and Paris have run such rooms, some for decades, and deaths on site are rare because reversal is immediate.
[P1.4] The model is adaptable, not foreign: in Latin American cities where smoked cocaine or bazuco causes the harm, the site can supervise inhalation as well as injection.
[P1.5] The public-order gain is equally direct. Today, police sweeps push users from one plaza to the next, scattering needles, pipes, and confrontations into parks, school routes, and residential blocks. A fixed licensed site pulls the most chaotic consumption indoors under rules against dealing and violence, cuts litter in public space, and frees police from repeating low-level arrests. Critics fear these sites attract crime or normalize drugs, but they serve people already dependent and the illicit market exists regardless; permission does not create demand, it relocates an existing activity into professional supervision.
[P1.6] The choice is not compassion versus order. It is supervised, indoor order versus repeated, fatal, outdoor disorder—and regulated sites reduce both deaths and public chaos.

[C1.2] **The arithmetic of death.** A dependent user consumes several times a day, every day, mostly at home, alone, or wherever the drug was just bought. A site with a dozen booths, limited hours and a walkable catchment supervises a sliver of those episodes. "Deaths on site are rare" [P1.3] tells you about the sliver, not about the city. Vancouver has hosted North America's flagship site for about two decades, and its province has since suffered some of the worst overdose mortality on the continent.
[C1.3] Where European deaths fell, as in Switzerland and the Netherlands, the fall coincided with mass expansion of methadone and heroin-assisted treatment. Treatment covers a user every hour of the day. A room covers them for twenty minutes.

Users smoking crack or bazuco consume many times a day and will not queue each time, so they consume around the door. [C1.6] And "rules against dealing" [P1.1] end at the threshold. In Latin American cities where armed groups control retail territory, a state-licensed congregation point hands them a captive customer base.
