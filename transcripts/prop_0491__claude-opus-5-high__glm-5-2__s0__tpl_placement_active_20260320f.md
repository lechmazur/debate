[P1.1] Start with the phrase doing all the concealing work: "anonymized." Anonymity protects a name. It does not protect a group. If an employer learns that its workforce, drawn heavily from a particular region or ancestry, carries elevated prevalence of a costly condition, the employer does not need to know *which* employee to act on it.
[P1.2] It can raise the deductible on exactly the drug class those people need, tier the specialty coverage that condition requires, or build a "wellness" incentive around a biomarker some employees are genetically less able to move. Nobody is identified. Everybody predisposed is charged. That is redlining by proxy, and it is invisible precisely because the data was scrubbed of names.
[P1.3] Second: the employer's interest here is structurally the wrong interest. A physician who learns your genetic risk uses it to treat you. An employer who learns a workforce's genetic risk uses it to price that workforce. The output of the analysis is not a care plan; it is a cost curve.
[P1.4] When the party designing your insurance is also the party paying for your claims and deciding your promotions, genetic risk data is not a health tool. It is leverage.
[P1.5] Third: consent cannot rescue this. No employee meaningfully declines a program their manager champions and their premium discount depends on. And genetic information is uniquely non-individual — your genome implicates your siblings and children, who never sat at the table at all.
[P1.6] Fourth: why *strict* prohibition rather than clever regulation. Actuarial models are proprietary, and their reasoning is untraceable. If the rule is "you may use it, but not to disadvantage anyone," no regulator can audit whether a benefit redesign was driven by genomic prevalence or by ordinary claims data. A bright line is the only enforceable line.
[P1.7] Weigh the trade. On one side, wellness programs whose cost savings have been repeatedly disappointing. On the other, the permanent normalization of employers reasoning about workers as genetic risk pools. That is a terrible bargain, and we should refuse it outright.
