[P1.1] When a local government allocates hundreds of millions in public funds to subsidize a professional sports team’s stadium, it is not cutting a blank check for private owners to cash out whenever a richer market offers a better deal. It is entering a binding public contract: taxpayers fund the facility in exchange for long-term civic benefits—local jobs, small business revenue, community pride, a permanent neighborhood anchor. Letting owners abandon that community without accountability to the people who paid for their home is a fundamental breach of that deal.
[P1.2] Look no further than the Oakland Raiders, who received roughly $100 million in public subsidies for 1980s Coliseum renovations, then abandoned the city for Los Angeles, leaving taxpayers saddled with a half-empty, money-losing stadium that sat unused for over a decade. They returned only to extract another $750 million in public funds for a Las Vegas stadium, uprooting again the second a better subsidy package materialized. Oakland residents had no say in either move, even though their tax dollars funded both facilities.
[P1.3] A binding community approval process is not a radical restriction on private property rights—it is a basic enforcement of the original agreement between the team and the public. Such a process, whether a supermajority vote of the local governing body that approved the original subsidies or a public ballot measure, would ensure owners cannot treat public funds as a free pass to extort one community after another. It would still allow relocation if the team is genuinely nonviable and the community consents; it just stops owners from prioritizing their own profit over the people who made their business possible in the first place.
