[P1.1] Right now, half the students who start bachelor’s programs at public four-year universities never finish within six years. For the lowest-performing degree tracks, that completion rate drops below one in three. These students do not get the graduate wage premium. They carry student debt.
[P1.2] They lose two, three years of working life. That is not opportunity. That is a publicly funded trap. This is not an attack on liberal arts or university education as such.
[P1.3] This is about reallocating marginal resources from programs that are already failing most people who enroll. Shorter vocational and technical pathways—aligned welding certifications, medical assisting, network administration, industrial maintenance—routinely post 80%+ completion rates across most states. They lead directly to unfilled middle-class jobs, with a tiny fraction of the debt burden of an unfinished degree. Critics will claim this closes doors.
[P1.4] But the door is already closed for the majority signing up for those low-completion tracks. No one is being barred from pursuing a four-year degree. We will simply stop pouring public money into broken paths, and instead build paths that reliably deliver the economic security public education is supposed to guarantee. No student should be lured into a program where two out of every three people who start will walk away with nothing but debt.
[P1.5] That is the status quo today. That is indefensible. This policy fixes that.

[C1.1] Low completion is not proof a program is worthless. It is often proof its students were given the least to succeed with.
[C1.2] In a low-completion degree program you will disproportionately find first-generation students, students working full-time, students arriving from underfunded schools who need tutoring, advising, childcare, and flexible schedules. Shift resources away, and you do not fix that failure — you guarantee it. Classes grow, advisors vanish, required courses are offered less often, time to degree stretches, and the next cohort completes at an even lower rate. PRO calls that a trap, then proposes to remove the ladder.
[C1.3] Their completion comparison is equally misleading. A short certificate is easier to finish than a bachelor's degree because it asks for far less. That does not make it a superior public investment, any more than a driving test is superior to medical school because more people pass. Many vocational credentials lead to jobs that are low-paid, physically taxing, or narrowly tied to one technology or employer.
[C1.4] When that technology changes, the worker has little to fall back on. A degree, even a harder one, builds writing, quantitative, and analytical skills that travel across careers over forty years.
[C1.5] The equity cost is the deepest problem. No policy labels students by income, but we know who gets steered when degree programs shrink and cheap short tracks expand. Affluent families will still buy tutors, unpaid internships, and second chances at a bachelor's. Working-class students will be nudged toward the fastest credential and a lifetime earnings ceiling.
[C1.6] That is not expanding opportunity; it is formalizing the expectation that some students should aim lower.
[C1.7] Public universities exist precisely to refuse that sorting. Their job is to make demanding, high-return pathways completable for students the system has underserved — through better advising, credit transfer, remedial reform, and funding tied to support. Defunding the programs those students choose solves the statistics by shrinking ambition, not by delivering education.

[C2.1] Saying no one is barred while shifting the money away is the central evasion. In a public university, resources are access: seats in required courses, advisors, sections at times working students can attend. Shrink those and a student is not formally expelled — she simply cannot get the classes to graduate on time, cannot afford another semester, and leaves. That is a bar built from scarcity.
[C2.2] The claim that supports have already been tried for fifteen years proves too little. A pilot advising grant for a few hundred students is not the same as funding a program where most students work, commute, and enter behind in math. And if life constraints — jobs, childcare, unstable schedules — are driving non-completion, they do not vanish in a welding or medical-assisting course. PRO assumes the two-thirds who leave a bachelor's will suddenly finish at the eighty percent rate quoted for today's certificate students [P1.3].
[C2.3] That confuses the students currently choosing short, job-specific programs with students who wanted a degree and struggled. Move the second group without solving the constraints that stopped them, and you import low completion into the new track while removing the old one.
[C2.4] Nor is it true those students 'get nothing.' Credits, literacy, quantitative skills, and transfer pathways have value, and many return later — but only if the program still exists when they can. A terminated sequence of courses cannot be resumed.
[C2.5] Finally, pointing to unequal graduation by income does not justify unequal horizons. If affluent students finish at higher rates under the status quo, the answer is to close the support gap, not to build the cheaper exit they will rarely take. Affluent families will still choose the bachelor's and its forty-year premium; displaced working-class students will be concentrated in credentials tied to one employer or technology. PRO would make today's unequal outcome tomorrow's unequal assignment, and call it realism.
