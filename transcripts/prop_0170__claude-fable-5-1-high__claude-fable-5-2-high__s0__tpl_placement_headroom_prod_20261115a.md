[P1.1] Every water-scarce region eventually faces the same arithmetic: conservation has a floor, and population does not. You can cut leaks, swap lawns for gravel, and price water aggressively, and you will buy perhaps a decade. Then the gains are exhausted, the city has grown, and you are rationing again, except now there is nothing left to squeeze. Conservation manages scarcity.
[P1.2] Desalination ends it. That is the whole debate.
[P1.3] Israel is the proof. A generation ago it lurched between drought emergencies and restrictions; today roughly the majority of its household water comes from the sea, reservoirs refill in dry years, and it sells water to Jordan. Costs fell as plants scaled, to well under a dollar per cubic meter, cheaper than the economic damage of the shortages it replaced. The lesson is not that Israel is special.
[P1.4] It is that the region chose supply as the foundation and conservation as the trim.
[P1.5] Compare the alternative. Cape Town's Day Zero, Chennai's water trucks, Mexico City's rotating cutoffs. Rationing looks cheap only because its costs are hidden: hospitals and factories on tankers, the poor paying vendors ten times the metered rate, investment fleeing because no one builds a plant in a city that may shut the taps. Rationing is a tax collected in misery, levied hardest on people who cannot drill a private well.
[P1.6] Rationing also depends on the one thing scarce regions cannot control: rain. A conservation strategy is a bet that next year's drought resembles this year's. Desalination is the only supply that does not care what the sky does, and paired with solar it increasingly runs on the region's most abundant resource.
[P1.7] Brine and energy are engineering problems with known solutions, and they are shrinking. Permanent thirst is not an engineering problem. It is a political choice. Regions should choose to build.

[C1.3] Copy the roof without the house and you get a wealthy, small, coastal country's bill without its results.
[C1.4] Three tests decide this debate.
[C1.5] The first is cost per cubic meter. Fixing leaks, lining canals, recycling wastewater, and pricing water honestly almost always deliver water cheaper than building new supply. [C1.6] The second is reach. Scarcity is often inland, uphill, and poor: the Sahel, Central Asia, highland Mexico, interior India. Seawater must be pumped hundreds of kilometers and paid for by people who cannot afford it.

[C1.8] PRO equates conservation with chaotic cutoffs [P1.5]. Those cutoffs are what happens when nobody manages demand. Efficiency should be the foundation and desalination the supplement, used where it fits.
