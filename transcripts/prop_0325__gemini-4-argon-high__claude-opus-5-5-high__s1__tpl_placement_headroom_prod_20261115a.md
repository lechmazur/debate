[P1.1] Imagine going to a grocery store for a carton of milk and being told you can only buy it inside a hundred-dollar cart filled with food you never eat. That is modern sports broadcasting. Most fans do not have omnivorous appetites for every sport on earth; they care deeply about one hometown team or a single competition, like a domestic soccer league or Formula One. Forcing those viewers into bloated subscription bundles extracts unfair rents by making loyal fans subsidize unrelated games and channels they will never watch.
[P1.2] Offering cheaper team-specific or competition-specific passes restores basic consumer fairness: fans should pay for the product they actually value, not an outdated cable package disguised as an app.
[P1.3] Furthermore, targeted passes are essential for the commercial survival of broadcasters and leagues themselves. Today’s all-or-nothing pricing creates massive deadweight loss. When a casual or younger fan is asked to pay exorbitant monthly fees across bloated platforms just to follow their local club, they rarely surrender and buy the bundle.
[P1.4] Instead, they turn to rampant digital piracy or disengage from live broadcasts entirely, settling for free social media highlights. Cheaper, targeted passes provide a vital financial on-ramp. By lowering the price barrier, broadcasters can convert millions of pirates and priced-out viewers, who currently generate zero revenue, into paying subscribers, protecting the long-term fanbase from aging out.
[P1.5] Defenders of forced bundling will argue that broad packages are needed to bankroll massive media rights fees and cross-subsidize less popular programming. Yet offering targeted passes does not outlaw keeping an all-access bundle for sports omnivores; it simply introduces smart tiered pricing. More importantly, forcing a basketball fan to invisibly subsidize a tennis tournament is neither equitable nor durable when consumers can easily cut the cord. Broadcasters must offer targeted passes to align prices with real fan demand.
