[C1.1] The proposition is framed as a rescue: help or no help. But we are not denying confidential counseling at a campus clinic or cash-pay therapy. We oppose a categorical right to keep parents and family insurance in the dark about treatment that family money is paying for. That distinction decides the debate.
[C1.2] [P1.3] insists that adults own their medical decisions, so hiding a visit is consistent. It is not. A 19-year-old may own the decision, but most are not paying the premium. When care rides on a parent's plan, an insurance claim is not gratuitous reporting; it is the financial fingerprint of a benefit someone else bought.
[C1.3] Ordering the system to suppress that fingerprint means creating a hidden claims channel that vanishes from the policyholder's statement while remaining billable to him or her. Either the visit is reimbursed and therefore documented, or the cost is shifted to clinics as unbudgeted charity. You cannot decree affordable, insured, invisible care for a dependent.
[C1.4] The real harm is to the sickest young adults. Serious mental illness often first strikes in this age range, and it impairs insight. Imagine a 20-year-old with bipolar disorder secretly prescribed mood stabilizers. Hidden billing means no one sees the prescription lapse after a bad side effect or a missed refill.
[C1.5] The parents who would have driven him to the pharmacy, noticed the stopped medication, or caught the manic spending spree, see nothing until he is psychotic in an emergency room. Secrecy does not produce support; silence removes the people best positioned to rescue him.
[C1.6] Total invisibility is not adulthood; it is abandonment wrapped as autonomy. Existing confidentiality rules already permit involving family when safety is at stake; the motion replaces that safeguard with permanent concealment. If a young adult wants absolute privacy, adulthood supplies the tools: individual coverage, self-pay, school-based care. What we reject is forcing families and insurers to fund treatment they are forbidden to know about.

[P2.1] CON's case rests on a false binary: either the visit appears on the policyholder's statement, or it is invisible and unbillable. That is not how claims processing works. An insurer can adjudicate a claim while the explanation of benefits suppresses the provider name and diagnosis-signaling codes — the same technique already used for sensitive services. Documentation for payment is not parental notification.
[P2.2] CON wants "the financial fingerprint of a benefit someone else bought" to equal a right to know the content of an adult's medical care. But a family plan does not buy parents the results of their adult child's STI test; premiums do not purchase records. Where cost-sharing matters, the young adult can be required to pay the copay — privately, to the clinic.
[P2.3] Second, the bipolar vignette does all of CON's work, and it cannot bear the weight. It assumes the young adult would have entered disclosed treatment in the first place, that a parent would have caught the lapse, and that confidentiality abolishes emergency intervention. The last claim CON hands me outright: "existing confidentiality rules already permit involving family when safety is at stake." Correct — duty-to-warn, capacity findings, involuntary holds for grave disability or danger.
[P2.4] None of these require an automatic notice to parents; they are triggered by clinical condition, not paperwork. So the real comparison is narrower than CON pretends: not monitored patient versus unmonitored patient, but patient in care versus the person deterred from ever presenting. The young adult who refuses treatment under parental notification does not lapse on medication — he never gets a prescription at all. CON's worst case is a mishandled treatment episode; mine is no treatment episode.
[P2.5] Third, "individual coverage, self-pay, school-based care" is not a safety valve — it is a price tag. Cash therapy is expensive, individual plans cost more than dependent status, and campus care expires in summer and at graduation. CON's answer is: buy your privacy at market rate. That is a deterrent policy dressed as principle, and it should be rejected.
