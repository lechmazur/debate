[P1.1] Public university funding is currently tied almost exclusively to enrollment, creating a perverse incentive where schools are rewarded for recruiting students, not for ensuring they succeed. This misalignment wastes billions in taxpayer dollars and traps vulnerable students in debt without the payoff of a degree or a career.
[P1.2] Tying funding to demonstrated improvements in graduation rates and post-graduation earnings fixes this broken dynamic, aligning institutional incentives with the core mission of public higher education: delivering tangible opportunity for students and returns for the communities that fund them.
[P1.3] First, graduation rate targets would push under-resourced public universities to invest in the supports low-income, first-generation, and working students need to persist and complete their degrees. Right now, just 60% of first-time full-time undergrads at 4-year public institutions graduate within six years, with rates at many regional campuses falling below 40%. If funding was tied to closing these gaps, schools could expand academic advising, emergency grant programs, and childcare support that we know boosts completion. For a student on the verge of dropping out due to a missed tuition payment or lack of academic support, this difference is the line between a degree and unpayable debt.
[P1.4] Second, tying funding to post-graduation earnings would eliminate waste of public funds on low-value programs that fill seats but leave graduates worse off. Too many public universities maintain under-enrolled, low-ROI programs simply because they generate enrollment counts that drive baseline funding. Shifting incentives to outcomes would push schools to align curricula with labor market needs, expand career services and internship pipelines, and redirect resources to degrees that deliver upward mobility.
[P1.5] This policy rewards schools for improving results for hard-to-serve populations, rather than punishing them. Taxpayers get a better return on their investment, students get the support they need to succeed, and public universities refocus on their core public purpose.

[C1.3] A university under financial pressure will face constant temptation to let rigor slide—and students from under-resourced backgrounds, who depend most on a degree meaning something, are the ones harmed.
[C1.4] Post-graduation earnings is worse. A philosophy graduate who becomes a teacher earns less than a finance graduate entering a hedge fund, yet may produce far more civic value. Tying funding to earnings systematically defunds programs that produce teachers, social workers, artists, and nonprofit professionals—fields society needs but markets undervalue. This doesn't nudge curriculum toward labor market needs; it tells universities to abandon entire missions.
[C1.5] Worse still, the approach punishes institutions doing the hardest work. A regional public university serving first-generation, low-income students will always show lower graduation rates and lower earnings than a flagship serving wealthy, well-prepared students—regardless of instructional quality. The school that takes risks on vulnerable students loses funding; the school that skims the easiest ones is rewarded. That's not accountability; it's a formula for reinforcing inequality.
[C1.6] The enrollment model has real flaws. But replacing it with metrics universities can game through grade inflation, that penalize service to the most vulnerable, and that take years to measure meaningfully—students don't graduate or earn salaries until long after enrolling—replaces one broken system with one harder to administer, easier to manipulate, and structurally hostile to equity.
