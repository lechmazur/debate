[P1.1] When parents can no longer support themselves, their care costs do not disappear. They are paid—by children, by charities, or by taxpayers who owe them nothing. The only question is whether the family that received decades of investment should bear a fair share, or whether that burden should be dumped on strangers. Adult children should have a legally enforceable duty to contribute, because it formalizes a debt of gratitude into a manageable, equitable obligation.
[P1.2] Parents are not merely biological donors. They typically invest decades of labor and income into raising children. When age and illness render a parent destitute, a legal duty recognizes that the child is the primary beneficiary of that investment. To deny the duty is to tell parents that the state will guarantee their starvation but their offspring need not prevent it.
[P1.3] The current default is arbitrary. Without a legal duty, the most dutiful child—often the one who lives nearby—bears the entire cost, draining savings while siblings inherit untouched. A widow with three children should not have her care subsidized by strangers while her heirs retain the house. Sharing costs among all adult children is more just than the lottery of geography and conscience.
[P1.4] This is not novel state paternalism. Tort law, child support, and estate law compel people to pay for harms and benefits. A duty to contribute to parental care extends that logic to the most fundamental social bond. It prevents free riding on public welfare and forces adult children to internalize a cost they created.
[P1.5] The motion asks only for a contribution, not indentured servitude, and only when parents are destitute. When care is unavoidable, fairness demands that the family pay first. Any other rule makes the public the insurer of every family obligation while letting heirs walk away. The duty should be enforceable.
