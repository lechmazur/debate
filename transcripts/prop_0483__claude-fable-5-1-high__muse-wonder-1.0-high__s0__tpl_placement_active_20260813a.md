[C1.1] A test does not need to imitate life to predict life. A blood-pressure cuff does not run a ward or negotiate a contract, yet it is not dismissed as too narrow, because it measures an underlying capacity affecting many tasks. IQ-style tests do the same for cognition.
[C1.2] What they isolate — reasoning with novel information, holding steps in mind, spotting structure quickly — is not a random sliver. It is the bottleneck underneath learning itself. The experienced nurse beats the novice because she has already turned that ability into domain knowledge. Give two novices identical training and an unfamiliar problem, and the one who absorbs and transfers new rules faster pulls ahead.
[C1.3] Stripping away prior knowledge is the point: it removes the head start from schooling, wealth, and connections.
[C1.4] That is why a narrow measure earns a large role: it predicts who learns fastest in training, masters complex instruction, and handles rising complexity, often better than interviews, resumes, or experience alone. A moderate correlation looks modest for one person, but for institutions choosing among hundreds, a consistent edge repeated at scale beats guesswork. The alternative is not omniscience; it is error without a yardstick.
[C1.5] The proposed replacements are not fairer for being broader; they are more biased. Portfolios reward polished help at home, work samples reward prior access to that work, teacher observations reward familiarity, and unstructured interviews reward charm and similarity. They are costly, hard to compare, and easy for the advantaged to game. A common test lets a child without tutors or an applicant without pedigree outscore the expected candidate.
[C1.6] Sorting will happen regardless; places are finite. The choice is between a transparent measure of learning capacity, normally used with grades and experience as a threshold and check, and softer judgments where privilege hides more easily. Narrow here is not blindness. It is focus on the capacity that makes broader abilities possible.
