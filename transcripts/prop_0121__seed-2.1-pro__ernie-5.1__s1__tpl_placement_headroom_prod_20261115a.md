[P1.1] Mandatory parental notification and automatic insurance billing disclosures for young adult mental health care do not protect families—they push vulnerable people away from life-saving support and hollow out the care they do manage to access. Young adulthood is the peak window for onset of most serious, long-term mental health conditions, from mood and anxiety disorders to psychosis and substance use challenges, when early, honest intervention most sharply cuts risk of crisis, chronic impairment, and suicide. For too many young adults, that early care is out of reach if a parent will receive an explanation of benefits naming a mental health provider in the mail, or if a clinic requires parental sign-off before they can book an appointment.
[P1.2] These rules treat 18-25-year-olds as if they lack basic medical decision-making capacity, even as they are legally permitted to vote, enlist, sign binding contracts, work full time, and make every other high-stakes life choice independently. There is no principled reason to carve out mental health care as the single category of adult medical care where default privacy rights do not apply. That risk is not hypothetical. A young adult still living at home, dependent on parental insurance while working part-time, may be navigating intimate partner violence, gender-related distress, self-harm impulses, or direct abuse from a parent themselves.
[P1.3] A paper trail disclosing their visit can lead to eviction, being cut off from financial support, forced unwanted treatment, or further retaliation. Even for young adults in supportive homes, the knowledge that visit details will be shared erodes therapeutic trust: the guarantee that a patient can speak freely about deeply personal struggles without those details reaching anyone they have not chosen to tell. When patients cannot be honest about what is hurting them, care stops working, no matter how well-intentioned the disclosure rule is.

[C1.1] Young adults deserve privacy, but the proposition demands an absolute ban on parental notification and automatic insurance disclosure—and that absolutism endangers the very people it claims to protect. Mental health treatment does not happen in a vacuum. It requires stable housing, medication adherence, crisis planning, and often financial support—all of which for most young adults flow through family systems. Cutting parents out of the loop does not liberate patients; it blinds the network that keeps them alive between appointments.
[C1.2] Consider a twenty-year-old experiencing a first psychotic episode who stops taking antipsychotic medication because no one notices. Without parental notification, that crisis unfolds in silence until it becomes an emergency room visit—or worse. Parents are not the enemy of therapeutic trust; for many young adults, they are the only safety net. The proposition treats all parental involvement as inherently toxic, when in reality the greater danger is invisible, untreated deterioration.
[C1.3] Insurance billing transparency is equally essential. Automatic disclosure is not a privacy gimmick; it is the mechanism that verifies coverage, prevents fraudulent claims, and enables coordination across providers. Eliminate it and you create a shadow system where treatment becomes unverifiable and uncoordinated—driving up costs for the same young adults who need affordability most.
[C1.4] The real tension is not privacy versus parental rights. It is short-term confidentiality versus long-term survival. A young adult who feels safe seeking help, then loses that help because their support system was excluded, has gained nothing. The proposition confuses secrecy with safety and calls it liberation.

Forced transparency is not care coordination—it is a one-size-fits-all rule that sacrifices the most vulnerable patients to avoid trusting adults to make their own medical choices.
