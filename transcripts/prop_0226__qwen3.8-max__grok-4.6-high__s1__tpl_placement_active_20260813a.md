[P1.1] The proposition asks whether international law should catch up with a world already creating people who cannot safely remain in their own countries. It should. A limited legal right to climate asylum is the minimum institutional response to displacement caused by sea-level rise and extreme heat.
[P1.2] The core gap is simple. Refugee law protects people fleeing persecution, not those whose homeland disappears beneath the ocean or becomes too hot to survive. A family in a low-lying atoll does not lose less when saltwater ruins wells and storms flood homes every year. If the state cannot provide a safe place to live, calling their flight “economic migration” is a legal fiction that leaves them unprotected, often stateless, and exposed to arbitrary removal.
[P1.3] The right should be narrow. It should apply where climate impacts make continued residence unsafe or practically impossible, where internal relocation is unavailable or unreasonable, and where the harm is severe enough to meet a protection threshold. It is not open-ended compensation for every climate impact; it is protection where life, water, or shelter is no longer viable. That excludes ordinary migration and preserves the distinction between choice and necessity.
[P1.4] It can include temporary protection, planned relocation, or residence permits, with review as conditions change.
[P1.5] Limited recognition also serves order. Without a legal channel, displacement happens through irregular routes, smuggling, and emergency crises. With a legal category, countries can plan quotas, share burdens, verify claims, and integrate people before collapse becomes chaos. For small island states, it affirms that sovereignty does not vanish when land becomes uninhabitable.
[P1.6] The opposition will likely say borders and costs. But the motion does not demand open borders; it demands a rule for the hardest cases. A system that can recognize persecution but not literal submersion is not principled, it is obsolete.

[P4.1] A1: A right need not mean unlimited choice of destination or infinite numbers. Under a limited climate-asylum framework, a successful claimant has a right not to be returned to an uninhabitable territory and to receive protection in a participating state. Which state admits is set by an agreed responsibility-sharing rule: family links, quotas, or regional rotation. Quotas distribute the obligation; they do not nullify it.
[P4.2] If one state’s allocation is full, the duty shifts to the mechanism or another participating state; otherwise the “right” would be meaningless. Eligibility remains legal, not discretionary charity.
[P4.3] A2: The cutoff is not “hard agricultural work” or “hot city.” It is the collapse of basic habitability: permanent submersion, chronic loss of fresh water, or recurrent extreme heat that makes ordinary life unsustainable even with reasonable adaptation and internal relocation. A claimant would need objective evidence—repeated inundation, salinized wells, abandoned infrastructure, official relocation failure, or sustained heat conditions incompatible with ordinary residence. That excludes a farm worker who faces lower wages or dangerous labor if water, shelter, and internal options remain viable, and it excludes a delta city where flood defenses or relocation make continued residence possible.
[P4.4] The atoll is covered because no such alternatives exist.
[P4.5] These answers expose the decisive flaw in the opposition’s case. They keep insisting the category cannot be bounded, but the proposition already supplies a bounded legal question: can the person remain alive and housed in their own country? Courts adjudicate harder predictive judgments in persecution cases all the time. Documented saltwater in wells and submerged land are not less manageable than forecasting whether a persecutor will strike again.
[P4.6] The opposition’s alternative—bargains and discretionary admission—means people whose homeland is disappearing must wait for politics, not law. That is not order; it is abandonment by procedure. A limited right is therefore both administrable and necessary.
