[C1.2] That is an assumption, not a policy. Unless a statute requires every dollar to be paid out as wages, a 20 percent service charge can simply replace a 20 percent tip—and management can retain a cut.
[C1.3] That is the first harm: workers lose the upside. The server who handles a large party, the bartender who manages a slammed Friday night, and the regular who gives exceptional service currently capture that value. Tipping rewards effort and skill in real time. A flat base-pay model caps that reward and pools it.
[C1.4] It protects the coasting employee and taxes the strong one. The legitimate goal of sharing with cooks can be achieved through tip-pool rules; it does not require ending tipping.
[C1.5] Second, customers lose the only after-the-fact performance mechanism they have. A compulsory service charge is collected before the diner can evaluate the experience. [C1.7] Third, the "honest price" argument is backwards. Diners already know tipping is expected and calculate for it. [C1.8] A direct tip has less institutional hiding than a management-controlled surcharge.
