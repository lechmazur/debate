[P1.1] The proposition is not that fusion will save next year’s climate budget; it is that large public investment in fusion is a rational hedge against a hard long-term problem. The right test is whether the option created is worth the premium, and it is.
[P1.2] First, fusion addresses a gap that other clean technologies may not close by themselves. Deep decarbonization needs firm, low-carbon power: dense, reliable electricity for industry, heating, transport, and grid stability. Batteries, transmission, wind, and solar can take us far, but uncertainty about storage costs, land use, materials, and public acceptance makes it reckless to bet only on today’s portfolio. Fusion, if it matures, offers firm power without carbon emissions and without the same fuel-constraint profile as fossil fuels.
[P1.3] Public funding buys a chance at that outcome.
[P1.4] Second, the private market systematically underfuses this kind of research. Fusion requires patient capital, specialized facilities, plasma physics, materials science, and safety regimes that no single firm can fully capture. Venture funding can help, but it usually seeks shorter exits and can abandon long-horizon work when markets turn. Large public programs keep the knowledge base alive, train engineers, and sustain infrastructure that would otherwise decay.
[P1.5] That is not a bug of public science; it is exactly what public science is for.
[P1.6] Third, the benefits will not wait for a commercial plant. Fusion research already forces progress in high-field magnets, vacuum systems, heat exhaust, remote maintenance, and radiation-resistant materials. Those advances can improve medical imaging, accelerators, fission safety, and industrial processes. Even if fusion power arrives late, the learning created along the way has value.
[P1.7] Opponents may say the payoff is too distant. But the alternative—starving a potentially decisive technology during the decades when we most need it—is the real gamble.

[C1.1] The central question is not whether fusion is fascinating science. It is whether, with climate timelines measured in years and cheap clean alternatives already deployable, pouring billions into a technology that may not deliver commercial power for decades is the right call. It is not.
[C1.2] First, opportunity cost. Every billion earmarked for a tokamak that lights up in the 2050s is a billion not spent on solar, wind, storage, or grid upgrades that displace fossil electricity this decade. Solar's levelized cost has collapsed by roughly an order of magnitude in twenty years. The marginal ton of CO2 avoided today comes from deployment, not from plasma physics.
[C1.3] Fusion research cannot tell us whether 2035 emissions targets are met; rooftop solar can.
[C1.4] Second, the "decades away" hedge keeps getting more expensive. ITER's budget has ballooned from a few billion to over twenty, its first plasma has slipped years, and its goal is not even commercial electricity. The National Ignition Facility barely achieved ignition at enormous cost. Treating fusion as cheap insurance ignores that the premium keeps rising while the alternatives get cheaper.
[C1.5] Third, private capital is no longer absent. Commonwealth Fusion Systems, TAE, Helion, and others have raised substantial sums precisely because compact and alternative designs promise faster paths. Public megaprograms now risk duplicating private effort, bidding up scarce superconducting and tritium expertise, and locking in architectures the private sector is already outflanking.
[C1.6] Fourth, the spillover argument proves too much. High-field magnets and materials science have value, but those advances come from focused basic research budgets a fraction of a national fusion program. We do not need a power-plant-sized bet to harvest those gains; a physics-sized one will do.
[C1.7] The proposition asks us to keep writing very large checks on the promise of fusion. Given cost, timeline, and the maturity of competing clean technologies, the case for that scale of public investment has broken down. Continue the science, yes. Continue it at today's scale, no.
