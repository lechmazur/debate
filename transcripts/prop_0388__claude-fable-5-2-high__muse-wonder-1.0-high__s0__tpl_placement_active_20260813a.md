[C1.1] Strict limits sound protective until you define the target. “Gambling-like” sweeps in loot boxes, gacha pulls, digital card packs, and blind-bag cosmetics — purchases where a minor pays a known price for virtual items that cannot be cashed out or wagered again. That is closer to a trading-card pack than to roulette. Regulating by resemblance — chance, lights, anticipation — treats almost every collectible toy as quasi-gambling, without the core gambling harm: staking money to win money.
[C1.2] The cure also fails mechanically. Caps and default-off spending only bind honest users on verified child accounts. Teenagers can misstate age, use a parent’s profile, borrow an adult account, or pay with gift cards. Making a cap real requires stronger identity checks — collecting documents and payment data from the very children supposedly protected.
[C1.3] That means more privacy exposure, more exclusion of teens without easy documentation, and easiest evasion by the heaviest users.
[C1.4] Families already have sharper tools. Purchase approvals, storefront passwords, bank alerts, refunds, and spending dashboards let parents set zero, five, or fifty dollars based on their child, income, and values. A uniform ceiling replaces that judgment with one number for a cautious twelve-year-old and a seventeen-year-old earning from a part-time job. Learning to budget an allowance through small, reversible purchases is development, not danger.
[C1.5] Free access is also not a mere developer excuse. Free-to-play lets millions of low-income young players share the same servers, updates, and social spaces without an upfront price. Squeeze revenue with broad caps and compliance costs, and firms will raise entry prices, push subscriptions or heavier ads, cut free content, or age-gate minors out entirely. The vast majority who spend little or nothing would lose access to solve a concentrated problem better handled through parental controls, honest odds disclosure, and action against truly deceptive designs.
