[P1.1] The question is not whether phones can ever teach. It is where the presumption should sit. A school-wide default ban makes “no phone” the baseline. Teacher discretion makes every classroom a separate jurisdiction.
[P1.2] That difference decides the policy.
[P1.3] Teacher-by-teacher rules create a coordination failure. Students move between rooms all day. If one teacher allows phones for a project and the next bans them, the rule is not about learning; it is about which adult you happen to be with. The permissive room becomes a loophole: students scroll, text, and charge there, then carry the distraction into the next class.
[P1.4] The result is also unequal. A confident veteran can enforce a ban; a new teacher, a substitute, or an overloaded instructor may quietly give up. What looks like flexibility becomes arbitrary enforcement that students quickly learn to game.
[P1.5] A default ban fixes this by making the norm collective. Phones go into lockers or pouches except for specific, supervised uses the school has approved in advance. Educational uses survive—research, polls, accessibility tools—but the burden flips: the exception must be justified and pre-cleared, not left to one teacher’s whim. Just as important, it stops forcing teachers to be phone police.
[P1.6] Under discretion, the teacher who wants a ban must relitigate it every day, against every student, alone. Under a default, the school backs the rule, and discipline stops being a personal grudge match.
[P1.7] The real stake is protected attention. Smartphones are engineered to interrupt; even silenced ones split focus, and the group chats, photos, and drama do not stop at the door. A patchwork lets the phone follow a child all day. A default ban gives every student—especially the bullied or anxious one—several hours where the feed is off and school is a face-to-face place.
[P1.8] The choice is not rigidity versus freedom. It is a clear institution-wide floor versus a lottery of enforcement. Build the floor: no phones by default.

[C1.1] The motion gets the direction of trust backwards. It treats teachers as inconsistent enforcers who need a central prohibition to back them, when teachers are the people best placed to match phone rules to the lesson, the moment, and the student.
[C1.2] A default ban promises coordination but mostly relocates the problem. Students under a school-wide rule do not become less phone-obsessed; they check devices in hallways, bathrooms, and under desks. The teacher who allowed a focused research task loses a tool, while the teacher who wanted silence gains no extra enforcement—the school’s rule still has to be policed by real adults every period. So the ban does not remove the burden on teachers; it replaces classroom judgment with confiscation and hall sweeps.
[C1.3] Nor is the classroom the same from room to room. A physics teacher may need students to photograph a lab setup; a language teacher may use translation tools; a music teacher may want a tuner; a student may use speech-to-text. Under a default ban, those are not ordinary teaching choices—they are bureaucratic exceptions. That chills legitimate use and teaches students that a phone in hand is suspicious by default.
[C1.4] Uniformity is also not equity. Blanket phone bans are often enforced most harshly against the students who already face the most scrutiny. And for students whose phone is their only way to look something up or keep an assignment organized, removing that default tool can widen gaps rather than close them.
[C1.5] The right default is not prohibition but a school-wide expectation: phones silent and away unless a teacher directs otherwise. That preserves consistency without making one-size-fits-all the rule. The aim should be protected attention, but attention is best protected by the adult in the room, not by a central office declaring every classroom a no-phone zone.
