[P1.1] The Arctic is already being redrawn by melting ice: new shipping lanes, untapped energy, militarized bases, and overlapping continental-shelf claims. If governance remains a contest of bordering states, the winners are those with the most power and the least patience. That is not a legal order; it is a race.
[P1.2] The Antarctic Treaty System proves binding law can stop exactly that race. It did not resolve every sovereignty claim; it froze them. It turned a strategic frontier into a demilitarized zone of shared science and enforceable environmental protection. The Arctic needs the same logic, adapted to inhabited territories and indigenous rights: a binding treaty that freezes new territorial and resource grabs, sets common shipping and spill rules, limits militarization, and creates real dispute resolution.
[P1.3] The current architecture is not enough. The Arctic Council is consensus-based and non-binding; it can publish good reports, not stop a drilling rush or a naval incident. UNCLOS helps with seabed claims, but it is not a comprehensive Arctic constitution, and not every relevant power has ratified it. Bilateral deals last only as long as the mood between capitals.
[P1.4] Competing claims make every new route and reserve a flashpoint: the Northwest Passage, the Northern Sea Route, the Lomonosov Ridge, fisheries moving north.
[P1.5] Opponents will say national interests make a treaty impossible. But the Antarctic was also carved by rival claims, and law changed the payoff. A binding treaty need not abolish Arctic sovereignty; it can recognize existing rights while capping expansion, mandating cooperation, and giving everyone a reason to comply. The decisive question is not whether a treaty is easy, but whether competing claims can produce legitimate, enforceable rules.
[P1.6] They cannot. The choice is not perfect governance versus sovereignty. It is enforceable common rules versus unilateral advantage. On a warming, militarizing frontier, only the first protects the Arctic and the states around it.

[C1.1] The Antarctic analogy collapses on contact with the Arctic map. Antarctica is an uninhabited continent with no permanent population, no cities, and no sovereign communities living under settled domestic law. The Arctic is the opposite: it is sovereign land and sea surrounding millions of residents, including Indigenous peoples whose land-claims, hunting rights, and self-government were negotiated with specific states. Freezing that landscape into a global commons does not protect them; it overrides them, treating their homeland as empty wilderness to be managed by distant signatories.
[C1.2] The proposition also misdescribes the present. The Arctic is not left mainly to territorial grabs. Continental shelf submissions proceed through the law of the sea, maritime boundaries have been negotiated bilaterally — as Norway and Russia did in the Barents Sea — shipping is covered by international polar safety rules, and the Arctic Council, for all its limits, gives Indigenous organizations a seat no Antarctic-style regime ever offered. This system is imperfect, but it is law anchored in the states that provide icebreakers, search and rescue, ports, spill response, and courts.
[C1.3] A binding treaty modeled on Antarctica would either fail or do damage. If it truly demilitarized the region, froze resource development, and subjected sovereignty to international veto, the major Arctic powers would not sign it — or would sign and ignore it when security was at stake. If it were watered down enough to attract them, it would add a ceremonial layer while freezing existing advantages in place. Either way, paper universality replaces accountable governance.
[C1.4] Stewardship follows responsibility, not diffusion. A spill off Greenland, a distressed ship in the Canadian archipelago, or a collapsing coastal village in Alaska will not be answered by a treaty secretariat; it will be answered by the coastal state with people, assets, and legal duty on the scene. Strengthen that existing, inhabited, law-based order. Do not replace it with an imported model built for a continent with no one home.
