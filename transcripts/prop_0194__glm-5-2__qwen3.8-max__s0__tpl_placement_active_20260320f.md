[P1.1] Democracies routinely make decisions whose consequences extend far beyond the next election cycle, yet the people who will bear those consequences have no seat at the table. This isn't a marginal flaw—it's a structural one. Electoral incentives push toward short-term thinking: governments promise immediate benefits, defer costs, and avoid hard trade-offs whose payoff comes decades later. Climate policy, infrastructure planning, biodiversity loss, and pension reform all follow the same pattern—present voters capture benefits, future citizens inherit liabilities.
[P1.2] The objection that this is "just how democracy works" concedes the problem rather than solving it. A system that systematically discounts people who cannot vote isn't representing the public will—it's representing the present public's will, which is a narrower and more dangerous thing.
[P1.3] Future-generations commissioners with binding veto power over *some* major long-horizon decisions address this gap directly. They don't legislate or set policy. They hold a negative power—a veto triggered only when a decision meets a threshold of long-term, irreversible harm. That's a targeted check, not a parallel government.
[P1.4] Advisory bodies already exist in several democracies and their track record is clear: they issue reports, those reports get praised, then ignored when politically inconvenient. Soft power doesn't override electoral pressure. Binding veto power does. It forces legislatures to negotiate with the long-term interest rather than perform concern and proceed anyway.
[P1.5] The scope matters. The proposition says "some major long-horizon policy decisions"—not all decisions, not ordinary legislation. This applies where the stakes are high, the horizon is long, and reversal is costly. When a decision risks irreversible environmental damage or locks in decades of fiscal liability, a future-generations veto is a brake, not a steering wheel.
[P1.6] The real question isn't whether this is a departure from pure present-tense majoritarianism. It is. The question is whether democracy's legitimacy depends only on representing the living, or whether it also owes something to the citizens who will inherit what the living decide.
