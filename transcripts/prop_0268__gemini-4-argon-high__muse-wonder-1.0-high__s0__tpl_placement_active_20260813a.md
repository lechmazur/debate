[P1.1] When a shopper grabs their usual cereal box in an aisle or taps "reorder" on a grocery app, they rely on two cues: the familiar package and the headline price. If the price still says four dollars and the box stands just as tall, the shopper assumes nothing has changed. Manufacturers know this. By indenting the bottom of a jar or putting less food in the same-sized box, brands execute a stealth price hike designed to evade consumer notice.
[P1.2] Free markets only work when buyers know what they are paying. Defenders of the status quo will claim that net weights and tiny unit prices are already printed on labels. That defense ignores how people actually shop. No parent pushing a cart or scrolling on a phone memorizes last month’s price-per-ounce for fifty different household staples.
[P1.3] Even if you squint at today's unit price, you cannot see the change. That opacity distorts competition. A producer that honestly raises its price to cover costs is punished at the shelf, while a rival that quietly hollows out its packaging is rewarded for deception.
[P1.4] Fixing this is straightforward. Supermarkets and food apps run on digital inventory systems that already track product weights, barcodes, and pricing histories. When a supplier replaces a sixteen-ounce item with a fourteen-ounce version without cutting the price, the retailer’s database already registers the shift. Generating an automatic badge on an app or a clear flag on a routine shelf-tag print run is a minor operational tweak.
[P1.5] For families on tight budgets, however, the benefit is immediate. Shrinkflation does not just quietly inflate per-unit costs; it sabotages meal planning when a shrunken package of pasta or meat no longer stretches to feed a family of four, forcing an expensive second purchase. Honest pricing should not require detective work. Requiring clear disclosure simply forces the grocery market to compete in the daylight.

[C1.1] A smaller package at the same price is frustrating, but mandating shrinkflation warnings turns every supermarket and food app into a product historian and arbiter, chasing a comparison shoppers do not need to choose well today.
[C1.2] What matters at the shelf is current value: how much is in this pack, what does it cost, and what does the rival next to it offer per ounce. Net weight is already printed on packaging, and unit prices are already shown on most shelf tags and app listings. If a cereal falls from sixteen to fourteen ounces without a price cut, its current unit price rises accordingly, and that figure lets a shopper compare it instantly with every competitor still available.
[C1.3] A flag about last month's version adds outrage, not information, because last month's version is no longer for sale.
[C1.4] The mandate also collapses on definition and responsibility. Retailers do not decide package sizes; manufacturers do, often replacing one barcode with another, reformulating a recipe, concentrating a detergent, cutting sugar, or changing suppliers. Is a lighter but more concentrated product shrinkflation? How long must a warning stay — thirty days, six months, forever?
[C1.5] Compared with which store, season, or promotional price? Supermarkets stock tens of thousands of lines that churn constantly. Forcing them to prove continuity between old and new items, on pain of penalty, guarantees errors, disputes, and defensive over-labeling.
[C1.6] Those errors have a price. Shelf edges and phone screens are finite; burying them under historical alerts creates clutter that shoppers learn to ignore, just as they ignore other overloaded warnings. Compliance systems, staff time, and redesigns cost money in a low-margin business, especially for small grocers and independent app sellers, and those costs return as higher prices. Voluntary unit-price sorting, clearer app filters, consumer reporting, and competition can improve presentation without freezing one vague, backward-looking label into law.

[C2.1] A badge saying the deal changed does not tell a shopper whether today's pack is still the best value on the shelf. [C2.2] The mapping argument confuses merchandising convenience with legal proof. A supermarket may put a new barcode in an old slot, and an app may suggest it under 'buy again' [P2.3], but that is a loose 'you might like this' link, not a finding that it is the same product, formulation and supplier, changed only by shrinkage. Retailers make such links for out-of-stocks, seasonal variants, flavour rotations and own-brand supplier switches. Turning a helpful suggestion into a liability-bearing accusation of shrinkflation invites the disputes my opponent claims vanish.
[C2.3] The 'regular price' analogy fails too. A retailer controls and can document its own price. It does not control a manufacturer's recipe, concentration or decision to retire a line and launch a successor months later. Food still reformulates without detergent: less sugar, added protein, a different pasta cut, cooked versus drained weight.
[C2.4] Which weight counts, against which predecessor, in a store that may never have stocked it?
[C2.5] Deterrence is not a costing either. If badges deter downsizing, manufacturers can evade them with 'new recipe' relaunches, multiplying definitional fights, or simply raise headline prices, which need no badge. If badges do not deter, they accumulate as clutter. Either way, supermarkets pay to police changes they did not make, while the real decision still rests on the unit price already in front of the shopper.
