[P1.1] Paid youth apprenticeships and employer‑linked career pathways generate higher long‑run earnings than expanding four‑year college subsidies because they deliver income while training, align skills with market demand, and avoid the debt that erodes college graduates’ net returns. The core advantage is timing: apprentices earn a wage from day one, so the opportunity cost of schooling is near zero. In contrast, a subsidized college student typically forgoes four years of earnings and accumulates tens of thousands of dollars in debt, which must be serviced for a decade or more.
[P1.2] Even when a college graduate earns a higher headline salary, the present‑value of that salary is often lower once debt repayment and delayed entry into the labor force are accounted for.
[P1.3] Employer‑linked pathways solve the mismatch that plagues many college graduates. Companies that sponsor apprentices invest in curricula that match their production needs, guaranteeing that trainees acquire skills that command premium wages. Germany’s dual system illustrates the result: apprentices in manufacturing and engineering earn wages comparable to university graduates within five years, and many surpass them after a decade because they have both firm‑specific experience and a clear promotion ladder. In the United States, Registered Apprenticeships in information technology, health care, and advanced manufacturing report completion rates above 80 % and median earnings that exceed those of comparable community‑college graduates after ten years, while participants carry virtually no student debt.
[P1.4] College subsidies, by contrast, inflate enrollment without guaranteeing labor‑market relevance. The surge in four‑year degrees has produced credential inflation: many graduates take jobs that do not require a bachelor’s, earning only modest premiums over high‑school peers while shouldering debt that depresses net earnings. Expanding subsidies therefore raises gross enrollment but not net earnings for the majority of participants.
[P1.5] Thus, when the metric is long‑run earnings, directing public resources toward paid apprenticeships and employer‑linked pathways yields a higher return for youth than further subsidizing traditional college.
