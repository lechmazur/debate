[P1.1] Every major policy decision is a bet on the future. The question in this debate is which oracle governments should trust when placing those bets: a handful of credentialed experts in a closed room, or a market that pays anyone in the world for being right.
[P1.2] Start with track records. Prediction markets have repeatedly outperformed the polls and experts they compete with — election markets calling results pundits missed, firms like Google using internal markets to forecast launches better than their own managers. The mechanism is not magic: markets aggregate dispersed information and attach a cost to being wrong. An expert who forecasts badly loses nothing; a trader who forecasts badly loses money.
[P1.3] That asymmetry is why prices tend to be honest.
[P1.4] Now the status quo. Expert panels suffer from groupthink, career incentives, and political pressure, and they speak in vague, unfalsifiable language — "credible threat," "significant risk" — that can never be scored. The intelligence consensus before Iraq, the near-universal failure to foresee the 2008 crisis: these were failures of panels and internal forecasts, produced with no penalty and no audit trail. A market price is public, timestamped, and gradeable.
[P1.5] We can measure who was right, and that discipline compounds over time.
[P1.6] Why subsidized? Because information is a public good, and thin markets fail. A modest subsidy — trivial against the billions riding on these decisions — pays traders to reveal what they know, just as governments fund weather satellites because private actors underproduce forecasting infrastructure. And "formal input" keeps democracy intact: officials still decide; they simply decide with better numbers than a committee's gut provides.
[P1.7] The obvious objection is manipulation. But attempts to push prices create profit opportunities for informed traders — manipulators end up subsidizing accuracy.
[P1.8] When a government misjudges a pandemic's spread or a program's cost, the public pays. The proposition asks only that the state buy the best available odds before it bets.
