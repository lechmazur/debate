[P1.1] The fastest way to slow warming in the next decade is not to build new infrastructure to bury carbon dioxide, but to stop venting a far more powerful gas we already know how to contain. Methane traps roughly eighty times as much heat as carbon dioxide over twenty years, yet breaks down much faster. That makes every leaked ton a near-term accelerant — and every prevented leak an almost immediate cooling benefit. Carbon capture targets a long-lived gas whose payoff arrives slowly, if it arrives at full scale at all.
[P1.2] Tightening leak detection and repair attacks waste with tools that already exist. Crews with optical gas imaging cameras, drones, aircraft surveys, and continuous sensors can find malfunctioning valves, unlit flares, leaking compressors, and super-emitter sites that account for a disproportionate share of oil and gas methane. Most fixes are unglamorous and cheap: replace a seal, tighten a flange, repair a vapor recovery unit, plug a vent. Because captured gas can often be sold, many repairs pay for themselves, so a dollar of compliance stretches further than a dollar spent subsidising capture plants.
[P1.3] Equivalent spending on carbon capture and storage buys the opposite: high cost, long delays, and uncertain performance. A project needs years of engineering, permitting, pipelines, and injection wells before it stores its first ton. It consumes large amounts of energy, rarely captures every emission from a facility, and can be paired with enhanced oil recovery that brings more hydrocarbons to market. Even successful projects lock in fossil infrastructure while delivering modest, delayed reductions.
[P1.4] On a near-term clock, the comparison is not close. Leak repair can be tightened by rule, deployed across existing fields within months, and verified from the air. It cuts a super-potent pollutant at low, sometimes negative, cost. CCS spends heavily today for partial carbon dioxide reductions tomorrow.
[P1.5] If the goal is the largest climate benefit soon, stop the leaks first.
