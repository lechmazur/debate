[P1.1] The choice between de-extinction and protecting living species is a false one. De-extinction is not a rival budget line; it is a way to build the tools, attention and ecological ambition endangered species desperately need.
[P1.2] A mammoth program requires mastering elephant cell reprogramming, reproductive monitoring and genomic selection. Those platforms can improve breeding, disease resistance and genetic rescue for endangered Asian elephants today. A thylacine program drives marsupial stem-cell and biobanking methods that help devils, quolls and numbats. The spending is dual-use: money aimed at a lost species upgrades the toolbox for living ones.
[P1.3] The opportunity-cost objection misunderstands conservation finance and attention. Conservation is not a fixed pie. De-extinction attracts donors, engineers and media coverage that routine habitat grants never attract. A thylacine or mammoth calf would be the most visible biodiversity event in a generation.
[P1.4] The child drawn by a thylacine does not ignore the Tasmanian devil; she becomes the donor or biologist who saves it. Endangered species usually suffer from invisibility more than competition.
[P1.5] De-extinction also restores missing ecological workers, not museum curiosities. A thylacine-like predator could reestablish predation pressure that shapes prey and vegetation. A mammoth-like grazer could maintain open grasslands and nutrient cycles. The goal is not perfect genetic identity; it is functional resilience that protects many living species at once.
[P1.6] Finally, refusal to fund or permit does not make the technology disappear. It pushes de-extinction into private or offshore hands without public safety, welfare or release standards. Government funding and permitting are how society controls these projects and directs their benefits toward conservation. Rejecting them is not neutral protection; it leaves conservation with weaker tools, lower public energy and fewer options.
