[P1.1] Strong driver opposition is no valid reason to block congestion pricing in central districts, because unrestrained private vehicle access imposes far larger, uncompensated harms on the vast majority of city residents, and congestion pricing is a proven, equitable tool that delivers net public benefit even amid vocal driver resistance. Unrestricted central district driving creates a systemic public drain: traffic congestion costs major cities an estimated $100 billion annually in lost productivity, delayed freight, and excess emissions, while idling vehicle exhaust pumps out dangerous particulate matter that disproportionately harms low-income residents and children living near busy corridors.
[P1.2] In nearly every major city, drivers make up a small minority of central district commuters: for example, just 22% of people traveling to Manhattan’s central business district drive alone, meaning a tiny, vocal minority currently gets to impose these massive costs on the 78% of people who walk, bike, take transit, or carpool. Congestion pricing works exactly as intended, even when drivers fight it hard. London’s 2003 central zone charge was met with furious driver backlash and dire predictions of economic collapse, but it cut congestion in the zone by 30% in its first year, reduced bus travel times by 15%, and cut nitrogen oxide emissions by 13% in its first three years.
[P1.3] Revenue from the charge was directly reinvested in bus service upgrades, expanding reliable transit access for low-income residents who had previously been locked out of affordable central district commutes. Similar results followed in Singapore, Stockholm, and Milan, all of which saw majority public support for the policy grow within two years of implementation, even as initial driver opposition was fierce. The motion does not require ignoring driver concerns entirely – it only rejects the perverse idea that a vocal minority of drivers gets a veto over policies that deliver clear, measurable good for the rest of the city.
