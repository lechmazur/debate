[P1.1] Democracy and consumer autonomy rely on a simple premise: we must be aware of who is trying to persuade us, and how. While the public has rightly demanded labels on AI-generated images and text, a far more insidious threat operates in the background: AI-optimized persuasion campaigns. These are not merely static advertisements; they are dynamic, algorithmically synthesized efforts that leverage massive personal datasets to exploit the specific cognitive biases, anxieties, and psychological profiles of micro-targeted groups. To protect cognitive liberty, major platforms must be required to label these campaigns.
[P1.2] When an algorithm analyzes a user’s behavior to determine they are susceptible to a specific type of fear or grievance, and then deploys AI to optimize the tone, framing, and delivery of a message just for them, it ceases to be ordinary persuasion. It becomes systemic, personalized manipulation. The target has no idea they are being subjected to a custom-tailored psychological probe. Because these campaigns are highly individualized, they evade collective scrutiny.
[P1.3] There is no public town square to debate or fact-check them because no two users see the same version.
[P1.4] Labeling these campaigns restores user agency. A mandatory disclosure—notifying the user that a message has been AI-optimized and targeted to their specific profile—triggers vital cognitive skepticism. It shifts the user from passive target to active critic, neutralizing the subconscious efficacy of the algorithmic hook.
[P1.5] Major platforms already possess the telemetry to implement this; they track every target parameter, impression, and optimization loop. Labeling is not censorship; it is basic consumer protection. It does not silence the speech, it merely unmasks the machine. If a campaign is so powerful that it requires artificial intelligence to micro-target and optimize its persuasive power, the recipient has a right to know.
