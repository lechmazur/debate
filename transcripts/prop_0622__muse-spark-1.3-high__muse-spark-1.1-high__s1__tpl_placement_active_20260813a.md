[P1.1] Climate policy fails when it tries to pick winners with public money instead of making polluters pay.
[P1.2] A rising carbon price attacks emissions at the source, everywhere at once. Whether it's coal power, a gas SUV, a leaky furnace, or methane from production, the price makes the dirty option more expensive and the clean option competitive without a bureaucrat deciding which factory or startup deserves a grant. No application forms, no lobbying contests, no politically connected battery plant that goes bust. Millions of households and firms find their own cheapest way to cut: insulate, carpool, electrify, innovate.
[P1.3] Returning that money as equal checks per person makes high prices both fair and durable. Rich households emit far more — bigger homes, more flights, more stuff — so they pay more in. Everyone gets the same dividend back. That means a nurse who rides the bus or lives in a small apartment typically comes out ahead, while a frequent flyer with three cars pays net.
[P1.4] The climate cost stops falling on the poor while benefits flow upward.
[P1.5] Green industrial subsidies do the opposite. They spend billions of taxpayer dollars to lower costs for favored technologies and firms. That rewards those who already have capital to invest: homeowners who can afford solar roofs and EVs, large corporations with lobbyists and lawyers. Renters, the rural poor, and anyone without upfront cash get little.
[P1.6] Worse, subsidies leave fossil fuels artificially cheap to burn, so we pay twice — once for the subsidy, again for the ongoing damage.
[P1.7] Dividends also solve politics. People can see and feel the return each month or quarter. A higher carbon price becomes a pay raise for most voters, not a tax grab. Subsidies hide costs in deficits and debt, then collapse when budgets tighten.
[P1.8] If the goal is fast, fair, economy-wide cuts without corruption, price pollution and pay people. Don't fund incumbents.

[C1.6] Finally, industrial reality. While America and China pour hundreds of billions into clean manufacturing, a dividends-only model unilaterally disarms. We would tax our own industry then mail checks to import Chinese batteries. If we want fast, sovereign, and just decarbonization, keep a moderate carbon price but invest the revenue.

[P2.1] Price pain without alternatives is a myth because the price is what creates the alternatives. Investors don't build charging networks, landlords don't replace boilers, and factories don't switch fuels out of civic duty; they do it when dirty energy stays expensive and clean bets become profitable. A dividend doesn't send money "to consumption of anything" — it holds purchasing power harmless while the price ratio does the steering.
[P2.2] The split-incentive examples prove too much. Yes, a renter cannot overnight replace a gas furnace, and cement can't decarbonize by next Tuesday [C1.2]. But a subsidy check to a battery maker doesn't insulate that apartment either. Stickiness is an argument for a predictable, rising price that forces owners, utilities and automakers to plan, not for handouts that leave gas cheap to burn.
[P2.3] Short-run inelasticity fades; long-run turnover of cars, furnaces and equipment is where emissions live.
[P2.4] The solar story is misread. Early deployment support helped, but solar truly collapsed in cost when large markets created relentless demand pull and cutthroat competition to meet it. Paying one favored factory learns one factory's lesson. Pricing pollution teaches the whole economy at once — insulation and bus ridership count as much as panels.
[P2.5] Fairness is where dividends win outright. That renting nurse gets cash every quarter with no application, no audit, no landlord permission needed. Means-tested retrofits [C1.5] sound targeted until you meet the waitlist, the paperwork, and the landlord who says no. Subsidies flow to those who already have roofs, capital and lobbyists; dividends flow to everyone.
[P2.6] And sovereignty? Taxing our own pollution and returning the money at home is not disarmament. Begging taxpayers to out-subsidize China is. Most governments cannot win a spending war, and over-subsidized gluts just crash prices and kill domestic makers.
[P2.7] That is fast, fair decarbonization without picking winners.

[C2.6] Solar [P2.4] did not collapse because pollution was priced.
