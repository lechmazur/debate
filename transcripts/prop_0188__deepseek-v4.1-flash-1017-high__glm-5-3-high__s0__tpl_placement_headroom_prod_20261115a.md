[P1.1] Publicly funded research is not a private hobby; it is an investment made on behalf of patients, communities, and taxpayers. When that investment produces evidence, the public has a right to see the whole result, not the flattering half. Mandatory preregistration makes the hypothesis, design, and primary outcomes a matter of record before data arrive. That single commitment changes incentives: researchers cannot quietly move goalposts, run many analyses, and publish only the one that "worked."
[P1.2] In clinical research, the cost is visible—patients may be exposed to treatments whose apparent promise rests on withheld null results. In social science, the same dynamic produces a literature that looks more confident than the evidence beneath it.
[P1.3] Voluntary regimes have had time to work, and the gaps remain because the incentive to hide inconvenient results is structural. If null and negative findings vanish, funders cannot learn which approaches fail, and future researchers repeat the same expensive mistakes. Mandate removes that discretion. The publication guarantee is not a courtesy; it is the enforcement mechanism.
[P1.4] Preregistration without publication just creates a more detailed file drawer. Journals should commit to publishing sound, preregistered studies regardless of outcome—not every flawed study, but those that answer a real question with adequate methods. This is feasible through registered-report models, where acceptance is based on the protocol and analysis plan before results are known.
[P1.5] The proposition does not outlaw exploration. It requires that confirmatory claims be tested against pre-specified plans, and that exploratory findings be labeled as such. That distinction is the difference between a reliable evidence base and a persuasive highlight reel. Public funders should not pay for evidence that is then selectively disappeared.
[P1.6] Mandate preregistration; guarantee publication of sound results; let the public see what it paid for.

[C1.1] Preregistration is a useful tool that becomes a harmful rule when made mandatory and paired with an unconditional publication guarantee. My case rests on three points: the mandate misfires, the guarantee backfires, and together they tax exactly the kind of science the public most needs.
[C1.2] First, mandates punish discovery. The proposition covers all publicly funded clinical and social-science research, not just confirmatory trials. But much of social science is exploratory by design: ethnography, longitudinal fieldwork, qualitative inquiry, machine-learning analyses of new datasets. Forcing a pre-specified hypothesis onto open-ended work doesn't improve it; it forces researchers to invent fake hypotheses retroactively or abandon unorthodox questions that no reviewer would approve in advance.
[C1.3] Some of the most consequential findings in the history of medicine—serendipitous drug effects, unexpected patterns in field data—would have looked like protocol violations under this regime. My opponent says exploratory findings can simply be "labeled as such." In practice, that label is a stigma: journals discount them, funders shy away, and careers suffer. The mandate doesn't accommodate exploration; it quarantines it.
[C1.4] Second, the publication guarantee floods the system. Journals have finite review capacity. Committing to publish every sound, preregistered study regardless of outcome means prioritizing procedural compliance over scientific interest. Peer review gets stretched thinner, and genuinely important null results—the replication failures we actually need—drown in a sea of adequately powered but trivial confirmations.
[C1.5] Publication should track importance and rigor, not paperwork.
[C1.6] Third, preregistration addresses the wrong lever. Selective reporting persists even in preregistered work—outcome switching, data dredging dressed up as exploratory analysis. The underlying disease is a reward system that prizes novelty. A mandate treats the symptom while adding bureaucracy, rigidity, and a new compliance theater that well-resourced labs game and underfunded ones drown in.
[C1.7] Mandatory is not the same as better. Better is targeted: preregister confirmatory trials, encourage it elsewhere, and fix incentives directly.
