[P1.1] Apartment buildings should treat pets as a manageable risk, not a categorical threat. The decisive flaw in blanket pet bans is that they aim at property damage but exclude responsible tenants who are willing to pay for the risk. A default allowing pets when a tenant carries standardized damage insurance or a deposit meets the landlord’s legitimate interest more precisely, avoids severe housing harm, and remains compatible with sensible conduct rules.
[P1.2] The direct case is risk allocation. A deposit or policy does not merely hope a pet behaves; it creates a dedicated fund or covered claim for scratched floors, chewed trim, or pet-related damage. That is exactly what a ban claims to prevent, but without forcing a family to choose between housing and a pet. If the pet causes damage, the landlord has a clearer remedy than a blanket ban that leaves a unit empty while a responsible tenant is turned away.
[P1.3] Blanket bans also do not distinguish a quiet senior cat from a large, poorly trained dog. That is the core weakness: they penalize the animal’s presence rather than actual behavior. A default rule flips the burden to the tenant to show financial responsibility, then leaves landlords free to evict or refuse renewal for genuine nuisance, noise, or a dangerous animal. Insurance addresses property risk; ordinary conduct rules address behavior risk.
[P1.4] Finally, the housing and welfare costs are real. When pet-friendly units are scarce, tenants surrender pets, shelters absorb the burden, and landlords lose a large applicant pool willing to pay deposits and premiums. A default allowing pets with standardized coverage is the only option that protects owners, rewards responsible tenants, and reduces unnecessary pet loss at the same time.

[C1.1] Apartment buildings are not just financial assets to be insured. They are forced shared living — thin walls, common halls, shared air, shared elevators. A deposit can refinish a scratched floor. It cannot refund a neighbor's asthma attack, a night shift worker's sleep destroyed by barking, or a child's fear after being lunged at in a stairwell.
[C1.2] The proposition treats pets as a billing problem. In dense housing, they are a neighbor problem.
[C1.3] Standardized money promises more certainty than it delivers. Policies have caps, exclusions for breeds, for bites, for gradual damage, deductibles and adjusters. Deposits are capped by law in many places and quickly exhausted. Pet damage is not tidy: urine wicks into subfloor and ductwork, odor seeps into adjacent units, flea treatments require whole-building action.
[C1.4] A few hundred dollars held upfront vanishes against torn-out carpet pad, sealed concrete, repainting, and weeks of vacancy while a unit airs out. The landlord is left to chase claims while neighbors live with the residue.
[C1.5] Reversing the default matters enormously. Under a ban, the owner prevents predictable conflict. Under a pet-default, the owner must tolerate the animal and then prove nuisance to remove it. Anyone who has watched a noise complaint or eviction process knows that “evict for real problems” means months of logs, warnings, hearings — months where the complaining tenant cannot sleep, cannot use the courtyard, and simply moves out.
[C1.6] Market variety solves what a mandate cannot. Let some buildings welcome pets and charge accordingly, and let others credibly promise quiet, allergen-reduced, pet-free living. That sorting respects both pet owners and the elderly tenant, the allergic child, the trauma survivor who needs predictability. A universal default does not expand choice; it abolishes the pet-free option.

[P2.6] The better comparison is remedy over exclusion, not the tidy market the opponent assumes exists.
