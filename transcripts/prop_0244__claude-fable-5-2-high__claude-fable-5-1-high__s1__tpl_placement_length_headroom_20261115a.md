[P1.1] The measure here is not whether body cameras did anything. It is whether they delivered what was promised. After Ferguson, the promise was enormous: put a lens on every officer, and misconduct would be deterred, lies exposed and discipline made routine. Measured against that promise, the cameras fell short.
[P1.2] Start with deterrence. Early small studies suggested dramatic drops in force and complaints, and those results sold the policy. But when larger, more rigorous trials followed, most notably a big randomized rollout in Washington, D.C., the effects on use of force and complaints were close to nothing. Reviews of the wider research describe mixed and inconsistent results.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, police in America have kept killing roughly a thousand people every year, a figure that barely moved through the decade of mass camera adoption. Advocates expected a bent curve and got a flat line.
[P1.4] The reason explains why this was predictable. Accountability depends on who controls the evidence, and police control it. Officers often decide when to press record. Departments decide whether and when footage is released, sometimes months later and sometimes only under court order.
[P1.5] In many jurisdictions officers may review the video before writing their reports. In daily practice, the footage is used far more often to prosecute civilians than to discipline officers. A tool sold as oversight of police became, in large part, an evidence-gathering tool for police.
[P1.6] Consider the case that actually shook American policing. The officers who arrested George Floyd were wearing body cameras, yet the department's first public account described a medical incident. What broke that story was a teenager's cell phone. In Memphis, the officers who beat Tyre Nichols were wearing cameras too, and the cameras did not restrain them.
[P1.7] Cameras sometimes help; that concession costs me nothing. The proposition is about the gap between promise and performance, and CON must show that gap does not exist.
