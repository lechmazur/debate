[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether a four-year college degree can be valuable, but which expansion raises the long-run earnings of the marginal young person most reliably. Paid youth apprenticeships and employer-linked pathways win because they combine earnings, skills, credentials, and direct attachment to work.
[P1.2] College subsidies mostly lower the price of enrollment. That can help some students, but at the margin it often pulls in young people who are poorly matched to academic programs, uncertain about majors, or likely to stop out. They forego four or more years of earnings, may borrow, and if they leave without a degree they carry costs without the credential’s wage premium. The policy pays for inputs—tuition and attendance—rather than outputs—employment and productivity.
[P1.3] Apprenticeships do the opposite. A paid apprentice in construction, advanced manufacturing, health technology, IT support, or logistics earns while learning, accumulates work experience, and receives a credential tied to tasks employers actually value. The employer helps design the curriculum, supervises on-the-job training, and often retains the apprentice. That reduces search frictions, credential inflation, and the mismatch that leaves many graduates underemployed.
[P1.4] The long-run earnings effect comes from three compounding mechanisms. First, earlier labor-force attachment: the apprentice’s wages, references, and seniority start years before a college graduate enters the job market. Second, lower downside risk: the participant builds a marketable skill even if the pathway is not perfect, without the same debt or lost-time penalty.
[P1.5] Third, stronger employer demand: employer-linked training is more likely to expand where firms have real vacancies and wage growth, not where students simply hope degrees will pay off.
[P1.6] This is not anti-college. It is about the better marginal dollar. If the goal is lifetime earnings, paying young people to acquire productive, certified skills inside firms is more direct, less risky, and more likely to translate into durable wages than expanding tuition subsidies for traditional four-year enrollment.

[C1.2] That is why the earnings gap typically widens with age rather than shrinking.
[C1.3] The tempting case is lower risk and earlier attachment. But “long-run” is the operative word. Starting at a modest wage at eighteen and reaching a journeyman rate at twenty-five is not the same as entering nursing, engineering, accounting, or software at twenty-two and then compounding raises, promotions, and mobility for decades. The apprentice may avoid debt; the college graduate may carry some, but the subsidy reduces that burden and the degree’s lifetime premium remains the better bet.
[C1.4] If completion is the problem, subsidize completion and advising, not abandonment of the strongest earnings engine.
[C1.5] Employer-linked pathways also have a structural problem: employers underinvest in general skills because trained workers can be poached. Publicly subsidized four-year enrollment, for all its flaws, creates transferable human capital that workers can carry across firms, industries, and regions.
[C1.6] Scale matters too. Apprenticeship slots depend on employer demand; subsidy dollars cannot conjure enough high-wage placements. College subsidies can reach far more students and steer them toward high-return fields. The question is not whether apprenticeships help some people.
[C1.7] It is whether expanding them raises long-run earnings more than expanding college access. The earlier paycheck is visible; the higher lifetime curve is decisive.
