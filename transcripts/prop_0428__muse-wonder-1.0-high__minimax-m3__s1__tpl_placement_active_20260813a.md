[P1.1] A neighborhood with nowhere to gather is not quiet and safe; it is just empty. When the only legal places to exist are private homes and expensive restaurants, teenagers hang out in parking lots anyway, older people sit alone, and everyone else drives elsewhere to find company. Allowing more low-cost cafés, hobby clubs, and late-hour social spaces gives people a nearby, affordable place to be together.
[P1.2] That matters most for people who do not own a large living room to host in. Renters, young people living with family, shift workers whose day starts at night, newcomers who know no one — they cannot buy community through dinner parties or club memberships. A chess club above a shop, a café open after work, a room for music practice or board games costs a coffee or a few dollars, not rent. Block it, and you reserve social life for those already housed comfortably enough to provide it themselves.
[P1.3] The objections are real frictions, but manageable frictions, not reasons for a veto. Noise can be addressed with conditions on outdoor areas, sound limits, and enforcement when actual disturbance happens. Parking pressure can be eased with permits, bike racks, shared lots after business hours, and the fact that a walkable local venue replaces car trips to distant venues. A worry about what might happen should trigger rules for how a place operates, not a ban on it existing.
[P1.4] The alternative has its own noise and parking costs, just exported. Drive-everywhere neighborhoods generate traffic, late-night returns home, and streets clogged by residents forced to leave to socialize. Local venues shorten trips, let people walk home, and put eyes and activity on streets that would otherwise be dark and deserted.
[P1.5] Residents deserve protection from genuine harm. They do not deserve a right to keep their block frozen against everyone who would benefit from having somewhere to go.
