[P1.1] Credit reports are prediction tools, not moral ledgers. A debt belongs there only if it helps answer: will this person repay future voluntary credit as agreed? Medical debt fails. It begins with illness, injury, or emergency care, not a shopping decision.
[P1.2] Patients do not choose a diagnosis, compare prices while being stabilized, or control an insurer's approval or denial. The bill measures a health shock and an administrative maze, not budgeting or willingness to pay.
[P1.3] Ordinary credit is different. A card balance or auto loan reflects choosing credit, accepting terms, then making repeated payment choices. A medical collection reflects getting sick and being billed through a system where amounts may be disputed, adjusted, or unclear for months. Some bills are real; some are errors; some are pending assistance or insurance review.
[P1.4] A field mixing illness, bureaucracy, and possible inability cannot isolate future repayment behavior. It is noise dressed as a credit signal.
[P1.5] Reporting it produces false positives. Take two workers with identical wages and clean histories. One has a child's surgery bill in collections during insurance review; the other has no medical event. A lender sees the first as riskier, though the bill tells us nothing about who will pay next month's rent or a future loan.
[P1.6] The report turns bad luck into bad credit, raising rates and blocking housing when the family can least absorb it. Lenders need signal, not every biographical wound.
[P1.7] Treating all debt as informative confuses the purpose of a report. Providers can bill, resolve disputes, or use ordinary collection channels; they should not wield a credit entry that follows a patient through scoring and review. An unpaid elective loan says something about chosen borrowing. An unpaid oncology bill mostly says cancer happened.
[P1.8] Because illness is not a meaningful indicator of future repayment behavior, medical debt should not appear on consumer credit reports at all.
