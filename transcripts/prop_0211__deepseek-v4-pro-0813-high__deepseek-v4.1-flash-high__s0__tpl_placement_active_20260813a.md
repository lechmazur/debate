[P1.1] A health system that pays full list price for every preventive obesity prescription is buying a promise, not an outcome. Broad reimbursement means potentially millions of patients, long treatment durations, and very high per-patient costs. Unless payment is tied to results or capped, payers are writing an open-ended check with no guarantee that real-world use produces durable health improvement. That is not a defensible use of pooled funds.
[P1.2] The central weakness of unconditional coverage is the evidence-practice gap. Clinical trials overstate real-world value because patients miss doses, discontinue, regain weight, or stop due to side effects. Without outcome-based pricing, the manufacturer receives the same revenue whether a patient loses substantial weight and keeps it off for years, or fills the prescription for a few months and stops. That weakens the incentive to support adherence, long-term safety, or formulations that maintain benefit.
[P1.3] Outcome-based pricing fixes this. Rebates or price adjustments can be tied to durable weight loss, reduced progression to diabetes, improved cardiovascular risk, or persistence. If the drug delivers, the manufacturer earns more; if it does not, the health system recovers money. Spending caps address the budget side, bounding total liability when uptake is faster or longer than forecast.
[P1.4] Together they turn an unmanageable open-ended commitment into a value-based, budgetable contract.
[P1.5] The alternative is not more access; it is less access elsewhere. Health systems have fixed budgets. Paying unconditional full price for broad obesity drug coverage will force cuts to vaccinations, cancer screening, mental health care, or other proven services. Without caps or risk-sharing, broad reimbursement invites rationing by fiscal crisis rather than by clinical value.
[P1.6] Finally, manufacturers’ refusal to accept these terms is itself informative. If companies believe their drugs are truly cost-effective in broad real-world use, outcome-based pricing should be attractive, not threatening. Demanding unconditional payment while health systems bear all the uncertainty signals that the value proposition is weaker than claimed. Broad reimbursement should therefore be conditional: cover these drugs broadly only when price tracks results or total spending is capped.

[C1.1] The motion does not say negotiate hard. It says “only when.” That single phrase turns a sensible bargaining tactic into a coverage veto: if manufacturers refuse outcome-based contracts or spending caps, patients are denied broad access even when the drug is effective and cheaper than treating the diseases it prevents.
[C1.2] Outcome-based pricing is superficially attractive and practically treacherous in prevention. What is the outcome? Five percent weight loss at one year? Maintenance at three years?
[C1.3] Avoidance of diabetes? Cardiovascular events? Those endpoints differ, take years to mature, and are confounded by adherence, diet, income, and genetics. A manufacturer can game the metric by targeting easy patients or abandoning hard ones; a health system can penalize the very patients who need support.
[C1.4] Building valid real-world registries and adjudicating rebates is expensive and slow. In the meantime, coverage is frozen.
[C1.5] Spending caps are no better as a precondition. A cap is a budget ceiling, not a measure of value. When it is hit, the rational response is to stop covering or restrict eligibility mid-year. A patient who has started a chronic preventive therapy is then cut off by accounting, not by a change in medical evidence.
[C1.6] That is rationing with extra steps.
[C1.7] Preventive obesity drugs can reduce diabetes, cardiovascular events, and joint disease. Their costs are real, but so are downstream costs. Health systems can negotiate prices, buy in bulk, use formularies, require prior authorization, and let competition lower prices. They should do all of that.
[C1.8] They should not make broad coverage conditional on one particular contract design, because that lets manufacturers’ willingness to sign determine whether patients get treated. Obesity is concentrated in low-income and rural populations least able to pay cash. A conditional gate becomes an access gate for the poor. Outcome contracts can be useful experiments, but not a universal precondition.
[C1.9] The proposition replaces medical need with a procurement ultimatum. That is why it fails.

[P2.6] For low-income patients, conditional contracts can also generate rebates that finance broader access; unconditional full price is more likely to collapse.
[P2.7] The entire force of the reversal is simple. Under unconditional coverage, payers own all the risk while manufacturers own all the upside. [P2.8] That should not be considered an extreme condition when pooled money is at stake.

[P4.1] A1: A cap caps total spending by capping the marginal expansion, not by revoking existing therapy. Existing patients are a known, budgeted cohort; the open-ended variable is new starts. When projected spending approaches the cap, the system can stop new enrollments, require rebates, or renegotiate price before anyone is cut off. That bounds total liability while preserving continuity.
[P4.2] If the manufacturer wants more patients, it lowers price or shares risk. So the cap is not a fake ceiling; it controls the only part of spending that is actually uncontrolled.
[P4.3] A2: The manufacturer absorbs the rebate loss, and that is exactly why the incentive works. The patient is not excluded; the contract can require coverage of all eligible patients and risk-adjust for housing, cost, or side effects. If a high-need patient stops adhering, the manufacturer loses revenue and therefore has a reason to fund adherence support, adjust formulation, or accept lower payment. Under unconditional coverage, the payer absorbs that loss and the manufacturer has no reason to help.
[P4.4] Exclusion is a contract design problem, not an inherent feature; we can forbid cherry-picking.
[P4.5] Those answers expose the opponent’s dilemma. They want broad access without manufacturer risk-sharing, but their own questions show that unconditional coverage either blows the budget or leaves high-need patients as poor bets with no support. The motion is not a veto; it is the only way to make broad reimbursement sustainable. If manufacturers refuse, that refusal—not the condition—denies access.
[P4.6] PRO wins because conditional payment turns uncertainty into shared responsibility, protecting both budgets and patients.
