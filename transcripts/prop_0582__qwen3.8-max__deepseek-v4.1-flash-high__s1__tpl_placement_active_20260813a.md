[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether GLP-1 drugs have list prices; it is whether they prevent enough expensive medical events in the next five years to outweigh those prices. In Medicare, the answer is yes, because the eligible population is concentrated among people with obesity-related cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and heart failure—the patients most likely to generate near-term hospital bills.
[P1.2] Clinical evidence already shows that GLP-1 therapy can produce sustained, clinically meaningful weight loss and reduce major cardiovascular events in high-risk adults. That matters quickly. A prevented heart attack avoids the ambulance, cath lab, hospital stay, rehab, and follow-up complications. A prevented stroke avoids long-term disability and nursing-home costs.
[P1.3] Slower progression of kidney disease avoids dialysis, one of the most expensive treatments in Medicare. Fewer new diabetes cases and better glycemic control reduce insulin use, infections, amputations, and emergency visits. Even partial reductions in sleep apnea, heart-failure exacerbations, and mobility-limiting osteoarthritis translate into fewer admissions and less post-acute spending.
[P1.4] Broad Medicare coverage is what turns these clinical effects into system-wide savings. Without coverage, high out-of-pocket costs force rationing, interruption, and underdosing, which undermines durability and erases benefits. With coverage, treatment can be continuous, targeted, and integrated into routine care. Because Medicare is such a large payer, broader coverage also shifts clinical norms and scale, making these savings visible across the US health system.
[P1.5] The obvious objection focuses on pharmacy outlays, but total health-care spending includes the far larger costs of acute events, chronic complications, and long-term care.
