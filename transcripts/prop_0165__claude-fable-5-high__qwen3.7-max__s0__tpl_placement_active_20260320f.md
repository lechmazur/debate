[P1.1] Imagine pulling into a gas station and being told your car can't fuel here because you drive a Honda, not a Ford. Governments should end this with mandatory open interoperability standards, and the case rests on three pillars.
[P1.2] First, charging is infrastructure, not a consumer gadget. Roads, electrical grids, and telecom networks all run on mandated common standards because fragmentation in infrastructure destroys its value. A charger only useful to a fraction of drivers is a half-built bridge. Public money already subsidizes most charging networks; taxpayers should not fund walled gardens that lock value inside one company's ecosystem.
[P1.3] Second, interoperability accelerates EV adoption itself, which is the entire policy goal. Range anxiety is really reliability anxiety: drivers fear arriving at a charger they cannot use. The market's most successful actor conceded the point.
[P1.4] Third, open standards intensify competition where it matters. Proprietary lock-in lets networks compete on captivity—forcing app downloads, memberships, and opaque pricing. [P1.5] The objection that mandates freeze technology fails: standards bodies version and upgrade constantly—USB went from trickle to 240 watts without breaking compatibility. The real choice isn't standards versus innovation; it's open competition versus private toll booths on public infrastructure. Drivers, taxpayers, and the climate all do better with open standards.
