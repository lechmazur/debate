[P1.1] The word "requiring" is doing crucial work in this proposition, and it should anchor everything that follows. Nobody here is arguing that workers must be shielded from useful technology. The question is whether an employer may *compel* a worker to submit, for every hour of the workday, to a system that watches their keystrokes, measures their pauses, prompts them when they seem distracted, and coaches them toward productivity targets the worker never set. That is a fundamentally different thing, and prohibiting it is justified on three grounds.
[P1.2] First, power. Employment is not a symmetric negotiation. Workers who need their jobs cannot meaningfully consent to conditions that carry implicit threats of termination. When an employer mandates an always-on AI companion, a worker's nominal "agreement" is coerced.
[P1.3] Coerced surveillance is not a feature; it is a harm.
[P1.4] Second, psychological integrity. Continuous monitoring does not merely record behavior—it colonizes cognition. When a person knows they are being watched, prompted, and scored in real time, they stop thinking and start performing for the system. Research on surveillance consistently shows it raises anxiety, suppresses creative risk-taking, and degrades the very judgment it purports to improve.
[P1.5] The most valuable cognitive work—complex problem-solving, ethical reasoning, genuine collaboration—requires mental space that always-on coaching systematically destroys.
[P1.6] Third, dignity. There is a meaningful moral line between a tool a worker chooses to use and a supervisor that never blinks, never leaves, and cannot be switched off. Reducing workers to optimizable units overseen by an AI that prompts them to stay on task treats persons as instruments of production rather than as agents with standing. Labor law historically draws exactly this kind of line—limiting invasive drug tests, restricting lie detectors—because some monitoring regimes are incompatible with the respect owed to workers as human beings.
[P1.7] The CON side will argue efficiency and consent. Neither survives scrutiny once we take the coercion problem seriously, and that is where this debate will be decided.
