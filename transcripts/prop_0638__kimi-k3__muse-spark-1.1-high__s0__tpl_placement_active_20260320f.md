[P1.1] Judge a funding system for fundamental science by one thing: whether it produces discoveries that change the world. By that measure, short-cycle competitive grants are failing, and long-term block grants win.
[P1.2] Start with horizons. Fundamental breakthroughs typically take a decade or more; project grants run two to five years and demand near-term deliverables. Rational researchers therefore propose only what they can almost guarantee, so the real gambles never get funded. Katalin Karikó spent years unable to win grants and was demoted for persisting with mRNA research — the very science that later made COVID-19 vaccines possible.
[P1.3] Under short-cycle logic, that work dies. Under funding that trusts people, it runs.
[P1.4] Add the selection effect. When success rates hover around one in five, review panels reward the predictable and punish the unconventional. Surveys of researchers suggest a third or more of working time goes to writing and reviewing proposals — a colossal tax on the talent we claim to support. Curiosity-driven work on obscure bacterial immune systems, with no application in sight, gave us CRISPR; no three-year proposal promising "gene editing" would have described it in advance.
[P1.5] Now the accountability the other side will defend: it is accounting, not accountability. Counting milestones and papers measures busyness, not discovery; nature does not reward tidy paperwork. Genuine accountability for fundamental science is retrospective — fund strong institutions and people, then judge the portfolio every several years, as with the Max Planck Institutes or HHMI's "people, not projects" model. That model reliably produces outsized breakthroughs.
[P1.6] The motion concedes less bean-counting per dollar. Accept the trade gladly: the beans were never the point, and lost decades cannot be audited back into existence. The real choice is between watching every dollar produce nothing surprising and trusting capable scientists to occasionally produce the insight that rewrites medicine, energy, or computing. Governments should buy discovery, not documentation.
