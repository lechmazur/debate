[P1.1] Police misconduct costs taxpayers billions annually—settlements, legal fees, payouts—while the officers responsible rarely pay a dime. This isn’t just unfair; it’s a moral hazard. When taxpayers foot the bill, officers face no personal financial consequence for misconduct, and departments lack urgency to discipline or reform. Requiring personal professional liability insurance fixes this by making officers—not the public—bear the cost of their actions.
[P1.2] In professions like law or medicine, personal liability insurance is standard. A lawyer’s malpractice insurance covers their errors; a doctor’s covers their negligence. This isn’t about punishing officers—it’s about aligning incentives. If an officer’s insurance premiums rise with misconduct, they have a direct financial reason to avoid excessive force, false arrests, or cover-ups.
[P1.3] Taxpayers, meanwhile, stop subsidizing recklessness.
[P1.4] Critics will claim officers can’t afford insurance, but that’s a solvable design problem. Premiums could be income-adjusted, or departments could offer subsidies—but the key is that the cost is tied to the officer’s risk, not the public’s. Unlike current systems, where a single bad apple’s mistakes drain community funds, insurance ensures the individual pays. This isn’t just fair; it’s practical.
[P1.5] It reduces taxpayer burden, incentivizes better policing, and mirrors accountability standards already accepted in other high-stakes professions. The status quo lets officers gamble with public money—insurance forces them to gamble with their own.

When an officer’s premium rises after a claim, the financial signal hits the individual, not the general fund.
[P2.3] Moreover, because premiums are actuarially linked to behavior, the total outlay can be lower than today’s unpredictable settlement spikes, saving taxpayers in the long run.

Insurers price risk; they do not ban activities, they charge more for them. [P2.5] Finally, you say [C1.4] internal affairs already punish misconduct, so insurance is redundant. The result is slower crime suppression and greater long-term harm, none of which is offset by internal-affairs reforms that already exist.
[C2.4] Because the policy adds a new layer of financial risk without altering the legal standards or evidentiary thresholds for misconduct, it duplicates existing accountability mechanisms at the cost of operational flexibility and staffing stability.
