[P1.1] When you buy milk you get an expiry date. When you buy a $900 smart phone, TV, or baby monitor - a device that will spy on you or open your door if its software isn't maintained - you get silence. That silence is profitable, and it's destroying consumer agency, security, and the planet.
[P1.2] A smart device is not hardware alone. Without security patches, OS updates, and app compatibility it becomes unsafe e-waste. A thermostat bricked after 18 months or a phone cut off after 2 years isn't a choice consumers made; it's a trap they couldn't see. The support lifespan IS the product lifespan.
[P1.3] Manufacturers know exactly how long they intend to support it - roadmaps, contracts with chip vendors, internal EOL dates. They hide it because short support is embarrassing and planned obsolescence is lucrative. The market can't reward long support if buyers can't compare it. Apple now offers 6+ years, some Android brands 2.
[P1.4] Same price, same shelf, totally different value. Without a label, you never know.
[P1.5] Requiring it on physical packaging fixes that at the point that matters: before purchase, in your hand, not buried in a website you have to hunt 3 hours later, not behind a QR code that dies when the company does. It's unavoidable, comparable, and enforceable like energy labels or ingredient lists. A simple line: "Guaranteed security & functionality updates until at least June 2030."
[P1.6] That creates competition on longevity, not just megapixels. It cuts e-waste - people keep what stays safe. It protects the internet itself - unsupported cameras and routers fuel botnets that attack hospitals. And it's cheap - one printed line is nothing next to designing the box.
[P1.7] Opponents will call it rigid. Good. A guarantee should be rigid. If you can't stand behind a date on the box, don't sell a device that lives or dies by your continued support.

[C1.1] The motion treats a living software relationship as if it were a carton of milk. A guaranteed support lifespan on every smart-device box is not missing honesty. It is a single number forced onto products whose real life depends on security patches, feature freezes, cloud contracts, app stores, and chip vendors who never appear on the carton.
[C1.2] That number will be a lawyer's number. Once printed, the rational move is the shortest date a court will accept, not the longest engineers might deliver. Extend later and the label looks incomplete. Miss it and you have a warranty-class failure.
[C1.3] Midrange phones, cameras, and routers get the same cautious floor, and the floor becomes the ceiling. People bin working devices because the box said so. Firms that would have patched quietly stop on the printed day.
[C1.4] Physical packaging is the wrong instrument. Most buyers never see the retail carton until the choice is made — online, in a mailer, after a gift. Global SKUs share one shell. A fifteen-dollar plug, a bulb, a kids' watch, a tracker from a two-person firm cannot carry a multi-year legal guarantee without vanishing or getting more expensive.
[C1.5] The mandate hammers the cheapest gadgets and barely touches brands already advertising long support.
[C1.6] A date on cardboard does not keep a start-up alive, force a chip vendor to keep shipping firmware, or resurrect a dead cloud. Botnets and e-waste come from abandoned backends and platforms that drop old hardware. Ink does not change that. It just freezes an oversimplified promise at print time and calls the result agency.
[C1.7] Buyers need living policies where they actually shop, updated when support changes. A rigid line on a box trains everyone to treat the minimum as the product. That manufactures shorter lives, not longer ones — and it does it in the name of a guarantee too crude to be true.
