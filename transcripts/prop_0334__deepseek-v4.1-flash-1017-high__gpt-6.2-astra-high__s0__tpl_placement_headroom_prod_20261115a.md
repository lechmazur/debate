[P1.1] Political content is where takedowns fail most predictably. A misleading edited video, a candidate’s quote stripped of context, a statistic that flips meaning when the baseline disappears: these are contested, fast-moving, and often posted by actors who want removal to become the story. Proactive takedowns force platforms to decide truth and intent before the public has seen the evidence, behind closed doors, at scale. That is both error-prone and legitimacy-destroying.
[P1.2] Community-note style context does the opposite: it keeps the speech visible, attaches reasoning and sources, and lets readers judge for themselves. Takedowns also teach speakers that controversial politics may vanish without explanation, chilling exactly the speech democracy needs.
[P1.3] The central comparison is not “perfect notes versus imperfect takedowns.” It is “visible, contestable correction versus opaque, irreversible suppression” for the category of speech where line-drawing is hardest. Political speech is uniquely vulnerable to strategic reporting, partisan pressure, and incumbent capture. Every mistaken takedown is a gift to demagogues: it supplies proof that the platform is hiding something. A note that says “this video is edited” or “this quote continues differently” can neutralize the trick without creating a martyr.
[P1.4] Notes also scale with the problem. Political misinformation travels through screenshots, reposts, and remixes; removing one copy invites another. Context can travel with the claim and remain attached to copies. And notes create a public record that can be challenged and improved, while takedowns produce no shared record except suspicion.
[P1.5] So the policy should invert the default: annotate first, escalate to removal only for narrow categories like direct incitement, credible threats, or coordinated fraud. Takedowns remain the exception; context becomes the governing layer. That is how a platform governs political speech without pretending to be the ministry of truth.
