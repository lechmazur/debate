[P1.1] Adolescents should gain a clear statutory right to demand removal of family-vlog material that names them, shows their daily routines, or records moments they never chose to publicize. The reason is straightforward: once a child reaches the age at which society already grants partial legal capacity for medical consent and educational choices, continued parental control over that child’s searchable digital identity becomes an unjust extension of authority.
[P1.2] Family vlogs convert ordinary childhood into perpetual public record. A single uploaded tantrum or medical visit remains indexed years later, shaping how future employers, peers, and romantic partners first encounter the person. Because the original decision to publish was made by parents pursuing views or income, the resulting exposure cannot be defended as the child’s own exercise of expression. Granting adolescents a removal mechanism simply restores parity between the person depicted and the person who benefits from the depiction.
[P1.3] Opponents often claim that such a right would chill family expression or invite frivolous demands. That objection collapses once the request is limited to the adolescent’s own identifiable presence rather than the entire video; parents retain the footage for private use and can still post anonymized or edited versions that exclude the objecting child. The narrower right therefore targets only the non-consensual publicity, not the underlying family activity.
[P1.4] Without this mechanism, adolescents remain legally tethered to content that can be weaponized through doxxing, school bullying, or reputational scraping. The asymmetry is especially stark because the same legal system already recognizes that adolescents possess developing autonomy in other domains. Extending that recognition to digital self-presentation prevents one generation’s monetization choices from imposing lifelong, irreversible costs on the next.
