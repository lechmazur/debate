[P1.1] You can cancel a stolen credit card in thirty seconds; you cannot replace your face, your iris, or the vein pattern in your palm. When shops, transit networks, and public venues turn human bodies into payment tokens, they ask people to entrust permanent biological identifiers to commercial systems that can be breached, subpoenaed, or linked to physical movement across daily life. Handing over irrevocable biometric data must always be a genuine choice, never the price of participation in ordinary commerce and public space.
[P1.2] That choice disappears unless consumers always retain a convenient non-biometric way to pay. Where venues go biometric-only, commuters, grocery shoppers, and concertgoers face an outright ultimatum: scan your body or stay outside. Where venues keep only an inconvenient opt-out, like a lone service window across a stadium or a twenty-minute manual queue, they manufacture consent through punitive friction. Real choice requires an equally accessible option right at the point of payment, such as tapping a standard bank or transit card or paying cash, without detours or surcharges.
[P1.3] Biometric exclusivity also punishes people whose bodies or circumstances do not fit a scanner’s assumptions. Construction workers with worn fingerprints, visitors unacquainted with a local vendor’s enrollment app, people with limb differences, cataracts, or facial scarring, individuals wearing religious face coverings, and groups facing higher sensor error rates should not have to beg an attendant for an exception just to catch a train or buy lunch. Keeping standard payment methods open also ensures that camera or network glitches never paralyze essential services.
[P1.4] Most importantly, this safeguard costs innovation nothing. Businesses can still deploy palm or facial payment for every customer who wants it. Because ordinary contactless card readers are compact, inexpensive, and clear transactions in roughly a second, even at automated gates, preserving a convenient non-biometric option surrenders no meaningful speed while keeping everyday life free from biological coercion.

Consent, deletion duties, template protection, breach liability, tracking limits, audits, and scan-failure accommodations address that directly; a second payment method does not. [C1.6] If it means “always equal,” the rule invites litigation and blocks deployments before benefits are tested.
[C1.7] Those benefits include children, unbanked customers, people with dexterity impairments, commuters with dead phones, and workers who cannot handle cards or cash cleanly. The objection is coercion or negligent data use, not biometric efficiency. A universal duplicate-payment mandate is the wrong instrument, and the proposed restriction should be rejected.
