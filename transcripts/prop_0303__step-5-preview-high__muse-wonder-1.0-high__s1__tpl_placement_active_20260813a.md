[P1.1] Meritocracy should make birth irrelevant. In practice, elite pathways begin filtering long before the supposed neutral competition. Affluent children inherit stronger schools, tutoring, admissions coaching, safer neighborhoods, family networks, and early exposure to law, finance, medicine, and technology. By the time they sit for admissions tests or case interviews, the traits labeled talent have already been purchased, rehearsed, and made to look natural.
[P1.2] The selection stage then converts advantage into credential. Legacy preferences, donor access, referral hiring, unpaid internships, and polished social signaling operate alongside grades and test scores. A first-generation applicant with a 3.7 and two jobs is not merely competing with a wealthy peer holding a 3.9; she is competing with a system that treats connections, unpaid labor, and cultural fluency as neutral measures of ability. The credential is then treated as if it had been produced from scratch.
[P1.3] Success narratives hide this. Effort and talent are real and necessary, but necessity is not sufficiency. The legacy student who worked hard for a high score benefited from conditions that made that score easier to reach. When the pipeline later rewards class-coded behaviors as leadership or fit, the winner can honestly say, “I earned it,” while the inheritance inside the process disappears from view.
[P1.4] Most successful people are not lying; they are explaining their effort, not the head start.
[P1.5] The reward stage confirms the pattern. Elite education leads into occupations where family contacts, school reputation, sponsorship, and unpaid risk-bearing often matter more after admission than any exam. Across rich democracies, adult outcomes remain strongly tied to parental position, even where mobility is higher. Meritocratic language then does political work: it tells losers their failure is personal and tells winners their advantage is moral.
[P1.6] Until institutions stop converting inherited resources into credentialed merit at preparation, selection, and reward, they are inheritance systems wearing competitive rituals.

[C1.1] Meritocracy never promised identical childhoods. It promised something historically radical: that elite places would be allocated increasingly by demonstrated ability rather than by birth, title, or direct purchase. Judged by that standard, modern pathways are not an inheritance system in disguise — they are the main reason inherited status has lost its veto.
[C1.2] Consider what selection actually rewards today. Many elite universities run need-blind admissions for domestic applicants, meet full financial need, and actively recruit first-generation and low-income students through free-access programs. Professional schools gate entry with standardized tests and graded performance, and major employers in law, medicine, consulting, finance, and technology screen for analytical tests, work samples, degrees, and structured interviews. Legacy and donor preferences exist, and they should be narrowed, but they involve a shrinking minority of seats; they cannot explain a system where the overwhelming majority must clear the same exams, grade curves, licensing, and performance reviews.
[C1.3] Preparation does not disprove merit either. Tutoring, stable homes, and good schools can build real reading, mathematics, and discipline — and when a student then scores higher and performs in college or on the job, institutions are rewarding competence that now exists, not faking it. Parental income correlates with outcomes partly because parents transmit habits, knowledge, language, and occupational information. That transmission is human development, not evidence that selectors prefer rich children for being rich.
[C1.4] Finally, successful people routinely admit this context. The standard graduation and interview script is now luck, mentors, family sacrifice, and timing alongside effort — hardly collective amnesia. What gets called denial is often refusal to accept the stronger claim that effort was irrelevant.
[C1.5] Class still confers advantages worth reducing. But advantage at the starting line is not proof that the race is rigged at preparation, selection, and reward, or that today's credentials mainly launder privilege. They mainly certify skills that predict real performance, and they have opened elite roles to people birth alone would once have excluded entirely.
