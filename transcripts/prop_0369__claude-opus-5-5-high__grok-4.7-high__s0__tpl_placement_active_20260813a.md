[P1.1] Defaults are never neutral. Whatever an app preselects is what most people end up buying, because people ordering dinner are tired, hungry, and tapping fast. So the real question is not whether apps should influence choices. They already do.
[P1.2] The question is whether that influence should serve the customer or quietly drain them.
[P1.3] Right now, the tilt runs one direction. Open a typical delivery app and you'll often find delivery preselected over pickup, a "priority" tier nudged forward with urgent language, a pre-checked tip, service and small-order fees revealed only at checkout, and pickup tucked behind a toggle. None of that is accidental. Each design choice routes the customer toward the path that earns the platform the most.
[P1.4] A family ordering from a pizzeria five minutes away can pay several dollars in fees for a trip they would happily have made themselves, simply because the screen never asked.
[P1.5] PRO's case rests on three points.
[P1.6] First, defaults should track likely preferences, not maximum revenue. When the restaurant is close, the customer has picked up before, or standard delivery is only minutes slower than priority, the cheaper option is plausibly what a reasonable person wants. Defaulting there respects the user; defaulting to the premium tier exploits inattention.
[P1.7] Second, this preserves choice completely. Nobody loses delivery. Anyone who wants the courier at their door taps once. CON can't claim we're restricting freedom when the expensive option remains one tap away.
[P1.8] We are only changing who bears the burden of the extra tap, the platform's margin instead of the customer's wallet.
[P1.9] Third, honest defaults build durable value. Platforms that feel like they're squeezing users breed fee fatigue, distrust, and regulatory backlash. Restaurants benefit too, because pickup orders often carry lower commissions and bring customers through the door. Fewer unnecessary car trips also mean less congestion near busy restaurants.
[P1.10] So judge this debate on one test: when the user isn't paying close attention, whose interest should the app serve? If the answer is the user's, PRO wins.
