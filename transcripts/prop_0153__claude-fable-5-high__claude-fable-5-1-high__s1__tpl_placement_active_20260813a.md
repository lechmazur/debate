[P1.1] Every year, thousands of couples—infertile, gay, cancer survivors—will pursue surrogacy whether their governments permit it or not. That is the fact this debate must start from. The question is never surrogacy versus no surrogacy. It is regulated surrogacy at home versus a gray market abroad and underground arrangements with no protections at all.
[P1.2] Prohibition does not end demand; it exports it to jurisdictions with the weakest oversight, where the women my opponent claims to protect are most vulnerable.
[P1.3] Three arguments carry the case.
[P1.4] First, the comparative reality. When countries ban compensated surrogacy, intended parents travel to wherever enforcement is thinnest—historically places with minimal screening, no independent legal counsel for surrogates, and no guaranteed healthcare. Bans in wealthy nations don't protect anyone; they shift the risk onto poorer women elsewhere while stripping domestic courts of any power to intervene. Legalization brings the practice into the light, where regulators can actually see it.
[P1.5] Second, autonomy with real safeguards. A woman who can consent to dangerous, poorly paid work—mining, military service, commercial fishing—can consent to carry a pregnancy for fair compensation. The motion explicitly demands strong protections: mandatory independent legal representation, psychological screening, health insurance, the right to make her own medical decisions, cooling-off periods, and compensation held in escrow. "Altruistic-only" regimes don't remove pressure; they just ensure the surrogate is the only participant—not the clinic, not the lawyer, not the agency—who works for free.
[P1.6] That isn't ethics. It's extraction dressed up as virtue.
[P1.7] Third, certainty for children. Legal frameworks establish parentage before birth, guaranteeing every child born this way has clear legal parents from day one. Underground arrangements leave children in legal limbo—the worst outcome for the most innocent party.
[P1.8] The burden this debate sets is simple: which policy produces fewer exploited women and more protected children in the world as it actually exists? Regulation wins that comparison decisively.
