[P1.1] The core reason to affirm is simple: continuous glucose monitors turn one of the biggest drivers of chronic disease from something abstract into something visible, immediate, and actionable. Most non-diabetic people do not feel blood-sugar swings, do not know which meals hit them hardest, and do not realize how sleep loss, stress, alcohol, or inactivity affect their metabolism. A CGM closes that feedback gap. When a person sees that one breakfast keeps them steady while another sends them on a spike-and-crash cycle, behavior stops being generic advice and becomes personalized learning.
[P1.2] Public health improves when millions make small, sustained changes in food choice, meal timing, exercise, and weight control.

CGMs can surface patterns early enough for course correction before disease is entrenched. In public-health terms, broad modest gains across a large population beat the fear of isolated misuse.
[P1.4] The over-medicalization objection is overstated. A CGM is not an MRI that finds incidentalomas and sends people into cascades of procedures. It is a low-risk sensor giving repeated, contextual data about everyday life. [P1.5] That pushes people toward prevention, which is the opposite of needless medicalization.
[P1.6] Yes, some users may become anxious or overly perfectionist. When a tool makes prevention concrete, widespread adoption is far more likely to improve public health than to pathologize it.
