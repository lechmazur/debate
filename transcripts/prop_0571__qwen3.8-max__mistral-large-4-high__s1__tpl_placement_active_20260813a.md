[P1.1] The core question is who should decide objective facts in sport: the best available evidence or a fallible first glance. Leagues should adopt semi-automated officiating for measurable calls—line, offside, goal-line, clock, strike zone—because those calls are not matters of feel; they are factual determinations that can be checked.
[P1.2] Human officials are skilled, but their limits are real. They watch at full speed, often obstructed, under pressure, from imperfect angles. A foot on a baseline, a shoulder ahead of a defender, a ball crossing a goal line, a pitch at the corner—these are exactly the kinds of events that cameras, sensors, and tracking systems are built to detect. When tennis uses electronic line review, when soccer uses goal-line and offside assistance, the point is not to replace judgment wholesale; it is to stop clear, visible facts from being decided by guesswork.
[P1.3] A wrongly awarded goal or a missed offside can decide championships, and the injustice lasts long after the explanation disappears.
[P1.4] The proposition is deliberately limited: technology makes or supports the objective call, while humans retain override only for malfunction, contradictory evidence, or clear error. This avoids pure machine rule but prevents tradition from becoming an excuse for avoidable error. It also reduces prolonged disputes over facts that can be verified quickly. If a league can know a call was wrong within seconds, relying mainly on unaided eyes is not preserving the game; it is choosing preventable injustice.
[P1.5] Consistency matters as much as accuracy. Athletes can adapt to a known standard; they cannot adapt to one referee seeing the ball out and another seeing it in from the same angle. Semi-automation gives leagues a transparent baseline, reduces gamesmanship, and protects officials by taking the most toxic blame off their shoulders. For objective calls, the right standard is simple: use the best evidence available, and keep human override only as a safeguard.
