[P1.1] Political persuasion is legitimate; engineering people’s anxieties in the dark is not. The proposition draws that line precisely: campaigns may still speak, advertise, target broad demographics and public records—but they may not build inferred psychological or emotional profiles and aim messages at those vulnerabilities.
[P1.2] Why ban this narrow practice? First, it bypasses consent and contestability. A voter can see a party’s platform, compare candidates, and argue back. She cannot see that a data broker has labeled her 'grievance-prone,' 'lonely,' or 'easily frightened,' still less correct it.
[P1.3] The campaign targets a guess about her inner life, often assembled from clicks, location, and purchases. When the inference is wrong, it does not fail harmlessly; it substitutes a fabricated vulnerability for the citizen’s actual reasons.
[P1.4] Second, this is not ordinary advertising; it is exploitation at scale. A message shown to an anxious parent about danger, or to a demoralized voter about futility, is designed to produce emotional reaction rather than deliberation. That corrupts democratic equality: some citizens become targets because their psychological profile makes them cheaper to move. The public cannot audit these appeals, and rival campaigns cannot answer messages delivered invisibly to narrow slices of the electorate.
[P1.5] Third, the privacy harm is structural, not merely individual. Psychological inference turns ordinary digital exhaust into a political weapon without meaningful notice or choice. Disclosure requirements cannot cure it, because the profiling models are proprietary and the individual cannot know which inferred trait triggered which ad.
[P1.6] The objection will be that this is just efficient communication. Efficiency is not the issue; coercion-by-manipulation is. If a campaign must persuade citizens as citizens—not as diagnosed emotional targets—democracy is stronger, not weaker. Ban the inferred psychological profile, and political speech remains robust.
