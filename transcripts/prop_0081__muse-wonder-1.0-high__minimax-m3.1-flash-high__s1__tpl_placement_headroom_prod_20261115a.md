[P1.1] Safety on a dating app is not a premium perk. It is the precondition for the product itself.
[P1.2] When someone matches, they decide whether to trust a stranger enough to talk, share photos, and meet in person. If the platform cannot say who is real, who was banned before, and what happens when a scammer appears, that trust collapses. Charging for identity verification, effective blocking, and rapid scam response turns basic trust into a luxury tier, leaving non-paying users — often younger, poorer, and newer — as the most exposed targets.
[P1.3] First, paywalling safety creates the risks it claims to solve. Scammers and repeat harassers hunt where verification is optional and reports vanish into a slow queue. A free user who cannot verify matches, block re-created accounts, or get help freezing a romance-scam conversation is not just unprotected; she becomes bait that keeps predators on the platform for everyone. Safety is a network good: one weak pocket undermines the whole pool.
[P1.4] Second, these features are cheap at the margin compared with the harm they prevent and profit already made from free users. Free accounts are not charity. They supply profiles, attention, and data that attract subscribers. Charging them again to avoid impersonation or stalking double-charges the people who make the marketplace viable, while the app profits from volume then externalizes fraud, harassment, and trauma onto victims.
[P1.5] Finally, the company is uniquely positioned to prevent foreseeable harm. It designs matching, controls messaging, and sees patterns across thousands of reports no individual can see. That control carries responsibility. A seatbelt is not an upgrade, and a landlord does not rent back the lock.
[P1.6] Verification, blocking, and scam response should be the floor, not the upsell.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a worthy principle—basic safety should not be a luxury—for a universal pricing rule that could make safety worse. It assumes that because a feature benefits users, its marginal cost is zero, and that giving every feature to every account maximizes protection. Neither follows.
[C1.2] Some protections should be included in every free account: blocking, reporting, and clear scam warnings. But the three categories conceal very different costs. A block button is cheap; investigating appeals, detecting coordinated account recreation, and reviewing romance-scam evidence are not. Stronger identity verification may require document handling, specialist review, privacy safeguards, and continual improvement.
[C1.3] If the “best” version is free by default, the platform must either subsidize it, cut moderation, or thin verification work so that nominal access produces little actual safety.
[C1.4] Paid tiers can be a screening and funding mechanism. Premium verification can reduce impersonation while generating revenue for fraud teams and systems that protect free users. A charge for a stronger check may deter fake accounts. Alternatively, unlimited best-in-class verification for everyone could attract scammers who create free accounts to exploit the workflow, consuming the capacity meant to catch them.
[C1.5] Consider a romance scam: warning signs are often behavioral, not a missing badge. A verified profile can still be fraudulent, while an unverified user can be genuine. Scam response depends on fast reporting, evidence preservation, payment-intelligence coordination, and trained review—resources that do not become more effective because access is priced at zero.
[C1.6] The sound policy is a free safety floor plus paid premiums for deeper verification and faster, higher-touch assistance. The motion requires the best resources for everyone and forbids pricing as a way to fund them. That categorical rule risks sacrificing effectiveness for uniform access. Safety should be universal; the best safety package need not be.

The policy that best expands protection is a generous free floor, cross-user detection, and targeted premium screening—not making the most expensive service universally available by mandate.
