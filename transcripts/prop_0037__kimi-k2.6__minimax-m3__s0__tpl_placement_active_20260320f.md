[P1.1] Congestion pricing is a market mechanism that mistakes access for justice. It does not solve the problem of private cars in city centers; it simply auctions the right to pollute, congest, and endanger to the highest bidder. Major cities should ban private cars from central districts entirely, because dense urban cores are not thoroughfares for private vehicles—they are shared spaces for millions.
[P1.2] A ban is the only policy that fully eliminates the negative externalities pricing merely mitigates. Even with tolls, cars still emit particulates, occupy disproportionate street space, and threaten pedestrians. A fee reduces volume; a ban eradicates it. In a central district, where population density is highest and public transit alternatives are most viable, eradication is both achievable and morally necessary.
[P1.3] The goal is not merely smoother traffic but a city core safe enough for children to cross.
[P1.4] Pricing also creates a two-tiered system that privileges wealth over need. Affluent drivers can continue treating streets as private expressways while everyone else absorbs the costs of congestion and pollution. An outright ban democratizes the city center. It says that clean air and safe streets are universal rights, not commodities to be purchased at a toll gate.
[P1.5] Furthermore, bans enable transformative redesign. When cars are removed entirely, asphalt gives way to wider sidewalks, cycling infrastructure, and public space. These changes are harder to justify when roads must still accommodate paying vehicles. Amsterdam and Paris have already shown that car-free central zones catalyze economic vitality and quality of life without the bureaucratic complexity of dynamic tolling.
[P1.6] Critics will warn of economic harm, but central districts thrive on foot traffic, not parking garages. Delivery vehicles and emergency services can operate under restricted permits, so the policy targets discretionary car trips, not commerce. Transit can absorb the shift in every major city with a metro or bus network worth the name.
[P1.7] An outright ban is cleaner, fairer, and more decisive. It treats the disease. Congestion pricing only manages the symptoms.

[C1.1] The right tool for dense central cities is congestion pricing, not outright prohibition. A blanket ban is the bluntest instrument available, and blunt instruments always break something important.
[C1.2] An outright ban does not just remove traffic; it removes access. Disabled drivers, night-shift workers, parents with infants, small businesses whose deliveries cannot be rescheduled to off-peak hours, tradespeople carrying tools to a job site: not edge cases, but the working texture of a city. A ban forces a permit bureaucracy to declare who is "essential," and that bureaucracy is always arbitrary, slow, and falls hardest on those without lawyers or lobbyists. Pricing lets the market do the sorting: it discourages discretionary trips while letting genuine trips through.
[C1.3] Equity also moves in the opposite direction from what the motion claims. London and Stockholm channel toll revenue directly into transit and sidewalks, lifting the system everyone uses. A blanket ban delivers the same air-quality gains only to the wealthy enough to live car-free, while concentrating the cost on working residents who clustered centrally precisely because they could not afford suburban commutes in the first place.
[C1.4] The proposition's examples flatter the case. Amsterdam's central ban rests on decades of cycling investment and dense tram networks built with public money. Paris expands car-free zones layered on top of, not instead of, robust transit and prior pricing. Cities without that scaffolding cannot import the result — they will inherit only the enforcement costs and the political backlash.
[C1.5] Pricing is dynamic. A flood event, a transit strike, a bad-air season, and the fee moves. A ban is static: the marginal vehicle it suppresses is increasingly one someone actually needed. The motion asks us to scrap a self-tuning, revenue-recycling tool in favor of inflexible prohibition.
[C1.6] That is a costly trade for limited gain.
