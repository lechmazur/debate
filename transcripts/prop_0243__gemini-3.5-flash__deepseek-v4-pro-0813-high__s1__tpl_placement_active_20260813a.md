[P1.1] Ride-hail platforms promised to revolutionize urban mobility by reducing car ownership and clearing our streets. Instead, they have systematically worsened traffic congestion in major cities. This decline is not an accidental byproduct; it is a structural consequence of how ride-hailing operates, driven by three compounding forces: deadheading, the cannibalization of public transit, and localized flow disruption.
[P1.2] First, ride-hailing introduces massive, artificial vehicle mileage to city centers through "deadheading"—the time drivers spend cruising empty while waiting for a fare or traveling to a pickup. Unlike private cars that park upon arrival, ride-hail vehicles remain constantly on the road, actively searching for passengers. This empty cruising accounts for roughly forty percent of ride-hail mileage, clogging narrow downtown corridors with vehicles that are not actually transporting anyone.
[P1.3] Second, instead of replacing private car ownership, ride-hailing has cannibalized the most space-efficient modes of transport. Research across major metropolitan areas consistently shows that a majority of ride-hail passengers would have otherwise taken public transit, walked, biked, or simply stayed home. By pulling commuters out of high-capacity trains and buses and placing them into individual, low-occupancy passenger cars, ride-hail platforms have fundamentally degraded the spatial efficiency of city streets. They have swapped one bus carrying fifty people for forty separate cars vying for the same lane.
[P1.4] Finally, ride-hail vehicles disproportionately disrupt the physical flow of traffic. The business model relies on constant, on-demand pickups and drop-offs. Because cities lack the curb space to accommodate this volume, drivers routinely double-park, block bike lanes, and stop in active travel lanes. Each sudden stop creates a cascading bottleneck, turning minor friction into city-wide gridlock.
[P1.5] By multiplying vehicle miles traveled, draining mass transit, and paralyzing street-level flow, ride-hail platforms have indisputably made urban traffic worse.

[C1.2] A ride-hail trip that replaces a personal car trip is not new congestion. [C1.3] The displaced alternative matters, and the other side never counts it.

[C1.5] The curb-disruption point also confuses platform behavior with municipal management. [C1.6] The motion says “overall.” That requires net addition, not visible ride-hail cars. Ride-hail serves demand in cities that were already congested and growing. It substitutes for many private trips, reduces parking search, and can be regulated at the curb. The other side has not shown that removing ride-hail would have left cities moving faster.
