[P1.1] A mass grave is not an exhibit. It is someone's grandmother, father, murdered child. No university department or tourism board inherits that bond. The families who do must decide when bones are lifted, how they are shown, and what story is told, because dignity is not a technical question.
[P1.2] Only descendant communities can keep the history honest. Outsiders have repeatedly turned plantations into wedding venues where forced labor is a footnote, put shackles in glass cases while erasing the people who wore them, and dug burial grounds for research while descendants learned about it from newspapers. Control reverses that gaze. When the African Burial Ground in Manhattan faced development, it was community pressure that forced respectful excavation, study, and reburial.
[P1.3] When Tulsa families demanded a say in searches for massacre victims, they prevented another bureaucratic burial of the truth.
[P1.4] This also makes better public explanation, not narrower. Descendants bring languages, burial rites, oral histories, names that archives never recorded. Archaeology can date a bone; it cannot say how to mourn it, whether display violates sacred law, or which silence in the official record is a lie. Substantial control means experts advise, but families decide.
[P1.5] States especially cannot be trusted as neutral stewards when they or their predecessors created the graves. Governments have every incentive to soften language from murder to tragedy, from forced labor to work, to move remains quickly and close debate. Descendant veto breaks that cover-up.
[P1.6] The alternative is continued extraction: scientists get careers, museums get tickets, states get sanitized plaques, while the wounded are told to watch quietly. That is not preservation. That is a second violation. Give the most affected the decisive voice, and excavation becomes recovery, display becomes testimony, history becomes something the public can finally trust.

[C1.1] Respect, consultation, and a right to be heard are owed to descendants. Substantial control is something else — and it would make excavation slower, display rarer, and public explanation less truthful.
[C1.2] First, the proposition hands power to a category that often cannot speak with one voice. Who counts after fifty, two hundred, or four hundred years? Distant relatives, diaspora organizations, religious authorities, and political movements can all claim descent — and disagree about exhumation, DNA testing, photography, or reburial. Substantial control does not empower “the families”; it empowers whichever faction is recognized as gatekeeper, and lets it silence other descendants and victims without clear lineage.
[C1.3] Second, mass graves are not only places of mourning. They are crime scenes and archives. Bones, bullets, bindings, and soil can identify the murdered, refute denial, and establish how a killing was organized long after documents are destroyed. That work requires independent forensic standards and timely access.
[C1.4] If excavation or display needs descendant permission, sincere beliefs against disturbing the dead, internal disputes, or delay can leave evidence to rot, be built over, or remain contestable. Deniers do not need to win an argument when the proof stays underground.
[C1.5] Third, explaining atrocity to the public is not an inherited right. Oral history and ritual knowledge are invaluable evidence, but not a verdict on evidence. Communities, like states, can prefer a usable past: heroic, unified, and free of complicity, internal conflict, or victims who complicate the preferred story. Independent archaeologists and historians must be free to publish findings descendants dislike, display objects descendants would hide, and name perpetrators — including when some were insiders.
[C1.6] The answer to state sanitization is not private ownership of the past. It is plural, transparent stewardship: descendant representatives at the table, independent experts, open records, legal protections for remains, and decisions that must be justified publicly. That protects dignity without giving any one group a veto over what everyone else may learn.
