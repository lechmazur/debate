[P1.1] Dating apps are not anonymous message boards. They are designed to move strangers quickly from a swipe to a chat, a date, a home, a bed, and sometimes a bank transfer. That pipeline demands more than a phone number and six self-selected photos.
[P1.2] Stronger verification — a government ID matched to a live selfie, age confirmation, and checks against fraud and ban-evasion signals — changes the economics of abuse. Catfishing, romance scams, and impersonation thrive because a fake account costs almost nothing. Scam one victim, get reported, change an email, and return in ten minutes. Make identity costly to fake and bans actually stick, and the repeat offender, the underage user, and the organized scammer cannot recycle themselves endlessly.
[P1.3] The harm is not hurt feelings. Romance fraud works by building intimacy over weeks, then inventing a medical emergency, a stuck shipment, or a crypto opportunity. Victims lose savings and feel too ashamed to report it. Fake profiles also create physical danger: a person who lies about who they are has already violated consent before the first meeting.
[P1.4] Verification will not stop every lie about a job or intentions, but it establishes the basic fact consent depends on — this is a real, accountable adult who is who they claim to be.
[P1.5] The cost we are asked to accept is a few extra minutes at sign-up and less anonymity toward the platform. That is trivial, one-time friction compared with months of deception. Nor does it require public exposure: users can still date under a nickname, hide their location, and control what matches see. The check sits between the user and the company, like showing ID at a bar without announcing your address to the room.
[P1.6] Anonymity on dating apps does not mainly protect the vulnerable. It protects those who benefit from having no consequences. Requiring proof of personhood restores the minimum accountability every other high-trust meeting place takes for granted.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a name for a safeguard. A dating app can verify that someone is a real adult and still host the exact harms PRO describes, because romance fraud is a behavior, not an identity defect. Scammers already use stolen IDs, purchased accounts, and synthetic selfies; a mandatory check raises their cost slightly and then hands everyone a false badge of trust.
[C1.2] Meanwhile the check cannot verify intentions, honesty about a job, or whether someone will become dangerous after the third date. It verifies the least predictive fact on the profile.
[C1.3] Worse, the proposition creates a new concentration of risk. To make sign-up “stronger,” platforms must collect and retain government IDs, live selfies, sometimes biometric templates, plus the intimate data already there: sexual orientation, location, health disclosures, messages. That is a honeypot. A breach, insider abuse, or subpoena can out someone to family, employer, or state.
[C1.4] PRO says the check sits between user and company “like showing ID at a bar” [P1.5]. A bouncer does not keep a copy of your ID and your dating history. This is not a few minutes of friction; it is permanent exposure. The “even if” is doing all the work: it asks us to discount the users for whom anonymity is a safety shield.
[C1.5] And mandatory identity excludes precisely the people most likely to need private dating: domestic-violence survivors, LGBTQ users in hostile communities, trans people whose documents do not match, undocumented people, and adults without easy ID access. They do not become safer; they leave or use unmoderated venues. The right response to fraud is behavior-based risk detection, device and payment signals, rapid reporting, and user controls—not a universal ID gate that punishes the cautious and misses the determined. Safety without privacy and access is not safety; it is surveillance with a dating interface.
