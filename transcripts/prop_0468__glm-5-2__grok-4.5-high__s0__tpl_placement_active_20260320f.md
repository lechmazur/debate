[P1.1] Anonymous shell companies exist for one purpose: to sever the link between assets and the people who control them. When that severance serves no legitimate regulatory function, it becomes a tool for laundering stolen wealth, evading taxes, financing terrorism, and shielding corrupt officials from accountability. A universal ban is justified because the harm is systemic, the legitimate alternatives exist, and partial measures fail.
[P1.2] The scale of harm is concrete. When a politician loots public funds, those funds rarely sit in a personal account—they pass through layered shell entities across multiple jurisdictions until the money appears clean. The same architecture serves drug cartels, sanctions evaders, and human traffickers. The cost falls on ordinary citizens whose public services are gutted by corruption and whose economies are distorted by hidden capital flows.
[P1.3] Legitimate uses do not require anonymity. Companies use holding structures for tax efficiency, asset segregation, and transactional simplicity every day through entities that disclose beneficial ownership to regulators. Privacy from competitors is achievable through limited disclosure to authorities, not through total opacity. The interests served by true anonymity are the interests of those who cannot survive scrutiny—so the cost-benefit balance is lopsided.
[P1.4] Partial bans fail because capital is mobile. If one jurisdiction closes the loophole, shell companies migrate to holdouts. The Financial Action Task Force has spent years pushing beneficial ownership transparency, yet gaps persist precisely because non-compliant jurisdictions face no universal prohibition. A coordinated ban eliminates the arbitrage that lets dirty money shop for the weakest gatekeeper.
[P1.5] International financial regulators already possess the mandate and the tools—registries, reporting standards, and supervisory authority. What is missing is the universal commitment to close the door on anonymous structures entirely. The motion demands exactly that: not another disclosure recommendation, but a prohibition on creating and operating entities designed to hide who ultimately benefits.

[P4.2] The chokepoint also moves upstream. Formation agents and corporate registries become gatekeepers who must refuse entities that cannot demonstrate verified beneficial ownership, rather than creating them and hoping disclosure follows. That is a structural difference, not a semantic one.
[P4.3] A2: Free-rider jurisdictions already exist under the status quo CON defends. The difference is that today they face no universal prohibition—only soft recommendations they can ignore. Dirty capital faces higher friction at every touchpoint with the regulated financial system.
[P4.4] Meanwhile, the clean multi-jurisdictional vehicle CON worries about is not banned at all—it is simply required to disclose ownership to regulators. "Non-obvious" to the public is not "obscured" from authorities. CON keeps collapsing that distinction.
