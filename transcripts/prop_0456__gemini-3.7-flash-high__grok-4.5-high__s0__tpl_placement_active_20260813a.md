[P1.1] Generative artificial intelligence cannot exist without training data, and human culture cannot survive if its creators are systematically uncompensated. We face a classic technological crossroad: an absolute veto right paralyzes innovation through insurmountable transaction costs, while an unregulated fair-use regime strips creators of their economic baseline. A compulsory licensing scheme resolves this tension by doing what copyright law has always done best—balancing access with remuneration.
[P1.2] First, training modern generative AI requires datasets containing millions, often billions, of creative works. Negotiating individual bilateral licenses across millions of independent authors, musicians, and artists creates prohibitive transaction costs. If prior permission is mandatory, only a handful of trillion-dollar tech monopolies can afford private licensing deals, cementing an anti-competitive bottleneck and shutting out independent innovation. Conversely, if training is deemed free use, creators suffer catastrophic economic displacement with zero return.
[P1.3] Compulsory licensing cuts this Gordian knot, replacing impossible clearance friction with a universal mechanism.
[P1.4] Second, compulsory licensing is the proven historical blueprint for media disruption. When radio broadcasting emerged, and when mechanical player pianos and cover recordings challenged sheet music publishers, copyright did not ban the technology or abandon the artist. Instead, statutory licensing channeled massive, ongoing revenue streams directly back to rights holders without halting technological adoption.
[P1.5] Finally, this framework guarantees equity. A compulsory statutory fee ensures that every publisher, label, and individual creator whose work fuels the AI engine receives structured, recurring royalties. It ends the current regime of unauthorized, uncompensated scraping while preventing a few dominant platforms from monopolizing the cultural commons.
[P1.6] Compulsory licensing ensures technology can build on human culture without cannibalizing the livelihood of the humans who create it. It is the only viable, equitable path forward.

[C1.2] The proposition lets publishers, labels, and studios train on copyrighted works without permission whenever they remit a statutory fee. That is the wrong bargain. Copyright is not a royalty coupon system. It is control over whether a use happens at all.
[C1.3] When a work can train a system that later undercuts the original market—voice models that replace session singers, image systems that flood style markets, story models that dilute series value—the injury is substitution, not mere redistribution. A compulsory check does not restore the lost choice or the lost scarcity. It monetizes the trespass and calls it equity.
[C1.4] PRO’s transaction-cost story overstates paralysis. Collective management, opt-in training corpora, library licenses, and bilateral deals already exist and expand when the law treats training as a licensable use. Declaring individual clearance “impossible,” then nationalizing access through a levy, is a policy choice that favors scale users, not a law of nature. Firms that cannot clear rights do not gain a moral claim to compel the world’s catalog into their models.
[C1.5] Historical analogies collapse under the same weight. Mechanical and broadcast licenses addressed narrow, constrained reuse of fixed works. Generative systems absorb patterns across entire catalogs and emit competing substitutes at near-zero marginal cost. Channeling a fee through that pipeline does not “do what copyright always did.” It rewrites the core exclusive right into a liability rule timed for the convenience of AI developers and their corporate patrons.
[C1.6] Nor does the scheme guarantee creator welfare. Rate-setting is political, delayed, and captured by intermediaries. Publishers and labels become collection funnels while individual authors, session players, and illustrators face opaque allocation and no veto. Unauthorized scraping is not cured; it is regularized.
[C1.7] Creators should keep the right to say no. Permission first, voluntary markets second—not compulsory access dressed up as fairness.
