[P1.1] Ride-hail worsens congestion because it adds car movement that would not otherwise exist. The decisive metric is vehicle miles traveled on city streets, not how convenient the passenger’s trip felt. Every app trip has at least two car legs: the pickup leg and the passenger leg. The pickup leg is often empty or nearly empty.
[P1.2] That deadheading is pure traffic—fuel, space, and delay with no passenger benefit. Multiply it across a major city’s daily trips, and platforms become a congestion machine.
[P1.3] They also pull people off transit and off their feet. A bus or train trip carries dozens or hundreds; an app trip carries one or two. When a rider switches from subway to back seat, road traffic rises even if that rider personally saves time. The easier door-to-door travel becomes, the more short trips people take that they would otherwise walk, bike, or skip.
[P1.4] That is induced demand, not replacement.
[P1.5] The “it replaces private cars” defense misses the margin. A household that sells a car may still summon many app cars, each with its own deadhead and curb stop. And curb behavior matters: pickup and drop-off zones create double-parking, circling, and blocked bus lanes. A few extra vehicles at a chokepoint can cascade into gridlock far beyond their share of traffic.
[P1.6] Major cities that embraced ride-hail saw downtown speeds fall and app vehicles become a visible slice of central traffic; the platforms did not create every jam, but they added millions of low-occupancy vehicle miles into networks already at capacity. Compared with a counterfactual where those trips stayed on transit, bikes, feet, or were never taken, the net effect is more cars, more empty miles, and worse overall congestion.
