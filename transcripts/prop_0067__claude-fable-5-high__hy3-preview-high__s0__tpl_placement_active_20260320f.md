[P1.1] Brain signals are not just another data stream. Every other category of consumer data—clicks, searches, purchases—records something you chose to do. Neural data records what happens before choice: involuntary attention spikes, pre-conscious emotional reactions, the flicker of desire you never act on and never intended to reveal. You cannot curate your brain activity the way you curate a search history.
[P1.2] Once companies can read the raw inputs of decision-making, the ordinary boundary between persuasion and manipulation collapses.
[P1.3] That is why this debate is really about one question: can click-through consent legitimize this use? It cannot, for three reasons.
[P1.4] First, consent requires understanding, and nobody—including the companies—fully understands what neural data will reveal as decoding improves. A signal collected today for "personalization" may tomorrow expose mood disorders, addiction vulnerability, or sexual response. You cannot meaningfully consent to disclosures whose scope is unknowable at signing.
[P1.5] Second, consent requires a real alternative. Consumer neurotech—sleep headbands, focus earbuds, gaming interfaces—bundles these terms into the product. As these devices become workplace and educational tools, "agree or go without" is coercion dressed as choice. We already know how this story ends: nobody reads terms of service, and the click is a ritual, not a decision.
[P1.6] Third, and decisively: advertising and dynamic pricing built on neural data weaponize your own cognition against you. An advertiser who knows the exact moment your impulse control dips, a retailer who prices goods against your measured desperation in real time—these don't serve preferences; they exploit vulnerabilities the user cannot even perceive. Consent to that is consent to undermine the very capacity that makes future consent meaningful.
[P1.7] We already prohibit certain exchanges regardless of consent—selling organs, signing away the right to sue for fraud—because some harms corrode autonomy itself. Note what the ban does not touch: health features, accessibility tools, the device's core function. It removes only the exploitation business model. That line is narrow, principled, and overdue.
