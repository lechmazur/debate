[P1.1] Stable prices are not an abstraction. When inflation runs hot, every paycheck shrinks, rents jump, and savings set aside for retirement or a child's education lose value. A modest uptick in unemployment hurts badly those who lose work, but runaway prices hurt everyone who works, and hit hardest those with no assets to hide in. Protecting the purchasing power of money protects the poor first.
[P1.2] Second, inflation tolerated becomes inflation expected. Workers demand bigger raises to keep up, firms raise prices to cover wages, and a temporary overshoot hardens into a permanent spiral. That is the lesson of the 1970s: trying to buy a little extra employment with a little extra inflation ended with both higher inflation and higher unemployment, broken only by a brutal recession. Nipping expectations early costs a modest rise in joblessness; indulging them guarantees a severe one later.
[P1.3] Central banks also have the right tool for only one job. They can anchor prices. They cannot retrain workers, build housing, or target help to struggling regions. Fiscal policy and safety nets can cushion unemployment directly.
[P1.4] No government check can cushion a collapsing currency — it just fuels more inflation.
[P1.5] That asymmetry matters most over the next three years. Labor markets remain historically tight, so a modest rise means normalization from overheating, not mass layoffs. But inflation memory is still fresh, and credibility is fragile. If central banks signal jobs come first, markets will price in higher inflation, long-term borrowing costs will rise, and investment that creates durable jobs will stall.
[P1.6] A short, shallow cooling now preserves the conditions for lasting employment later. Letting inflation drift does the opposite: it trades temporary comfort for permanent insecurity.

[C1.1] Read the motion carefully: it asks central banks to *prioritize* a number, for three years, and to accept job losses as the price. That is not inflation targeting as practiced; every major central bank already runs a flexible mandate that tolerates temporary deviations. The motion wants a hierarchy. It should be rejected for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the "modest rise" is a fiction of control. Unemployment does not move in half-points on command. Historically, once joblessness rises by roughly half a percentage point from its low, it keeps rising, because layoffs cut spending, which triggers more layoffs. Central banks aiming for a gentle cooling have repeatedly produced recessions instead, because policy works with lags of a year or more.
[C1.3] Setting rates today to hit target in 2027 means steering by looking backward. The ECB's 2011 hikes to defend its target delivered a double-dip recession and years of below-target inflation. Over-tightening is not a hypothetical.
[C1.4] Second, the costs are not symmetric in the direction PRO assumes. Inflation at three percent rather than two is a diffuse, recoverable cost. Unemployment is concentrated and scarring: the laid-off warehouse worker or new graduate loses not one year of income but a permanently lower earnings path, and the first fired are the young, the low-skilled, and minorities. PRO says inflation "hits the poor first"; joblessness hits the same people, harder, and for longer.
[C1.5] Third, the remaining inflation gap today is largely supply-side: lagging shelter costs, insurance, tariffs, energy shocks. Rate hikes do not build apartments or lower tariffs. They only reduce demand until enough people lose jobs that landlords and insurers relent. Prioritizing the target against supply shocks means deliberately manufacturing unemployment to fight a problem interest rates cannot reach.
[C1.6] PRO's own framework concedes fiscal policy should handle unemployment. Fine: then central banks should not create it. Anchored expectations give them room to be patient. The next three years call for judgment, not a number pursued at any human cost.
