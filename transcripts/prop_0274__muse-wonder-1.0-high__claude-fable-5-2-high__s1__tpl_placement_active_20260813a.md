[P1.1] A trail, cove, or alley locals used for generations can be overwhelmed in a single season — not by gradual tourism, but by one geotagged reel sending thousands of strangers to the exact coordinates at once. That is the harm this proposition targets, and why high-tourism destinations should limit influencer-style promotion of fragile spots when that virality predictably causes damage.
[P1.2] First, virality breaks normal management. Ordinary tourism spreads across hotels, seasons, and guidebooks, giving places time to add bins, paths, and staff. Algorithmic promotion does the opposite: it concentrates demand on one photogenic ledge, rice terrace, temple courtyard, or residential lane, often with no toilets, parking, or erosion control. We have seen the pattern in a canyon in Iceland temporarily closed after a music video made it famous, Maya Bay in Thailand closed to let sharks and coral recover after film-and-social-media fame, and Kyoto’s Gion district restricting photography on private lanes after residents were harassed for pictures.
[P1.3] The location did not change; the amplification did.
[P1.4] Second, the costs are borne by people who did not consent to become content. Residents face blocked driveways, noise at dawn for sunrise shots, litter, trespass into gardens and sacred places, and rents pushed up by short-term lets chasing the hotspot. Fragile ecology is even less resilient: trampled moss, widened informal trails, and disturbed wildlife do not bounce back because a post moves on. The influencer gains followers and sponsorship; the destination inherits cleanup, enforcement, and irreversible wear.
[P1.5] Limiting geotagging and paid promotion in these defined, vulnerable places is proportionate, not a ban on travel or speech about a country. Visitors can still come in managed numbers, discover places through visitor centres, and share personal photos without broadcasting precise coordinates or selling the spot. Destinations can redirect promotion to sites built for crowds.
[P1.6] The choice is not tourism versus no tourism. It is stewardship that keeps a place visitable versus algorithmic extraction that consumes the very thing people came to see.
