[P1.1] Every graduate will have to eat, keep a roof from quietly failing, and stop money from leaking away. Those are not hobbies for the already interested. They are the practical baseline of adult independence, and schools that leave them optional guarantee uneven preparation precisely where the costs land hardest.
[P1.2] Cooking is daily health and daily economics. Students who never learn to turn ordinary ingredients into reliable meals default to whatever is fast and marketed, then pay for it twice—once at the register and again in energy, weight, and medical bills. Basic home repair is the difference between a shutoff valve and a flooded apartment, between a loose outlet and a contractor’s emergency rate. Personal finance is how a first paycheck becomes rent, savings, and avoided debt rather than fees, minimum payments, and fine print no one explained.
[P1.3] These failures are common, concrete, and expensive. They do not wait for students who “happen to seek them out.”
[P1.4] Optionality sounds like respect for choice. In practice it tracks what families already provide. Households with time, tools, and know-how pass cooking, repairs, and budgeting along. Households stretched thin often cannot.
[P1.5] The students who most need a structured introduction are the ones least likely to find the elective, clear the schedule, or even know the course exists. A core requirement closes that gap instead of congratulating the students who were already going to be fine.
[P1.6] Schools already mandate subjects whose daily use is uneven because judgment and literacy compound. The same logic applies more tightly here: nearly every adult must do these things, and missing them reliably costs health, safety, or money. They need not crowd out academics. They can be sequenced, practiced, and assessed like any other foundational skill—because competence in ordinary life is not a side catalog.
[P1.7] It is part of what a school owes every student who walks out the door.

[C1.1] Nobody in this room thinks a graduate should flood an apartment or sign a payday loan. The real question is narrower and harder: does moving cooking, wiring, and budgeting from the elective catalog into the mandatory core actually produce adults who can do those things, and what gets sacrificed to find out?
[C1.2] Start with the school day, which is not elastic. "Core" means required seat time, graded, for everyone. Every hour of it comes from somewhere—reading, mathematics, science, or the electives students actually chose. Pro says these subjects "need not crowd out academics."
[C1.3] That is a hope, not a plan. Schools already struggle to get students to proficient reading and algebra, and those are the skills that compound: a student who leaves unable to read a lease or compute interest cannot be rescued by a budgeting worksheet. Cooking can be learned at twenty-two from a phone. Algebra rarely is.
[C1.4] Second, schools deliver these particular skills badly when compelled. Personal finance taught to fifteen-year-olds with no paycheck, no rent, and no credit card is knowledge with nothing to attach to; the general pattern with state financial-literacy mandates has been small or fading effects, because the lesson arrives years before the decision. Home repair without a home is a slideshow about shutoff valves. Cooking with thirty students and four burners is a food-safety quiz.
[C1.5] Mandating a subject does not mandate the conditions that make it stick.
[C1.6] Third, compulsion changes what these classes are. The shop and culinary programs that genuinely work run on students who wanted to be there, teachers who chose that craft, and time to actually build things. Make them universal requirements and they become the thing everyone dreads: standardized, assessed, and forgotten by June.
[C1.7] Pro's equity argument cuts the other way. The students most dependent on school are the ones who cannot afford a diluted academic core. Fund the electives, advertise them, teach compound interest in math class where it belongs—but the finite school day should go to what only school can give.
