[P1.1] The question is not whether survivors may keep distinct memories. They will, and they should. It is what public memorials—built with common resources, on civic ground, and addressed to future citizens—should teach. After civil conflict, their central task must be a shared account of what violence did, why it must not recur, and why every person now belongs to the same political community.
[P1.2] “Emphasize” matters. A shared civic account need not flatten testimony into one official voice or declare all sides equally guilty. It can preserve different experiences, name perpetrators and victims honestly, and expose disputed claims to scrutiny. What it rejects is the CON alternative: fully separate memorial narratives in which each ethnic group receives its own self-contained story of innocence, suffering, and enemy guilt.
[P1.3] Plural testimony inside a common civic frame is remembrance; parallel national stories are segregation extended into the past.
[P1.4] That distinction is decisive because civil war is sustained by more than weapons. It is sustained by stories that turn neighbors into categories and make violence appear defensive, inevitable, or inherited. If memorials simply institutionalize those stories, children can learn the names of the dead without ever learning to recognize the humanity of the living across the divide. Separate narratives may offer immediate recognition, but they leave each community’s grievance immune to common questioning.
[P1.5] A shared account instead creates a common evidentiary and moral floor: civilians were not legitimate targets, suffering was not confined to one nation, and political disagreement cannot again justify domination.
[P1.6] This also protects equal citizenship. A state memorial organized primarily around ethnic ownership tells visitors that their standing depends on which community’s dead they claim. A civic memorial tells them that mourning crosses those boundaries and that public institutions owe protection to everyone.

[C1.1] PRO closes by promising "many remembered voices, deliberately oriented toward one civic conclusion." That phrase is the whole problem. After a civil war, someone decides what the conclusion is, and that someone is almost never neutral. It is the winning army, the dominant majority, or the government that emerged from the fighting.
[C1.2] A shared civic account is not a floor beneath the conflict. It is the conflict's final battle, won by whoever controls the budget and the land.
[C1.3] The pattern recurs. Franco's Valley of the Fallen was presented as a monument to reconciliation, holding dead from both sides. Yet Republican remains were moved there without families' consent, and the site glorified the victor. Tito's Yugoslavia buried wartime massacres beneath "brotherhood and unity."
[C1.4] Those grievances were never resolved, only denied a public outlet, and nationalists in the 1990s exploited exactly that silence. Rwanda's single national story leaves little public room for Hutu civilians killed by the forces that won. Each promised a common floor. Each built a ceiling.
[C1.5] Second, recognition must come before reconciliation. A Bosniak mother at Srebrenica does not need her son folded into "civilians suffered on all sides." That symmetry is precisely what deniers want. Group-specific memorials let communities name what was done to them, and by whom, in their own terms.
[C1.6] That is not segregation extended into the past. It is the minimum condition for trusting a state that recently failed to protect you.
[C1.7] Third, separate narratives are not frozen narratives. When each community's account stands publicly, it becomes visible, contestable, and comparable. Historians, journalists, and students can set the competing claims side by side and argue over them. A unified civic text forecloses that argument by settling it in advance.
[C1.8] Convergence, where it happens, comes slowly and from below, when people choose it rather than receive it.
[C1.9] So the central question is this: when a state that fought in the war writes the one story of that war, who checks it? PRO's model has no answer. Ours does. The other stories, left standing.
