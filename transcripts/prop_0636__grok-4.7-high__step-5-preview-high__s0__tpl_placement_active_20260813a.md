[P1.1] Synthetic media can now impersonate a candidate's voice, place a journalist at a crime scene they never visited, or manufacture a confession that never happened. When audiences cannot tell the real from the fabricated, democratic deliberation and personal reputation both suffer. The remedy is not to police who may speak, but to require that synthetic audio and video carry standardized, interoperable labels and provenance signals.
[P1.2] A common technical standard—visible marks plus cryptographically signed metadata recording whether content was generated or substantially altered—lets platforms, newsrooms, and ordinary viewers assess what they are seeing. Mandate the standard, and a clip created in one tool remains legible in another. Fragmented voluntary schemes fail because distributors who prefer opacity simply choose the unlabeled path. Regulation creates the floor: synthetic media distributed at scale must carry the signal with it.
[P1.3] Detection after the fact is a losing race; provenance travels with the file.
[P1.4] That signal need not name the speaker. Provenance can attest to process—generated, edited, or captured by a device—without a government identity check, a real-name registry, or a centralized database of posters. Requiring identity to publish would trade one harm for another. It would expose activists, abuse survivors, and dissidents.
[P1.5] It would chill anonymous criticism that open societies have long protected. And it would concentrate sensitive records in systems that leak and get misused. Transparency about the medium is not surveillance of the messenger. A whistleblower can still publish; the public can still know the clip was synthesized.
[P1.6] Unlabeled fakes already mislead voters and ruin reputations. A mandated label does not force belief, but it supplies a reliable cue that does not exist at scale today. Pair that cue with a firm refusal to demand identity, and regulators protect both informational integrity and the right to speak without first showing papers. That proportionate rule is what this moment requires.

[C1.1] A mandate without an identity anchor is a mandate without enforcement. PRO wants synthetic media to carry provenance signals, but then strips out the only thing that makes provenance meaningful: accountability. If no one has to prove who created a file, the "signed metadata" PRO imagines is just a sticker anyone can forge and anyone can strip. Re-encoding a video destroys the wrapper.
[C1.2] Screenshots defeat it. A determined deceiver does not need to defeat cryptography; they only need to route around the mandate, publishing the same lie through a compromised host or a stripped file. The label becomes a compliance tax on honest creators, while bad actors treat it as an obstacle course.
[C1.3] Worse, the mandate demands regulators define "substantially altered," a boundary that will inevitably capture ordinary speech. Is a voice clone for a documentary synthetic? Is a color-graded press photo altered? Is a satirical deepfake synthetic?
[C1.4] Each forced choice creates a chilling effect, because the safe answer for a platform is to label everything, and the safe answer for a creator is to avoid the gray zone entirely.
[C1.5] PRO says this is not surveillance. But provenance requires a chain of custody, and chains require links. Without identity verification at creation or distribution, platforms become de facto judges, making opaque takedown decisions about files they cannot authenticate. That concentrates power in intermediaries, not transparency.
[C1.6] The proposal asks us to believe that a floating label changes audience behavior. It will not. Viewers already see "satire" tags and "manipulated media" warnings and share anyway. A mandate adds bureaucratic friction, not epistemic clarity.
[C1.7] If the goal is protecting democratic deliberation, this rule fails the people it claims to help: it burdens the honest, leaves the dishonest unmoored, and invites definitional abuse without stopping a single confident lie.
