[P1.1] Every dollar spent rebuilding a flood-destroyed beachfront home is a dollar spent on a demolition date. That is the heart of this debate, and it is a question of compounding losses versus compounding gains.
[P1.2] The economics are brutal and asymmetric. Rebuilding exposed coastal communities means paying the same bill again and again: emergency response, insurance payouts, infrastructure repair, displacement costs. A house rebuilt in a floodplain that floods every decade is not an asset; it is a subscription to disaster. Federal disaster spending in the United States already runs to tens of billions of dollars annually, and a large share repeatedly returns to the same counties hit by the same hurricanes.
[P1.3] Planned inland cities, by contrast, convert one-time capital investment into durable stock: roads, housing, water systems, and jobs that appreciate rather than wash away.
[P1.4] This is not fantasy. Planned cities have historically redirected migration successfully—from company towns to postwar new towns to modern industrial cities built deliberately around infrastructure and employment. Climate migration is already happening; the only question is whether it happens chaotically, through informal settlement and overloaded inland metros, or whether states get ahead of it with planned capacity.
[P1.5] Three advantages decide this. First, resilience: inland sites on higher ground away from storm surge and saltwater intrusion face structurally lower risk. Second, moral hazard: guaranteed rebuilding subsidizes exposure, encouraging people to stay in harm's way while insurance markets buckle—we already see private insurers withdrawing from coastal states.
[P1.6] Third, opportunity: a planned city concentrates schools, transit, and jobs where climate migrants arrive anyway, rather than scattering relief payments into places destined for repeat destruction.
[P1.7] The proposition does not demand abandoning coastal residents—it demands redirecting investment from repetition to transition. Rebuilding is a cost with a countdown. Planning is an investment with a future. States should choose the future.
