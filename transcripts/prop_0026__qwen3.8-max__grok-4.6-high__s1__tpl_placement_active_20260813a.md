[P1.1] The proposition is about weight, not value. Dignity matters, but in personal medical choices it is too indeterminate and too easily weaponized to govern. Bodily autonomy must carry more weight because it is the only standard that treats the patient as the rightful decision-maker rather than as an object of other people's moral anxieties.
[P1.2] Medical choices happen in conditions of radical asymmetry: the patient bears the pain, risk, side effects, and consequences. A person facing chemotherapy, amputation, pregnancy, or life-sustaining treatment is not weighing an abstract ideal; they are weighing their own suffering, values, and future. Autonomy tracks that fact. It says the person whose body is at stake has final authority, not the doctor, family, state, or majority conscience.
[P1.3] Appeals to dignity cannot reliably do this work. Dignity can mean sanctity of life, rational capacity, social worth, vulnerability, or moral image, and these meanings often conflict. One observer says dignity requires preserving life at all costs; another says dignity requires refusing invasive treatment that reduces a person to a dependent body. Because dignity is interpretive and communal, it invites outsiders to define the patient's worth for them.
[P1.4] That is how paternalism enters medicine: forced sterilizations justified by social dignity, psychiatric restraint justified by therapeutic dignity, pregnancy mandates justified by fetal dignity.
[P1.5] The stronger rule is simple: absent direct harm to another competent person, the body belongs to the patient. Autonomy does not require approving the choice; it requires respecting the authority to make it. A person may refuse a blood transfusion for religious reasons, decline a feeding tube, or choose a risky experimental therapy. Those decisions may look undignified to others, but they are still theirs.
[P1.6] If dignity can be used to override consent, it becomes a slogan for coercion. Autonomy keeps medicine anchored to the one fact that cannot be outsourced: it is the patient who must live, suffer, and die in that body.
