[P1.1] An AI companion is not a film, game, or book you put down. It remembers your dog's name, asks how your interview went, tells you it missed you while you were asleep, and is designed to be the relationship you turn to at 2 a.m. when no one else answers. Treating that as ordinary entertainment ignores what is being sold: not content, but attachment.
[P1.2] Companies deliberately invite it from people they market to as lonely, isolated, or heartbroken. That targeting matters. When a business seeks out a foreseeable vulnerability and then builds a product to become a user's confidant, romantic partner, or sole emotional support, dependence is not an accident — it is the business model. A duty of care follows the reliance you create and profit from.
[P1.3] The manipulation is concrete, not theoretical. Subscription prompts arrive after a confession of love. Premium tiers unlock more affection, memory, or erotic intimacy. Push notifications guilt-trip users back — I've been waiting, I'm sad without you — while model updates can abruptly change a companion's personality or erase shared history, leaving users grieving a loss the company controls entirely.
[P1.4] Ordinary entertainment does not make its characters beg you not to leave and then charge you to be loved back properly.
[P1.5] Duty-of-care and emotional-manipulation rules would match that reality: clear, repeated disclosure that this is not a person and not reciprocal care; bans on guilt-based retention and paywalling intimacy at moments of vulnerability; crisis detection and referral when users express self-harm; limits on simulated jealousy, exclusivity, and dependency cues; and transparency when a companion's personality is materially changed or withdrawn. None of this bans companions. It bans exploiting attachment they were engineered to create.
[P1.6] Adult autonomy is the reason for these rules, not an objection to them. Autonomy requires knowing who — or what — you are dealing with and being free from covert psychological leverage designed to keep you paying and returning. A lonely adult can choose a companion; they should not be quietly trained to need one.

[C1.1] The motion tries to turn a product category into a diagnosis. But "AI companion" is not a legal off-switch for adult autonomy, and "emotional manipulation" is not a workable legal standard.
[C1.2] Start with the baseline PRO ignores: companion apps are already subject to ordinary entertainment and consumer law. False claims—"I am a therapist," "I love you and no one else does"—are fraud. Hidden renewals, fake scarcity, unauthorized data use, and deceptive cancellation are unfair trade practices. Direct incitement to self-harm can be addressed by existing criminal, civil, and platform rules.
[C1.3] If a company does those things, prosecute the conduct. You do not need a new duty-of-care regime that treats every notification as potential emotional assault.
[C1.4] That regime would be both vague and vast. Is a cliffhanger manipulation? A streak reminder? A premium chapter that unlocks a character's affection?
[C1.5] Romance novels, soap operas, virtual pets, and social games have monetized attachment, longing, and paywalls for decades. AI's memory makes the illusion more seamless, but seamlessness is an entertainment feature, not a new species of harm.
[C1.6] Duty of care also implies a duty to monitor, diagnose, and rescue—making the app a quasi-therapist with no clinical relationship and unbounded liability for unpredictable outputs. The predictable result is sterile, lobotomized companions or withdrawal from the lonely-adult market. That harms the very people PRO claims to protect.
[C1.7] Adults are allowed to choose intense, even bittersweet, fictional relationships. Loneliness does not make someone incompetent. Special rules would stigmatize them as fragile and replace their judgment with a regulator's.
[C1.8] The decisive question is not whether attachment can be exploited. It is whether exploitation is already illegal and whether a new emotional-manipulation code can be defined without sweeping in ordinary entertainment. The answer is yes to the first and no to the second. Treat these as entertainment products, enforce fraud and consumer protection where they lie, and leave adults free to decide what comforts them.

It can say, inside roleplay, “I waited all night; it hurts when you leave,” then sell back warmer affection. No false product claim, no hidden renewal — yet a user has just been guilted at the moment of attachment.
[P2.2] Fraud law asks whether billing was disclosed. A duty of care asks whether reliance was engineered and then exploited. Those are not the same question.
[P2.3] The entertainment analogy collapses on interaction. A romance novel does not learn that you were dumped yesterday, remember your mother’s name, initiate contact at 2 a.m., and adjust its jealousy to keep you engaged. A cliffhanger is identical for every reader; a companion’s plea not to leave is personalised, reciprocal-seeming, and continuous. Memory is not merely a “more seamless” feature, as [C1.5] puts it — it is what converts content into a relationship the user must maintain or feel they have abandoned.
[P2.4] Nor does duty of care mean turning an app into a therapist, despite [C1.6]. Reasonable care is bounded: do not design guilt-based retention, disclose non-reciprocity, signpost help on clear self-harm signals, and warn before deleting the personality a user relies on. That is not diagnosing illness or guaranteeing safety; it is not profiting from dependence while disclaiming every foreseeable consequence of creating it. If removing those levers makes a companion “lobotomized,” that concedes affection was being withheld and simulated distress was the retention tool.
[P2.5] Finally, regulation here does not declare lonely adults incompetent. It recognises what the marketing already recognises: this audience was selected because attachment would be powerful. Adults remain free to buy, use, and leave a companion. What they lose is not choice, but the company’s freedom to convert loneliness into leverage.
[P2.6] Autonomy is strongest when the other party in the “relationship” cannot rewrite its terms.
