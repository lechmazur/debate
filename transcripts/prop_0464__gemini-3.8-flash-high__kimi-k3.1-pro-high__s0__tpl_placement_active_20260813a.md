[P1.1] The United Nations Security Council was built to prevent catastrophic conflict, yet the permanent-member veto has turned it into an engine of impunity. Whenever a permanent member commits aggression, annexes territory, or shields an ally committing atrocities, collective security collapses. We see the consequences across contemporary crises: paralyzing gridlock over Ukraine, Syria, and Gaza. The veto does not temper great-power rivalry; it guarantees that the most powerful states can violate international law without institutional consequence.
[P1.2] The inevitable objection is that abolishing the veto risks destroying the institution through great-power withdrawal. This objection mistakes institutional survival for institutional purpose. An international body that can only act when the strongest nations have no stake in the outcome is already broken. What value is a universal table if that table exists solely to legitimize helplessness?
[P1.3] Moreover, the threat of withdrawal is primarily a tool of coercive leverage. Major powers remain in multilateral institutions because these bodies confer diplomatic legitimacy, global reach, and convening power that cannot be replicated unilaterally. Surrendering to their threats simply codifies an unjust hierarchy where five nations hold humanity hostage to maintain an unearned privilege.
[P1.4] Even if a major power carries out its threat to walk away, an institution governed by a decisive majority is vastly superior to a paralyzed consensus. A council capable of organizing collective action, authorizing humanitarian missions, and coordinating sanctions without a single nation's arbitrary obstruction carries genuine moral and operational authority. The League of Nations did not fail because it lacked universal membership; it failed because it lacked the institutional will and capability to confront aggression.
[P1.5] Collective security cannot mean absolute immunity for the world’s largest arsenals. True multilateralism cannot be held hostage by the very powers it was created to restrain. To restore the credibility and efficacy of global governance, the veto must be abolished.

[C5.1] The decisive defect in abolition is that it removes a rule, not the power that makes the rule necessary. A veto can block UN authorization; it cannot block sanctions, collective defense, condemnation, or prosecution. Conversely, repealing the veto cannot supply the unity, enforcement capacity, or willingness to risk escalation that coercive action requires. PRO’s “liberated majority” was never legally imprisoned from acting.
[C5.2] Keeping an aggressor at the table does not sanctify conquest; aggression remains unlawful. Nor does withdrawal turn that aggressor into an isolated outlaw whose departure costs the world nothing. The motion insists on accepting that rupture even when threatened, without guaranteeing any additional protection for the people whose suffering justifies the change.
[C5.3] A Council that cannot stop every atrocity is tragic. A Council that issues stronger commands while losing indispensable members is not thereby more just; it may simply be less obeyed and less able to cooperate where agreement remains possible. The veto’s moral cost is real, but abolition must be judged by consequences, not the purity of its voting rules. When the proposal offers unenforced authority in exchange for a functioning universal forum, the safeguard, however ugly, remains the wiser choice.
