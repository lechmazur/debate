[P1.1] Public money should buy public results. That is the core of this debate, and the proposition states it cleanly: funding should follow demonstrated graduation and post-graduation outcomes, not just bodies in seats.
[P1.2] Consider what the current system actually rewards. Under enrollment-based funding, a university collects the same dollars whether a student earns a credential in four years or disappears after one semester carrying debt and no degree. Worse, the institution has a built-in incentive to expand aggressively—recruit underprepared students with glossy marketing and reduced standards—because each head brings revenue. The results are predictable: programs that graduate fewer than half of those they admit, graduates underemployed in fields unrelated to their training, and taxpayers subsidizing failure while being told to trust the process.
[P1.3] The proposition corrects the incentives. Tying public funding to graduation rates forces institutions to invest in retention: better advising, clearer degree pathways, and remedial support that actually moves students forward. Linking funding to post-graduation earnings aligns the system with the public's actual interest—whether higher education produces graduates who can support themselves, repay loans, and contribute to the economy. A program that consistently leaves graduates worse off than they would have been entering the workforce directly should not be subsidized at the same rate as one that does not.
[P1.4] This is not a radical idea. It is the direction serious policy reformers have pushed for years, precisely because enrollment-only models failed to deliver. The motion does not ask us to abandon public investment in higher education; it asks us to make that investment accountable. Adjustments can be made for mission—research universities, community colleges serving non-traditional students, fields with delayed but real returns—but the principle holds: public funding is a contract, and contracts are measured by results, not promises.
