[P1.1] Ultra-processed foods supply most calories in many modern diets, and they are deliberately loaded with salt, sugar, and fat. Caps that force reformulation change those calories at the source. Labeling and education do not. They ask people to notice, interpret, and override a product designed to be cheap, fast, and hard to refuse.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is why mandates improve population health more.
[P1.3] Information-based change is fragile. Nutrition labels are scanned by a minority and understood by fewer. Front-of-pack warnings shift some purchases, yet effects shrink once novelty fades and marketing adapts. Campaigns raise knowledge without reliably changing what lands in the cart, especially where time, income, and food skills are scarce.
[P1.4] The households education misses eat the most ultra-processed food. A cap requires no literacy, motivation, or spare hour. It lowers salt in everyday bread, sugar in yogurt and cereal, and fat in sauces and ready meals for every buyer.
[P1.5] Those cuts add up because they are near-universal. Blood pressure tracks sodium intake. A modest drop in salt hidden in staples moves the whole distribution and prevents strokes and heart disease no pamphlet can match. Cutting free sugars reduces excess calories and dental decay without each person recalculating portions.
[P1.6] Fat limits trim energy density in foods chosen by habit. Compensation is incomplete. Shoppers do not replace every lost gram with table salt or homemade alternatives, and gradual reformulation often goes unnoticed as taste adapts. Even partial compliance beats the typical campaign, whose gains erode within months.
[P1.7] Industry prefers voluntary pledges and better labels because those preserve the current formula. Public health should prefer the lever that does not wait for perfect consumers. Education can support a mandate. It cannot replace one.
[P1.8] The healthier population is the one whose ordinary food is less harmful, not the one told to try harder.
