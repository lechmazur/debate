[P1.1] Consent is morally indispensable because it protects agency. But it answers only one question: did these parties authorize this exchange? Ethics asks further questions: What are they authorizing, under what conditions, and at whose expense? Consent is a gate, not a verdict.
[P1.2] First, people cannot consent away the rights of others. Two companies may willingly agree to fix prices; a business and an official may willingly exchange money for preferential treatment; a buyer and seller may willingly contract for stolen property. Each participant can be informed, capable, and enthusiastic. The transaction remains unethical because its costs are imposed on customers, citizens, or the rightful owner, none of whom authorized them.
[P1.3] Permission over one’s own choices does not create permission to harm third parties.
[P1.4] Second, agreement does not guarantee fair terms. Imagine an employer who knows a desperate worker faces hunger and offers dangerous labor for trivial pay. There need be no fraud or explicit threat; the worker may rationally prefer the offer to having nothing. Yet the employer is still exploiting vulnerability—using another person’s constrained circumstances to extract terms that could not be justified as fair cooperation.
[P1.5] Saying “they agreed” shows the transaction was preferable to the worker’s terrible alternative. It does not show the employer acted ethically in creating or taking advantage of that choice.
[P1.6] Third, consent cannot erase independent duties. A public official cannot make accepting a bribe ethical merely by finding a willing payer. A trustee cannot sell entrusted assets to a willing buyer for personal gain. The wrong lies partly in betrayal, corruption, or misuse of authority—features untouched by mutual agreement.
[P1.7] The opposing view must therefore defend an implausible principle: whenever the immediate parties consent, fairness, consequences, third-party rights, and role-based duties become morally irrelevant. But autonomy is not sovereignty over everyone affected, and choice is not proof of justice.
[P1.8] Consent gives us a strong reason to permit a transaction. It does not give us the only reason that matters. An ethical transaction requires consent plus respect for rights, fair dealing, and responsibility for harms.

[C1.1] Consent is not a rubber stamp uttered under deception or at gunpoint. It means competent adults, informed about material terms, free from coercion by the other party, agreeing to dispose of what is rightfully theirs. Consent of that kind alone makes a transaction ethical, because a transaction wrongs someone only by overriding their will or taking what is theirs — and consent is precisely the act that removes that wrong.
[C1.2] Notice that the alleged counterexamples all smuggle in a missing consent. Stolen property is not the seller's to sell; the owner's agreement is absent. A trustee selling entrusted assets, or an official selling public favors, is disposing of authority that belongs to others who never authorized that sale. Those are not cases of consent making theft or betrayal ethical.
[C1.3] They are cases where the person consenting had no right to consent to that thing.
[C1.4] The same discipline dissolves the exploitation charge. If an employer lies about danger or pay, there is no informed consent. If he threatens the worker, there is no voluntary consent. But if he honestly offers dangerous, low-paid work and the worker, understanding it, prefers it to hunger, who is wronged?
[C1.5] To call the deal unethical anyway is to substitute an outsider's idea of a fair wage for the worker's own judgment, and the usual remedy — forbidding the deal — leaves him with the hunger he was trying to escape. Background poverty may be unjust, but an offer that improves his options did not create it.
[C1.6] Fairness, beyond honesty and performance, has no non-paternalistic measure. Prices, wages, and risks are ethical when the people bearing them accept them for reasons of their own. Otherwise officials may veto payday loans, organ compensation, sex work, and any other bargain they dislike — not because someone was deceived or forced, but because consenting adults chose “wrong.”
[C1.7] Where every rights-holder truly consents, no victim remains for ethics to protect. Adding further vetoes does not protect agency; it replaces it.
