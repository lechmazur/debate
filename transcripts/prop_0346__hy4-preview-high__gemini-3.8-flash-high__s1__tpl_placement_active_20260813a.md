[P1.1] The case for output-first evaluation is simple: in remote and hybrid work, what an employee produces is the only thing that reliably tracks value, while constant monitoring of activity or presence mostly tracks obedience.
[P1.2] Presence signals reward the visible, not the valuable. A green chat dot, a mouse jiggle, or ten hours of camera time can be manufactured without a single useful deliverable. Yet a worker who thinks hard for two hours, takes a walk, then fixes a critical bug is invisible to activity trackers and invaluable to the team. When evaluation keys on busyness, rational people perform busyness: they answer emails at midnight to stay "active," attend every optional meeting to be seen, and avoid deep work because it looks like idleness.
[P1.3] Outputs cut through that theater. Code shipped and reviewed, tickets resolved, sales closed, designs approved, reports delivered—these are harder to fake and closer to what the organization actually wants.
[P1.4] Nor is this impractical for collaborative or hard-to-quantify work. The alternative to surveillance is not vagueness; it is clearer contracting. Teams can set short-cycle goals, demo work-in-progress, agree on what "done" means, and use peer and customer feedback. A remote analyst's value is not her keystrokes but whether the forecast informed a decision.
[P1.5] That is measurable through artifacts and review, not screenshots.
[P1.6] The motion says "mainly," not "only." Availability windows and responsiveness can matter for coverage and urgent coordination. But making presence the primary metric imports the office's worst habit—confusing face time with contribution—into homes, where it is both more intrusive and less fair. Caregiving, disability, time zone, and chronotype all make equal presence impossible, while equal output remains possible.
[P1.7] If we want remote work to keep its productivity and inclusion gains, we should evaluate people mainly by what they deliver, and treat activity data only as a narrow diagnostic, never as the scoreboard.

[C1.1] Evaluating remote workers "mainly on outputs" sounds enlightened, but it relies on a flawed analogy: treating modern knowledge work like nineteenth-century piece-rate labor. Remote teams do not operate as isolated freelancers submitting finished widgets; they succeed or fail on continuous coordination, mutual responsiveness, and operational cohesion. Elevating individual outputs above presence and activity signals severely undermines that collective foundation.
[C1.2] First, an output-first regime actively destroys "glue work." PRO dismisses presence as empty theater, but predictable availability is the bedrock of synchronization. In distributed environments, an unmonitored worker who vanishes during core hours imposes a compounding latency tax on everyone else. When evaluation keys primarily on discrete artifacts—tickets closed, code pushed, or reports delivered—rational workers stop performing unquantified collective labor.
[C1.3] They stop mentoring junior colleagues, decline to troubleshoot shared infrastructure, and ignore peer questions because those critical tasks produce no personal output metrics. Tracking presence and responsiveness ensures employees remain active participants in a shared enterprise, not isolated mercenaries.
[C1.4] Second, output is a dangerously lagging indicator. When an employee quietly burns out, disengages, or struggles with an impossible task, an output-only model discovers the crisis weeks or months later when a deliverable is missed. By then, the damage to the client, the project, and team morale is irreversible. Continuous activity and presence monitoring provides real-time telemetry, allowing managers to identify workflow bottlenecks, redistribute unfair burdens, and intervene before quiet struggles become catastrophic failures.
[C1.5] Finally, output metrics are inherently vulnerable to gaming and structural distortion. Deliverables depend heavily on external variables, client responsiveness, and task difficulty. Evaluating mainly on outputs incentivizes cherry-picking easy work and cutting corners on quality, while penalizing those who shoulder high-friction challenges. Transparent activity and presence signals provide the only reliable, ongoing baseline of fair contribution.
[C1.6] Abandoning continuous visibility does not emancipate remote workers; it isolates them, breeds resentment, and leaves organizational health entirely to chance.
