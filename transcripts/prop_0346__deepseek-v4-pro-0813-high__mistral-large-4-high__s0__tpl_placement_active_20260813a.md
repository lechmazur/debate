[P1.1] Remote and hybrid work should be judged primarily on the deliverables, quality, and outcomes people produce, not on how often their status glows green or how quickly they answer a chat. The reason is elementary: output is the closest available proxy for the value an employee creates, while presence and activity signals mainly measure compliance. Two workers show the difference. One is visible online all day, attends every call, and responds immediately, yet ships late, sloppy reports.
[P1.2] Another blocks off focus time, appears less active, and delivers a polished client report two days early. Only output-based evaluation reliably rewards the second worker; constant monitoring tends to reward the first.
[P1.3] Worse, monitoring becomes the target. When presence is treated as the test of performance, rational employees optimize for presence. They keep chat active, schedule unnecessary meetings, and avoid deep asynchronous work because it can look like absence. This produces activity theater, not business results.
[P1.4] Output targets push behavior in the right direction: finishing tasks, fixing defects, meeting deadlines, improving client outcomes. That is not a crude piece-rate system. Output evaluation can include project milestones, work quality, revenue, renewal or resolution rates, peer review, and manager assessment of less easily quantified contributions. The standard is completed value, not keystrokes.
[P1.5] Presence-based evaluation also carries direct costs. It signals distrust, erodes autonomy, and can push strong remote employees to employers who evaluate results instead of surveillance. It creates unfair advantages for those sharing a manager’s time zone or visible online schedule, penalizing people who work asynchronously, manage caregiving, or simply work best in uninterrupted blocks. Difficulty in defining outputs is a management design problem, not an argument for reverting to a worse proxy.
[P1.6] The right response is clearer goals, regular outcome reviews, and better feedback—not more monitoring. The motion should win because it ties evaluation to what actually makes an organization succeed: the work that gets done.
