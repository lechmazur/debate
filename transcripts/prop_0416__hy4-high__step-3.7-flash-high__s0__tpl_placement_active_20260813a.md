[P1.1] Egg-freezing ads sell a future; patients buy a probability. Clinics are paid now for retrieval, drugs, and storage. Patients receive no guaranteed usable embryo or live birth. Because the seller holds the data, expertise, and profit motive, advertising is part of informed consent.
[P1.2] A large “preserve your options” with buried age limits shapes the decision by omission.
[P1.3] Age is not a detail; it is the product’s central variable. Egg quantity and quality decline with age, so a 33-year-old and a 41-year-old are not buying comparable odds. Realistic success rates must be age-banded, live-birth-oriented, and expressed as ranges with assumptions: how many eggs or cycles, whose data, and what denominator. They must also say plainly that losses can occur at thaw, fertilization, embryo development, transfer, or implantation, and that later use adds uncertainty.
[P1.4] Prominence matters because truthful fine print does not cure a misleading frame. Ads are built to leave one impression: timely empowerment. If flexibility, financing, or “options” are memorable while uncertainty is not, patients may spend heavily, overestimate security, and postpone other family planning. Egg freezing is not insurance; it is a chain of attrition.
[P1.5] The rule can be modest: no ban, no compelled pessimism, no state instruction about when to reproduce. It requires age-based success rates and uncertainty in the same ad, same visual field, and ordinary language, alongside any claim about preserved options. Clinics can still market convenience, autonomy, and hope; they simply cannot make the hope louder than the limits. Complexity is no excuse: a range with assumptions is simpler than a slogan that hides them.
[P1.6] Voluntary disclosure fails because the least candid ad can look most attractive; “freeze now, choose later” can beat a sober warning. A uniform requirement stops that race to the bottom and protects autonomy by making the advertised choice resemble medical reality: an age-sensitive chance, not a promise.

[C1.1] Mandating that egg-freezing advertisements display age-based success rates more prominently than other messaging would harm patients, undermine informed choice, and replace actionable personal information with misleading one-size-fits-all statistics. Any aggregated age-banded success rate is inherently incapable of representing the odds of any specific patient. Egg freezing outcomes depend on a dizzying array of individual factors: ovarian reserve, the number of eggs retrieved in a given cycle, clinic-specific freezing and thawing protocols, the patient’s future health when they attempt to use their eggs, and even how many eggs they ultimately thaw for fertilization.
[C1.2] A single average for 38-year-olds, for example, could reflect data from patients who froze 25 eggs across three cycles, and would be completely irrelevant to a 38-year-old who only retrieves 8 eggs in a single retrieval. Prominently displaying these averages would give patients a false sense of either security or futility, replacing the personalized context that actually determines their odds with a generic, misleading number. This requirement would also chill the very advertising that helps people recognize egg freezing as a viable option for them in the first place.
[C1.3] Current ads act as a critical first touchpoint for people who may not yet know egg freezing is available, or that earlier freezing yields better outcomes. Forcing clinics to frontload complex, variable statistical risk would dissuade many—especially younger people who would benefit most from early freezing—from even seeking a consultation where they can get accurate, personalized information tailored to their own biology. Existing medical and regulatory norms already require clinics to share detailed, individualized success data with patients during the informed consent process before any procedure is scheduled.
[C1.4] Advertising is not a substitute for medical counseling; forcing complex statistical disclosures into ad space would clutter messaging, prioritize abstract statistical anxiety over the personal, contextual decision-making that actually determines whether egg freezing is the right choice for a given patient.
