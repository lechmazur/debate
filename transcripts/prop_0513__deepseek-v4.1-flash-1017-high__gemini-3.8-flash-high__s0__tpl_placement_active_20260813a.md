[P1.1] A merger can leave grocery prices unchanged and still leave a town with one hospital, one meatpacking plant, or one dominant warehouse employer. That is the blind spot the motion targets. Labor-market monopsony is not a labor-relations afterthought; it is market power exercised on the other side of the transaction. When employers combine, workers in thin local labor markets lose the practical ability to bid wages up.
[P1.2] The resulting wage suppression, slower hiring, and worse conditions are an output restriction—just measured in labor rather than milk.
[P1.3] The consumer-price screen cannot see this. A merged employer may face enough product-market competition to keep prices flat, or it may capture efficiencies that offset price effects, while still using increased labor-market concentration to hold down pay. A price-only review then gives a false clearance: shoppers are protected, workers are not.
[P1.4] Treating labor monopsony “as seriously” does not mean pretending every merger is a wage conspiracy. It means giving labor effects comparable analytical weight: define local labor markets, examine employer concentration and switching costs, request wage and hiring data, scrutinize claimed efficiencies that depend on suppressing pay, and impose remedies when harm is shown. Consumer effects stay in the analysis; they stop being the only entry ticket.
[P1.5] The asymmetry is indefensible. We do not ignore price effects because market definition is hard; we do the work. The same should be true when a merger gives an employer power over nurses, technicians, or line workers. Competition policy protects the competitive process, not only the checkout counter.
[P1.6] A merger that is a wash for shoppers and a disaster for workers is not benign.

[C1.1] Equating labor-market monopsony with consumer-price effects in merger review tears antitrust away from its coherent core and turns it into an unworkable, contradictory regulatory regime. Merger policy works precisely because it possesses a unifying anchor: protecting total output and consumer welfare. Elevating labor monopsony to co-equal status introduces an intractable conflict of interest that competition policy cannot resolve.
[C1.2] Consider the inevitable collision. The primary justification for many pro-competitive mergers is the realization of operational efficiencies—streamlining overlapping distribution networks, consolidating administrative overhead, and eliminating redundancies. These synergies lower marginal costs, enabling firms to reduce prices, expand production, and invest in innovation, directly benefiting broad consumer bases. Yet under a co-equal labor standard, the moment a merger rationalizes redundant positions or dampens localized hiring competition, it faces prohibition.
[C1.3] Regulators would be forced to block transactions that deliver cheaper food, medicine, or goods to millions of working-class households simply to insulate a narrow pool of local workers from labor adjustments.
[C1.4] Furthermore, labor markets are fundamentally ill-suited for the mechanical concentration screens applied to product markets. Unlike standardized consumer goods, labor is deeply heterogeneous, shaped by commuting tolerances, subjective amenities, transferrable skills, and cross-industry mobility. An agency cannot reliably delineate whether a warehouse worker’s relevant market is strictly limited to local fulfillment centers or realistically spans regional retail, manufacturing, and transport. Elevating this speculative inquiry to a dispositive threshold will mire merger review in arbitrary, subjective litigation.
[C1.5] Antitrust authorities are not wage boards, and merger review is far too blunt an instrument for labor regulation. Worker protections are vital, but they belong to purpose-built labor policy—collective bargaining rights, minimum wage statutes, and non-compete bans—which empower labor directly without sacrificing productive efficiency. Forcing merger review to serve two opposing masters will paralyze dynamic investment, protect operational waste, and ultimately tax the consumers competition policy was created to protect.

A distribution merger that genuinely lowers costs can show expanded output and hiring; a merger that “saves” money only by weakening the local wage floor should not get a free pass because shelf prices happen to fall.
