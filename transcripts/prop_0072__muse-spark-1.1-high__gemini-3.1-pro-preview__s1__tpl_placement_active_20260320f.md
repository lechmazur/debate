[P1.1] A government that runs public schools has no right to run a public relations campaign in them. If the state compels attendance, funds the books, and certifies the teachers, it takes on a duty not to lie by omission about its own record.
[P1.2] The core case for this requirement is democratic competence. Citizens cannot judge what their government should do if they do not know what it has done. A student who learns that America won World War II but not that it interned Japanese-American families, who learns about westward expansion but not the systematic removal and killing of Native peoples, who learns about the Cold War but not about state-sanctioned segregation or torture programs, is not educated. He is curated.
[P1.3] That curation leaves him helpless when a politician again proposes mass internment, voter suppression, or "enhanced interrogation" in the name of security.
[P1.4] Second, facing self-committed atrocities is uniquely preventative. Foreign crimes teach that others can be cruel; domestic crimes teach that *we* can be cruel, under specific legal, bureaucratic, and ideological conditions that still exist. Germany requires teaching the Holocaust not to shame Germans forever, but because German students need to recognize how German law, police, and ordinary conformity made it possible. Countries that hide their own abuses — Russia skipping the Gulag, Japan downplaying Nanjing, Turkey erasing the Armenian genocide — do not get more unity; they get fragility and repeated denial.
[P1.5] Requiring this study does not mean self-hatred. It means honest patriotism: the love that is strong enough to look at slavery and segregation and still say the country is worth improving. That is the only kind of civic pride a free state deserves.

[C1.3] In a polarized democracy, this turns the classroom into a perpetual partisan battleground. One administration might mandate the study of historical structural racism; the next might redefine "abuses" to include foreign interventions, border policies, or abortion, mandating their study instead. We do not eliminate curation by demanding this; we merely change its political flavor. Politicians will dictate historical guilt to score points with their base, leaving students as pawns in endless culture wars.
[C1.4] Second, public education must forge a shared civic identity. While historical awareness is valuable, a rigid requirement emphasizing self-committed atrocities shifts the core purpose of civic education from building shared foundational values to institutionalizing national guilt. [C1.5] Instead of inspiring students to engage in "honest patriotism," it teaches them that their institutions are irredeemably corrupt. You cannot build a cohesive society on a state-mandated curriculum of self-indictment.
[C1.6] Finally, public schools are not universally equipped for a mandate of historical trauma. Forcing every district to study major atrocities pushes teachers into emotional minefields they often lack specialized psychological or historical training to navigate safely. We should trust local educators and communities to contextualize difficult history appropriately, rather than deploying a blunt government mandate that prioritizes political self-flagellation over sound, developmentally appropriate pedagogy.
