[P1.1] Access to clean water is a fundamental human right and an ecological necessity, not a commodity to be strip-mined for corporate profit. When governments allow private corporations to own, trade, or extract municipal water and aquifers for commercial bottling, they violate the public trust. They trade long-term ecological survival for short-term corporate gain.
[P1.2] The commercial bottling industry operates on a model of geographic extraction and economic exploitation. Corporations seek out high-quality municipal sources and pristine aquifers, paying negligible permit fees—often fractions of a penny per gallon. They pump millions of gallons daily, package it in single-use plastic, and sell it back to consumers at markups exceeding a thousand percent. This is not wealth creation; it is a parasitic extraction model that depletes local water tables, lowers domestic well levels, and damages surrounding ecosystems, all while generating massive plastic waste that municipalities must then pay to manage.
[P1.3] This extraction creates a dangerous asymmetric vulnerability. Aquifers are finite, shared resources that recharge slowly over decades. As climate change accelerates droughts and worsens municipal water stress, allowing private entities to hold extraction and trading rights paralyzes local democratic governance. When a multinational corporation owns the water rights, public authorities face costly litigation or prohibitive buyouts just to prioritize the basic drinking and sanitation needs of their own citizens during a shortage.
[P1.4] We cannot allow corporate profit margins to dictate who gets to drink during a drought.
[P1.5] By banning private commercial extraction for bottling, we re-establish a vital boundary: water must remain a shared public utility. This ban does not stifle legitimate commerce; it protects the foundational resource that makes all other economic and human activity possible. We must ensure that municipal water serves the communities that rely on it, rather than the balance sheets of multinational bottling companies. Water is life, and life cannot be privatized.

[C1.3] A ban eliminates that incentive entirely, leaving municipalities to fund costly upgrades alone.

[C1.6] Finally, water is already a commodity in agriculture, industry, and even municipal supply. Treating bottling as a unique moral failure ignores the consistency of our water policy. Regulation—not prohibition—is the proven path to keep water affordable, protect ecosystems, and let the private sector shoulder part of the cost. Banning bottling would sacrifice jobs, reduce investment, and leave communities poorer and less resilient.
