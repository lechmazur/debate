[P1.1] A rating system is only legitimate if it measures the worker. The moment it measures the customer's prejudice, mood, or malice instead, it stops being a performance metric and becomes a mechanism for transmitting abuse into someone's livelihood. That is the heart of this debate, and it is why workers must be able to opt out of corrupted ratings without losing platform access.
[P1.2] Consider how customer-facing gig ratings actually work. A rideshare driver can be rated down for declining a conversation, for their accent, for their appearance, for being a woman driving at night. A delivery courier can be rated down by a customer who was angry the elevator was slow. We know from widespread reporting and platform data releases that Black drivers and workers with foreign-sounding names receive systematically lower ratings, and platforms themselves have acknowledged this enough to hide tipping until after rating in some apps—conceding that customers punish workers for things outside their control.
[P1.3] When a rating reflects bias rather than service, forcing the worker to accept it is forcing them to absorb discrimination as if it were feedback.
[P1.4] The stakes are absolute. On most major platforms, falling below roughly a 4.6 average triggers deactivation. That means a handful of abusive or biased raters can end someone's income entirely. The proposition asks for something modest: when ratings are strongly shaped by abuse, bias, or harassment, the worker can decline that system and keep working.
[P1.5] Not every rating scheme—only corrupted ones. Not immunity from consequence—only from being fired by bigots and trolls.
[P1.6] The alternative is indefensible. It says a customer's right to rate anonymously outweighs a worker's right not to be deplatformed by harassment. No other industry works this way: an employee can report a hostile customer to a manager; a gig worker's "manager" is the hostile customer, holding a button that can fire them.
[P1.7] Platforms can still enforce safety through ride records, delivery confirmation, complaint review. The only thing lost by letting workers opt out is the power to launder discrimination through a star rating.
