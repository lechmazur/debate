[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether a border adjustment is flawless, but whether it changes emissions incentives faster and more widely than domestic subsidies and regulations alone. Its central advantage is that it reaches goods produced abroad, which U.S.-only policies structurally miss.
[P1.2] Domestic regulation prevents pollution inside the United States, but when domestic carbon prices make higher-carbon production more expensive, foreign producers can retain an export-market advantage. Production can move abroad, transferring emissions rather than removing them. A border adjustment attacks that leakage channel directly: if a steel shipment carries more embedded emissions, it faces a rising import cost. That changes the exporter’s economics, not merely its reputation.
[P1.3] This matters over five years. New clean-energy infrastructure, factory retrofits, and vehicle turnover take time. An import adjustment instead works through an existing commercial channel, rewarding foreign producers who use cleaner power, higher-grade materials, and more efficient processes. Where cutting a product’s carbon intensity is cheaper abroad than decarbonizing comparable U.S. production, the border signal can drive the first reductions globally.
[P1.4] Domestic subsidies remain useful, but are vulnerable to capture, may subsidize emissions that would have declined anyway, and cannot alter every trading partner’s competitiveness. Regulations are stronger, yet their direct effect remains geographically bounded. The proposition compares the adjustment with domestic measures alone; it does not forbid combining them. Border revenue can support clean energy, workers, and reductions in distortionary domestic subsidies.
[P1.5] Objections about trade rules, administration, and foreign retaliation do not erase this comparative advantage. A phased design, credible carbon-intensity accounting, and predictable treatment of export credits can address those concerns while preserving the emissions signal. Most importantly, domestic-only policy leaves foreign marginal decisions unchanged; border adjustment changes them too. Over the next five years, that broader incentive is the stronger route to reducing global emissions, not merely relocating them nationally.
