[P1.1] DOGE‑style efficiency drives are marketed as rapid waste‑busters, but they systematically erode the very capacity that makes government work. Their core method—slash headcount, freeze hiring, and shut programs with little analysis—treats the state like a corporation that can be trimmed without cost. In reality, government capacity is a public good: every experienced employee, every maintained database, every routine process is a layer that keeps services running. When those layers are ripped out, the savings are tiny compared with the cascade of failures that follow.
[P1.2] Consider the U.K.’s austerity experiment after 2010. Councils were forced to cut staff by up to 30 % in a few years. The headline figure was a few billion pounds saved, yet the result was collapsed social‑care services, rising homelessness, and a surge in emergency‑room visits that cost the NHS far more than the cuts ever saved. The same pattern repeats in the United States: rapid staff reductions at the FAA and CDC during the early pandemic slowed safety inspections and delayed vaccine rollout, generating downstream economic losses measured in tens of billions.
[P1.3] The mechanism is simple. DOGE drives chase visible, easy‑to‑measure waste—duplicate reports, redundant meetings—while ignoring the hidden, systemic inefficiencies that actually drain budgets. The “low‑hanging fruit” they cut is often the staff who keep complex systems alive. Their departure forces agencies to spend months rehiring, retraining, and fixing errors, a cost that dwarfs the original savings.
[P1.4] Moreover, morale collapses across the civil service, prompting a wave of voluntary resignations that further hollows capacity.
[P1.5] Thus the proposition holds: the damage to state capacity and service delivery far outweighs any modest waste reduction. The real test of an efficiency drive is not the headline number it saves, but whether the government can still deliver the services citizens depend on. DOGE‑style drives fail that test decisively.
