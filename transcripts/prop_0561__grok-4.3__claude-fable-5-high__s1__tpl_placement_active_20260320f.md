[P1.1] Platform gig work has normalized full-time schedules without the minimum security net that prevents workers from absorbing every shock alone. Prorated benefits tied to hours close that gap by matching health coverage, leave, and retirement contributions to actual time contributed rather than to legal labels platforms choose to avoid.
[P1.2] This requirement forces platforms to internalize costs they currently export. A driver or courier logging thirty-five hours a week generates steady revenue yet carries all medical risk and zero pension accrual; society later pays through emergency care and public assistance. Tying benefits to verified hours ends that subsidy without converting every participant into a salaried employee.
[P1.3] Flexibility survives under proration. Occasional users trigger only marginal added cost, while consistent workers receive scaled protection that scales with engagement. Platforms retain variable staffing; they simply can no longer treat labor as cost-free capacity.
[P1.4] Existing rules in several jurisdictions already require similar accruals for some platform categories. Companies adjusted pricing and scheduling rather than exit markets, demonstrating that modest per-hour obligations do not destroy the model. The real threat to long-term viability is instead the churn and income volatility that result when workers lack any buffer against illness or downtime.
[P1.5] Opponents claim that added obligations will price out low-margin tasks, yet the same platforms already absorb marketing, insurance, and technology expenses that far exceed prorated benefit estimates. The choice is therefore not between flexibility and collapse, but between continued externalization of risk and a basic alignment of contribution with minimal security.

[C1.1] The proposition takes the single worst feature of twentieth-century labor policy—chaining your health insurance and pension to one paymaster—and bolts it onto the one labor market built to escape it. That is the central mistake, and it produces three predictable failures.
[C1.2] First, "hours worked" is a fiction in gig work. A courier can have three apps open simultaneously, accepting whichever job pings first. Is she working ninety minutes per hour? Who pays?
[C1.3] Platforms will answer this ambiguity the only rational way: by metering and rationing access. Expect algorithmic caps that boot drivers offline before benefit thresholds, restrictions on idle app-on time, and lockouts during slow periods. Workers lose the very thing they came for—the ability to log on whenever they choose—and earn less because access itself becomes the scarce, rationed resource.
[C1.4] Second, the costs do not vanish into corporate margins; they land on workers and consumers. Per-task pay falls, prices rise, demand shrinks, and the marginal workers—the student, the caregiver doing ten hours around school pickup, the retiree topping up Social Security—are exactly the ones platforms shed first. Surveys consistently show most gig workers are part-time supplementers who already have coverage elsewhere; this mandate taxes their earnings to fund benefits they neither need nor can meaningfully accrue across five different apps in fragments.
[C1.5] Third, there is a better instrument sitting in plain view: portable, worker-owned benefit accounts funded through transaction-based contributions, or simply strengthening universal public provision. Those approaches deliver security without inviting platforms to game hour definitions, without punishing multi-app workers, and without rebuilding the employer-tether that already traps salaried Americans in jobs they hate.
[C1.6] So the real choice is not "security versus externalized risk." It is whether we want genuine, portable security designed for fluid work—or a clumsy hours-based mandate that shrinks opportunity, rations flexibility, and entrenches the broken model it claims to fix. Mandating the wrong architecture is worse than building the right one.

Prorated benefits tied to verified hours simply stop that selective exemption rather than inventing new chains.
[P2.2] The multi-app claim collapses once measurement shifts from abstract availability to platform-specific transaction logs. Each company already records accepted offers, active time, and payouts; prorating contributions against those records requires no unified employer and creates no single paymaster. Platforms that ration access to dodge the obligation will face the same labor shortages and higher churn costs that already plague high-turnover operations, giving them incentive to compete on reliability instead.
[P2.3] Cost incidence arguments ignore existing price discipline. Platforms have repeatedly absorbed added per-transaction expenses—insurance minimums, fuel surcharges, regulatory fees—without eliminating part-time participation. The workers who drop off under modest per-hour accruals are precisely those already logging enough hours for benefits to matter; the truly marginal user remains unaffected and continues to subsidize no one else’s risk.
[P2.4] Portable accounts and expanded public provision are compatible with the mandate, not substitutes for it. Without an hours-based funding floor, transaction levies remain too small and too easy to evade, leaving the externalized costs untouched. The proposition does not lock workers into one platform; it only requires that whoever captures the labor hours also contributes to the minimal buffer those hours currently lack.
