[C2.1] The proposition's centerpiece is a "lifespan cliff"—the claim that post-war concrete sits quietly for decades then fails all at once. This is rhetorically vivid but technically false. Concrete does not deteriorate on a timer. It fails through specific, observable mechanisms: chloride ingress, carbonation, rebar corrosion, freeze-thaw cycles.
[C2.2] These are exactly what modern inspection regimes monitor with sensors, drones, and core sampling. A bridge does not "turn 50" and collapse; it shows measurable degradation years in advance, allowing load restrictions or targeted repairs. The "cliff" exists only if governments ignore their own engineers—which is a management failure, not a material property of concrete.
[C2.3] The claim that civil engineers have secretly demanded tripled budgets for a decade is equally suspect. If thousands of professionals had submitted identical funding demands to every wealthy government for ten years, this would be public record, not a whispered consensus. Governments do not "choose not to publish" engineering assessments; they publish budgets, and those budgets show steady, substantial maintenance spending across OECD nations—not the "one third of minimum" the proposition alleges.
[C2.4] The sequencing argument collapses under scrutiny. The proposition insists new megaprojects never retire dangerous assets, yet replacement is the ultimate maintenance strategy. A new tunnel does not merely coexist with an old bridge; it removes traffic load, allowing the old structure to be downgraded or demolished. Retiring obsolete infrastructure reduces the future maintenance burden and eliminates the risk entirely.
[C2.5] The proposed moratorium would force engineers to keep patching 1960s concrete while forbidding the modern replacements that would actually reduce system-wide risk.
[C2.6] Finally, the cynicism that governments will never catch up if allowed to build is an argument against political will, not for tripling budgets. Tripling spending does not fix misallocation; it amplifies it. Blanket budget inflation funds politically visible repaving while fracture-critical bridges wait. The choice is not between panic and neglect, but between triage and tripling.
