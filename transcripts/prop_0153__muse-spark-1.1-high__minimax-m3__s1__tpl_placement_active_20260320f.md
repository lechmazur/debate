[P1.1] There is no principled reason to single out gestational labor as the one form of bodily risk and care we must do for free.
[P1.2] We allow people to work as firefighters, boxers, clinical trial subjects, and to carry pregnancies that are their own. We treat compensation as respect for risk and effort. Surrogacy involves months of work, medical risk, and emotional labor. To say a woman may do this only altruistically is to say her labor is not worth paying for, and that only women with wealthy support networks who can afford to work for free should participate.
[P1.3] Paid, regulated surrogacy recognizes what the work is.
[P1.4] Bans do not make surrogacy disappear. They export it. Intended parents with resources travel to poorly regulated markets; surrogates without legal protections sign unenforceable contracts, face no insurance guarantee, no independent lawyer, no right to make medical decisions. That is where real coercion lives — in secrecy and legal limbo.
[P1.5] Legalization at home keeps the practice visible where labor law, medical standards, and courts can reach it.
[P1.6] Regulation is not a slogan. It means mandatory independent legal counsel paid for by intended parents but loyal to the surrogate, comprehensive medical and psychological screening with opt-out at any time before transfer, escrowed funds held by a third party, full health insurance including postpartum, life insurance, no clause that can force a medical decision against her will, and an age and prior-birth requirement to avoid first-time vulnerability. That architecture directly answers exploitation concerns instead of abandoning women to an underground market.
[P1.7] Finally, for people who cannot gestate — women without uteri, cancer survivors, gay couples — surrogacy is often the only path to a biologically related child. A legal regime gives that child clear parentage from birth, not years in court.
[P1.8] Autonomy, safety, and clarity all point the same way: legalize, compensate, and protect.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to trust that "strong protections" can neutralize the coercion inherent in commercial surrogacy. They cannot, because the coercion in this market is not a bug — it is the system working as designed.
[C1.2] In every functioning surrogacy market, intended parents are comparatively wealthy, and the women who gestate are comparatively not. Gestational labor is one of the few markets where the buyer's preferences determine which bodies qualify, and the laborer's economic circumstances determine who shows up. A contract lawyer paid for by the buyer cannot make that asymmetry symmetric. She can only formalize it.
[C1.3] This is structural coercion — the kind no checklist cures.
[C1.4] The historical record undermines the regulation-solves-it claim. India permitted commercial surrogacy for years under formal guidelines; reporting documented surrogate mothers in dormitories, separated from families, with limited recourse. Ukraine offered a permissive framework until war suspended it. Even in heavily regulated U.S. states, surrogates have described pressure to terminate or reduce for the commissioning couple, and contracts routinely include selectivity clauses that subordinate the woman's medical decisions to the buyer.
[C1.5] The premise that surrogacy is "often the only path" to a biologically related child is rhetorical pressure, not medical fact. It narrows the conversation away from adoption, and reframes reproductive contingency as a problem the market must fix — rather than a limit societies might respect.
[C1.6] Then there is the child. Commercial surrogacy produces, before birth, a human being who is already the subject of a contract between three or more adults. "Strong protections" rarely specify what happens when a child is born with a disability, when intended parents divorce mid-pregnancy, or when a surrogate changes her mind at thirty-six weeks. The motion's safeguards describe entry.
[C1.7] They say little about the person at the center, or about the women who will bear the consequences when contracts meet reality.
