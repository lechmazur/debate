[P1.1] Conservation matters. But when a region faces structural scarcity, the central question is whether primary investment should expand supply or merely squeeze demand. My answer: large-scale desalination, because only supply expansion can break the ceiling that conservation cannot raise.
[P1.2] Cities grow, industry expands, agriculture feeds people. Conservation has real gains — fixing leaks, pricing water, recycling greywater — but demand-side measures only redistribute a shrinking pie. Once efficient fixtures, drip irrigation, and closed industrial loops are in place, gains taper off. Rationing then does what it always does: it allocates misery.
[P1.3] The poorest cut bathing and cooking first; small businesses close; agricultural exports collapse. Conservation-plus-rationing is a managed retreat from prosperity.
[P1.4] Desalination adds new molecules of fresh water. Seawater is effectively inexhaustible; fossil aquifers are not. Reverse osmosis costs have fallen steeply over two decades, and energy demand per cubic meter has dropped alongside. Pair plants with solar or waste-heat cogeneration and the climate footprint shrinks further.
[P1.5] Israel, Singapore, and the Gulf states have shown that a region can convert chronic shortage into reliable supply by betting on engineered water, not just on thrift.
[P1.6] The expected rebuttal is energy. Desalination is thirsty for electricity — true. But so is pumping from ever-deeper aquifers, so is transporting surface water long distances, and so is the air-conditioning load rationing forces during heat waves. The honest comparison is total system energy per reliable cubic meter through a drought.
[P1.7] On that metric, modern desalination with renewables is competitive and improving.
[P1.8] "Primarily" is not "exclusively." When a region chooses where to anchor capital, grid capacity, and technical talent for the next thirty years, it should bet on the technology that makes water abundant rather than the policies that make it scarce. Conservation is the brake. Desalination is the engine.
[P1.9] A car without an engine does not run on better brakes.

[C1.2] Primary investment is a ranking of scarce capital. Conservation and rationing should take that rank. The largest, cheapest, fastest source of water in a dry basin is water already being lost: leaky urban networks, flood-irrigated fields, thirsty export crops, unpriced household use. Those are not a pie that has already been squeezed dry.
[C1.3] In most water-stressed regions, agriculture still takes the bulk of withdrawals, and cities still lose a large share of treated supply before it reaches a tap. Efficiency, recycling, crop choice, and honest prices raise the usable supply without building a new ocean plant.
[C1.4] Desalination adds molecules only where the ocean, the grid, and the treasury already meet. Inland basins still face pipelines over desert and mountains. Plants take years. They lock in energy demand, brine discharge, and a high unit cost that poor users cannot pay without perpetual subsidy.
[C1.5] Pairing them with solar does not erase the capital intensity or the marine damage. And once the plant exists, the political incentive to stop waste collapses.
[C1.6] Israel, Singapore, and the Gulf prove a narrow point: rich or oil-backed coastal states can industrialize their tap. They do not prove that Sahelian, Central Asian, or inland Indian basins should put desalination first. Rationing, designed to protect drinking water and high-value use, is allocation of a common resource, not allocated misery. Unpriced abundance is the policy that manufactures shortage.
[C1.7] Conservation is not the brake. It is the water itself, recovered. Desalination is a costly coastal add-on. It should never be the primary bet.

[P2.5] Third, the coastal objection misreads the motion. It says "water-scarce regions," not "landlocked arid basins." The most acute scarcity today sits on coasts — North Africa, the Levant, southern California, southern Spain, Australia, the Gulf. The proposition addresses them directly.
[P2.6] Even for inland regions, falling transport costs and modular plants are extending reach year by year.
[P2.7] Fourth, the moral hazard claim — that abundant supply kills conservation incentives — would, taken seriously, ban all supply expansion everywhere. [P2.8] Finally, the opponent treats Israel, Singapore, and the Gulf as if their transformation were embarrassing. It is the opposite. They turned chronic shortage into reliable supply — the precedent water-scarce regions need.
