[P1.1] Meat production imposes massive, unpriced external costs that every taxpayer currently subsidizes, regardless of income. Industrial livestock accounts for roughly 15% of global greenhouse gas emissions, drives deforestation, depletes scarce water supplies, spreads antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and generates billions in annual diet-related health costs from heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and other conditions that strain public health systems low-income households rely on far more heavily than wealthy ones. For context, producing one pound of industrial beef requires roughly 1,800 gallons of water, yet that cost is not reflected in its price, and is often drawn from aquifers low-income rural communities depend on for drinking water.
[P1.2] Right now, these harms are hidden in food prices and public budgets—meaning low-income communities already pay a disproportionate share of these costs via higher taxes, more frequent climate disaster fallout, and worse local environmental quality, even if they buy little meat. A tax on meat production tied to its full environmental and health externalities corrects this market failure rather than creating a new burden.
[P1.3] Critics claim this tax will raise food prices for low-income households, but that argument ignores the policy’s revenue use and long-term supply effects. Tax proceeds can be ring-fenced for targeted food assistance, subsidies for affordable plant-based proteins, and public nutrition programs for vulnerable communities, offsetting any short-term price hikes. Over time, shifting land and resources away from inefficient industrial meat production will boost output of nutrient-dense, low-cost crops, lowering overall food prices.
[P1.4] The status quo is a hidden, regressive tax on the poor. A well-designed meat externality tax, paired with targeted rebates, makes costs fair, reduces public harm, and delivers long-term savings for the households that need them most.

[C1.6] We must not balance environmental budgets on the backs of hungry families. The path to sustainability lies in agricultural innovation, not in starving the working class.
