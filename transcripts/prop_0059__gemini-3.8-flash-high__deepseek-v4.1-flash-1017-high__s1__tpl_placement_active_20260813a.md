[P1.1] The dominant dating-app model—the swipe-based, freemium architecture that governs the market—operates on a fatal contradiction: a business that successfully pairs its users permanently destroys its own customer base. These platforms are not digital matchmakers; they are engagement engines engineered to monetize prolonged singlehood. By transforming courtship into a casino-style feedback loop, they systematically undermine the psychological conditions necessary for real relationships to form.
[P1.2] First, the interface enforces hyper-commodification and choice overload. Swiping reduces multifaceted human beings to two-dimensional visual thumbnails evaluated in fractions of a second. This frictionless parade of profiles manufactures the illusion of infinite romantic supply. Users naturally adopt a consumerist mindset, perpetually wondering whether an algorithmically superior match lies just one flick away.
[P1.3] Why tolerate normal human friction or invest the emotional labor that bonding requires when a replacement feels instantly accessible? The result is not better selection, but chronic indecision, disposability, and an inability to commit.
[P1.4] Second, the structural mechanics degrade the dating experience for nearly everyone involved. Dating platforms deliberately engineer asymmetric frustration to sell premium subscriptions. For the majority of men, algorithmic sorting creates a visibility desert, producing severe rejection fatigue, loneliness, and pressure to pay for basic exposure. For women, it yields an overwhelming flood of low-effort, low-intent matches, turning courtship into an exhausting filtering chore.
[P1.5] Both sides end up cynical, defensive, and burnt out. By replacing shared physical context, mutual friends, and organic social accountability with gamified sorting, these apps cultivate an environment where ghosting and deception carry no social penalty.
[P1.6] While an algorithmic lottery occasionally produces a lasting couple, it does so only as a collateral byproduct of a system whose primary goal is retention. When a technology actively trades emotional connection for addictive swiping, it does not improve relationship formation—it fundamentally poisons it for most users.

[C1.1] The motion fails at the comparison it never makes. “Worse for most users” than what? Not than a fantasy matchmaker with perfect information, but than the realistic alternatives: a bar, a friend’s wedding, a workplace, a small town’s eligible pool. Judged there, the dominant app model is a search technology—and search technologies are judged by whether they expand access and reduce matching costs, not by whether their corporate incentives are pure.
[C1.2] Apps create relationship formation where it was previously thin. The queer teenager in a conservative town, the disabled user for whom physical venues are difficult, the divorced parent with two hours a week, the specialist whose interests do not exist locally—these are not edge cases; they are the core of what online matching unlocks. A larger pool with cheap screening produces more first dates and more relationships than happenstance.
[C1.3] The case against apps mistakes friction for harm. Swiping is fast because it is low-cost filtering; a bad match costs a minute, not an evening. Choice overload is real at the margins, but the remedy is better filtering and intentional use, not proving the model worse for most. And the retention story is a motive, not an outcome.
[C1.4] Users exit when they pair; apps grow through churn and word of mouth. A business can want engagement and still deliver introductions—gyms want members but still improve fitness.
[C1.5] Most users are not seeking a spouse tonight. They are seeking a plausible introduction. The dominant model gives them more of those, across more geography and identities, than the social lottery it replaced. The other side must show that the same users would have formed better relationships without it.
[C1.6] Frustration, ghosting, and paid tiers are costs; they are not evidence that the net effect on formation is negative. On the only comparison that matters, apps make more relationships possible for more people.
