[P1.1] Residents of an overtouristed city are not just another customer segment. They absorb the noise, crowded transit, waste, and housing pressure that peak-season tourism creates, and they often sustain these sites through local taxes and daily civic life. When a fragile museum or historic quarter is at capacity, the fair question is not why a resident should jump the queue; it is why a visitor who contributes to the congestion should have an equal claim on a scarce local asset at its most fragile moment.
[P1.2] Resident priority is a rationing tool, not a wall.
[P1.3] Peak season is precisely when demand outruns the fabric and function of a site. Cities already manage that scarcity through timed entry, group caps, and peak pricing. Reserving a share of slots for residents, or charging them less, simply directs that scarcity toward the people with the strongest stake and the smallest ability to choose another city. A tourist can visit off-peak or choose another destination; a resident lives with the consequences year-round.
[P1.4] The policy leaves tourists abundant access—just not unlimited access at the exact peak when the heritage asset is most vulnerable.
[P1.5] There is also a civic survival argument. If locals feel locked out of their own museums and old quarters while tour groups monopolize the best hours, support for tourism hardens and cities reach for blunter restrictions. Resident access builds the political constituency that preservation needs. A parent in a museum city should not have to compete with commercial tours for a weekend slot at the city’s own institution.
[P1.6] The proposition does not ask cities to choose residents over visitors forever. It asks them to choose residents first when the site cannot absorb everyone. That is how peak-season access becomes legitimate rather than extractive.

[C1.1] Peak-season scarcity justifies limiting numbers; it does not justify sorting people by postcode. This proposition converts a conservation problem into a residency preference, imposing new unfairness without necessarily reducing pressure on fragile sites.
[C1.2] Start with conservation. If a gallery or historic building can safely admit 1,000 people, reallocating slots from visitors to residents still admits 1,000. Lower resident prices may even stimulate additional demand and queuing. Timed entry, firm capacity limits, and restrictions on large commercial groups address physical stress directly; checking local identity documents does not.
[C1.3] The parent PRO invokes can be protected by limiting tour-group block bookings without declaring every tourist’s claim inferior.
[C1.4] PRO also reverses the flexibility comparison. Residents have year-round opportunities to visit, including quieter months, mornings, and repeat trips. A visitor on a single journey often has fixed leave, transport, and accommodation. During peak season, the proposal preferentially serves the group with the most future chances while displacing people who may have only one.
[C1.5] Tourists can theoretically choose another destination, but residents can much more realistically choose another date.
[C1.6] Residency is also a crude measure of contribution and attachment. The motion does not require that a site be locally funded. It can privilege a wealthy newcomer over a regional taxpayer, former resident, student without the right paperwork, or descendant whose family history is inside the museum. Visitors already contribute through spending and, in many destinations, accommodation or tourism charges.
[C1.7] Local taxes may justify funding decisions; they do not establish that every resident has a stronger cultural claim at every site.
[C1.8] Finally, preferential booking and discounted admission can reduce conservation revenue precisely when demand is greatest, while creating verification costs and disputes over eligibility. And the promised civic peace is largely symbolic: a cheaper museum ticket does not quiet nighttime noise, unblock a bus, or restrain rents.
[C1.9] Cities should cap damage and make tourism finance mitigation, not distribute heritage access as compensation for leaving those harms intact. The proposition sacrifices equal access and potential revenue while the crowds outside remain.
