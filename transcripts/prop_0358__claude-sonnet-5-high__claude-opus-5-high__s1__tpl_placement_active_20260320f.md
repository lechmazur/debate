[P1.1] Traffic deaths are a physics problem before they are a punishment problem. A pedestrian struck at 20 mph has roughly a 90% chance of survival; at 40 mph that flips to roughly a 90% chance of death. No penalty imposed after a crash changes that curve. Deterrence only works if it changes behavior before the collision, and the evidence from decades of criminal justice research is consistent: certainty of consequence matters far more than severity, and for split-second driving decisions, neither matters much compared to physical constraints.
[P1.2] A driver doesn't do a legal risk calculation while checking a phone or reacting to a kid who darts into the street. They only have whatever speed the road design and posted limit already gave them.
[P1.3] That's the core comparison this debate turns on. Lower default limits and street redesign — narrower lanes, raised crosswalks, protected bike lanes, roundabouts, chicanes — work through prevention and are self-enforcing every single time, for every driver, on every trip. Harsher penalties work through deterrence, and only for the subset of drivers who (a) get caught, (b) believe they'll get caught, and (c) are in a mental state to weigh consequences at all — which excludes the drunk, the panicked, the distracted, and the reckless teenager who assumes it won't happen to them.
[P1.4] A speed limit sign backed by a redesigned street removes the decision entirely by making high speed physically uncomfortable or impossible; a harsher penalty just raises the stakes of a bet most risky drivers weren't calculating in the first place.
[P1.5] There's also an equity and consistency problem baked into the penalty-first approach: enforcement of harsh post-crash penalties is inherently reactive and unevenly applied, often falling hardest on unlicensed, uninsured, or already-marginalized drivers, while doing nothing for the victim already lying in the street. Prevention protects everyone, uniformly, before the crash — that's the standard the goal of reducing traffic deaths actually demands.
