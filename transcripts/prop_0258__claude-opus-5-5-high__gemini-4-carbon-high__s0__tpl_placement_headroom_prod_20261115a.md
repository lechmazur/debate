[P1.1] "Ignorance of the law is no excuse." Every legal system tells ordinary people that. Yet most statutes are written so that ordinary people cannot read them. That is the core injustice this motion fixes: a state cannot fairly punish, tax, or evict people under rules it has made effectively unreadable to them.
[P1.2] First, legitimacy. Law binds citizens, not lawyers. A tenant facing eviction, a small-business owner filing taxes, a parent applying for disability benefits: each is expected to obey statutes they cannot parse without paying someone hundreds of dollars an hour. When understanding the law becomes a purchased service, equality before the law becomes equality for those who can afford translation.
[P1.3] Plain language is not a stylistic preference. It is the precondition for consent and fair notice.
[P1.4] Second, the precision being defended is largely an illusion. Dense, nested, cross-referenced statutes do not eliminate ambiguity; they multiply it. Every extra clause and qualifier is another seam for litigation. Courts spend years untangling page-long sentences.
[P1.5] Clear structure, with short sentences, defined terms and worked examples, often makes meaning sharper, not looser. Jurisdictions that have rewritten tax or consumer laws in plainer form have generally kept them enforceable.
[P1.6] Third, opacity has winners. Complex drafting rewards whoever can hire specialists to find loopholes: large corporations, sophisticated litigants, and lobbyists who bury favors in subsection (c)(iv). Plain drafting exposes those favors to voters, journalists, and legislators voting on bills they never fully understood.
[P1.7] So the weighing is simple. On our side, the cost is some marginal precision, which courts, regulations, and definitions sections can recover. On their side, the cost is millions of people governed by rules they cannot read, systematically disadvantaged against those who can pay. A law ordinary people cannot read is precise only for those who can pay to have it read.
