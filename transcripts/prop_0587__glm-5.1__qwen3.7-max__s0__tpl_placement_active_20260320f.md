[P1.1] The central question is not whether domestic climate policy matters—it plainly does—but whether domestic policy *alone* can drive down *global* emissions effectively over the next five years. It cannot. A carbon border adjustment is the indispensable missing mechanism.
[P1.2] Domestic subsidies and regulations suffer from a structural flaw: carbon leakage. When U.S. policy raises the cost of carbon-intensive production, production migrates to jurisdictions with weaker standards, and emissions follow. The U.S. may report domestic reductions while global emissions stay flat or rise. A border adjustment neutralizes leakage by pricing the carbon embedded in imports at the same level as domestic production faces, eliminating the arbitrage incentive that undermines domestic-only approaches.
[P1.3] More critically, a border adjustment exports the price signal. Trading partners face a direct economic choice: pay the adjustment on carbon-intensive exports to the United States, or adopt comparable climate standards and avoid the fee entirely. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism has already prompted trading partners like Turkey and Indonesia to accelerate their own climate frameworks precisely because the market incentive is immediate and tangible. One U.S. policy can catalyze regulatory action across dozens of economies simultaneously.
[P1.4] No domestic subsidy achieves that multiplier.
[P1.5] Over five years, this leverage matters enormously. Global emissions trajectories are locked in by infrastructure and investment decisions happening now in steel, cement, and chemicals—the sectors a border adjustment targets. Domestic subsidies for clean energy are necessary but slow; they build U.S. capacity while leaving the world's highest-emitting facilities untouched. A border adjustment makes the cost of inaction visible to every exporter accessing the U.S. market today.
[P1.6] The proposition asks which approach reduces *global* emissions more effectively. Domestic subsidies can lower U.S. emissions but cannot compel reductions abroad. A border adjustment makes global reductions a feature of the policy's design, not a hopeful externality.
