[P1.1] A return-to-office mandate is not a neutral management memo. It imposes commuting time, disrupts family and caregiving arrangements, and can decide whether a disabled employee, or one hired remotely, can keep a job. When a large employer exercises that power, “culture” is not an explanation; it is often a label placed over a decision already made. The burden should rest on the party imposing the cost.
[P1.2] This motion does not ban office work, require scientific proof of productivity, or give employees a veto over management. It asks for a proportionate justification: what does this role accomplish in person, why is presence necessary or substantially valuable, and why does the mandate fit the days and teams covered? A laboratory technician needs specialized equipment; a client-support team may need secure systems; an apprenticeship cohort may depend on supervised practice. Those are intelligible reasons.
[P1.3] They can be examined, applied consistently, and revisited when conditions change.
[P1.4] Role specificity also produces better management. A blanket five-day order may bring together employees whose collaborators are in other cities, while failing to coordinate the people who actually need shared time. Asking about functions forces employers to distinguish mentoring from attendance, collaboration from badge swipes, and genuine operational need from executives’ comfort with visible busyness.
[P1.5] Culture matters, but precisely because it matters, it deserves more than incantation. If the claimed mechanism is faster feedback, spontaneous problem-solving, or onboarding, identify the roles and workflows where that mechanism operates and design office attendance around it. Otherwise “culture” can justify virtually any mandate and can conceal unequal exceptions for favored employees while shifting heavy costs onto caregivers and others with less bargaining power.
[P1.6] Large employers are especially able to supply reasons: they already write job descriptions, assess workflows, and run compliance processes. The cost of explanation is small; the cost of an unsupported mandate falls on thousands of workers. CON must defend managerial power with no meaningful duty to connect coercion to business need. PRO offers the better rule: preserve genuine in-person work, but require reasons concrete enough to test.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes the value of office work can be sliced into role-by-role tasks and audited in advance. That is a mistaken view of how large organizations operate, and it would turn workplace policy into a compliance exercise.
[C1.2] Most benefits of shared presence are systemic, not role-specific. Trust, informal mentoring, faster escalation, and cross-team awareness emerge from patterns of interaction, not from a checklist attached to one job description. A junior analyst may complete reports remotely, yet gain judgment by overhearing senior colleagues handle a crisis. A product team may need spontaneous whiteboard sessions even though every individual task can be done on Zoom.
[C1.3] If the employer must prove a business reason for each role, it will either fabricate narrow reasons or abandon policies whose real value is collective.
[C1.4] That is the danger: PRO’s rule rewards what is easily documented, not what is true. Operations are fluid. Teams reorganize, projects shift, and workers move across functions. A mandate adopted to rebuild coordination after rapid expansion may make sense even if no single role’s duties require a particular office task.
[C1.5] Requiring granular justification freezes strategy at the moment of announcement and invites litigation over which explanation is “specific enough.” Large employers will respond with rigid categories, endless memos, and risk-averse HR screens.
[C1.6] Culture is not a magic word. It is shorthand for norms, communication habits, and shared accountability, things that affect productivity and retention. Employers may be wrong, and workers can vote with their feet. But the remedy is accountability through results, competition for talent, and existing anti-discrimination law, not a rule that forces management to dress broad organizational judgments in fake role-specific clothing.
[C1.7] The better path is discretion with consequences. Let employers set attendance policy, judge its success by performance and retention, and replace it when it fails. The proposition replaces that practical discipline with bureaucratic second-guessing.
