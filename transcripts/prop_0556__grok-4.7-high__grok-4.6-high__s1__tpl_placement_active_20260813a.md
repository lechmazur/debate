[P1.1] Precise location data is a continuous map of a private life: the home returned to at night, the clinic visited in secret, the house of worship, the protest, the person met after dark. When data brokers sell that map without real consent, they convert movement into a commodity the individual neither controls nor can realistically escape.
[P1.2] Ordinary consent has already failed. Location permissions sit inside sprawling terms almost no one reads; the resulting trails are then packaged, enriched, and resold far beyond the original app. A person who once allowed a weather service to know their city has no practical way to discover, much less refuse, a later sale of minute-by-minute coordinates to advertisers, insurers, investigators, or anyone with a credit card. Opacity is the business model.
[P1.3] A prohibition on sale without explicit, time-limited opt-in is therefore not a technicality. It is the narrow point at which the law can still restore agency.
[P1.4] The rule need not forbid collection for a service someone actually wants, nor block properly aggregated statistics that cannot single out a person. It should forbid the broker’s sale of precise individual location unless that person has affirmatively agreed, for a defined period, to that sale. Explicit means a clear yes, not a pre-checked box. Time-limited means the permission expires, so a moment of inattention does not become a permanent license.
[P1.5] Unrestricted sale creates harms the market will not price. Abusers and political opponents can buy routines. Employers and insurers can infer health, faith, or organizing from where someone goes. Even supposedly anonymized precise tracks are often re-identifiable, because home and work are fingerprints.
[P1.6] The people exposed are not the brokers’ customers, so reputation will not protect them. Only a clear legal prohibition puts the burden on the seller who profits from the exposure.
[P1.7] Legitimate uses survive through real opt-in or through data that cannot identify a person. What should not survive is the quiet trade in people’s movements as if privacy were optional. Refusing this line is not neutrality. It is a choice for the broker.

[C1.3] First-party platforms that already collect location inside their own products would keep mapping people. Independent advertisers, fraud shops, logistics firms, and researchers who buy from brokers would disappear.
[C1.4] That concentration is the policy. Smaller buyers cannot run perpetual re-consent campaigns. Super-apps can. The individual is still a trail; only the number of firms holding it shrinks, and the competitive pressure on those firms shrinks with it.

Law can forbid those uses, punish buyers who single people out, require aggregation when identification is unnecessary, and give a real opt-out and deletion right. A weather permission is not a license to sell a clinic visit to a stalker. This motion refuses that distinction and treats the data type itself as contraband.
[C1.6] Time limits make the ritual worse. Expired permission means interruption or shutdown. Users will click through or lose free tools funded by advertising. Neither is agency.
[C1.7] Ordinary uses need the traces PRO would lock up: traffic, disaster response, fraud detection, store siting. Aggregates begin as precise points.
