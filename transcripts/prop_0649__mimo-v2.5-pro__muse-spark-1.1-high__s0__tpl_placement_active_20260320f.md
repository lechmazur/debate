[P1.1] Right now, millions of rooftop solar owners generate more electricity than they use during peak sunlight hours. Under most regulatory regimes, that surplus either vanishes back into the grid at a token feed-in rate, or the homeowner installs expensive battery storage to capture it. Either way, value is destroyed. Peer-to-peer rooftop solar trading simply lets that producer sell directly to a neighbor—often in the same neighborhood—who needs power at that moment.
[P1.2] The case for allowing this is straightforward: it is more efficient, more equitable, and more aligned with climate goals than the status quo.
[P1.3] Consider the economics. A household with rooftop panels has no practical use for its midday surplus. Meanwhile, the neighbor across the street is buying electricity from the grid at retail rates that include transmission costs, distribution markup, and utility profit margins. P2P trading lets both parties transact at a price below retail but above the feed-in tariff, creating value for both.
[P1.4] No new infrastructure is needed—both are already connected to the same local distribution network. The transaction simply recognizes what is physically already happening: electrons generated on one roof flow to nearby loads with minimal transmission loss.
[P1.5] This matters beyond individual transactions. P2P trading creates a visible financial signal that rewards solar adoption. When homeowners can see a real revenue stream from surplus generation—not a token credit from their utility—the payback period for rooftop panels shrinks. That accelerates deployment.
[P1.6] Every additional rooftop solar installation displaces fossil-fuel generation, and because the energy is consumed locally, grid-level transmission losses—which can account for significant waste in centralized systems—are reduced.
[P1.7] The regulatory barrier is not safety or grid stability. Countries like Australia, the United States, and parts of Southeast Asia have already piloted P2P solar trading platforms, and grid operators have found ways to manage local load balancing. Allowing P2P trading introduces competition at the household level and forces utilities to adapt rather than extract rents from a captive customer base.

[C1.3] If those who can generate stop contributing, the fixed costs fall on those who cannot.

[C1.5] P2P turns shared infrastructure into a private toll road for the affluent.
[C1.6] The technical claim that no new infrastructure is needed is false at scale. Local low-voltage networks were built for one-way flow from substation to home. [C1.7] Managing that chaos requires smart meters, real-time settlement platforms, dispute resolution - costs ultimately socialized.
