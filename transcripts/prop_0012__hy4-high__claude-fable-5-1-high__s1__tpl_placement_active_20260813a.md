[P1.1] Human remains are not research materials that happen to have cultural owners; they are the bodies of persons whose families and nations were often made powerless at the moment of taking. A grave opened during conquest, a skull collected for racial classification, a skeleton shipped to a university as proof of supposed hierarchy—each stayed because an institution could define the dead as data and the living as petitioners. That definition is the injury. Prompt return is not a courtesy after research concludes; it ends an ongoing possession to which rights-holders never consented.
[P1.2] Scientific value is real, but it is not a trump. Museums and universities do not get to convert an unconsented taking into a public-interest license because a future project might extract migration, diet, disease, or ancestry information. If a living patient's tissue were taken without consent, no one would say the hospital may keep it until every assay has been run. Colonized dead deserve at least that baseline, especially when acquisition was tied to coercive rule, broken treaties, burial disturbance, or racial science.
[P1.3] Delay is not neutral. “Keep them a little longer” means another year in which elders negotiate over relatives' bones, descendants prove kinship to the bureaucracy holding the evidence, ceremonies remain blocked, and the same institution sets the timetable and decides when the community has been reasonable. Promptness matters because the party that benefited from the taking cannot hold an indefinite veto justified by its own research appetite.
[P1.4] Science still has a route: communities can authorize study, set protocols, invite collaboration, or refuse destructive testing. But authority must run from kinship and sovereignty to the researcher, not from the catalogue outward. The question is not whether knowledge may be gained; it is whether a museum may hold the dead as collateral for knowledge. It may not.
[P1.5] Repair the dispossession first, promptly, and let research proceed only on terms that do not repeat it.

[C1.1] The motion isn't asking whether the dead should go home. It's asking whether institutions must send them home promptly even when study would be valuable—that is, whether scientific value should be categorically ruled out of the decision. That rule is a mistake, and it is a mistake that harms the very communities it claims to honor.
[C1.2] Start with the hardest fact: most contested remains are not labeled. Museums hold boxes marked "skull, Plains, 1890s" or "skeleton, Pacific, provenance unknown." Return requires an answer to the question: return to whom? Promptness and accuracy pull in opposite directions.
[C1.3] A rushed return sends a Pawnee ancestor to a Sioux burial ground, or hands a 9,000-year-old individual to whichever nation filed first, and once reburied the error is permanent. The study PRO wants subordinated is frequently the only thing that can establish affiliation at all. Ancient-DNA and isotope work on the Kennewick-type cases is what converted an unresolved dispute into a return the tribes could claim with evidence behind them. [P1.4] says authority should "run from kinship"—but kinship is precisely what is often unknown until someone looks.
[C1.4] Second, the hospital analogy in [P1.2] breaks. A living patient's tissue has a living owner who can speak. Remains that are centuries old have no individual consenter; what exists are competing, overlapping claimants, and no neutral process can honor "sovereignty" without first deciding whose. "Promptly" means the institution decides that question fast, which is the same unilateral power PRO condemns in [P1.3], just pointed in a different direction.
[C1.5] Third, the knowledge is not abstract. Skeletal evidence has supported land continuity claims, documented disease and famine imposed by colonial rule, and refuted the racial pseudoscience that justified collection. Reburying that record on a deadline can destroy a community's own evidence against the empire.
[C1.6] The right rule is deliberate, documented repatriation where scientific value is weighed openly—not a clock that treats careful study as the enemy of justice.
