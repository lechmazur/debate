[P1.1] Free speech protects a person's right to say 'I disagree.' It does not protect a billionaire's right to buy a hundred thousand times more amplification than the nurse next door and call that 'more speech.' The clash is whether campaign cash is ordinary expression or unequal economic power purchasing political influence—it is the latter, auctioning off the republic.
[P1.2] Begin with corporations. A firm is not a citizen with a conscience, a vote, or views to defend. Its treasury pools other people's money from workers, investors, and customers who often disagree; managers spend it to buy access, soften rules, and win contracts. That is the classic vehicle of corruption and its appearance: policy tailored for the payor.
[P1.3] Banning corporate treasury donations silences no natural person. Every shareholder, executive, and employee keeps full speech rights under lower individual caps. The ban removes only artificial aggregation—concentrated capital speaking where no voter actually believes the thought.
[P1.4] The same principle demands much tighter caps on large individual donations. A hedge-fund principal may sincerely give $5 million, while a schoolteacher gives $25; the smaller gift is equal citizenship, but the larger buys retreats, briefings, staff vetoes, and final authority over a candidate's time. Wealth earned is real, yet democracy cannot permit money to become proportional command over public decisions. Low caps and a small-donor base widen who can run and pull politicians from millionaire roundtables back to town halls.
[P1.5] This is content-neutral: it limits transfer size, not ideas.
[P1.6] This is no utopia. Several democracies already bar corporate treasury gifts to parties and cap individual contributions modestly; elections stay competitive. The question is thus which freedom counts: the few's freedom to convert cash into crushing speech, or every citizen's freedom to meet politics as an equal. Uncapped corporate and plutocratic donations sacrifice the latter, and with it, legitimacy.
[P1.7] Vote PRO.

[C1.1] Spending on campaigns is not a rival to the ballot. It is how a message reaches people who do not already know the speaker. The proposition calls that cost corruption and answers with rationed political communication. That does not create equal citizens.
[C1.2] It shields whoever already owns an audience: incumbents, party machines, celebrities, and media outlets that dominate the argument without donating a cent.
[C1.3] A corporation is an association, not a wallet with no speakers inside it. If treasury money is illegitimate because workers, investors, or customers may disagree, the same logic silences unions, churches, nonprofits, and newspapers. Used only against firms, it is a viewpoint screen. A ban plus low individual caps does not leave speech intact.
[C1.4] It stops people from pooling resources at the scale of the government they hope to contest. Executives may still whisper. They may not fund a challenge voters can hear.
[C1.5] Lower caps do not abolish influence. They move it toward self-funders, famous endorsers, and officials already known. A challenger without name recognition needs concentrated early money simply to become visible. Twenty-five dollars is citizenship.
[C1.6] It is not a campaign. Bribery can be punished without treating every large gift as a purchased government. Donors mostly back candidates who already share their aims, and voters still cast the only ballots that decide.
[C1.7] Countries that bar corporate gifts often swap private money for state subsidies and tighter party control. The channels change; outsider entry gets harder. A cap can be content-neutral and still decide who can afford to be heard.
[C1.8] Equal votes do not require equal volume. They require freedom to organize criticism at scale. Disclosure and a ban on bribery already target the purchase of office. Rationing gifts targets reach.
[C1.9] Ban corporate donations and crush large gifts, and you do not get town halls. You get official scarcity, owned by those who speak for free.
