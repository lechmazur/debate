[P1.1] Landlords should not hold a veto over a tenant’s ability to electrify transportation. The proposition does not require every building to install chargers overnight at the owner’s expense. It requires a stronger legal entitlement to ask, and a correspondingly weaker landlord power to stall, ignore, or refuse without a legitimate engineering or safety reason. Under that rule, the renter can request a circuit, outlet, or managed charging solution; the landlord must respond within a reasonable time, offer objective reasons if denying, and may impose reasonable conditions—who pays, load management, insurance, restoration, compliance with codes.
[P1.2] That is the balance the debate should be about.
[P1.3] The status quo gives too much power to the wrong party. A landlord can block EV access simply by doing nothing: no reply, vague concerns, waiting for capital plans, saying the panel may be inadequate someday. For a tenant, that delay is decisive. Someone who needs an EV for a long commute, medical appointments, or family care cannot rely on sporadic public charging if they have no predictable place to plug in overnight.
[P1.4] Renters in apartments are often exactly the people policy wants to bring into electrification—households without driveways, in denser housing, or unable to buy a single-family home. If they are effectively barred from charging where they live, EV adoption becomes a privilege of homeownership.
[P1.5] That exclusion is also bad public policy. Transportation electrification depends on charging being as ordinary as electricity, water, or broadband. Tenants already have recognized interests in basic utility access; a charging request is the modern extension of that principle. Landlords keep substantial rights: they do not subsidize the charger, can require licensed installation, can manage total building load, and can protect common areas.
[P1.6] What they should lose is the right to delay or block access for convenience, speculation, or indifference. Stronger request rights make the default permission-with-conditions instead of denial-by-inertia.
