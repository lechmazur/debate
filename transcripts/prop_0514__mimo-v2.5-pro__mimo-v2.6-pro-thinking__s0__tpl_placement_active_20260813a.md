[P1.1] When public money builds digital infrastructure for schools and hospitals, the architecture itself must serve the public good. That means favoring open standards and switching portability over closed, single-vendor stacks. This isn’t about specific brands; it’s about ensuring our critical institutions are not perpetually held hostage by one company’s roadmap, pricing, or failures.
[P1.2] Vendor lock-in is a direct threat to fiscal responsibility and operational sovereignty. Once a district or clinic is deeply embedded in a proprietary ecosystem, the cost of switching becomes prohibitively high—not just financially, but in retraining, data migration, and lost continuity. This shifts power from the public institution to the vendor, stifling competition and allowing quality and pricing to drift unchecked. An open, portable approach reverses this dynamic, creating a competitive market where vendors must continually earn their place.
[P1.3] More fundamentally, schools and health systems are not isolated entities. Data must flow—between a student’s classroom and counseling office, between a patient’s primary care provider and specialist. Proprietary silos create dangerous friction in these vital exchanges. Open standards are the proven foundation for true interoperability, enabling innovation and allowing institutions to adopt best-of-breed components as needs evolve.
[P1.4] A school shouldn’t be forced to discard its entire learning management system because one function is poor; a hospital shouldn’t lose its patient data because it changes EHR vendors.
[P1.5] The proposition ultimately asks: who should control the technology that educates our children and cares for our sick? The answer must be the public institutions themselves, empowered with choices, not locked into a single supplier’s ecosystem. Open standards and portability are the tools of that control, ensuring technology serves its users, not the other way around.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses a good default with a good mandate. Public procurement should buy outcomes—safe care, functioning classrooms, accountable vendors—not architectural ideology.
[C1.2] Consider who actually operates this software. A rural clinic or a school district often has one or two IT staff, if that. Telling them to assemble "best-of-breed" components around open standards hands them the integration burden: identity, permissions, roster sync, audit logs, upgrade sequencing. When the lab result doesn't reach the pharmacist, or the third-party gradebook desyncs from the student information system after a summer update, "who do you call?" becomes a real question with no answer.
[C1.3] Fragmented accountability is not neutrality; it's diffusion of responsibility. A single integrated stack gives one vendor end-to-end liability and one throat to choke. In health IT especially, that matters: a handoff failure between stitched-together systems is a patient safety event, and safety-critical fields prize validated, certified, coherent suites over theoretical competitive dynamism.
[C1.4] Second, the switching portability mandate buys optionality at a price paid today, by institutions least able to absorb it. Continuous migration readiness—canonical data models, adapters, retraining, dual-running—costs money and attention that schools and hospitals would otherwise spend on instruction and care. And the premise is empirically shaky: when exit is genuinely cheap, contracts, data escrow, and export rights achieve most of the same discipline without dictating architecture. Note that the PRO case cites no instance where mandated open-standards-first procurement actually produced cheaper switching; the lock-in story is asserted, not demonstrated.
[C1.5] Third, interoperability is being oversold. Real health and education exchange is semantic, not syntactic—coded meaning, workflows, consent regimes. An open API standard guarantees that data can move; it does not guarantee it means the same thing on arrival. Integrated vendors internalize those semantics end to end.
[C1.6] Open standards as a contractual floor for data export are reasonable; open standards and portability as the *preferred* procurement posture reorganize public IT around a hypothetical future exit instead of present-day reliability.
