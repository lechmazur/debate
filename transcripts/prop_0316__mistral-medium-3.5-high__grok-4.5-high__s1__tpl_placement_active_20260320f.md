[P1.1] Imagine logging into a dating app daily to find your profile buried in search. You never know why—the app’s AI silently ranks you low, shaping who sees you and who you see, without a word. This isn’t hypothetical; it’s how many apps work. Users have a right to know when AI makes such consequential decisions about their social lives.
[P1.2] AI-driven ranking and matching aren’t neutral. They embed choices about attractiveness, compatibility, and value that can reinforce existing biases or arbitrary criteria. For instance, algorithms can create self-fulfilling prophecies by suppressing profiles with certain traits. Without transparency, users cannot consent to these systems, nor can they challenge unfair outcomes.
[P1.3] Informed consent requires knowledge of the process. We expect transparency in other high-stakes domains like credit scoring and hiring; dating, which shapes intimate relationships and emotional well-being, is no different. The stakes are high: missed connections and self-doubt can stem from being unfairly sidelined.
[P1.4] The opacity creates a power imbalance. Apps control the data and matching logic, while users remain in the dark about why they’re shown or hidden. This erodes trust and leaves users vulnerable to manipulation. The emotional toll is real: users may internalize rejection as personal failure when it’s actually the algorithm at work.
[P1.5] For example, apps might prioritize paying users or use criteria that disadvantage groups. Without disclosure, users cannot make informed choices about presentation or platform use.
[P1.6] Some argue disclosure could enable gaming. But that risk doesn’t justify secrecy. If an algorithm is so fragile that transparency breaks it, the problem is the algorithm, not the right to know. The right to know doesn’t require revealing details—just that AI is used and perhaps its broad criteria.
[P1.7] In a world where algorithms shape personal opportunities, transparency isn’t optional. Users deserve to know when AI ranks their profile or decides who they see. Without that knowledge, they’re not agents in their own love lives—they’re subjects of an opaque experiment.

[C1.1] Dating apps sell a matching product, not a public utility or courtroom. Users have no right to inspect how profiles are ranked any more than diners demand the chef’s proprietary recipe or shoppers insist on knowing the exact inventory algorithm that put one brand of pasta at eye level. The proposition invents a new entitlement that would make the service worse at the one thing users actually want: useful introductions.
[C1.2] AI ranking is simply scaled editorial judgment. Apps have always decided who appears first, who gets boosted, who is throttled. Labeling the process “AI” adds almost nothing actionable while handing opportunistic users a clear target. Once the broad criteria or even the mere fact of machine ranking is publicized, profiles get reverse-engineered into signals: timed log-ins, optimized photos, manufactured engagement.
[C1.3] The pool fills with gaming; genuine matches get buried under optimized noise. That is not empowerment. It is product sabotage dressed up as transparency.
[C1.4] Consent is already present. Users open an account, accept terms, choose free or paid tiers, and exit when results disappoint. Markets already discipline opacity: apps that deliver poor matches lose people; apps that earn reputation for fairness gain them. A legal “right to know” freezes that process into compliance theater, invites endless fights over how much detail satisfies the right, and raises barriers for smaller competitors.
[C1.5] Dating opacity is not uniquely sinister. Bars favor the loud, parties favor the connected, blind dates have always involved hidden filters. Treating algorithm-mediated matching as high-stakes civic process—like credit or hiring—collapses a crucial distinction. Those domains gate material survival under public law.
[C1.6] Romantic platforms sell voluntary, emotionally charged entertainment. Mandating disclosure confuses the two and would degrade matching quality without delivering better partners.
[C1.7] Users care about outcomes, not process lectures. A right to know that AI ranks or filters does not fix loneliness or bias; it mainly equips the most strategic actors to game the system while everyone else still swipes in the dark.
