[P1.2] Here's the mechanism CON has to defend: an artist or venue sets a price meant to balance access and revenue. Then bots—automated scripts buying hundreds of tickets in seconds, faster than any human fan could click—strip the inventory before real buyers ever see it, and resell at two, five, sometimes ten times face value. That markup isn't payment for a service. Nothing was added.

[P1.4] Worse, the platforms themselves often profit on both ends. When the same company operates the primary sale and takes a cut of secondary "resale" listings, it has a direct incentive to let scarcity and scalping run wild—every markup is also platform revenue. [P1.5] That's precisely why a regulatory requirement, not market trust, is necessary.
[P1.6] Face-value resale channels aren't hypothetical—artists have already run successful verified-fan resale systems where tickets that fans can't use go back into circulation at the original price, and shows still sell out, fans still get tickets, and no one wonders whether the "market" priced access fairly. That existence proof matters: this isn't imposing an artificial price on a functioning market. It's restoring the price the seller already chose, before bots and bad actors overrode it.
[P1.7] The core question isn't "should prices float freely." It's whether unearned rent extracted through bot fraud and platform self-dealing deserves protection just because it happens through a resale button. It doesn't. A cap or face-value channel returns tickets to the people the price was set for.

[C1.1] Scarcity is not scalping. When far more fans want seats than seats exist, most fans will miss out under any rule. A markup cap creates no new seats. It only decides whether disappointment is allocated by willingness to pay — visibly — or by luck, speed, connections, and patience — invisibly.
[C1.2] The mechanism PRO attacks actually starts with deliberate underpricing. Artists often set face value below market to reward loyalty and avoid outrage over expensive seats. That gap is a flashing prize for whoever grabs tickets first. Bots are a real problem, but banning resale profit does not close the gap; it just changes who harvests it.
[C1.3] Instead of a listed premium anyone can see and choose to pay, you get presale codes sold in chats, professional queuers, insider holds, and parking-lot cash deals. The rent is still collected, only less transparently and less fairly.
[C1.4] Imposing face-value channels for most tickets drives honest trade underground. A season-ticket holder hit with illness, a parent stuck with a schedule change, cannot lawfully adjust price for a premium matchup on a regulated platform, so she lists elsewhere — in group texts and offshore sites with no verification and no refund when the code fails at the door. The major platform, for all its fees, at least guarantees the ticket is real. Push volume off that system and fraud rises while the cap is evaded anyway.
[C1.5] Leave that choice to creators and owners. An artist who wants face-value-only resale can already offer it; one who wants auction pricing to pay crew or fund charity should be free to do so. A fan who owns a ticket should be free to resell it, and a buyer who treasures one night should be free to pay for a guaranteed transfer rather than lose a lottery. This mandate does not end tradeoffs; it hides them while delivering less safety and no more seats.
