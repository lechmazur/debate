When a syllabus covers founding triumphs but skips slavery, honors wartime courage but omits the burned villages, omission is itself a decision — propaganda by subtraction. [P1.3] Three reasons this mandate should pass.
[P1.4] Democratic accountability. Students become voters who fund armies, police, and prisons, and who will be asked to take the state at its word. Victimhood-only narratives are not harmless; they are revanchism in waiting.

Canada's truth-and-reconciliation work on residential schools, grim as it was, produced a shared national account sturdier than the silence before it. Trust built on concealment collapses when students discover the concealment — and they always do.

[C1.1] The issue is not whether slavery, dispossession, or abusive wars happened, or whether students may study them. It is whether government should require schools to teach children their country primarily through its atrocities. That converts history from inquiry into a state-prescribed verdict.
[C1.2] A mandate needs an author, and that author will be politicians. Who decides which events count as major, how much time they get, and what moral conclusion a twelve-year-old must draw? Each election becomes a chance to rewrite the national guilt list, condemning the other coalition’s heroes and softening its own record. Students learn not that truth is discovered, but that truth is enacted by whoever holds power.
[C1.3] That is not the opposite of propaganda by subtraction; it is propaganda by compulsion.
[C1.4] It misfires in the classroom. Public-school pupils are a captive audience, at the age when identity and belonging form. A required atrocity-centered account sorts them, however carefully teachers try, into heirs of perpetrators and heirs of victims. Some absorb shame for acts they did not commit; others meet the state first as an abuser.
[C1.5] Without a prior shared foundation in institutions, rights, and reforms citizens won, indictment produces alienation or grievance, not accountability. Democracy needs attachment sturdy enough to permit criticism; beginning with national guilt corrodes that attachment.
[C1.6] Finite school time makes the distortion worse. A checklist of required national sins crowds out world history, civics, and the reform movements that show how abuses were challenged. Prescribing conclusions in law also invites backlash, litigation, and teacher self-censorship: every lesson becomes a culture-war exhibit, and nuanced teaching disappears first.
[C1.7] Germany’s reckoning followed total defeat, occupation, and unique civic reconstruction; it does not prove a mandate works everywhere. Rejecting suppression in Turkey or Russia does not entail adopting compulsion here. Forbid whitewashing, protect teachers and historians, and let hard history be taught well. Do not let the state that compels attendance also compel self-condemnation as official truth.

[C2.4] That distinction answers time too. Integrating dispossession into westward expansion is good teaching — and needs no atrocity mandate. Requiring separate, assessable study of each listed abuse does take hours, rubrics, and tests, and those come from somewhere.
[C2.5] Finally, “forbid whitewashing” is enforceable without compelling a canon: transparent standards, public textbook review, protections against banning topics, and rules against falsifying or politically excluding major events. [C2.6] The choice is not truth versus silence. It is contestable teaching by educators versus a state-enforced list of national sins.
