[P1.1] A college degree is now a default ticket for jobs whose daily work never depended on it: customer support, sales development, office administration, IT help desk, project coordination. The proposition is narrower than often assumed: stop requiring degrees only where completion is not genuinely necessary for performance. That distinction is the issue.
[P1.2] The harm is categorical exclusion. A degree requirement does not select the strongest performer; it selects whoever cleared an earlier, often unrelated hurdle. An applicant with relevant experience, a certification, or a portfolio can be filtered out by software before anyone assesses whether they can do the job. That is self-inflicted talent loss and an arbitrary wall.
[P1.3] The wall deepens inequality. Degree completion tracks parental income, wealth, and race. Using it as a hard screen turns background advantage into employment advantage even where the credential adds nothing to performance. A fair screen would examine job-relevant ability, not the credential.
[P1.4] Better screening already exists. Work samples, structured interviews, short skills tests, references, and targeted training evaluate what the job actually requires. They are often cheaper than the credential premium and avoid discarding capable people. If a particular degree genuinely signals needed analytic or writing skills for a role, an employer may still consider it as one positive factor; it just should not be an automatic bar.
[P1.5] The usual defense is that a degree signals persistence or intelligence. But a group average cannot justify excluding individuals. Many non-degreed candidates show persistence through work history or self-directed learning; many degree holders lack specific job skills. Where the degree is not necessary, direct assessment of ability is fairer and more efficient.
[P1.6] The requirement should go.
