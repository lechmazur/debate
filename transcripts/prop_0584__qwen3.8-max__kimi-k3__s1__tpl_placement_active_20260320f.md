[P1.1] The central question is not whether unemployment matters; it is what central banks can credibly control over the next three years, and what failure would cost. If they let inflation drift above target to avoid a modest rise in joblessness, they risk losing the one asset that makes their policy effective: anchored expectations. Once households and firms begin setting wages, prices, and contracts around persistently faster inflation, the problem compounds.
[P1.2] Prioritizing the target is the more humane and more durable choice. High inflation is a tax on cash balances, fixed incomes, and workers whose wages lag. It distorts saving, makes business investment harder, and forces lenders to demand higher risk premiums. A modest, temporary increase in unemployment is painful, but it is visible, measurable, and can be mitigated by fiscal policy, training, and safety nets.
[P1.3] Eroded money value, once embedded, is much harder to reverse.
[P1.4] The alternative invites a larger bill later. Monetary policy works with lags. If central banks ease prematurely because the labor market softens slightly, they may validate inflationary expectations, then need to tighten harder. That is the lesson of stop-go policy: delaying price stability tends to produce deeper recessions and higher unemployment in the end.
[P1.5] Accepting a small near-term labor cost now is insurance against a much larger labor cost later.
[P1.6] This is especially true over a three-year horizon. Firms making capital decisions, unions negotiating contracts, and households planning consumption need a stable nominal anchor. Central banks also have a comparative advantage in controlling demand, not in solving supply bottlenecks or raising labor-force participation. Asking them to chase full employment at the expense of inflation asks them to do what they do poorly and abandon what only they can do.
[P1.7] The proposition is therefore not a call for indifference to jobs; it is a commitment to the conditions under which jobs are sustainable.

[C1.2] First, the tradeoff. The last few years were a live test: inflation fell sharply from its 2022 peaks across the US and Europe while unemployment stayed near historic lows. Much of that inflation was supply-driven—energy, shipping, shortages—and higher unemployment produces neither gas nor semiconductors. The next three years rhyme: energy shocks, trade fragmentation, housing costs.
[C1.3] Tightening into supply shocks crushes investment and hiring without touching the cause. "Target first, jobs second" prescribes the wrong medicine exactly when it hurts most.
[C1.4] Second, the asymmetry. A "modest" rise in unemployment is modest only in aggregate; in the US, each point means well over a million job losses, concentrated among the young, the low-paid, the last hired. Joblessness scars: the displaced earn less for years, their health worsens, their children's prospects dim. Three percent inflation instead of two is a diffuse, reversible irritation; unemployment is concentrated, compounding, and permanent for those it strikes.
[C1.5] Trading the second to fine-tune the first is not prudence—it is mispriced insurance.
[C1.6] Third, hysteresis. Europe's disinflations of the 1980s and 1990s ratcheted unemployment upward for over a decade as skills decayed and workers detached from the labor force. Output sacrificed to deliberate slack is never recovered; it is a levy, not a loan.
[C1.7] Nor does the credibility argument in [P1.1] rescue the motion. Long-run inflation expectations barely moved even at the recent peak—the anchor is sturdier than 1970s nostalgia admits, and 2% is a convention, not a law of nature. What truly threatens central banks is being seen to inflict joblessness for a rounding error, inviting the backlash that genuinely unanchors policy.
[C1.8] The motion demands a rigid hierarchy; sound policy requires balance. Reject the hierarchy.
