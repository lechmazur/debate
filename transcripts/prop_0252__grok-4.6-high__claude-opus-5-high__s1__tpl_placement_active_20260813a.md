[P1.1] Unpaid internships outside genuinely educational placements are unpaid jobs. A newsroom assigning interns real reporting, a campaign office staffing constituent work with people who pay their own rent, a gallery running events on unpaid labor: that is productive work. The employer receives output. The intern receives no wage.
[P1.2] Calling it experience does not change the exchange.
[P1.3] Governments should restrict this heavily because the status quo sorts opportunity by wealth. Only those who can live without pay can take these roles for months at a time. Working-class graduates work paid shifts elsewhere while richer peers collect the contacts, references, and CV lines that later become jobs. The pipeline filters for privilege, not merit.
[P1.4] That is not a side effect. It is the design of a market that prices access in unpaid time.
[P1.5] The educational line is workable and necessary. A placement integrated into a degree, with defined learning goals, supervision, and academic credit is training. The primary purpose is the student's development. When an organization's day-to-day output depends on the intern, the intern is a worker.
[P1.6] Heavy restriction forces that distinction instead of letting employers write their own labels and call any junior task a learning opportunity.
[P1.7] Without restriction, entry-level work keeps migrating into unpaid internships. Employers have every incentive to reclassify junior roles. Applicants facing crowded fields accept terms they cannot refuse. Voluntary here means choosing between remaining invisible and self-funding someone else's payroll.
[P1.8] Small charities, campaigns, and creative industries get no moral exemption. If the labor is valuable enough to use, it is valuable enough to pay. Restriction does not ban genuine education. It bans converting jobs into gifts extracted from people who cannot afford to give them.
[P1.9] Pay the worker, or run a genuine educational placement. There is no third honest category.
