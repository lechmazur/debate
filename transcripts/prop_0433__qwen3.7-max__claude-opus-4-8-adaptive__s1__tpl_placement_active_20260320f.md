[P1.1] A lease is one of the largest financial contracts a person signs, yet the rental market suffers from massive information asymmetry. Landlords possess intimate knowledge of a building’s chronic flaws, while prospective tenants are forced to rely on a polished thirty-minute tour. The proposition targets three specific, severe issues: recurring short-term rental parties, severe noise, and chronic package theft. These are not isolated incidents; they are structural failures of quiet enjoyment and basic security that a landlord is uniquely positioned to track.
[P1.2] When landlords hide these realities, they trap tenants in twelve-month financial cages. A tenant might sign a lease expecting a quiet home, only to discover their building operates as an unregulated weekend hostel with constant stranger traffic and stolen deliveries. Breaking a lease over these issues usually requires expensive legal battles to prove a breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment. This is a notoriously high legal bar, leaving most tenants to simply absorb the misery and the financial cost.
[P1.3] Mandatory disclosure rightfully shifts the burden of truth to the party holding the historical data.
[P1.4] Furthermore, disclosure does not mean these buildings become unrentable; it simply allows the market to properly price the risk. If a landlord discloses chronic package theft, a tenant might accept the unit in exchange for a rent discount, or negotiate for a secure smart-locker system before signing. Informed consent transforms a predatory trap into a negotiated trade-off.
[P1.5] Opponents may argue that landlords cannot control tenant behavior, but this debate is about the duty to disclose known, chronic conditions, not liability for the noise. Without baseline transparency, the rental market structurally rewards deceptive landlords and punishes honest ones. We must mandate disclosure so tenants sign a contract for the reality they will live in, not the illusion they were sold.
