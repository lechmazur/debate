[P1.1] Legislatures left free to set fiscal policy repeatedly choose present benefits over future costs. Spending programs create concentrated constituencies; tax cuts are politically easy; the interest bill arrives later, often under a different government. That incentive structure produces a systematic deficit bias. Good intentions and electoral accountability have not solved it, because the people who authorize the debt are rarely the ones who fully pay for it, and debt service eventually crowds out the programs those same politicians claimed to defend.
[P1.2] A constitutional balanced-budget rule reverses the default. Balance becomes the legal baseline, not a bargaining chip waived by a simple majority whenever politics demand it. Constitutional status matters because ordinary statutes are amended by the same politicians the rule is meant to constrain. A higher bar for change makes the commitment credible to markets and to successor governments.
[P1.3] Automatic-stabilizer exceptions keep the rule from becoming an economic straitjacket. When recession raises unemployment claims and lowers tax receipts, those movements should be permitted without an immediate constitutional violation. The point is to constrain discretionary loosening and structural overspending, not to force pro-cyclical cuts that deepen downturns. Balance can be defined over the cycle, or net of clearly identified stabilizers, so countercyclical insurance survives while permanent deficits do not.
[P1.4] Discretion offers no such distinction. Temporary emergency spending hardens into the baseline, accounting devices hide structural gaps, and debt climbs until lenders or a crisis impose discipline far harsher than any constitutional rule. The real choice is not flexibility versus rigidity. It is between rules that separate stabilizers from structural deficits, and politics that reliably confuse the two until the bill comes due.
[P1.5] Countries should lock in that separation. Constitutional balanced-budget provisions with automatic-stabilizer exceptions discipline the political bias without sacrificing macroeconomic sense.

[C1.1] Constitutions should protect the rules of self-government, not pre-decide its results. Taxing, spending, and borrowing are not technical details; they are what elections are about. A balanced-budget amendment tells voters they may choose any future they want, as long as they never collectively decide to borrow to build it. That is not discipline.
[C1.2] It is disenfranchisement dressed as responsibility.
[C1.3] The automatic-stabilizer exception hides the rigidity rather than fixing it. In a real recession no one knows in real time what is cyclical and what is structural. Revenues collapse, forecasts are rewritten, and the clean line between permitted stabilizers and forbidden action dissolves into guesswork. Governments will face pro-cyclical cuts when a prolonged slump is labeled structural, or they will game the labels to evade the rule.
[C1.4] Worse, stabilizers alone never save an economy in a true emergency. Unemployment checks did not recapitalize banks in 2008 and did not pay for vaccines, furloughs, and emergency lending during the pandemic. Those required huge, fast, discretionary choices that a constitutional balance rule would have made illegal at the exact moment delay costs lives and livelihoods. Requiring supermajorities or constitutional litigation to act turns speed into procedure.
[C1.5] Debt is also not proof of failure. Borrowing to build rail, grids, schools, or to fight climate change shifts some cost to the future, but it also shifts the asset. Future generations are richer, not robbed, when we leave them infrastructure that pays for itself. A constitutional rule treats productive investment and political pork as the same sin, punishing precisely the long-horizon spending responsible governments should protect.
[C1.6] Finally, who enforces this? Either judges order tax hikes and cuts — the least democratic officials deciding the most democratic questions — or leaders ignore the constitution when it binds, teaching citizens the highest law is optional. Real discipline comes from open legislative debate, transparent accounts, and judgment by voters and lenders.

[C2.2] A mechanical trigger does not remove politics, it buries it. Someone must constitutionalize which unemployment rate counts, what trend revenue means, whose numbers rule. Data are revised for months, redefined for years. When the trigger flips off while people are still jobless, the sequester fires anyway.
[C2.3] That is pro-cyclical austerity by autopilot, not the promised "imperfect sunset beats a perfect excuse" [P2.3].
[C2.4] Emergencies are not rare footnotes on constitutional time. 2008 and 2020 hit within twelve years; war, panic, earthquake will hit again. A pre-capped, sunsetted facility cannot know in advance if rescue needs one percent of GDP or ten. Set the cap low and it fails when needed; set it high and you have legalized the discretion you claimed to ban.
[C2.5] Nor does balance spare investment. A rail line or grid cannot be squeezed from one year's taxes without punishing rates, which is why its cost is spread across decades of users. Under pay-as-you-go, the winner is annual consumption that fits inside yearly revenue, while the lumpy, transformative project dies. You privilege handouts today over assets tomorrow.
[C2.6] Automatic enforcement does not avoid the judge problem, it automates it. No robed official writes the budget; a formula slashes effective and wasteful programs alike because the text demands it. If breach brings only political cost, we are back to discretion with fancier language. Either the rule bites hardest in crisis and strangles self-government, or it is waived and teaches that constitutions are optional.
