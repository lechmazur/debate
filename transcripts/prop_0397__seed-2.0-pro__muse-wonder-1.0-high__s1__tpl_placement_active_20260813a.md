[P1.1] Right now, student debt is almost impossible to discharge in bankruptcy. You have to meet an undue hardship test so brutal most consumer bankruptcy lawyers will not even attempt these cases. People who become permanently disabled, single parents working full time below the poverty line, adults who dropped out after a family medical crisis, still carry this debt for decades. Wages get garnished, tax refunds seized, even social security checks docked when they retire.
[P1.2] This is not how bankruptcy is supposed to work. Bankruptcy exists to give people a fresh start when they cannot reasonably repay, not to punish them forever for a choice they made when they were barely adults.
[P1.3] Opponents will argue this would raise interest rates for future students. That is a real tradeoff, but it is a tradeoff we already accept for every other category of debt. You can discharge credit card gambling debt, failed business loans, even seven figure medical bills in bankruptcy. We do not tell someone who opened a small diner that collapsed they owe that money until the day they die.
[P1.4] We do not insist a cancer patient deserves permanent financial ruin. There is no moral or economic justification for treating an 18 year old who tried to get an education worse than every other kind of borrower.
[P1.5] This rule only applies to borrowers with proven long term inability to repay. These are people who will never pay off the principal anyway. Right now we gain nothing: we never collect the money, we just destroy their ability to buy a home, start a family, or participate normally in the economy. We are only inflicting pointless permanent punishment.
[P1.6] That is why this change is not just fair, it is common sense.

[C1.1] Student loans are not treated differently from credit cards or a failed diner loan to punish borrowers. The degree cannot be repossessed. Once the money is spent, the borrower keeps the education and any earnings it produces for life, while the lender holds nothing it can recover.
[C1.2] That difference is why the original bargain works. Credit-card companies price in default, cap limits, and refuse risky applicants; mortgage lenders take a house. Student loans — mostly taxpayer-backed — go to teenagers with no collateral, no credit history, and no underwriting of school or major, at fixed rates. Make repayment optional when earnings disappoint, and that bargain cannot survive on the same terms.
[C1.3] The loss does not vanish. Private lenders will raise prices, demand cosigners, tighten underwriting, or exit; federal losses fall on taxpayers. The borrowers hurt most will be low-income and first-generation students, suddenly judged by school prestige and expected major earnings. Schools, too, gain freedom to charge more when repayment becomes someone else's problem.
[C1.4] Access is broad today precisely because repayment is unusually reliable.
[C1.5] “Long-term inability” sounds like a safeguard, but bankruptcy is most attractive exactly when that judgment is least reliable. A healthy 24-year-old graduate has low income, few assets, and little to lose from filing, yet may earn comfortably at 34. A much easier test invites filing at the earnings trough and asks courts to forecast decades of health, family, and work. Different judges will forecast differently.
[C1.6] Borrowers who truly cannot pay need relief tied to actual earnings, not a courtroom gamble: income-linked payments that drop to zero in bad years, disability discharge, and forgiveness after a defined repayment period. Fix enrollment and generosity there. Do not destabilize the lending system itself through a bankruptcy escape hatch that rewards strategic timing, subsidizes weak programs, and charges the bill to the next student.

[C2.1] The meal, the chemotherapy, and the diner’s inventory are gone when those debts are discharged. The education is not. A discharged graduate keeps the credential, the skills, and every dollar of future earnings they produce, while the lender — usually the taxpayer — loses any share of that upside. That is why [P2.1] fails: credit-card issuers can underwrite, set low limits, price risk, and cut borrowers off.
[C2.2] Federal student lending is deliberately barred from that screening. You cannot keep no-underwriting access and add an easy exit without changing the price or availability of the next loan.
[C2.3] Their safeguard creates a dilemma. If “long-term inability” in [P2.5] really means ten or fifteen years of failed repayment, relief already exists on a more predictable track: payments fall with income, can be zero in bad years, and the balance is forgiven after a defined period — without asking a judge to certify a lifetime. If “much easier” instead lets a court make that call sooner, the 24-year-old problem returns. Nobody needs to wait fifteen years if the test invites a prediction that earnings will stay low.
[C2.4] And a borrower unable to repay at 30 may earn well at 42; discharge permanently cuts off recovery just before it becomes possible.
[C2.5] That answers the claim that we collect nothing anyway. We often collect something, over time and in proportion to earnings, and partial repayment across millions of borrowers is not “zero public gain” — it helps fund the next cohort. Income-linked plans are designed to prevent unaffordable payments for enrolled borrowers; the fix for non-enrollment is automatic enrollment, not courtroom discharge.
[C2.6] Fraud deserves its own remedy, and it has one in borrower-defense and disability discharge. Using a defrauded student to justify a generally easier standard helps the strategic and well-advised first, while the cost lands on future first-generation borrowers facing tighter scrutiny.

[C4.2] A2: Medical care is usually urgent, its amount is rarely chosen as an investment, and it does not itself confer a marketable credential designed to raise earnings for forty years. Education does. A discharged engineer or nurse keeps the precise income-producing asset the loan financed and every later dollar it generates, while the taxpayer who financed it recovers nothing from that upside. Medical providers also operate amid insurance, pricing, and the ability to decline non-emergency care; federal student loans deliberately lend to teenagers with no underwriting.
[C4.3] Those answers defeat the claim in [P4.3] that “Nobody pays anything extra” because the loss “has already occurred.” That is static accounting for yesterday’s borrower and ignores tomorrow’s behavior. Announce a much easier exit and schools can sell more expensive, weaker programs, borrowers have less reason to choose affordable options or persist in repayment, and the federal lender must expect lower recovery before it makes the next loan. The payer is then explicit: taxpayers generally, or the next low-income applicant if private credit tightens and public budgets shrink.
[C4.4] Nor is bankruptcy the clean closure advertised in [P4.1]. It still demands filing, proof, litigation risk, and years of credit damage. If a borrower has truly repaid on income for the long term, income-linked forgiveness already supplies closure on predictable terms. If bankruptcy supplies it much earlier, it does so by forecasting—and that forecast is exactly what invites strategic filing and unequal court outcomes.
