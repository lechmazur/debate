[P1.1] Sixteen-year-olds already live under the law as citizens, not children. They work and pay taxes, can face criminal responsibility, consent to medical care in many places, leave school for work, and will live longest with decisions on education, debt, war, and climate. To tax their wages and punish them as responsible, then tell them they are too irresponsible to vote, is incoherent. Democracy does not demand wise voters; it demands that those with a deep stake have a voice.
[P1.2] The line at eighteen is habit, not science. There is no sudden leap in judgment on an eighteenth birthday that separates a student from a recent graduate. Teenagers follow the same news, sit in civics classes adults have forgotten, and answer basic political questions roughly like eighteen to twenty-one-year-olds. If imperfect knowledge disqualified voters, millions of adults would lose the franchise.
[P1.3] We rightly reject tests for adults; we should reject the pretense that eighteen is magical.
[P1.4] Sixteen is also the better first vote; eighteen is the worst. At sixteen most young people are still at home and in school, easy to register, teach, and bring to the polls with support. At eighteen they are graduating, moving, starting work or university — the most chaotic, lowest-turnout moment in life. Offer the first ballot where community still scaffolds it, and voting becomes a habit rather than a missed rite.
[P1.5] Experience confirms it. Where the franchise was extended — nationally in Austria, in Scottish votes, in several German states — sixteen and seventeen-year-olds turned out at rates matching or beating voters just above them, without debasing debate. Fears of manipulation collapsed because every new voter is influenced; adults answer to bosses, partners, and media. Influence is not a reason for exclusion.
[P1.6] Participation is how dependence becomes responsibility.

[C1.1] The franchise should track full legal adulthood, not partial childhood. At eighteen you can sign contracts, be sued, marry without permission, join the army, serve on a jury, and live independently. At sixteen you generally cannot. That package matters because voting is not a reward for having opinions; it is authorization to impose binding laws, taxes and wars on others.
[C1.2] Reserve it for those who bear full duties and can live with the consequences alone.
[C1.3] The claim that eighteen is just habit [P1.2][P1.3] ignores what changes between sixteen and eighteen. Those two years are typically leaving home, working full time, paying rent, navigating the state without parents as intermediaries. In a cold classroom quiz teenagers look adult-like; in hot, socially-pressured decisions with peers and authority watching, they do not. That is why society withholds other adult powers at sixteen, and why susceptibility matters more for voting than for a civics test.
[C1.4] Working and paying tax [P1.1] does not create a franchise claim. Tourists pay tax. Fourteen-year-olds pay sales tax. We don't give them ballots.
[C1.5] And the "longest stake" logic proves too much - a ten-year-old has an even longer stake in climate, debt and war, yet no one thinks that alone entitles them to decide them.
[C1.6] Sixteen as the "better first vote" [P1.4] is an argument for guided voting, not independent voting. Registration drives in classrooms with teachers watching, parents driving you to the polls, friends voting in blocks - of course turnout ticks up. That is mobilization by authority, not habit formation. Adults are influenced by bosses and media, but they are not housed, fed and graded by their influencers.
[C1.7] With sixteen-year-olds, dependency makes the parent or teacher effectively get an extra vote.
[C1.8] Finally the motion is for all elections. Low-salience local trials [P1.5] do not justify expanding the electorate for presidents, tax referendums and criminal justice. We should not add the least experienced, most dependent citizens to the most consequential decisions as a socialization exercise.

Rights arrive as a staircase, not a single door: work and income tax at sixteen, driving, criminal responsibility, medical consent — while even at eighteen you often cannot drink, rent a car, or sign away large risk without constraint. Voting, marking a secret choice among offers, requires less solitary independence than signing binding debt or sitting on a jury. If a sixteen-year-old is adult enough to be punished and taxed like one, she is adult enough to judge those who tax and punish.
[P2.2] The tourist paying sales tax [C1.4] is a dodge. Sixteen-year-olds are not visitors buying souvenirs; they are citizens working shifts, paying income deductions, subject to labor law, school policy, and criminal courts. That combination is why the “ten-year-old” reductio [C1.5] fails: ten-year-olds do not work, leave school for employment, or face criminal liability. Sixteen marks where stake plus responsibility converge, not mere longevity.
[P2.3] Susceptibility proves too little, not too much. Yes, teenagers feel peer pressure in hot settings [C1.3], but so do adults at rallies, in churches, in workplaces, in families that share a kitchen table at eighteen and eighty. The ballot is secret precisely to break the chain from influencer to vote. Teachers cannot see it, parents cannot cast it.
[P2.4] To say dependence gives parents “an extra vote” [C1.7] would disqualify every voter who is housed, employed, or cared for by someone with opinions.
[P2.5] And calling school-supported voting mere “mobilization by authority” [C1.6] concedes the point. All good habits begin scaffolded — driving with an instructor, first job with training. Austria extended the vote nationally, Scotland decided independence and national futures, and teenagers voted seriously and returned. That is not a socialization exercise smuggled into consequential elections [C1.8]; it is democracy recruiting citizens where they can actually learn it.
