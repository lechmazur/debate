[P1.1] Conscience deserves protection, but in medicine it cannot mean the power to close the only door in front of a sick person. A conditional right — refuse for religious reasons only when the patient can still get timely, equivalent care without major burden — is the line that protects both faith and patients. Anything broader turns private belief into a veto.
[P1.2] Clinicians are not ordinary sellers. They hold a state-granted license, often a local monopoly, and meet people at moments of pain, fear, and urgency. That asymmetry creates a duty. A shopper turned away can cross the street; a woman bleeding in a rural emergency department, a teenager seeking time-sensitive contraception, or a patient whose only nearby pharmacy will not dispense prescribed medication may have nowhere equivalent to go.
[P1.3] Delay is not inconvenience there — it can be loss of efficacy, worsened illness, or humiliation that deters care altogether.
[P1.4] The condition also takes religion seriously rather than treating it as either absolute or irrelevant. When another clinician on site can provide the same treatment promptly, when a seamless referral does not add cost, travel, or stigma, the objecting clinician steps aside and the patient is unharmed. Conscience is honored. What is not honored is imposing the cost of that conscience on a stranger who did not choose the belief and cannot bear it safely.
[P1.5] Freedom of religion protects worship and personal conduct; it does not entitle professionals to shift serious burdens onto those they serve.
[P1.6] Without that guardrail, access becomes a postcode lottery determined by someone else's theology. The same lawful prescription is filled in one town and denied in the next. That undermines trust in medicine itself: patients cannot know whether they will receive standard care or a moral lecture until they are most vulnerable.
[P1.7] This proposition is therefore not anti-religious. It is anti-abandonment. If equivalent care is truly timely and burden-free, refusal may stand. If not, the clinician's professional duty must prevail.

[C1.1] The condition sounds like a guardrail; in practice it is a permission slip. A bedside refusal is not made legitimate because someone else can clean it up afterward. State-granted clinical authority carries a public duty: for lawful, clinically indicated care within the clinician’s role, patients should receive standard treatment without theological screening. If a professional cannot provide that service, the answer is not a conditional veto over a particular patient; it is not standing in that role for that service.
[C1.2] When PRO says “the patient is unharmed” [P1.4], that assumes administrative clairvoyance. Timeliness, equivalence, and burden are not stable facts before a refusal: on-call colleagues get sick, same-day slots vanish, records do not travel, and substitutes make patients repeat intimate histories. A teenager in a small town may technically have another appointment, but she may also need a ride, an explanation at home, missed wages, and a second disclosure to someone who knows her family. The rule licenses abandonment whenever a form can say “minor.”
[C1.3] The defect is “major.” PRO notices that delay can be harmful [P1.3], yet the test protects only burdens a decision-maker later deems serious. Equal access is not merely avoiding catastrophe. A lawful prescription should not become a scavenger hunt because a pharmacist objects; a clinically appropriate visit should not require extra travel, repeated explanation, embarrassment, or lost wages because the first clinician’s religion intervened. Those costs are not acceptable when imposed on rural, poor, young, disabled, or closeted patients; they are easier to dismiss.
[C1.4] A clean handoff does not erase the wrong: the clinician has still sorted ordinary care by doctrine. PRO’s “postcode lottery” [P1.6] is not abolished; it is laundered through staffing promises. Accommodations belong upstream, in rostering and scheduling, with no patient singled out. But the asserted right to refuse this patient this care for religious reasons should not exist.
[C1.5] The duty is not to make refusal harmless; it is not to refuse.

[P2.2] The opposing case also concedes the central accommodation while objecting to its name. Rostering so an objector is never assigned a contested service is still a clinician not providing that service for religious reasons; another clinician provides it instead. The only difference is whether we hide that arrangement in scheduling or allow a quiet, immediate step-aside when scheduling fails. If the upstream version is legitimate, conscience itself is not the poison.
[P2.3] The question is whether the patient is protected — which is exactly the condition imposed here.
[P2.4] “Major” is not a demand for clairvoyance or catastrophe. Medicine and law constantly apply contextual standards — urgency, reasonableness, undue hardship — and doubt here resolves against refusal. Extra travel, cost, delay, embarrassment, or a second forced disclosure can be major for a poor, young, disabled, or rural patient even if trivial on a form. That is a stricter, patient-centred reading, not permission for a scavenger hunt.
[P2.5] An absolute ban carries its own access cost. A rural clinician who can competently provide nearly everything but objects to one procedure would be told to leave that role entirely. That does not create a willing provider; it often removes the only provider, delays all care, and drives religious professionals out of fields patients need them in. Conditional refusal keeps them serving where conscience and care can coexist.
[P2.6] That is protection without expulsion.

If any of that is uncertain, refusal is barred and the assigned clinician must provide the care.
[P5.3] That condition therefore already answers the fear of abandonment. The patient is never left to test whether a ride, record, or appointment materializes. She receives the same care, at the same time, on the same terms.
[P5.4] What remains on the other side is a demand that the objector personally perform the act even when an identical substitution is literally in the next room. That demand does not give the patient faster treatment, greater privacy, or better medicine. It only insists that belief yield even where yielding protects no one.
[P5.5] A profession strong enough to guarantee care does not need to crush conscience when care is guaranteed. Protect the patient where burden is real; permit conscience where burden is absent.
