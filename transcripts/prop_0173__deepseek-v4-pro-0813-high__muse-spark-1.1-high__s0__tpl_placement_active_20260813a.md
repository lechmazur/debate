[P1.1] Short flights under 500 miles carry the environmental and time penalties that high-speed rail is best positioned to remove. Air passengers spend time getting to an outlying airport, arriving early, clearing security, taxiing, and then making a second transfer at the destination. Once that overhead is counted, a short flight often becomes a three-hour trip. A high-speed train running city-center to city-center can cover the same corridor in roughly two hours, with almost none of the airport friction.
[P1.2] The evidence from corridors like Paris–Lyon, Madrid–Barcelona, and Tokyo–Osaka points the same way: once fast rail enters, short-haul air demand collapses or airlines cut flights. Rail does not merely reduce aviation emissions at the margin; it removes entire flights.
[P1.3] The emissions advantage is structural. Steel wheel on steel rail uses far less energy per passenger-mile than lifting batteries, passengers, and a pressurized cabin through the air. High-speed rail draws on the grid, so every improvement in renewable electricity makes the whole corridor cleaner automatically. Electric short-haul aviation would also use grid power, but it needs more energy per passenger and remains constrained by battery weight, charging turnaround times, and battery replacement.
[P1.4] It cleans the fuel source while keeping the airport system, weather delays, and security overhead intact.
[P1.5] Cost-effectiveness also favors rail. Electric short-haul aircraft still require major battery, certification, and airport charging investment, with batteries degrading and needing replacement. A high-speed rail line is expensive up front, but it is proven technology serving millions of passengers over decades. Its cost is spread across one long-lived corridor asset, not across an uncertain aviation transition.
[P1.6] For busy major city pairs under 500 miles, the cheapest ton of carbon avoided is the one from replacing short-haul flights with fast, grid-powered trains.

[C1.2] Travel time claims ignore that reality. [C1.3] Electrifying a short-haul flight does not add flight time; it keeps the speed advantage of flight while using the runways and terminals we already own.
[C1.4] The cheapest ton of carbon avoided is not a new railroad. It is a cleaner motor in an existing airframe. A rail line serves one corridor.
[C1.5] To replace flight emissions nationally you would need dozens of such corridors at a cost in the trillions, each taking a generation to build, during which those aviation emissions continue.
[C1.6] Using grid power to move steel wheels is efficient only after you have built a second national grid of track to run them on.
