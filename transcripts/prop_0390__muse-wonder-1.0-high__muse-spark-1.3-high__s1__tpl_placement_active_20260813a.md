[P1.1] A ban that can be undone with a new email address is not a ban. It is a short inconvenience for the cheater and a permanent tax on everyone else who queued honestly, lost ranked points, or logged off after being targeted.
[P1.2] That is how enforcement mostly works today. A player caught using an aimbot or running a coordinated harassment campaign is removed from one title or network, then reappears in the next lobby under a fresh name. Studios detect the same cheats, moderate the same abuse, and protect the same players, but punishments stop at their own borders. Deterrence collapses because the worst cost is starting over, while victims and honest competitors pay again and again.
[P1.3] Serious bans should travel across major ecosystems — Steam, PlayStation, Xbox, and large publishers — through a shared registry for confirmed, high-severity offenses: commercial cheating, match manipulation, doxxing, credible threats, and sustained targeted harassment. Not a rude chat line. Not one disputed report. The threshold matters because the consequence is broader.
[P1.4] This is normal everywhere else trust matters. A doping ban follows an athlete across competitions. A revoked license is not honored by the next town because that office did not issue it. Games are private services, not entitlements; no one has a right to access another community's servers after abusing them.
[P1.5] The due-process objection identifies a real risk — false positives, opaque anti-cheat, mistaken identity — but it does not justify the status quo. The alternative to imperfect shared enforcement is not perfect justice, it is near-perfect impunity for repeat offenders. That risk can be reduced with hashed identifiers, evidence standards before listing, independent appeals, time limits, and removal for errors. Some residual risk remains, and it should be accepted: protecting thousands from a known cheater or harasser outweighs preserving that person's access to every other lobby.
[P1.6] When bans stay local, innocence is punished repeatedly. When they follow, consequences finally mean something.
