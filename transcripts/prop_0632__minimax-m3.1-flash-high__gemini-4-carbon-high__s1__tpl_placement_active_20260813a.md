[P1.1] The central question is not whether society should pay for obesity treatment, but whether insurers should pay for effective treatment when a clinician determines it is indicated. FDA authorization means a drug has met the required evidence and safety standards for its approved use. Coverage should then be a clinical decision, not an invitation to spend without limits. The requirement can preserve both access and fiscal discipline: cover the medication, but attach coverage to clear eligibility criteria, monitoring, and value-based payment terms.
[P1.2] Value-based pricing is the key mechanism. Insurers can negotiate a lower net price, a rebate, or a risk-sharing agreement tied to measurable outcomes such as clinically meaningful weight reduction, improved diabetes control, reduced blood pressure, or continued treatment response. If a manufacturer’s product does not deliver the promised results, the contract can return part of the payment. That is fundamentally different from simply accepting an unlimited list price.
[P1.3] It also gives manufacturers an incentive to produce effective, adherent, and durable treatments rather than merely increasing prescriptions.
[P1.4] The alternative is not cost control; it is cost shifting. Excluding authorized medications leaves patients to pay out of pocket, pushes costs toward employers, Medicaid, and public health systems, and worsens disparities between those with and without generous coverage. A covered medication with appropriate monitoring can generate the real-world evidence needed to judge whether it works.
[P1.5] Critics will raise high prices, side effects, nonadherence, and patients who do not respond. Those concerns support safeguards, not blanket exclusion. Prior authorization, confirmation of clinical indication, periodic reassessment, discontinuation when treatment is ineffective, and outcomes-based refunds can address each concern while preserving access. A patient who cannot obtain treatment cannot generate outcomes data or benefit from a contract based on outcomes.
[P1.6] The policy therefore aligns three objectives: clinically appropriate access, insurer accountability, and spending control. Coverage should be required, but payment should be conditional on demonstrated value.
