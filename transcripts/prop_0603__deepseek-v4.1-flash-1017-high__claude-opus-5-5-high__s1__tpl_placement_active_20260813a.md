[P1.1] The question is not whether deep-sea mining might someday be acceptable. It is whether the International Seabed Authority should issue commercial approvals before anyone can say what “acceptable” means in the deep ocean. When harm is potentially irreversible, knowledge is contractor-controlled, and the regulator lacks an independent yardstick, the burden of proof must fall on the applicant. A pause preserves that burden.
[P1.2] Deep-sea ecosystems are the wrong place to learn by doing. Nodule fields that took millions of years to form host slow-growing organisms; sediment plumes can travel far beyond a mine site, and recovery timelines are measured in decades or longer, if at all. Once habitat is stripped or a plume spreads, you cannot “adaptive manage” it back. Environmental baselines are what tell us whether a plume stayed local, whether biodiversity fell, and whether mitigation worked.
[P1.3] Without independent baselines, monitoring becomes theater: contractors model their own impacts, and regulators compare those models to what?
[P1.4] That is why independent verification is central. The ISA has a resource-development mandate, and contractors hold the survey data. If the baseline is chosen, paid for, and interpreted by the applicant, approval becomes self-certification. Robust baselines require common methods, deposited data, and verification before mining—not after harm.
[P1.5] The opposing side will call this anti-development. It is the opposite: it is refusing to run a planetary experiment without a control. A conditional pause costs delay. A bad approval costs ecosystems we cannot rebuild and creates legal and financial lock-in that makes correction harder.
[P1.6] If industry cannot establish robust baselines, that is not a reason to proceed; it is the strongest reason to wait.
