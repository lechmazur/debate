[P1.1] Domestic subsidies and regulations reshape only what happens inside U.S. borders. A carbon border adjustment reshapes what happens everywhere the United States trades — and that is where the real emissions problem lives. Over five years, this distinction is decisive.
[P1.2] Consider carbon leakage. When the U.S. tightens standards on steel, cement, or aluminum, producers don't vanish. They relocate to jurisdictions with looser rules, and global emissions stay flat or rise. The EU's experience with its own emissions trading system showed that free allocation of permits to trade-exposed industries was explicitly designed to prevent this leakage — and even then, studies suggested significant displacement was occurring.
[P1.3] A border adjustment closes that escape valve by imposing a charge on imports equivalent to the carbon cost domestically paid.
[P1.4] But the leverage effect is what makes the five-year horizon critical. The U.S. is the world's largest single importer. When it demands carbon-equivalent pricing at the border, exporting nations face a choice: adopt their own carbon pricing and keep the revenue, or pay the adjustment and lose competitive ground. China, already piloting a national emissions market, has strong incentive to accelerate and deepen that system rather than surrender billions to U.S. coffers.
[P1.5] One policy instrument, applied at the point of trade, catalyses decarbonisation across dozens of economies simultaneously.
[P1.6] Domestic subsidies — think clean energy tax credits, EV incentives — are powerful but inherently inward-facing. They accelerate U.S. clean technology deployment, yet they do nothing to stop a factory in Southeast Asia from burning cheap coal to make goods shipped to American shelves. Regulations face the same boundary problem. Without a border mechanism, every domestic gain can be partially offset by foreign emissions growth.
[P1.7] The proposition asks which approach reduces global emissions more. The answer is the one that extends U.S. climate ambition beyond U.S. territory — and does it through a mechanism already available under existing trade law, deployable far faster than rebuilding the domestic subsidy architecture from scratch.

[P2.1] The opponent's case rests on three moves: that America lacks the infrastructure for a border adjustment, that it touches too little trade, and that subsidies alone do the real global work. None of these hold up under scrutiny.
[P2.2] On infrastructure, the claim that the U.S. starts from a blank sheet ignores what already exists. Domestic regulations — vehicle emissions standards, power plant rules, methane fees — provide the carbon-cost baseline a border charge references. The EU built its CBAM precisely this way, anchoring to its ETS price without requiring every member state to have identical systems. Trade law authority under Section 301 and existing customs infrastructure mean the mechanism is not invented from scratch; it is assembled from available parts.
[P2.3] On scope, calling steel, cement, aluminum, and fertilizer a "narrow band" misrepresents their weight. These sectors account for roughly fifteen percent of global emissions, and the adjustment does not stop at the literal list. It sends a signal to every exporter that carbon cost will follow the product. That signal accelerates national policy adoption far beyond the covered goods — which is exactly the leverage the proposition describes.
[P2.4] On resource shuffling, the opponent raises a real risk but draws the wrong conclusion. If exporters merely reroute clean production to America and dirty production elsewhere, the global total is unchanged — but the exporting country still faces pressure to adopt genuine pricing to keep the revenue rather than surrender it. Shuffling is a leakage problem that a border adjustment directly addresses, whereas subsidies and regulations have no answer to it at all.
[P2.5] And on subsidies as the true global lever: cost-curve reduction is vital, but it is slow, indirect, and still leaves the leakage gap wide open. A factory in Southeast Asia installing cheap solar panels does nothing about the coal-fired smelter next door making steel for export. Subsidies change technology; a border adjustment changes incentives across entire economies. Over five years, the latter produces faster, broader global abatement because it operates where emissions actually grow — outside U.S. borders.
