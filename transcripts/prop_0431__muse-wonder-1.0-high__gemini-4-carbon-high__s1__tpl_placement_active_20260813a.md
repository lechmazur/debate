[P1.1] A public school classroom is not thirty private living rooms stacked together. When a parent can require teachers to confiscate a traded snack, forbid a child from sitting with classmates of the opposite sex, or pull one girl aside for extra modesty checks beyond the dress code, the school stops delivering a common education and starts enforcing one household’s lifestyle on public time — and on other people’s children.
[P1.2] Limiting that power protects three things at once. First, it protects the shared mission. Schools must enforce rules tied to safety, health, and learning: severe allergies, harassment, disruption, dress standards that apply to everyone. Highly restrictive rules — no birthday foods for anyone, no mixed-gender group work, no novels, dances, or celebrations — do not meet that test.
[P1.3] They convert a preference into a veto, and the most insistent families end up governing the classroom for everyone else.
[P1.4] Second, it protects teachers from an impossible job. One teacher with two dozen students cannot be a dietitian policing ingredient purity, a chaperone policing every friendship, and a tailor policing individual family codes. Enforcement means surveillance and singling a child out as different. That breeds shame and bullying, consumes teaching time, and puts teachers in conflict with both the demanding parent and the child caught between home and school.
[P1.5] Third, it protects children’s developing independence. School is where children first learn to encounter different foods, families, clothes, and customs and begin making small choices of their own. A child who wants to trade a carrot stick, join a group project, or wear what the school code already allows is not in danger; she is growing up. Parents retain enormous authority — what they serve at home, what they teach, what activities they choose outside school, and whether to exit for private or home education.
[P1.6] What they should lose is the ability to conscript teachers as lifestyle police inside a public institution built for everyone.

[C1.2] Because young children naturally cave to peer pressure, telling parents they still govern "at home" [P1.5] is hollow when five days of school undo household rules, while telling them to "exit for private or home education" [P1.5] makes parental authority a luxury good that working-class families cannot afford.
[C1.3] Reducing parental power also forces administrators to decide which family commitments are "mainly personal lifestyle preferences." Real families do not sort neatly into medical emergencies, formal religion, and trivial whims. When an ethical vegan household bans animal products, an immigrant family maintains unwritten cultural modesty norms, or health-conscious parents prohibit ultra-processed sweets, why should their rules rank below clergy-certified dogma? Downgrading "lifestyle" boundaries forces principals to interrogate family motives and penalizes secular conscience, preventative health, and minority custom.
[C1.4] Finally, stripping this authority backfires on teachers, children, and common schools alike. Worse, making public schools adversarial drives strict families with means out entirely, fracturing the shared classroom [P1.1] and isolating those children far more than reasonable enforcement ever would.
