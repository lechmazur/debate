[P1.1] The question isn't whether people will drink in public spaces. They already do. The question is whether we'd rather have them do it under streetlights and security, or in stairwells, parking lots, and parks where nobody is watching. The proposition simply takes the option that already works.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism that decides this debate: visibility regulates. A designated nightlife zone with open-container permissions comes bundled with everything informal drinking lacks — police who expect crowds and staff accordingly, vendors with licenses to lose, lighting, foot traffic, and a clear geographic boundary that tells everyone where the rules live. Push that same drinking into alleys and apartment parties and you get the worst of both worlds: all the alcohol, none of the oversight. House parties don't card.
[P1.3] Back alleys don't have medics. Nobody calls for help when they're afraid of being caught.
[P1.4] Second, harm reduction beats moral aspiration. Cities that treat public drinking as a vice to be hidden don't reduce consumption; they relocate its harms. [P1.5] Savannah's to-go district and the common European plaza model aren't utopias; they're just honest about where drinking happens and pragmatic about managing it.
[P1.6] Third, economics and community. Concentrated nightlife zones concentrate business, tax revenue, and a shared sense that a district "belongs" to the city. The alternative — dispersal — means noise complaints on residential streets, vomit on suburban lawns, and bars shouldering legal risk for behavior that will occur regardless.
[P1.7] The burden here matters. Opponents must show not that open containers carry risks — everything does — but that prohibition actually reduces net harm better than management. History suggests otherwise: prohibition-style approaches to alcohol consistently produce displacement, not disappearance.

People pre-load less cautiously, arrive earlier, stay outside longer, and carry drinks between venues. [C1.3] Visibility does not automatically regulate; it can advertise permission.
[C1.4] “Tightly managed” is the weak point. Management means police overtime, sanitation, medics, boundary patrols, and constant discretion about who is too drunk to enter. The costs are concentrated on residents who live beside the zone, not the visitors who generate the revenue.
[C1.5] A city that cannot enforce an open-container ban reliably will not enforce a perfect zone boundary either.
[C1.6] The harm-reduction argument also cuts against the proposition. [C1.7] A stranger with a legal cup has no equivalent stake.
[C1.8] And the “displacement” claim is asserted, not shown. Banning public drinking may move some behavior, but legalization expands the total market for street drinking and concentrates its harms in one visible place.
