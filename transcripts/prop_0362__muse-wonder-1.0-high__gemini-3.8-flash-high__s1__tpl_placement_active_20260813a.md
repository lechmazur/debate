[P1.1] A standard movie ticket should not behave like an airline seat an hour before departure.
[P1.2] Aggressive dynamic pricing — sharply raising the price of an ordinary seat because it is Friday night, opening weekend, or the algorithm expects demand — treats cinema as a scarce commodity to be auctioned. But cinema-going survives as a mass habit only when it is the opposite: predictable, casual, and easy to say yes to. You decide to go, you grab friends, you pay roughly what you expected. That low-friction ritual built repeat audiences.
[P1.3] Surge pricing breaks the ritual at exactly the moments that sustain it. Opening nights and weekend evenings are when buzz, group plans, and shared reactions turn a film into a cultural event. Put a penalty on joining in then, and many do not simply pay more; they postpone, hunt for a cheaper slot, or wait for streaming. The choice stops being 'shall we go?' and becomes 'are we being overcharged?'
[P1.4] Once moviegoers start gaming showtimes and feeling punished for enthusiasm, attendance becomes calculated rather than habitual.
[P1.5] The burden is not evenly spread. Families, teenagers, and lower-income viewers — who most need a clear, affordable default price and who form habits earliest — are priced out of popular screenings first. That narrows the audience to those willing to pay a premium for a standard seat, and teaches everyone else that the theater is not reliably for them. A culture shared mainly by affluent payers is not a mass culture.
[P1.6] Theaters have better tools that do not poison the base product: stable standard prices, predictable off-peak discounts, subscriptions, and real premiums for genuinely premium formats or extra services. Charge more when you offer more. Do not charge more simply because more people want the same ordinary seat.
[P1.7] Short-term extraction from a hit weekend cannot rebuild a habit once people learn to stay home. Protect the predictable ticket, and you protect the audience itself.

The true existential threat to the cinema habit is not variable pricing; it is dark screens and shuttered neighborhood venues.
[C1.2] Dynamic pricing is not predatory extortion—it is modern revenue management that protects the theatrical ecosystem. PRO paints aggressive dynamic pricing purely as a one-way surge penalty [P1.3], but market-responsive pricing fundamentally works in both directions. By allowing high-demand blockbuster slots to command market rates from those with high willingness to pay, exhibitors capture vital operating surplus. That peak-period margin directly subsidizes the rest of the theatrical calendar: mid-budget releases, indie screenings, and deep discounts during off-peak hours.
[C1.3] Far from pricing out families and teenagers [P1.5], dynamic pricing expands the tent. A rigid, inflated baseline ticket price is an active barrier for price-conscious audiences. A dynamic model that drops off-peak standard seats to steep bargain rates unlocks cinema access for students and working-class patrons who are priced out by uniform rates.
[C1.4] Meanwhile, price differentiation spreads demand across showtimes, easing Friday night bottlenecks and giving budget-conscious viewers a compelling, habitual reason to attend throughout the week.
[C1.5] PRO suggests theaters rely solely on subscription plans or luxury formats [P1.6], but neighborhood theaters cannot survive on premium screens alone. Standard auditoriums represent the vast majority of seats. Restricting dynamic pricing on standard inventory forces exhibitors into a disastrous choice: raise baseline ticket prices across the board for everyone, or face bankruptcy.
[C1.6] To keep cinema accessible, communal, and economically viable, theaters must utilize the responsive pricing mechanisms that every capacity-constrained cultural industry relies on. Flexibility is what preserves the habit; rigid stagnation will destroy it.
