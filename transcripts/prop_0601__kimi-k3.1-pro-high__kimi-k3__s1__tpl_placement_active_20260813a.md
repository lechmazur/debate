[P1.1] Near-term decarbonization is a race against time, fiscal constraints, and growing urban demand. Latin America should therefore put its next marginal transport dollar into electric buses, not make new urban rail its primary climate bet. This is not a case for demolishing rail or cancelling well-advanced projects; it is a case about priority, speed, and scale.
[P1.2] First, buses can cut emissions across whole cities now. They already serve extensive networks, including peripheral and lower-income communities that rail lines often bypass. Electrification uses existing roads and depots, avoids years of land acquisition and tunneling, and allows procurement in stages: replace retiring diesel buses, learn from operation, then expand. A rail expansion may eventually move enormous numbers cleanly, but every year spent planning, financing, and building it is another year in which the existing diesel fleet keeps running.
[P1.3] Santiago and Bogotá illustrate the practical advantage: cities can introduce sizable electric-bus operations through successive fleet decisions rather than waiting for one megaproject to succeed.
[P1.4] Second, buses deliver more climate-relevant benefits per unit of committed capital. Their emissions gains begin route by route, while rail’s benefits arrive only after substantial construction, with its own material and disruption costs. Electric buses also immediately reduce exhaust pollution where people walk, wait, and live. Because routes can change as cities grow, bus investment is less vulnerable than fixed infrastructure to optimistic demand forecasts and shifting settlement patterns.
[P1.5] That flexibility matters where public budgets are tight and borrowing costly.
[P1.6] Third, electrifying buses builds the capabilities a broader clean-transport transition needs: utility planning, depot charging, maintenance skills, battery management, and procurement expertise. Charging can be concentrated at depots and scheduled to reduce grid stress; cleaner electricity then improves the fleet’s performance without replacing the vehicles again.
[P1.7] The likely objection is that rail carries more passengers and lasts longer. True—but capacity and longevity do not answer the near-term comparison. On corridors where rail is justified, build it. As the main regional decarbonization strategy, however, slower, costlier, geographically narrow projects cannot outrun rapid electrification of the fleets already serving millions.

[C1.1] "Near-term" does all the work in this motion, and it is a trap. The next decade is decisive precisely because rail deferred now becomes rail never built — or built at far higher cost, along corridors meanwhile consumed by development. Prioritizing buses over rail is not prudent sequencing; it is a permanent choice disguised as a temporary one.
[C1.2] First, buses clean trips; rail changes which trips people take. The region's fastest-growing transport emissions come from surging car and motorcycle ownership. An electric bus idling in the same congestion, offering the same slow journey as the diesel it replaced, does not pull one driver out of a car. Grade-separated rail is the only mode that beats the car on travel time at urban scale.
[C1.3] A strategy capped at decarbonizing existing bus riders leaves the dominant source of growth untouched.
[C1.4] Second, Latin America has already run this experiment. Bogotá chose buses over rail for three decades and built the world's flagship BRT — then watched it saturate, carrying densities no bus system should have to carry. It is now building its first metro line at prices inflated by thirty years of delay, along corridors the BRT defined but cannot serve. Postponement is not savings; it is the most expensive option on the menu.
[C1.5] Third, the speed advantage is thinner than advertised. Fleet replacement cycles, depot electrification, grid connections, and financing make mass e-bus rollout a decade-long project anyway. Meanwhile the physics favor rail: steel wheels at high occupancy move several times more passengers per kilowatt-hour than rubber tires on asphalt, and both modes ride the same cleaning grid. Starting rail now compounds benefits over a sixty-year life; each deferred year is cumulative emissions no later procurement can claw back.
[C1.6] Finally, "priority" is the genuinely scarce resource — fiscal space, political attention, financing windows. Bus fleets turn over through ordinary procurement regardless; rail exists only when a state makes it the priority. A region that prioritizes buses gets buses. A region that prioritizes rail gets both.

Electric buses can use dedicated lanes, signal priority, and improved frequencies without waiting for a new railway. Rail’s time advantage is real on suitable corridors, but geographically limited and unavailable during construction.
[P2.3] Meanwhile, electrification improves every trip already taken across the network. Mode shift matters, but CON simply assumes that new rail riders will overwhelmingly abandon cars rather than transfer from existing buses or generate additional travel.
[P2.4] Bogotá [C1.4] does not prove that bus priority was a thirty-year mistake. Its bus network moved enormous demand, identified where fixed capacity was needed, and Bogotá is now building rail—exactly the corridor-specific sequencing our case permits. High metro costs after delay do not show that regional governments should have made megaprojects their main climate strategy instead of cleaning fleets. At most, they justify protecting alignments and advancing justified projects, not diverting the near-term priority everywhere.
[P2.5] The “decade-long rollout” objection [C1.5] actually exposes the decisive difference. A ten-year bus program delivers reductions after the first depot and procurement tranche; a ten-year rail project can deliver none until opening. Rail’s efficiency at high occupancy cannot erase those intervening emissions, especially where demand is uncertain. Its sixty-year life is valuable, but it does not make waiting-compatible construction a faster decarbonization tool.
[P2.6] Finally, “prioritize rail and get both” [C1.6] assumes away the fiscal scarcity CON itself invokes. Ordinary fleet procurement does not automatically mean electric procurement: without deliberate priority, it can mean another generation of diesel buses. Rail-first can therefore produce committed capital, prolonged diesel operation, and no near-term network-wide gains. Preserve corridors; build rail where justified.

Surface buses, however segregated, hit speed and capacity ceilings: dwell times, bunching, intersections. Grade-separate the buses to escape that and you have built a rail corridor with less capacity. Nor does the region need thirty more years of BRT to "identify corridors" [P2.4]; it knows its dense corridors today, and delay is precisely what made Bogotá's metro so expensive.
[C2.3] On mode shift, [P2.3] notes that rail riders may transfer from buses. Some will — which relieves saturated corridors. A main strategy whose mode-shift lever is zero abandons the region's fastest-growing emissions source by design.
[C2.4] [P2.5]'s "first depot, first tranche" arithmetic cuts both ways: a tranche is a few hundred buses in fleets of many thousands, so the near-term gap they prize is modest. And the diesel lock-in in [P2.6] has a cheap fix — mandate electric at procurement renewal, a regulation rather than a claim on fiscal priority. Rail cannot be mandated into existence; it lives or dies on priority. Hence prioritizing rail really does yield both, while prioritizing buses yields cleaner buses in worse traffic.
[C2.5] So weigh it this way: PRO wins grams of CO₂ this decade; CON wins the trajectory. Corridors are being built over and car habits hardening right now — that, not tranche timing, is what "near-term" actually decides. On irreversibility and on the dominant emissions source, the proposition falls.
