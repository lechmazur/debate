[P1.1] A rideshare driver who works fifty hours a week for three years, then wakes up to an automated deactivation email, is unemployed in every sense that matters. He has lost income he counted on, through no decision of his own, exactly the event unemployment insurance was invented for. The only reason he gets nothing is a paperwork category. That is the whole issue: not whether he faces the risk, but whether the state bothers to record that he was working.
[P1.2] Start with the insurance logic. Social insurance exists because earnings shocks are unpredictable individually and predictable in aggregate, so we pool them. Nothing about that logic turns on employment classification. A courier's illness, a care worker's injury, an app's sudden algorithmic downranking — these are the same risks, hitting the same household budgets.
[P1.3] Excluding this work does not eliminate the cost; it shifts it to emergency rooms, food banks, evictions, and means-tested welfare that taxpayers fund anyway, only later and more expensively.
[P1.4] Second, the mechanism is unusually easy here. Platform work is the most precisely measured labor in the economy. Every fare, delivery, and task is timestamped and priced to the cent because the company needs that data to take its commission. Requiring standardized earnings reporting to a social-insurance authority asks firms to transmit numbers they already compute.
[P1.5] Compare the alternative we actually tried during the pandemic, when governments improvised gig-worker aid on self-attested income: slow payments to people who needed them fast, and wide-open fraud. Standardized records are the fix for both problems at once.
[P1.6] Third, without this rule, misclassification pays. A firm that routes work through contractors avoids contributions its competitor must make for identical labor. That is not flexibility; it is a subsidy for one business model, financed by everyone else's payroll taxes.
[P1.7] Counting platform earnings does not require abolishing flexible schedules or multi-apping. It requires pro-rata contributions on measured earnings and portable credits that follow the worker. The plumbing exists. What is missing is the requirement.

Strip that away and you no longer have unemployment insurance — you have an income-smoothing program wearing its name.
[C1.2] That is the motion's fatal gap. PRO calls platform work "the most precisely measured labor in the economy" [P1.4]. True — and irrelevant. Earnings records tell you how much a worker made.
[C1.3] They say nothing about why the earnings stopped. Was the driver deactivated, or did she switch to a competing app, take a month off, move, cut hours to study? The standardized record is silent on exactly the question UI exists to adjudicate: involuntariness. A mandate built on this data either pays people who are not unemployed in any meaningful sense, or requires a verification apparatus the motion never describes.
[C1.4] We ran this experiment. Pandemic gig aid collapsed into delays and fraud not merely because records were bad, but because nobody could verify a job loss that has no employer to confirm it. Standardized earnings data fixes the easy half of that failure and leaves the hard half untouched. Worse, it invites manufactured eligibility: workers control their own hours, so they can front-load earnings to qualify, then claim.
[C1.5] Traditional UI guards against this with experience-rating and employer verification of separations. Here, the insured party controls the insured event.
[C1.6] And notice what follows if platforms must pay contributions. Experience-rated premiums give firms a direct financial stake in controlling hours and separations — which is to say, in becoming employers. Expect capped hours, tighter control, deactivation discipline, and years of reclassification litigation. The flexibility PRO promises to preserve is the first casualty.
[C1.7] The motion records the earnings and ignores the event. No insurance system can run on that.
