[P1.1] The dominance of English does not merely coexist alongside other languages—it displaces them, and with them, entire frameworks of knowledge. Three mechanisms make this harm structural and severe.
[P1.2] First, epistemic erasure. When English becomes the gatekeeping language of science and scholarship, knowledge that cannot be cleanly translated simply vanishes from global consideration. Indigenous taxonomies of ecology, medicine, and agriculture—refined over generations and encoded in languages that carry precision English cannot replicate—get flattened into approximations or ignored entirely. A plant called by its Yup'ik name carries therapeutic information embedded in that name; rendered as a Latin binomial, it becomes just another specimen.
[P1.3] The loss is not linguistic decoration but cognitive content.
[P1.4] Second, systemic disadvantage. Non-native English speakers must invest years and substantial resources achieving fluency just to enter the conversation. Anglophone researchers start at the finish line. This asymmetry means that important questions pursued in Osaka or Ouagadougou get fewer citations, less funding, and less visibility—not because they lack merit, but because they were articulated in the wrong language.
[P1.5] The global south produces knowledge that remains local, while Anglophone mediocrity circulates globally.
[P1.6] Third, cultural homogenization. Language shapes thought. When the internet, business, and science funnel all expression through English, the conceptual vocabulary of humanity narrows. Humor, metaphor, moral reasoning, and social analysis that depend on grammatical structures English lacks become harder to sustain.
[P1.7] Communities abandon their languages not because English enriches them, but because the dominance of English makes their own tongues appear insufficient for modern participation.
[P1.8] The question is not whether English offers convenience for transnational coordination. Any lingua franca would. The question is whether this particular dominance, with its built-in epistemic bias and cumulative cultural cost, leaves non-anglophone cultures better off. It does not.
[P1.9] The convenience accrues mostly to anglophones; the harm accrues almost entirely to everyone else.

[C1.1] The proposition frames English as a gatekeeper. It is actually a door—and one that opens in both directions.
[C1.2] Consider what the world looked like before English achieved its current reach. A researcher in Senegal publishing in French reached Francophone Africa and parts of Europe. Today, that same researcher reaches Tokyo, São Paulo, and Bangalore. The alternative to English dominance is not a vibrant ecosystem of equally circulating languages.
[C1.3] It is fragmentation: dozens of regional networks, each internally rich, but mutually inaccessible. PRO calls this "convenience." For a clinician in Uganda who needs the latest trials, or an engineer in Vietnam adapting climate technology, it is not convenience. It is participation.
[C1.4] PRO's epistemic erasure argument cuts the wrong way. Yes, translation loses nuance. But without a shared language, that Yup'ik plant knowledge never reaches a pharmacologist in Mumbai who might develop it. The choice is not between English and perfectly preserved indigenous taxonomies.
[C1.5] It is between English as an imperfect bridge and no bridge at all. Bridges carry traffic in both directions—non-anglophone knowledge systems gain audiences they could never otherwise reach.
[C1.6] The systemic disadvantage claim has the same flaw. Non-native speakers invest in English because the return is access to a global audience no other language provides. Remove English and you remove the asymmetry—by leveling everyone down to local isolation, not up to equal global participation.
[C1.7] Finally, English is not monolithic. It is being claimed, bent, and reshaped by its non-native speakers. Nigerian, Indian, Singaporean, Caribbean Englishes carry the cognitive architectures PRO fears losing. The language is not narrowing thought; it is being expanded by precisely the cultures PRO says it erases.
[C1.8] The motion asks whether non-anglophone cultures are better off. A world where they can speak to each other is better than one where they cannot.

The current arrangement doesn't just translate; it replaces.
[P2.3] A Yup'ik plant name rendered into English loses the therapeutic logic encoded in the original term. [P2.4] World Englishes prove my point rather than undermining it. [P2.5] Remove the framing of English as mere tool and the asymmetry becomes clear: anglophones gain effortless global reach while everyone else subsidizes that reach with their linguistic inheritance.
