[P1.1] Picture the standard vacation-rental search: a listing advertises $120 a night, you spend twenty minutes comparing it to alternatives, you click toward booking—and suddenly it's $212 with a $150 cleaning fee, a "resort fee," and a rule banning the family gathering you booked it for. That bait-and-switch is not a glitch. It's a design choice called drip pricing, and platforms use it because behavioral economics shows that once people invest effort in a choice, they swallow surprise costs rather than start over.
[P1.2] Requiring fees and house rules to appear before checkout simply ends a deliberate exploitation of human psychology.
[P1.3] Three reasons this requirement is right.
[P1.4] First, prices only work when they're real. Markets discipline sellers through comparison shopping. A listing's advertised nightly rate is meaningless if half the cost hides behind a click. Hidden fees don't just harm consumers—they punish honest hosts, because the host who folds cleaning into a transparent rate looks more expensive than the one who games the search ranking with a fake-low headline price.
[P1.5] Mandatory disclosure restores competition on actual value.
[P1.6] Second, house rules are material terms, not fine print. No pets, no children, quiet hours at 9 p.m., mandatory checkout chores—these can make a property unusable for the very purpose someone is booking it. A "request to book" often involves a soft credit hold and a commitment; learning the dealbreakers afterward wastes everyone's time, including hosts fielding doomed requests.
[P1.7] Third, the fix is trivially cheap. The platform already has every fee and rule in its database—it chooses where to display them. Airlines were forced to advertise all-in fares years ago and the industry thrived. Airbnb itself added a total-price toggle under public pressure, proving feasibility; a requirement just makes honesty the floor rather than an opt-in feature.
[P1.8] The question is simple: should the true price and terms appear before or after the consumer is psychologically committed? Before is what informed consent means.
