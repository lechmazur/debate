[P1.1] Merger review contains an asymmetry: concentrated sellers threaten consumers, while concentrated buyers of labor are largely overlooked. Workers often sell their labor into local markets where a few employers possess substantial bargaining power. A merger that intensifies that power can suppress wages, reduce job mobility, and worsen conditions even when consumer prices barely change. Labor-market power deserves the same scrutiny as consumer-price effects, not dismissal because workers are not the buyers of the merged product.
[P1.2] When a dominant hospital system, care provider, restaurant chain, or logistics firm combines with a nearby rival, it may gain power over applicants, contractors, and employees. It can reduce opportunities to switch jobs, shape schedules, and capture productivity gains without raising pay. Even a merger that saves consumers money may worsen the balance between employer and worker. A dominant buyer can also set terms smaller competitors cannot afford to resist.
[P1.3] Treating monopsony seriously does not mean presuming every labor merger is anticompetitive. It means asking core antitrust questions: Can the merger materially increase employer power? Are wage suppression, reduced mobility, or degraded job quality likely? Are claimed efficiencies real and attributable to the merger?
[P1.4] Could worker-protective remedies preserve consumer benefits? A price-only analysis misses this contest between employer and employee. A merger yielding modest consumer savings but stripping bargaining power from thousands of workers should not pass merely because its shelves remain cheap.
[P1.5] Complexity does not justify ignoring labor harm. Courts and agencies already analyze concentration, market definition, entry, efficiencies, and competitive effects; applying that reasoning to the buyer side requires discipline, not new economics. Where evidence is thin, agencies can use targeted information requests and structural presumptions. Uncertainty should not become asymmetry.
[P1.6] Why should consumer savings justify disregarding worker injury? Consumer welfare is important, not a trump card making monopsony irrelevant.

[C1.1] The proposition demands that merger review treat labor-market monopsony as seriously as consumer-price effects. That is a structural error, not a moral one. Consumer prices are the most direct, measurable, and universally felt consequence of market power. Labor-market effects are diffuse, context-dependent, and far harder to isolate from a single transaction.
[C1.2] Elevating them to equal status would distort enforcement, not correct it.
[C1.3] Consider what "as seriously" means in practice. Agencies would need to build or borrow analytical frameworks for buyer-side power that lack the decades of case law, economic modeling, and judicial precedent supporting consumer-price review. Every merger would face dual scrutiny with potentially conflicting conclusions. A hospital merger that lowers patient costs but concentrates nursing labor markets would become a toss-up rather than a net benefit.
[C1.4] That uncertainty chills procompetitive consolidation and invites litigation over speculative wage effects years after a deal closes.
[C1.5] The asymmetry PRO describes is real, but the remedy is not equal treatment—it is targeted tools. Minimum wages, collective-bargaining rights, and sector-specific regulation address monopsony more precisely than antitrust, which is blunt by design. Antitrust merger review was built to catch price-fixing and output restriction, not to second-guess every local labor market. Stretching it to cover buyer power risks false positives: blocking mergers that genuinely help consumers because an agency cannot confidently predict wage trajectories in a thin labor market.
[C1.6] Complexity is not a reason to ignore harm, PRO says. But complexity is precisely why consumer-price effects should remain the primary lens. The harm is clearest there, the tools sharpest there, and the accountability most transparent there. Treating monopsony as equally serious does not balance the scales—it tips them toward paralysis.
