[P1.1] A government may enforce immigration law without granting itself the power to imprison people for an unknown and potentially unending period. Indefinite detention separates families, destroys livelihoods, and inflicts severe psychological harm before any criminal conviction. Its uncertainty is itself coercive: a person cannot know whether confinement will last weeks, months, or years. Border control does not require a legal black hole.
[P1.2] Presumptive release is not unconditional release, and it does not end immigration proceedings. It means liberty is the default unless the government demonstrates an individualized, serious risk of flight or danger. Case-management programs then provide scheduled check-ins, hearing reminders, address updates, legal orientation, and referrals for housing or medical support. Conditions can escalate proportionately where risk is shown.
[P1.3] Any exceptional detention should be time-limited and regularly reviewed. That model preserves enforcement while forcing the state to use the least restrictive tool that works.
[P1.4] It also improves compliance. People are more capable of attending hearings when they understand the process, receive reminders, and can obtain basic stability. Detention often does the opposite: it isolates people from documents, lawyers, relatives, and witnesses needed to resolve their cases accurately. A frightened family moved among facilities is not easier to process fairly than one living at a known address under supervision.
[P1.5] The practical comparison is decisive. Detention requires secure facilities, guards, transport, healthcare, and constant administration. Case management is inherently less resource-intensive, allowing governments to focus detention capacity on the rare person who presents a demonstrable threat rather than using confinement as a default bureaucracy.
[P1.6] CON must justify not merely temporary detention in exceptional cases, but detention with no reliable endpoint even where structured community supervision can manage risk. Administrative convenience cannot carry that burden. When a cheaper, more humane, and enforceable alternative exists, indefinite confinement becomes punishment without sentence. Governments should preserve control over immigration cases, but they should do so through accountable supervision—not limitless custody.
