[P1.1] Millions of long-term undocumented residents already live, work, pay rent, and raise children inside the country. They are not going to vanish. Mass removal would require raids on neighborhoods, workplaces, and schools on a scale that would shatter budgets, clog courts, and tear apart communities. A conditional pathway to legal status simply acknowledges reality and replaces permanent limbo with order.
[P1.2] That order pays. Right now many undocumented workers labor off the books, which invites wage theft, undercutting, and exploitation that drags down standards for everyone. Legal status pulls them into formal employment, payroll taxes, and labor protections. Employers can hire openly, workers can change jobs and report abuse, and governments collect more revenue to fund services citizens use.
[P1.3] In sectors like agriculture, construction, elder care, and hospitality where native-born workers are scarce, keeping experienced long-term workers stabilizes businesses instead of forcing constant turnover.
[P1.4] It also strengthens public safety and the rule of law. The proposal is not open borders: background checks screen out serious criminals, while work history, tax payment, and community ties prove contribution. That is more control, not less. When people can gain status by passing checks, they have reason to come forward, be vetted, and live under the same laws as everyone else.
[P1.5] Police chiefs have long argued that trust matters; victims and witnesses who fear deportation stay silent, letting real criminals operate with impunity.
[P1.6] Finally, it protects families and fairness. A mother who has worked a decade, paid taxes through an ID number, kept a clean record, and sent kids to local schools has earned a chance to regularize. Keeping her permanently deportable punishes children, churches, and neighborhoods for a status offense. Earned legalization with strict criteria rewards responsibility, ends a permanent underclass, and lets governments know who is here.
