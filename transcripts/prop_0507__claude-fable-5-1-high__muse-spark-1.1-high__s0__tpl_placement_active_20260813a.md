[P1.1] The question is not whether governments should shape clean-tech markets. They will. The question is which end of the factory you pay: the loading dock where product leaves, or the front gate before anything is built. Pay the loading dock.
[P1.2] Producer subsidies buy capacity. Demand-side policy buys outcomes. When a government hands a firm a factory grant or a per-unit manufacturing credit, it is betting that this company, with this chemistry, at this location, will still be competitive in five years. Governments are bad at that bet.
[P1.3] Northvolt received lavish support and stumbled into insolvency before reaching scale. Britishvolt collected political enthusiasm and collapsed without producing a commercial cell. Solyndra's story needs no retelling. In each case the money went out before the market had spoken, and the market spoke anyway.
[P1.4] Demand-side tools invert that risk. A purchase rebate, a procurement contract for electric buses, a zero-emission vehicle mandate, a battery storage auction, a carbon price: each says "we will buy X, deliver it," and lets firms fight over who delivers cheapest. The state pays only when something is sold and working. Capital is disciplined by customers rather than lobbyists.
[P1.5] And the tools are technology-neutral: a mandate does not care whether the winning battery is nickel-rich, iron-phosphate, or sodium-ion. A factory grant locks in whichever chemistry looked best on the day the cheque cleared.
[P1.6] This is not theory. The biggest battery buildout in history was driven above all by demand pull: purchase subsidies tied to range and efficiency thresholds, licence-plate quotas, mass bus procurement. Weak makers were culled by customers, not planners. Norway built the world's deepest EV market with almost no domestic production subsidy at all.
[P1.7] Producer subsidies also start bidding wars between treasuries, with open-ended manufacturing credits whose costs balloon far past forecasts, and they invite trade retaliation that demand-side measures largely avoid.
[P1.8] Favoring demand pull does not mean zero support for builders. It means the default lever should be the one that pays for results, not promises.
