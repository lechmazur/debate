A right to the whole record, including identifying information, compels the state to reveal private facts about third parties who never consented to be identified.
[C1.2] PRO says disclosure “does not require any biological parent to meet” [P1.2]. That understates what a name does. Identifying information opens contact, relatives, social media, and family discovery. A no-contact rule after disclosure locks the door after handing over the address: the parent’s anonymity, and often a spouse’s or sibling’s privacy, is gone.
[C1.3] Privacy is not fear of an awkward knock, as [P1.5] suggests; it is control over whether a traumatic chapter becomes known inside another family.
[C1.4] The medical argument proves too much. A patient may need family history; a patient rarely needs a birth parent’s name or contact details. Redacted medical updates, genetic testing, mutual-consent registries, intermediaries, and narrow court orders can obtain health facts without making identity automatic. PRO’s “whole relevant record” rule [P1.4] gives a name even where the need is medical or biographical.
[C1.5] That is not tailored access; it is a trump card.
[C1.6] There is also reliance. Adoption systems often depend on assurances that a confidential relinquishment will not later be undone by disclosure. Tell a frightened pregnant woman that anonymity lasts only until the child turns eighteen, and foreseeable incentives are false histories, agency avoidance, or abandonment outside supervised channels. Adults can get the story, medical facts, and contact by consent.
[C1.7] They should not have a categorical legal right, automatically at eighteen, to unseal someone else’s life. The state should keep its promises; confidential intermediaries and mutual consent meet those needs instead.

[P5.1] CON’s strongest argument is that a name can sometimes enable unwanted discovery. That is a serious risk, but it does not justify making one adult’s identity permanently dependent on another person’s consent. Privacy can be protected through no-contact orders, limits on publishing contact details, redactions of unrelated medical or trauma information, and penalties for harassment. What CON cannot protect is the adult’s right to learn what the state recorded about their origin.
[P5.2] Its consent-based alternatives fail precisely when the need is strongest. If a biological parent is deceased, absent, or unwilling, an intermediary cannot reveal the name, produce family history, or resolve which relatives are available. Record auditing may confirm that an entry is unverified, but “unverifiable” is not an answer to identity. Nor does disclosure assert biological truth: an adult receives a qualified record entry, not a public accusation, and can pursue correction through the proper process.
[P5.3] The decisive principle is therefore simple: the state may protect third-party privacy, but it should not retain permanent custody of the adult’s own identity. Once adulthood begins, the law should let the adopted person learn whom the state identified, while regulating contact rather than pretending secrecy can remain absolute forever. CON offers ways to obtain information if someone else agrees. PRO offers an actual right when agreement is impossible.
