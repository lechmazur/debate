[P1.1] Advanced surveillance technologies become weapons of mass repression in the hands of authoritarian regimes. [P1.3] Exporting such technologies to these regimes guarantees complicity in future violations. Authoritarian governments routinely repurpose surveillance systems from their stated law-enforcement purposes to identify, track, and silence dissent. End-use monitoring and contractual restrictions are routinely circumvented; once the technology is transferred, the exporting state loses any real control over its deployment. Worse, every export normalizes the idea that mass surveillance is a legitimate tool of governance, encouraging a global race to the bottom in digital rights.
[P1.4] The supposed benefits for legitimate policing are vastly overstated. In repressive contexts, security concerns are frequently pretexts for control, and less intrusive, targeted methods can address actual criminal threats without enabling systemic rights violations. The very presence of these systems creates a chilling effect, deterring free expression and assembly long before any arrest is made. Multilateral export control regimes already restrict dual-use goods with similar risks; advanced surveillance technology, with its demonstrated capacity to enable wide-scale oppression, clearly warrants the same treatment.
[P1.5] Banning these exports removes a critical enabler of repression, aligns action with principle, and sends a clear message that the international community will not be complicit in digital authoritarianism. The moral and practical case is overwhelming: we must not supply the tools that make modern tyranny efficient and scalable.
