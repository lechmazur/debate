[P1.1] A public university still harvests timber from a forest the state took from a neighboring Indigenous nation a century and a half ago. A state park system still sells campsite permits on the same land. Their budgets and endowments are not built only on tuition and taxes; they draw on continuing income from land the state seized. That pipeline, not the distant treaty, is the case for restitution.
[P1.2] Any property system worth defending forbids profiting from another's dispossession and keeping the profit. If a private trustee had invested stolen land, a court would order return or disgorgement. The state cannot claim property law's protection while demanding exemption from its justice. A seizure does not become legitimate through long public possession; the institution is still there, still collecting rent, resource revenue, or tuition-supported value from an asset it should not hold on those terms.
[P1.3] That is why the proposition is narrow, not revolutionary. It is triggered by a live condition: a public institution still benefits in identifiable ways from land taken without fair consent, and the harmed nation is still identifiable. Where land can be returned without destroying core public functions, return it. Where it cannot, long-term revenue sharing or equivalent restitution stops the ongoing transfer instead of normalizing it.
[P1.4] The target is the state's continuing gain, not every ancient title or private landowner.
[P1.5] Land-grant universities are the clear test. They were seeded by the proceeds of Indigenous land and still hold land, endowments, and income from it. A land acknowledgment that names the nation but leaves the revenue untouched is ceremony, not repair. Restitution makes the state stop treating dispossession as an endowment and start treating it as a debt still on the books.
[P1.6] Until then, those public institutions are not neutral educators or parks; they are ongoing beneficiaries of a seizure the state would punish in anyone else.

[C1.1] Continuing benefit is a reason to investigate a historical claim, not a sufficient basis for a statewide duty to transfer land or bind future revenue. PRO’s “pipeline” skips the steps that make restitution coherent: responsibility for the taking, a surviving wrongful gain, an entitled claimant, and a remedy proportionate to that gain.
[C1.2] A private trustee has an identified beneficiary, a defined fiduciary duty, and a traceable asset. A university or park system serves the whole public and may hold land under a different chain of grants, sales, purchases and public investment. Permit or timber receipts are not automatically stolen profit; they may also fund conservation, maintenance and education. Present benefit therefore cannot do the work of both title and valuation.
[C1.3] Land-grant institutions expose the tracing problem. Original land proceeds can be mingled with later appropriations, donations, earnings and expenditures. A modern endowment is not a labeled account of dispossession. Nor does “equivalent restitution” explain whether the measure is historical land value, current value, net income, or gross revenue.
[C1.4] Those choices can produce radically different transfers.
[C1.5] The claimant problem is equally serious. Multiple nations may assert connections to one territory, while a recognized governing body may not represent every descendant community. An institutional benefit test supplies no rule for priority, overlap, or earlier settlements. It invites the state to pick winners before the claim is resolved.
[C1.6] And the remedies have ongoing public consequences. Returning a working forest can transfer fire, conservation and access obligations; perpetual revenue sharing can bind future budgets even when the institution’s finances change. If “offer” means a meaningful entitlement, these standards are indispensable. If it means only an invitation to negotiate, the motion creates no reliable repair at all.
[C1.7] States should honor treaties, judgments and negotiated settlements. But this proposition turns a moral warning into an automatic remedy without the rules needed to make that remedy just.

[C2.1] PRO’s trustee analogy assumes what the motion must establish. An endowment known to contain rents from an embezzled estate has an identified victim, a wrongful acquisition, and a traceable fund. A university receiving timber income today does not thereby concede that it committed the taking, holds another nation’s property, or owes that nation each receipt. Calling the institution a “knowing trustee” [P2.1] is a conclusion dressed as an analogy.
[C2.2] Historical seizure may be established while institutional responsibility, surviving gain, and present entitlement remain contested.
[C2.3] Nor is tracing merely arithmetic. Equitable accounting can apportion a fund once liability and the relevant baseline are known; it cannot decide whether restitution means original proceeds, subsequent appreciation, net income, or gross revenue. Those choices determine whether an award repairs a gain or exceeds it. “Public purpose does not launder dispossession” [P2.2], but dispossession does not launder an unmeasured present debt either.
[C2.4] Interpleader likewise answers a different problem. It protects an identified disputed fund while competing claims are resolved. It does not authorize the state to designate an institution’s land or finances as disputed merely because multiple nations assert connections to the territory. An offer to the wrong claimant, or a settlement ignoring overlapping claims, can create fresh injustice rather than postpone repair.
[C2.5] The weakest claim is that “a perpetual benefit” justifies “a perpetual duty” [P2.6]. Institutional benefits are not necessarily perpetual: resources become depleted, programs close, revenues fluctuate, and current income may reflect later investment as much as the original asset. If revenue sharing continues regardless, it is no longer disgorgement of benefit. If it varies with actual benefit, then duration and amount require precisely the standards PRO postpones.
[C2.6] The choice is therefore not repair versus delay until perfection. It is claim-specific restitution versus a categorical duty to offer land or long-term revenue before responsibility, entitlement, and proportionality are fixed. Uncertainty should not automatically burden Indigenous communities—but neither should it authorize uncertain transfers from the whole public.
