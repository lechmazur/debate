[P1.1] Democracies should give future-generations commissioners binding veto power over some major long-horizon policy decisions because the central failure in such decisions is not ignorance but missing representation. The people who will bear the largest costs—climate damages, depleted fisheries, contaminated aquifers, locked-in infrastructure, nuclear waste—cannot vote, campaign, or lobby. Elected officials, by design, discount them. Even well-intentioned governments face reelection horizons of a few years, and policies with diffuse future costs and concentrated present benefits create predictable bias toward borrowing, extraction, and delay.
[P1.2] A binding veto is the only mechanism with sufficient force to correct that bias. Advisory councils, impact assessments, and nonbinding future tests are easily ignored when short-term pressure rises. A veto changes incentives before the decision is made. Ministries must design projects that can survive scrutiny for durability, reversibility, and intergenerational harm.
[P1.3] That is not government by philosopher-kings; it is a constitutional-style check, like judicial review or fiscal rules, deliberately limited to major long-horizon choices.
[P1.4] The proposition is carefully bounded. It does not give commissioners authority over ordinary policy, taxation, or day-to-day spending. It applies to decisions whose consequences are large, durable, and hard to reverse: emissions trajectories, critical ecosystem destruction, major energy or transport lock-in, long-lived radioactive waste, perhaps permanent alterations to constitutional or fiscal foundations. The commissioner should have to justify vetoes with public reasons tied to evidence and thresholds, and legislatures should be able to override only under demanding conditions, if at all.
[P1.5] This makes democracy more legitimate, not less. Democratic legitimacy comes from giving appropriate weight to all affected interests. If a majority today can impose irreversible harms on people who will be citizens tomorrow, calling that pure self-government mistakes power for fairness. A future-generations veto forces the polity to govern as a trust, not a fire sale.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds noble but collapses on a single problem: nobody can represent people who don't exist. A future-generations commissioner doesn't speak for future citizens; he speaks for his own predictions about what future citizens will want. Those predictions are shaped entirely by present knowledge, present ideology, and present appointing authorities. We are not adding missing representation—we are sanctifying one faction's guesswork and handing it a veto over elected government.
[C1.2] That matters because veto power is absolute in effect. PRO calls it a "constitutional-style check," but judicial review at least grounds itself in text and precedent that a legislature can amend. A commissioner's veto rests on no comparable anchor—only the commissioner's reading of "durability," "reversibility," and "intergenerational harm," terms with no settled meaning. The proposition asks us to believe this power stays bounded, but a veto over "major long-horizon policy" reaches energy systems, infrastructure, fiscal foundations, and constitutional change—the substance of modern governance.
[C1.3] Call it bounded if you like; the bound covers most of what governments do.
[C1.4] Meanwhile, inaction is itself a long-horizon decision with intergenerational costs. A veto that blocks new energy infrastructure, housing, or industrial investment doesn't preserve options for the future; it imposes poverty, fragility, and slowed adaptation on the very people it claims to protect. PRO assumes commissioners will veto only harmful choices.
[C1.5] But commissioners can err toward caution just as elected officials err toward short-term gain—and with a veto, their errors are unilateral and uncorrectable.
[C1.6] Democracies already have tools for long-horizon concern: supermajority requirements, sunset clauses, constitutional review, independent fiscal councils, and the simple fact that today's young become tomorrow's voters. These are imperfect but accountable. The proposition trades accountability for a fiction of representation and calls it legitimacy. It is the opposite: government by an appointed few, justified by imagined consent from people who never consented to them.

A commissioner need not guess their ideology to defend those basic conditions. Appointment is not faction if independence, evidence, and public reason constrain discretion.
[P2.2] That is not fiction; it is trusteeship, and democracies already use it when courts protect future rights or regulators limit toxic waste.
[P2.3] CON says the veto is “absolute in effect.” But the motion is not a roving veto over governance. It is limited to major long-horizon decisions with large, durable, hard-to-reverse consequences, and it requires public justification tied to evidence and thresholds. That is not government by whim; it is a reason-giving check, closer to constitutional review than CON admits.
[P2.4] The strongest CON point is error: commissioners can be too cautious. Yet elected governments err too, predictably toward present benefits and future costs. The question is which error is more dangerous on long-horizon choices. If a parliament underinvests or delays, later assemblies can correct it.
[P2.5] If it locks in emissions, collapses a fishery, or contaminates an aquifer for centuries, the error cannot be undone. A veto biased toward preventing irreversible harm is not recklessness; it is insurance where mistakes are permanent.
[P2.6] CON also says inaction harms the future. True, but a veto does not require inaction. It can distinguish between building adaptive capacity and burning future options. A project that genuinely broadens opportunity, resilience, or clean energy can pass evidence-based scrutiny.
[P2.7] What it should fail is the bargain that says “prosperity now by writing the largest costs onto people who cannot vote.” That is the bargain democracies should refuse.

The commissioner's authority is a veto over the legislature itself, grounded in terms like "durability" and "intergenerational harm" that no legislature has defined and no precedent constrains. That is not a check within the system; it is a power above it.
[C2.4] The irreversibility asymmetry is PRO's strongest card, but it cuts both ways. Inaction is also irreversible. [C2.5] Elected officials who err face elections. A commissioner who errs toward caution faces nothing.
[C2.6] PRO's closing image—refusing "prosperity now by writing costs onto non-voters"—assumes the commissioner knows which prosperity is real and which costs are hidden. That is exactly the guesswork the proposition cannot escape. Calling it "trusteeship" does not make the guess less of a guess; it just makes the guesser unaccountable.
