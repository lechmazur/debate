[P1.1] A democracy can survive rude cartoons, anonymous pamphlets, and savage satire. It cannot survive a world in which a candidate's face and voice can be forged so perfectly that voters have no reliable way to know what that candidate actually said or did. Hyper-realistic political deepfakes should be banned, even if the ban trims some satire and some anonymous speech, because they do not merely add another falsehood to a noisy marketplace. They counterfeit the evidence itself.
[P1.2] Consider the week before an election. A video appears of a frontrunner taking cash in a parking garage, or whispering a slur, or privately vowing to gut a program she publicly defends. It looks like her. It sounds like her.
[P1.3] It spreads before any newsroom can authenticate it. Some voters never hear the denial. Others hear it and do not know whom to believe. The harm is not abstract offense.
[P1.4] It is a corrupted choice, made under a deadline no correction can rewind. The point of a ban is not that every file vanishes. It is that campaigns, platforms, and courts have a rule inside the only window that counts.
[P1.5] The damage runs both ways. Fakes invent scandals and erase real ones. Once they are common, a genuine recording of corruption becomes optional to believe, and every politician gains the excuse that it was generated. A ban protects the innocent from forged guilt and the public from forged innocence.
[P1.6] Those costs are real and still the wrong ones to treat as decisive. Satire works because the audience can tell it is satire. A clip built to pass as an unaltered recording is impersonation, not commentary. Anonymous argument, cartoons, and obvious parody all remain.
[P1.7] What disappears is the power to speak with someone else's face. Democracies already ban forged ballots. A forged face offered as political proof is the same attack on the choice. That is a cost worth paying.

[C1.1] The forgery in the parking garage is coming regardless. That is the brutal fact the proposition avoids. The tools that generate a hyper-realistic clip run on consumer laptops, the code is open-source, and the internet does not stop at a democracy's border. A law can punish the file, but it cannot unsend it in the seventy-two hours before an election, which is the only window my opponent says matters.
[C1.2] So the ban buys almost none of the promised protection, while the costs to satire and anonymous speech are paid in full and forever. That is a terrible trade.
[C1.3] Worse, the ban actively backfires. Once synthetic video is illegal and rare, "it's a deepfake" becomes a courtroom-tested alibi. The politician caught on genuine tape now points to a statute built around the premise that convincing video can be faked. My opponent calls this the liar's dividend, then proposes a policy that pays it out.
[C1.4] And "hyper-realistic" is a moving target. Detection is an arms race the forgers currently win, so enforcement collapses into human judgment about intent and belief, which means chilling effects. The satirist who makes a cutting, plausible clip of a president announcing war, the animator whose parody is too good, the dissident abroad whose anonymity depends on never appearing on camera as herself: all of them now face a criminal statute whose line nobody can draw in advance. Vague speech crimes get enforced against the unpopular first.
[C1.5] The alternative is not surrender. Provenance standards, watermarking obligations on generators, rapid-response labeling by platforms, and harsh penalties for fraud committed with a deepfake, all target the harm, deceptive use, without criminalizing the category of speech itself. We already have laws against defrauding voters; we can aim them better.
[C1.6] The motion asks us to ban a form of expression because of what bad actors might do with it. Democracies do not survive by that logic. Reject the trade.
