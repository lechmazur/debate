[P1.1] Authoritarian rivals have weaponized Western capital against the West itself. When democratic nations allow unrestricted investment into the semiconductor and AI sectors of countries like China or Russia, they finance their own strategic vulnerability. Screening outbound investment is not protectionism—it is self-preservation.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. Capital flows carry more than money. Joint ventures in semiconductor fabrication transfer process knowledge that takes decades to develop. AI investments bring algorithmic expertise and training data pipelines.
[P1.3] These are not passive financial stakes; they are technology transfers in disguise. When a Dutch lithography company partners with a Chinese foundry, or when an American AI firm opens a research center in Shanghai, the know-how migrates. The authoritarian partner gains capabilities it could not have developed independently—capabilities that feed directly into military modernization, surveillance infrastructure, and economic coercion.
[P1.4] The semiconductor industry illustrates the stakes vividly. Advanced chips enable precision-guided missiles, autonomous weapons systems, and the supercomputers that model nuclear stockpiles. A single generation of technology advantage in chipmaking can determine whether an adversary can target American aircraft carriers or decrypt allied communications. AI compounds this danger: facial recognition systems that track dissidents, predictive policing that crushes dissent, disinformation engines that destabilize democracies—all run on hardware and software that Western investment helps build.
[P1.5] The objection that screening chills innovation inverts reality. Innovation suffers more when authoritarian rivals use acquired technology to dominate markets, extort supply chains, and force technological dependence. We have already watched rare earths become a lever of coercion. Semiconductors and AI are far more potent—and far more dangerous if captured.
[P1.6] Democratic governments have already acknowledged this logic through export controls and inbound investment screening. Outbound investment screening closes the final gap. Private capital should not subsidize the strategic capabilities of regimes that openly declare their intent to supplant the liberal order. The only question is whether we act before or after the consequences arrive.
