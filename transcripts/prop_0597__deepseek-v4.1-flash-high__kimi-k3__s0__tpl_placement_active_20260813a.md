[P1.1] The motion is conditional, and that condition is decisive. It permits heritable editing for serious monogenic diseases once safety thresholds are met. Opponents cannot win by invoking mosaicism, off-target effects, or uncertain long-term outcomes; those are exactly what the threshold excludes. The real question is whether, when the science is demonstrably safe enough, the law should allow families to prevent catastrophic genetic disease from passing to their children.
[P1.2] For some couples, there is no alternative. Imagine a couple whose embryos are all affected, or for whom embryo selection cannot yield an unaffected child. Their only route to a biological child free of that disease may be correcting the mutation at the embryo stage. If the edit is safe, denying it is not caution; it is cruelty dressed as principle.
[P1.3] Heritable editing changes the child and descendants. That is exactly why it matters: the disease stops, not just for one patient but for a lineage. Somatic therapy treats one body; heritable editing prevents the next generation from inheriting the mutation. If we can do that safely, the moral upside is enormous.
[P1.4] Opponents will invoke eugenics, inequality, and slippery slopes. Those are arguments for strict regulation, not prohibition. International rules can require case-by-case review, long-term follow-up, transparent registries, and limits to serious monogenic disease. A ban does not stop editing; it pushes it to unregulated clinics and wealthy couples who can travel.
[P1.5] That is the worst of both worlds: no safety, no equity, no accountability.
[P1.6] Finally, a child born free of a devastating disease has lost nothing. No one has a right to be born with Huntington’s or severe immunodeficiency. The motion draws a defensible line: therapy for serious disease, not enhancement; safety first, not safety never. When the threshold is met, permission is the ethical default.

[C1.6] Third, the line cannot hold. "Serious monogenic" has no stable legal edge: BRCA risk, deafness, polygenic scores each arrive incrementally and sympathetically. A ban did not stop He Jiankui; a permit would not have stopped him either — it would license a thousand more.
