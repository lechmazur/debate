[P1.1] Post-conflict societies are profoundly fragile. When a nation emerges from the ashes of civil conflict, its primary task is to construct a shared future. Public memorials are not merely passive stone and bronze; they are active, state-sanctioned projections of national identity. [P1.2] Preserving separate ethnic narratives in public spaces entrenches a zero-sum landscape of competitive victimhood. When memorials are designed to validate mutually exclusive grievances, they reinforce the "us versus them" dynamic. Instead of fostering healing, they become physical flashpoints that perpetuate resentment, reminding citizens of why they fought rather than how they must coexist. This paralyzes the development of a cohesive civic identity, leaving the state permanently vulnerable to renewed conflict.
[P1.3] We see this in divided societies where separate monuments act as silent, provocative borders.
[P1.4] Conversely, a shared civic account of violence does not erase individual suffering; rather, it reframes it. It focuses on the collective tragedy of institutional failure, the universal loss of human life, and a shared commitment to "never again." By highlighting how violence harmed the entire body politic, a civic memorial establishes a common baseline of truth. It shifts the public focus from ethnic blame to shared civic responsibility and the rule of law.
[P1.5] Public space must belong to everyone. If the state funds and elevates separate, hostile historical accounts, it abdicates its role as a guarantor of peace and instead subsidizes division. A shared civic narrative provides the necessary scaffolding for a fragile state to transition from tense coexistence to genuine integration. To build a stable future, post-conflict nations must use their public squares to unite citizens under a common civic banner, rather than carve sectarian animosities into stone.
