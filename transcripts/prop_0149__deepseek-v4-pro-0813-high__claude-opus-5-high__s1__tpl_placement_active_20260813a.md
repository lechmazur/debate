[P1.1] A city that cannot stop a rented house from becoming a weekend nightclub has not chosen tourism over regulation; it has chosen the profits of a few owners over the sleep, safety, and use of everyone nearby. The proposition is not a ban on short-term rentals. It is basic accountability for noise, trash, parking, overcrowding, and repeat disorder. Those rules should apply most forcefully where turnover is highest and local relationships are weakest.
[P1.2] The owner's "tourism depends on flexible hosting" argument treats nuisance as the cost of economic activity. But the costs are not borne by the platform or owner; they are dumped on the block. A family next door cannot opt out of a 2 a.m. party, a flooded street of guest cars, or a rental where the "host" is a remote manager. Nuisance rules exist precisely because such external costs are not voluntary.
[P1.3] If flexibility requires immunity from enforcement, it is asking for a subsidy paid in lost sleep and falling home values.
[P1.4] Strong nuisance enforcement actually protects the legitimate short-term rental market. The biggest political threat to flexible hosting is the predictable backlash after a few notorious party houses: neighbors demand bans, permits are capped, and honest operators lose access. Clear, enforced standards separate responsible stays from rogue properties and make co-existence possible. Without them, tourism's social license collapses.
[P1.5] The tourism argument also confuses volume with quality. A destination that tolerates chaotic party rentals drives away the very visitors who sustain restaurants, shops, and family travel, and it makes the housing crisis worse by converting quiet homes into unregulated commercial venues. Cities can welcome visitors and still say: respect the block, obey occupancy limits, control noise, and face escalating penalties if you do not. That is not hostility to tourism; it is the precondition for a tourism economy that does not consume the city it depends on.
