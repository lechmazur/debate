[P1.1] The Arctic is opening, and the rules are not. Retreating sea ice is exposing shipping shortcuts between Asia, Europe and North America, seabeds rich in oil, gas and minerals, and new military approaches, while governance rests mainly on overlapping claims by a handful of bordering states. That scramble is what a binding treaty modelled on the Antarctic Treaty System is built to solve.
[P1.2] First, it would freeze the most dangerous competition. The Antarctic model did not pretend rival claims did not exist; it set them aside, banned militarisation and nuclear activity, and made science the shared purpose. The Arctic needs the same circuit-breaker. Russia, Canada and Denmark have advanced overlapping continental-shelf claims around the North Pole, the United States and Canada dispute the Northwest Passage, and bases, icebreakers and patrols are multiplying.
[P1.3] Under claim-based governance, every survey, flag-planting gesture or transit becomes a sovereignty test. A treaty suspending new claims, demilitarising the central Arctic Ocean and requiring peaceful use would lower the temperature before an incident becomes a crisis.
[P1.4] Second, the Arctic is too ecologically important to manage as national backyards. Melting permafrost, methane release, black carbon and an oil spill in ice do not respect exclusive economic zones. Yet the Arctic Council is consultative and cannot impose binding shipping, drilling or emissions standards, so national rules fragment where risk is shared. An Antarctic-style environmental protocol could strictly condition or ban hydrocarbon and mineral extraction in the high Arctic, create marine protected areas, mandate spill-response and heavy-fuel standards, and guarantee open scientific monitoring of this fast-warming region.
[P1.5] Third, a treaty gives Indigenous communities across borders and states hit by sea-level rise a seat, rather than leaving a global climate regulator to whoever has coastline. Coastal states keep land sovereignty; they surrender only the right to turn a shared ocean into a competitive grab.
[P1.6] Without binding rules, access will be decided by capability and confrontation. With them, it can stay peaceful, protected and studied.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes ice for emptiness. Antarctica has no permanent population, no settled sovereign jurisdiction, and no nuclear submarines under its ice. The Arctic has millions of residents, Indigenous nations with homeland rights, cities, oil platforms, and the Kola Peninsula nuclear bastion. An Antarctic-style treaty works because it froze a distant scientific commons.
[C1.2] Transplant it to the Arctic and you are asking Russia, the United States, Canada, Norway, and Denmark to suspend sovereignty, demilitarize, and possibly ban extraction in waters central to their economies and defense. That treaty will not be ratified in any form strong enough to matter. And a binding paper that great powers ignore is worse than no paper: it creates false confidence while real competition continues.
[C1.3] Nor is the Arctic a legal vacuum. UNCLOS governs maritime zones and continental-shelf claims; the IMO Polar Code sets binding shipping standards; regional fisheries agreements and bilateral boundary settlements already resolve disputes. The Arctic Council is consultative precisely because states will not hand military and resource decisions to a supranational body. Where governance fails, the cause is enforcement and geopolitical will, not the absence of an Antarctic clone.
[C1.4] A treaty would not make Russia enforce greener shipping or stop testing submarines.
[C1.5] Worse, it would dilute accountability. Sovereign states are answerable to their citizens, courts, and Indigenous rights frameworks. A distant treaty secretariat would turn Inuit and other Arctic peoples into stakeholders at a table dominated by states, while energy and security decisions migrate to vague exceptions and closed meetings. The realistic outcome is not a protected Arctic; it is a treaty full of carve-outs for the very activities the proposition wants banned.
[C1.6] The better path is concrete: use UNCLOS to settle shelf claims, expand targeted agreements on fisheries, shipping, search-and-rescue, and spill response, and manage disputes through existing regional bodies. The Arctic is not Antarctica. It is inhabited, strategic, and already regulated. A binding Antarctic-style treaty is a mirage, and chasing it would weaken the real governance we have.

[P2.1] The core error is treating “similar to” as “identical to.” No one needs to evacuate Arctic cities, erase Indigenous homelands, or pretend the Kola Peninsula is a penguin colony. The model borrowed is legal, not geographic: set aside overlapping ocean claims, restrict military escalation in the shared core, protect the environment by binding rule, and make science open. Land sovereignty and settled jurisdiction stay exactly where they are.
[P2.2] That answers the ratification panic. States are not asked to surrender homeland; they are asked to stop converting the central Arctic Ocean and disputed shelf into a sovereignty contest. Cold War rivals accepted that bargain in Antarctica despite holding claims, because freezing a dispute was cheaper than defending it forever. Difficulty is not impossibility, and it does not vindicate a status quo where agreement is easy because nothing important is required.
[P2.3] UNCLOS tells states how to advance continental-shelf claims; it does not demilitarise the ocean, impose a comprehensive environmental regime, or settle the Northwest Passage dispute. The Polar Code regulates ships that arrive; it does not decide whether rival militaries and drillers should turn melting ice into a competitive frontier. Listing institutions is not governing the scramble they operate around.
[P2.4] The enforcement objection proves too much. Their UNCLOS claims process, fisheries deals and spill agreements also depend on state compliance; if binding law will simply be ignored, incrementalism is equally hollow. A treaty instead creates common red lines, monitoring and inspection expectations, and a diplomatic cost for breach, rather than treating each violation as a bilateral sovereignty affront.
[P2.5] Nor does coastal sovereignty now give circumpolar Indigenous peoples one binding voice across borders, or protect them from decisions made in distant national capitals. A treaty can entrench them as permanent rights-holders. Accountability is diluted now, when power, icebreakers and claim maps decide.
