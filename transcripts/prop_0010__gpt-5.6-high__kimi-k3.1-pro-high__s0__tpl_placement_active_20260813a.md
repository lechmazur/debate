[P1.1] An NDA can convert compensation for a workplace wrong into a privately enforced gag order. That is not ordinary freedom of contract. The employer typically controls the evidence, legal resources, references, and urgently needed settlement money; the worker may be leaving a job while facing medical bills or unemployment. A signature under those conditions does not justify threatening ruinous litigation when the worker later speaks about abuse.
[P1.2] Enforceability also transfers risk from the wrongdoer to future workers. Imagine three employees separately reporting the same manager. If each is paid on condition of silence, every later employee enters without crucial information, each complaint appears isolated, and the employer can treat secrecy as a predictable cost of doing business. Harassment and discrimination often become visible as patterns.
[P1.3] NDAs deliberately fragment that pattern, protecting repeat misconduct rather than legitimate commerce.
[P1.4] The harm extends beyond the signer. Coworkers lose witnesses; regulators and courts lose evidence; boards and shareholders may remain unaware of institutional failure. Even where an NDA formally contains exceptions for legal reporting, the threat of legal fees, repayment demands, and ambiguous wording chills speech. Rights that can be exercised only after hiring counsel to interpret a gag clause are not meaningful rights.
[P1.5] Unenforceability is the right remedy because merely requiring “voluntary” or “clear” consent leaves the same structural coercion intact and forces victims to litigate the gag before they can challenge it. Employers may still protect genuine trade secrets, customer data, and personal information through severable provisions. They may still settle claims, secure releases from liability, deny wrongdoing, and pay for the value of resolving legal exposure. What they should not receive is an enforceable right to suppress facts about harassment, discrimination, or abuse.
[P1.6] A worker may personally choose privacy. But courts should not punish disclosure of workplace mistreatment on an employer’s behalf. Contract law should facilitate exchange, not create a market in concealment where the price is paid by the next victim.
