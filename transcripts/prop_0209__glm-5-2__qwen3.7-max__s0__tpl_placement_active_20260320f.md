[P1.1] The EU's asylum architecture has a structural design flaw: geography dictates responsibility. Under the Dublin framework, the country where an asylum seeker first arrives bears primary responsibility for processing that claim. This means Greece, Italy, and Spain—by virtue of their Mediterranean location—absorb the overwhelming majority of arrivals, while northern and central member states process comparatively few. This is not a policy choice; it is an accident of cartography elevated to legal principle.
[P1.2] The result is predictable and already visible. Frontline states face infrastructure strain, political radicalization, and humanitarian crisis simultaneously. Processing centers overflow. Local populations grow resentful—not of Brussels, primarily, but of migrants and of a Union that seems to have left them holding the bag.
[P1.3] When Greece or Italy threatens to suspend Dublin or to wave arrivals through to northern borders, the entire system buckles. The status quo is not "leaving frontline states to manage most arrivals" in some stable equilibrium. It is a slow unraveling that periodically becomes a crisis.
[P1.4] Burden-sharing quotas directly address this. By distributing recognized asylum seekers across member states according to population, GDP, and capacity, the EU would transform an involuntary geographic burden into a collective fiscal and administrative commitment. This is not charity to frontline states—it is the logical extension of a union that guarantees free movement, common borders, and shared external policy. You cannot have a Schengen area with internal freedom of movement and an external border managed ad hoc by whoever happens to sit on the Mediterranean coast.
[P1.5] The objection will come that quotas are unpopular in certain member states and that mandatory redistribution violates sovereignty. But sovereignty within a union is already constrained by design. Member states accept common trade rules, competition law, and fiscal frameworks. Asylum burden-sharing is no more coercive than any other binding EU obligation—and far less than the coercion embedded in Dublin, which forces a few states to shoulder a continental challenge alone.
[P1.6] The real choice is between managed solidarity and recurring crisis. Quotas choose solidarity.
