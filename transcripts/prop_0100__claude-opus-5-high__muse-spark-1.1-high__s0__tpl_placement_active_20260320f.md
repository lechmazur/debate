[P1.1] The proposition is not "nuclear is evil." It is narrower and stronger: in most countries, a decarbonization plan built around new nuclear is no longer the necessary path, because something cheaper and faster arrived.
[P1.2] Start with what has actually happened. Solar and wind costs collapsed by roughly an order of magnitude over two decades, and global additions are now dominated by them — hundreds of gigawatts a year, mostly built by ordinary construction firms on ordinary schedules. Over the same period, Western nuclear's flagship projects became cautionary tales: Vogtle in Georgia, Flamanville in France, Olkiluoto in Finland, Hinkley Point C in Britain — each years late, each multiples over budget. That is not a smear; it is the sample.
[P1.3] When your reference class is "roughly a decade late and double the cost," you cannot make that the centerpiece of a 2035 or 2040 grid.
[P1.4] The decisive concept is opportunity cost against a carbon clock. Emissions are cumulative. A dollar and a decade spent on a reactor that delivers clean power in 2038 does less climate work than the same dollar spent on wind, solar, and storage delivering in 2027 and displacing coal now. Even if nuclear eventually produces excellent electricity, financing and engineering capacity are finite, and steering them into the slowest option is a real loss measured in tonnes.
[P1.5] Second, "most countries" matters enormously. Most nations have no reactor fleet, no nuclear regulator with real depth, no fuel supply chain, no grid large enough for gigawatt-scale single units, and no appetite for sovereign-scale cost overruns. For Morocco, Vietnam, Chile, Kenya, the practical menu is solar, wind, hydro, transmission, and storage. Telling them nuclear is *central* is telling them to wait.
[P1.6] Third, the firming problem is being solved by other tools: battery storage costs falling fast, continent-scale transmission, demand flexibility, existing hydro, and a shrinking residual role for dispatchable backup.
[P1.7] Keep existing reactors running — that is cheap and sensible. But betting the transition on new ones is no longer necessary, and increasingly it is a delay dressed as prudence.

Solar and wind are adding terawatts, but global emissions keep rising because additions chase demand growth, not replace the fossil stock providing firm power. [C1.2] PRO's cost story cherry-picks first-of-a-kind disasters in the West. Where nuclear is built as a program, the curve looks like solar's: South Korea, Barakah in the UAE delivering four reactors in under a decade, China commissioning reactors on rhythm. System cost matters more than panel cost — overbuild, transmission, and storage for the last 30% dwarfs sticker savings.
[C1.3] The opportunity-cost clock cuts both ways. A solar panel built in 2027 lasts 25 years at 20-30% capacity factor. A reactor started now runs to 2085 at 90%, through winter, night and Dunkelflaute. Batteries handle afternoons, not January.
[C1.4] Germany spent vast sums on renewables and still burns lignite when wind drops; France and Sweden with nuclear have Europe's cleanest grids. Continent-scale lines take longer than reactors to litigate in most democracies.

[C1.6] Central doesn't mean nuclear-only; it means without firm clean power, decarbonization fails and fossil backup is relabeled transition.
