[P1.1] Adolescence is the developmental stage where young people learn to manage risk, make mistakes, and build the internal compass that will guide them when parental oversight inevitably ends. An always-on surveillance regime—location tracking on every movement, monitoring of every private message—does not protect teenagers from danger; it teaches them that safety is something done to them by authority rather than something they learn to navigate themselves. When parents default to tracking and message surveillance, they replace the gradual transfer of responsibility with a system of control that persists until the day the teenager leaves home, leaving them unprepared for the freedom they will suddenly possess.
[P1.2] This approach corrodes the very relationship it claims to protect. Trust is not built through transparency forced by software; it is built through the parent demonstrating confidence in the teen's growing judgment. When a fifteen-year-old discovers their parent reads every text, the message is unmistakable: the parent does not believe them capable of discretion. The inevitable result is not safer teens but more skilled ones—at hiding, at deleting, at routing around the surveillance.
[P1.3] A teenager who fears punishment for a minor mistake will not call home when they are truly in trouble; they will simply ensure the GPS shows them somewhere else. The surveillance creates an adversarial dynamic where the parent sees compliance while the teen practices deception.
[P1.4] The proposition does not deny that specific safety concerns—documented self-harm, credible threats, a history of dangerous behavior—may justify intensive oversight. It insists only that such measures remain exceptional, transparent, and time-limited, not the default posture of the household. The default should be trust, with verification available when warranted. Anything less treats teenagers as suspects in their own homes and undermines the autonomy that adolescence exists to cultivate.

[C1.2] Teenagers deserve respect, but they are not yet fully equipped for adult risk management. Always-on location tracking gives a family a simple answer to ordinary emergencies: where you are, whether you are moving, and whether help is needed.
[C1.3] Message monitoring gives parents a chance to notice patterns—coercion, despair, predatory adults, illegal sales—that a teenager may hide out of fear, embarrassment, or simply not recognize as dangerous.

[C1.5] The proposition’s alternative asks parents to remain ignorant by default and then scramble after a crisis. But once a teenager is missing, exploited, or in danger, autonomy has no value. Safety comes first; privacy is a privilege that grows with judgment.
