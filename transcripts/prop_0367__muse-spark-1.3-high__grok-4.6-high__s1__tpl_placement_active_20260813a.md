[P1.1] Cable did not collapse by accident. It collapsed because households paid for two hundred channels to watch eight, deciphered broadcast fees and box rentals buried in fine print, and needed a phone call and a retention script to leave. When streaming bundles copy that lock-in and opacity, they import the exact resentment that drove cord-cutting in the first place.
[P1.2] Viewing is not constant, so billing should not pretend it is. A fan needs live sports for four months, not twelve. A family wants kids’ movies in summer, prestige drama when new seasons drop, a documentary service for a school project. Monthly pause, split, and reconfigure options simply align payment with use.
[P1.3] That is not fickleness; it is efficiency. Money stops subsidizing unwatched libraries and starts rewarding what people actually watch.
[P1.4] That discipline makes the market healthier. Services terrified of an easy exit must compete the honest way: better shows, better interfaces, clearer prices. Lock-in does the opposite. It lets a bundle coast on inertia, raise prices quietly, and bury the true cost across joint bills where no one can tell what anything costs.
[P1.5] Transparency is a competitive weapon for good providers, not a burden.
[P1.6] Flexibility also keeps customers closer than coercion does. A pause button is a promise to return; a cancellation maze is a reason never to come back. Someone who can drop from three services to one during a tight month stays in the ecosystem and resubscribes when the next season arrives. Someone forced to pay year-round for thin content learns to pirate, password-share, or quit entirely.
[P1.7] The core question is who controls the household budget. The old answer was the package designer. The new answer should be the viewer. Let people build the bundle they need this month, change it next month, and see exactly what they pay for.
[P1.8] That is how streaming keeps its founding promise instead of becoming cable with faster internet.

[C1.1] Cable died from a bad product and coercive contracts, not from packages that last more than thirty days. Treating every stable streaming bundle as a rerun of that failure is a category error. Making pause, split, and monthly reconfigure the default would make the product worse, not freer.
[C1.2] Content is lumpy and expensive. Leagues sell seasons. Studios greenlight series and film slates years before anyone hits play. Those bets are repaid by a subscriber base that stays through ordinary months, not just football, summer kids' movies, or premiere week.
[C1.3] If households can frictionlessly pause, split, and rebuild every month, they will do the rational thing: buy the peak, drop the trough, and return for the next drop. Remaining customers then finance everyone else's highlight reel. That is not efficiency. It is adverse selection.
[C1.4] PRO says money should stop subsidizing unwatched libraries. Unwatched months are how watched months get made. Prices for those who remain rise, catalogs thin, ads creep in, and the promised "honest competition" of better shows never arrives because the revenue to make them became optional.
[C1.5] The opacity charge misses. A published annual discount is not a buried broadcast fee and a truck roll. Streaming already cancels in a browser. Commitment is an open trade: a lower price for a more stable relationship.
[C1.6] Force every bundle into monthly atomization and the discount dies. Tourists get a cheap month; stayers pay the churn tax.
[C1.7] A pause is not a promise to return. It trains people to treat the service as a pop-up. Families do not want to rebuild a media stack twelve times a year, and writers' rooms cannot be staffed on pop-up cash. Viewer control of this month's bill is a hollow victory if next year's library is the casualty.
[C1.8] Streaming's promise was a better on-demand library, not a vending machine for four hot weeks.

The motion says bundles should be easier to pause, split, or reconfigure monthly — not that every customer must rebuild twelve times a year. If an annual discount is truly, as claimed, 'an open trade,' then let it compete beside a clear monthly option.
[P2.2] Lock-in only looks efficient when the flexible alternative is hidden, bundled with a phone bill, or punished with a maze.
[P2.3] Content is lumpy, yes, but that is an argument for honest seasonal pricing, not for taxing the inattentive. Leagues already sell seasons; studios already survive box-office summers and dry winters. To say 'unwatched months are how watched months get made' is just cable's old cross-subsidy in new clothes: make the quiet majority overpay so the peak can look cheap. When that quiet majority notices, they do not thank you for funding prestige drama.
[P2.4] They cut the cord — again.
[P2.5] The so-called churn tax gets causality backwards. Tourists already buy the peak and vanish; friction does not stop them, it just ensures they never return. A student who can drop from three services to one during exams stays addressable; a family forced to pay year-round for four good weeks learns to rotate passwords, pirate, or quit for good. A pause is not a pop-up mentality.
[P2.6] It is a low-cost way to stay in the relationship instead of filing for divorce.
[P2.7] And families do not want homework. That is precisely why reconfiguration must be easy: one toggle, saved profiles, visible per-service prices — not twelve customer-service chats. The choice is not between a rich library and a vending machine. It is between a library people willingly sustain because they control the bill, and one they abandon because the bill controls them.
