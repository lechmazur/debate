[P1.1] Every gallon a water-stressed city flushes away is a gallon it already paid to capture, clean, and transport. Direct potable reuse turns that waste stream into a local supply; importing water simply buys the same scarcity problem at a higher price, from someone else's backyard.
[P1.2] Start with energy and cost. Imported water in places like Southern California or Arizona is lifted over mountains and pumped hundreds of kilometers. Pumping water uphill is one of the most energy-intensive things a city can do, and the energy bill compounds every year as groundwater tables drop and pumping distances grow. Direct potable reuse, by contrast, treats water the city already holds at the treatment plant next door.
[P1.3] The physics of proximity is decisive: moving water costs more than cleaning it once you have modern membranes, reverse osmosis, and advanced oxidation.
[P1.4] Second, independence. Imported water makes a city a supplicant to distant basins, agricultural districts, and neighboring states with their own droughts and their own politics. Every interstate water dispute from the Colorado River to the Sacramento Delta shows how fragile that dependence is. Reuse keeps the supply inside the city's own control.
[P1.5] When the next drought comes, the river allocation shrinks—but the sewage keeps flowing regardless of rainfall. That is the only truly drought-proof source a city can own.
[P1.6] Third, environmental justice. Importing water exports harm: depleted rivers, damaged deltas, and collapsing ecosystems in the donor basins, usually rural communities with less political power. Reuse internalizes the cost instead of externalizing it onto ecosystems and neighbors.
[P1.7] Fourth, safety. The technology is proven. Windhoek has drunk recycled sewage for decades, and Texas's Wichita Falls ran direct potable reuse through a severe drought without incident. Modern multi-barrier treatment routinely produces water cleaner than many surface sources.
[P1.8] The honest tradeoff is public perception and the "yuck factor." But perception yields to transparent monitoring and good communication, while the costs of dependence—energy, money, ecology, leverage—only compound. Cities that wait buy yesterday's answer at tomorrow's price.

[C1.1] The motion is a ranking, and that is where PRO’s case collapses. Showing that direct potable reuse can work is not showing it should be every water-stressed city’s first dollar. In many cities, imported water arrives through existing gravity-fed aqueducts and reservoirs; DPR requires a new advanced treatment train, continuous monitoring, brine disposal, and redundant fail-safes. PRO’s “physics of proximity” [P1.3] ignores that the expensive part of DPR is not moving water—it is cleaning it to potable standards every hour of every day, then managing the concentrate.
[C1.2] DPR also has a hidden capacity ceiling: it can only recycle the wastewater a city produces. When drought conservation succeeds, sewage flows fall—precisely when supply is scarcest. PRO says the sewage keeps flowing regardless of rainfall [P1.5], but it flows less, and it depends on the same electric grid and treatment workforce. Imported water can be stored and banked in reservoirs across wet years; DPR is just-in-time.
[C1.3] A membrane failure, power outage, or contamination detection can knock out a large block of supply with no environmental buffer. That is not resilience; it is a single point of failure dressed as independence.
[C1.4] PRO’s safety examples [P1.7] prove feasibility, not priority. Windhoek is a small, exceptional system; Wichita Falls used DPR as an emergency drought measure. Neither shows that routine DPR should outrank imports. The “yuck factor” is not mere irrationality: direct potable reuse demands extraordinary public confidence and real-time verification because failure is severe.
[C1.5] Imported water often has natural storage and dilution, and can be blended.
[C1.6] Environmental justice cuts both ways. Imports can damage donor basins—but DPR is energy-intensive, produces brine, and can concentrate contaminants. The responsible sequence is fix leaks, expand nonpotable reuse, protect local sources, then compare imports and DPR on cost, reliability, and ecological footprint. The proposition replaces that comparison with a blanket mandate.
[C1.7] Cities need least-cost resilience, not a compulsory preference for the plant next door.
