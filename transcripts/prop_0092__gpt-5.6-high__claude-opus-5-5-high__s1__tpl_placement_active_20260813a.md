[P1.1] City streets are shared, scarce public space. The fairest rule is simple: when one vehicle predictably imposes greater costs on everyone else, its owner should pay more rather than receiving a subsidy from drivers of smaller cars, transit riders, cyclists, and pedestrians. Flat fees treat unequal burdens as equal.
[P1.2] Oversized SUVs and pickups create higher safety costs through basic physics and geometry. Greater mass transfers more force in a collision. Taller, blunter front ends are more dangerous to people outside the vehicle, while higher hoods and larger bodies worsen close-range visibility—especially around children. Their width also leaves less margin on narrow streets and can obscure sightlines near crossings.
[P1.3] Those risks are not fully borne by the purchaser; they fall on strangers and on publicly funded emergency and health systems.
[P1.4] They also consume more of what cities manage. A large vehicle occupies more curb length and width, makes neighboring spaces harder to use, and can force cities to provide larger bays or accommodate reduced roadway clearance. Heavier vehicles generally impose more wear than lighter ones. Charging identical parking or registration fees despite these differences effectively lets the higher-cost user shift part of the bill to everyone else.
[P1.5] The policy can be precise rather than punitive: graduated charges based on objective weight and dimensions, applied only where those characteristics generate added costs. Cities can exempt emergency and disability-adapted vehicles, distinguish genuine commercial use where justified, and provide targeted relief for low-income households. Those refinements answer hardship far better than preserving a blanket subsidy, including for affluent owners who simply prefer ever-larger vehicles.
[P1.6] Fees also improve incentives without banning anything. Buyers remain free to choose a large pickup, but manufacturers and consumers gain reason to favor safer dimensions and right-sized vehicles. Even where behavior does not change, the city gains revenue to manage curbs, repair streets, and protect vulnerable road users.
[P1.7] The choice is not freedom versus prohibition. It is whether the public must absorb private size costs. Those who impose more should pay more.
