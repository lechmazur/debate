[P1.1] The flow of capital into an adversary’s most sensitive industries is not ordinary commerce. It is a transfer of leverage. When democratic countries allow firms, funds, and engineers to pour money and know-how into the semiconductor and AI sectors of authoritarian rivals, they help those regimes close the gap in the technologies that will decide military advantage, economic coercion, and political control for a generation.
[P1.2] Semiconductors and frontier AI are not widgets. Advanced chips power missiles, drones, and signals intelligence. Large models can automate surveillance, cyber operations, and weapons design. Authoritarian states fuse commercial AI with state power.
[P1.3] An investment that looks like a venture bet on a chip designer or model lab can become a production line serving a military or internal-security apparatus. Screening simply asks whether that outcome is acceptable before the money moves.
[P1.4] Export controls already try to stop the shipment of the most dangerous tools. They fail if capital, talent, and process knowledge can still cross the border and build those tools on the other side. Screening closes that gap without banning trade or ordinary portfolio investment. It targets a narrow set of sectors and a defined class of rivals, reviews transactions for national-security risk, and blocks or conditions only those that would materially strengthen an adversary’s strategic capabilities.
[P1.5] Governments already apply the same logic to inbound investment and arms exports.
[P1.6] The costs are real but bounded: some deals delayed, capital redirected. The downside of inaction is not. Financing a rival’s catch-up in the domains where lead times are shortest and military payoff highest is self-defeating. Countries that intend to preserve a free and secure order should not subsidize the erosion of their own advantage.
[P1.7] Screening outbound investment into these sectors is the minimum discipline required to stop doing so.
