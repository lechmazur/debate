[C1.2] Start with the core flaw: overcrowding harms shift. One year the binding constraint is sewage overflow in the old town; the next it is wildfire smoke, a heat dome killing elderly residents, a port strike, a flood, a public health emergency. A statute that says "primarily housing, transit, sanitation" tells a mayor she cannot move tourist-tax money to cooling centers, hospitals, coastal defense, cultural heritage preservation, or police staffing in nightlife districts — even when those are the actual tourism externalities that year.
[C1.3] Rigid earmarks optimize for yesterday's crisis.
[C1.4] Second, earmarking does not create new money; it just relabels it. This is the well-documented fungibility problem. If tourist tax revenue is locked to transit, the council quietly cuts the general-fund transit allocation by the same amount and spends it elsewhere. Residents get no net gain in buses or housing — they get an accounting illusion while losing budgetary transparency.
[C1.5] The general budget, by contrast, forces every dollar to compete on visible priorities through the democratic process.
[C1.6] Third, PRO's own Barcelona story cuts against them. Cities that genuinely prioritize residents do so through elected councils and budget votes. Cities that don't will simply route the "marketing" spending through a different revenue stream.
[C1.7] Earmarks bind honest governments and barely inconvenience captured ones.
[C1.8] Fourth, "primarily" three categories ignores that the worst overtourism damage is often elsewhere: heritage sites crumbling in Venice, emergency services in Dubrovnik, environmental remediation in Amsterdam's canals. PRO's list is parochial.
[C1.9] The decisive comparison: PRO offers symbolic rigidity that fungibility neutralizes. CON offers democratic flexibility that lets residents — through their elected government — actually match spending to the harm of the moment.
