[P1.1] Resource-rich developing countries face a fundamental choice: spend commodity windfalls today or invest them for tomorrow. Sovereign wealth funds are the superior mechanism because they break the boom-bust cycle that has crippled so many economies. When oil prices spike, direct cash distributions flood households with money they cannot productively absorb. In Nigeria, ad-hoc handouts during the 2008 oil boom fueled inflation, distorted local markets, and left no lasting capital when prices crashed.
[P1.2] A sovereign fund, by contrast, smooths revenues across generations, turning volatile commodity income into stable financial returns. Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global—built on oil revenues—now holds over $1.4 trillion, generating annual returns that fund public services without depleting the principal. That is intergenerational equity in action: today’s citizens benefit from yesterday’s windfalls, and tomorrow’s citizens inherit a productive asset rather than a depleted resource.
[P1.3] Cash distributions also fail the test of macroeconomic stability. When Angola tried direct payments in 2014, the sudden liquidity surge overheated the economy, pushing inflation to 40% and forcing the central bank into emergency measures. Sovereign funds act as automatic stabilizers: during booms, they sterilize excess liquidity by investing abroad, preventing currency appreciation and Dutch disease. During busts, they provide counter-cyclical spending.
[P1.4] Botswana’s Pula Fund, financed by diamond revenues, has allowed the government to maintain critical infrastructure and social programs even when diamond prices plummet, shielding the economy from the volatility that has bankrupted neighbors like Zimbabwe.
[P1.5] Finally, cash distributions trap countries in a low-skill, low-productivity equilibrium. Handouts encourage consumption over investment, reinforcing dependence on raw commodity exports rather than diversifying the economy. Chile’s Copper Stabilization Fund, by contrast, has financed innovation, education, and infrastructure, helping the country transition from a copper-dependent economy to a regional leader in technology and renewable energy. The fund’s returns are reinvested in human and physical capital, creating jobs that outlast the commodity cycle.
[P1.6] Direct cash transfers may buy short-term political popularity, but they doom countries to repeat the resource curse. Sovereign wealth funds offer a disciplined, long-term alternative that turns windfalls into engines of sustainable development.

[C1.2] The wealth belonged to the people, yet never reached them.
[C1.3] Direct cash distributions work precisely because they do not depend on perfect institutions. When citizens receive resource rents directly, they become stakeholders who demand accountable governance—they treat the wealth as their own and hold officials responsible for public services.

[C1.5] The Norway and Botswana examples are outliers. Norway was already developed and institutionally strong before its fund existed. [C1.6] It respects citizen autonomy, meets urgent needs now, and builds democracy from the ground up. Deferring wealth to a future generation while current citizens suffer is not prudence; it is a deferral of justice.
