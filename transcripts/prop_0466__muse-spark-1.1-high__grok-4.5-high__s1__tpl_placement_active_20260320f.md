[P1.1] For coastal fishermen, a no-take reserve sounds like theft: walling off the sea. Biology shows the opposite. Close a reef properly and fish get bigger, live longer, and produce exponentially more eggs. That surplus doesn't stay inside the line.
[P1.2] Adults spill over the boundary, larvae drift for miles on currents. You catch more right outside the reserve than you ever caught inside it before.
[P1.3] The alternative is not steady income. It is managed collapse. A heavily fished zone with no refuge selects for smaller, younger fish that reproduce less. Catches decline, boats go further, burn more fuel, catch less.
[P1.4] Families take on debt. Governments subsidize fuel to keep them afloat. This is what dependency looks like under open access — not prosperity, but a slow grind toward zero. Small reserves scattered nearshore cannot fix that.
[P1.5] Only large, networked no-take areas keep enough breeding stock intact to reverse the cycle.
[P1.6] Thirty percent is not arbitrary punishment. It is the minimum scale at which this recovery works. With 30% closed, 70% remains open — a huge fishing ground made more productive by the closed portion. Evidence from places like Mexico's Cabo Pulmo or the Philippines' community reserves is consistent: after a few years of protection, total biomass multiplies severalfold and catches around the border rise and stabilize.
[P1.7] That's income insurance. When a cyclone hits or warm water bleaches a reef, the protected broodstock survives to restock fished areas. Without that buffer, one bad year wipes a community out.
[P1.8] Yes, closing grounds hurts tomorrow morning. But keeping everything open guarantees coastal people have nothing the year after next. If we care about fishing income, we cannot defend a model that eats its own seed corn. Large no-take reserves are not charity for fish.
[P1.9] They are a hard investment by fishing nations in their own future food security, jobs, and bargaining power. Accepting 30% is not sacrificing communities. It is refusing to sacrifice them.

A handful of small, community-driven sites do not prove the case for nationwide sacrifice at this scale. One lean season without income can empty a village of its next generation of fishers long before any “biomass boom” arrives.
[C1.5] Fishing nations should refuse the proposition because it confuses conservation with exclusion. Protect the resource by keeping viable livelihoods attached to it, not by walling off a third of sovereign waters and hoping communities absorb the blow.
