[P1.1] The case for this proposition is not cultural uniformity; it is social reciprocity. Settling in a country is not merely renting a home or using its schools, courts, roads, and labor market. It is entering a shared civic order. That order functions when newcomers can participate and institutions can communicate with everyone in a language they can reasonably be expected to understand.
[P1.2] Expectation matters because integration is cumulative. If a child cannot follow instruction in the common public language, the educational bargain weakens. If neighbors and officials must rely on ad hoc translators, emergency help, workplace coordination, and political deliberation become slower and more expensive. A common public language is a practical bridge, not a demand that immigrants abandon private culture.
[P1.3] The same logic applies to core civic norms: obey the law, accept equal protection, contribute to the community, and reject discrimination. These are not merely “our” traditions; they are publicly stated rules that make diverse people safe and able to cooperate. Asking newcomers to respect them respects their equal membership rather than granting them a permanent exemption from membership.
[P1.4] This proposition answers a risk: social fragmentation mistaken for diversity. A society may permit cultures, religious practices, and languages in homes and voluntary associations, but it cannot remain without a shared means of public understanding and a baseline of enforceable civic conduct. The choice is not assimilation or permanent separation. It is integration with room for cultural variety.
[P1.5] Government should provide language instruction and fair help toward learning, just as civic education helps citizens understand the rules. But assistance is most effective when paired with a clear expectation: learn enough of the common public language to participate, and accept the core norms on which equal membership rests. This is neither punitive nor coercive; it is a condition of functioning together.

[C1.3] Second, watch the bundling. "Obey the law, accept equal protection, reject discrimination" are already binding on everyone, native-born included. [C1.4] Strip the bundle and the motion is much smaller than it sounds.

[C1.7] That is the state's side of reciprocity, and it does not expire because a patient's grammar is poor. Optional does not mean refused. It means the state earns participation instead of conditioning it.

[P5.1] The decisive issue is whether membership can remain one-sided. The state can provide classes and exemptions, but if newcomers may use them indefinitely without acquiring the means to participate, there is no reciprocal settlement bargain. What “optional integration” lacks is a clear duty to move toward participation, with proportionate consequences only after opportunity and accommodation.
[P5.2] The opponent calls deferred permanent status a “probationary life,” yet [P4.1] permits instruction, essential-service exemptions, and longer timelines. A decision to learn cannot immediately become lost family reunion or precarious work. The baseline should be functional competence, not perfect fluency: a nurse aide who can understand instructions and speak reliably to patients needs no translator for every ordinary interaction.
[P5.3] Adaptation is the bridge, not the destination. Interpretation addresses temporary inability; shared language creates durable independence. Geneva and Canada confirm this: multilingual accommodation can work, but newcomers still have a practical direction toward the public language rather than indefinite institutional dependency.
[P5.4] Conditionality adds responsibility. Shared institutions do not merely receive newcomers; those choosing settlement are expected to become capable participants, supported where ability is limited and held accountable where refusal is deliberate. That is not coercive assimilation. It makes community more than a state service extended to permanent outsiders.
