[P1.1] Local journalism is dying not because people stopped needing it, but because its business model did. Advertising moved to platforms, hedge-fund owners bought papers to strip them, and communities lost the reporter in the council chamber. That collapse is not neutral. It is making politics more corrupt and more polarized — and firewalled public funding reverses both more than it risks capture.
[P1.2] First, corruption thrives in darkness. A mayor steering a contract, a sheriff wasting funds, a developer buying a zoning change rarely make national news. They are stopped, if at all, by a local journalist filing records requests and showing up week after week. When that job disappears, officials learn no one is watching, bids grow less competitive, and voters learn of scandals after the money is gone.
[P1.3] A stable grant allocated by formula, not by politicians picking favorites, pays for the unglamorous watchdog work the market no longer sustains.
[P1.4] Second, polarization fills a vacuum. Without trusted local reporting, residents do not become less political; they become nationalized. Their diet shifts to cable outrage and social feeds that sort by identity. Local news does the opposite: it forces Democrats and Republicans to argue from the same facts about the same school, road, and tax bill.
[P1.5] It rewards problem-solving over team loyalty. Restoring it rebuilds a shared civic floor beneath national disagreement.
[P1.6] The objection is state capture, which is why the firewall matters. Funding set in multi-year cycles, distributed by an independent body under a statutory ban on editorial direction, with transparent criteria and mixed revenue, is not a ministry of information. It is insulation — more insulation, arguably, than today, when a partisan owner or single advertiser can kill a story quietly.
[P1.7] The choice is not independent journalism versus funded journalism. It is a vanishing press captured by collapse and national outrage, or a deliberately insulated local press strong enough to watch power and hold a community together.

[C1.1] The choice in [P1.7] is false. The alternative to a struggling local press is not a state-insulated press; it is a press that exchanges dependence on hedge funds and platforms for dependence on the government. The firewall sounds structural, but it is built from ordinary politics. Legislatures write the formula, politicians and governors appoint the board, auditors can investigate, and appropriations must be renewed.
[C1.2] The proposal calls this "not a ministry of information" [P1.6]; the danger is subtler and more likely.
[C1.3] Capture does not require a minister dictating headlines. It requires a small newsroom whose survival depends on money controlled by the subjects it covers. Anticipatory obedience does the work: editors learn which investigations risk a hearing, which council allies are "partners," and which watchdog stories look ungrateful at renewal time. We have seen formally independent public media bend without a single explicit order.
[C1.4] Hungary shows the pattern: formally independent public media bent once a governing coalition controlled appointments and budgets.
[C1.5] Even if some watchdog work continues, the gain is modest and diffuse. Corruption is checked primarily by auditors, inspectors, procurement rules, and prosecutors, not only by a reporter with records requests [P1.2]. Polarization is driven by national media, primaries, and algorithmic feeds; a subsidized local site with modest reach will not outcompete outrage. Local coverage can even narrow the public to engaged homeowners rather than build a shared floor [P1.5].
[C1.6] Worse, funding turns the press into a recurring prize. Once money is on the table, parties fight over what counts as local, civic, and nonprofit. Statutory bans can be amended; boards can be packed. Every disputed story becomes a budget fight.
[C1.7] That mechanism increases partisan capture and propaganda risk far more than the speculative reduction in polarization and corruption. The real test is not the first grant, but the first scandal the grantee wants to publish and the government wants buried; a firewall written today is rewritten after the next election.
