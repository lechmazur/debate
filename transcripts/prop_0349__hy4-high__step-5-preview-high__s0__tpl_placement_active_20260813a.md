[P1.1] For a patient who has already failed repeated medications and psychotherapy, “wait for more trials” is not a neutral safeguard. It is a policy that assigns that patient more months of illness, more crisis care, more disability, and sometimes more suicide risk. Severe treatment-resistant conditions change the ethical arithmetic: when standard options have failed, the danger of denying a plausible supervised intervention belongs on the same scale as the danger of providing it.
[P1.2] Governments should permit psychedelic-assisted therapy beyond narrow clinical-trial settings, but only as medicine: documented treatment resistance, qualified diagnosis, informed consent, psychiatric and medical screening, exclusion of psychosis or mania risk, on-site dosing, no take-home supply, continuous monitoring, emergency protocols, integration follow-up, mandatory adverse-event reporting, and outcome registries. This is not recreational legalization or wellness retreats. It is a tightly licensed service built around preparation, supervised dosing, and therapeutic support.
[P1.3] The trial-only objection sounds cautious but fails the patients least able to wait. Trials are designed to be narrow: they often exclude complex comorbidities, acute suicidality, unstable patients, and people who cannot travel repeatedly to research sites—the very people most likely to be treatment-resistant in ordinary practice. A permanent research bottleneck also deprives clinicians of broader safety data. Regulated access with outcome tracking creates continuing evidence from real patients instead of leaving practice frozen or underground.
[P1.4] The alternative to permission is not zero risk. It is black-market dosing, untrained facilitators, delayed care, and desperate self-experimentation by people already failed by the system. Psychedelics can cause panic, destabilization, or medical events; that is an argument for screening, monitoring, and accountability, not for pushing the whole practice into an evidence-poor shadow market. The question is not whether psychedelics are harmless.
[P1.5] It is whether governments should keep a high-need population trapped between indefinite trial eligibility and no legal care when supervised access can be narrower, safer, and more humane than the status quo.

[C1.1] The motion does not ask whether psychedelic-assisted therapy might one day help. It asks whether governments should permit it now, outside trials, for the most unstable patients in medicine. Those are very different questions, and the gap between them is where this debate is decided.
[C1.2] "Carefully regulated" is doing enormous work in the proposition. It assumes a regulatory capacity we do not have. Trials concentrate expertise, protocol fidelity, and adverse-event capture in a small number of sites. Once access expands to clinics, the state must verify therapist training, screen for hidden contraindications, and police off-label drift across a diffuse network.
[C1.3] Psychedelic-assisted therapy is not a pill with a fixed dose and a black-box warning. It is a psychotherapeutic relationship chemically amplified, and quality varies wildly by practitioner. Outside trials, there is no reliable way to separate a competent integration session from a merely charismatic one.
[C1.4] Severe treatment-resistant conditions make this worse, not better. These patients arrive with suicidality, complex comorbidity, polypharmacy, and psychosocial chaos. PRO's own safeguards—excluding psychosis risk, requiring continuous monitoring, banning take-home supply—are not proof of safety. They are admissions that the intervention is dangerous enough to need armed guards.
[C1.5] But you cannot screen instability out of a population whose defining feature is instability. A registry documents the disaster after it happens; it does not stop the panic attack at minute ninety.
[C1.6] Nor is the alternative abandonment. Trial-only access exists alongside crisis care, inpatient observation, and other legal interventions. The motion would fracture that system with a parallel marketplace, justified by the sickest patients, but paid for by them. Permission is easy.
[C1.7] Careful is a promise, not a policy.

[P2.1] The objection treats trial walls as the only possible form of control. They are not. Government can regulate the episode: licensed center, documented refractory diagnosis, screening, credentialed team, supervised on-site dosing, no patient dispensing, emergency protocol, follow-up, inspection, and mandatory outcome reports. “Careful is a promise, not a policy” [C1.7] lands only if CON assumes licensing rules cannot be specified or enforced. Safeguards are what high-risk medicine looks like, not evidence it must remain experimental; governments already regulate anesthesia, ECT, and controlled opioids through credentials, setting, documentation, and adverse-event duties.
[P2.2] The second mistake is to collapse every safeguard into the weakest one. [C1.5] says a registry “does not stop the panic attack at minute ninety.” No: screening is not pretending instability is absent; it is selecting level of care. Trained staff in the room, the dosing protocol, and emergency response stop panic from becoming an abandoned crisis. The registry does different work: exposing bad providers, refining contraindications, and producing real-world evidence trial exclusions cannot supply.
[P2.3] CON removes the clinician from the supervised session, then complains the room is empty.
[P2.4] Practitioner variability also does not rescue trial-only access. If therapy is a “psychotherapeutic relationship chemically amplified” [C1.3], that is why it needs accountable, trained, inspectable clinicians rather than underground charisma. Trials can have charismatic therapists too; outside trials, licensure and outcome review make competence a condition of practice. Trial-only refusal leaves competence to the shadow market.
[P2.5] Finally, “not abandonment” [C1.6] lowers the bar to containment. Crisis beds, observation, and another failed medication cycle manage deterioration without treating the refractory condition producing it. Trial-only access is exclusion by eligibility plus delay; being too unstable for a trial is not being protected by one. The choice is not perfect regulation versus risk-free waiting; it is audited, supervised care with revocable licenses and reportable harms versus unregulated demand at a closed legal door.
[P2.6] Licensed care is policy; blanket refusal is the promise.

[P4.1] A1: Verification does not mean certifying rapport; it means making practitioner judgment observable and bounded. This is not anesthesia: the regulated object is the judgment-heavy episode, not a milligram. Licensed clinics can require trained teams, preparation/dosing/integration protocols, consented boundaries, no solo or take-home dosing, continuous monitoring, stop authority, records, inspection, and complaint review. Those are prospective controls: no monitor, no dosing; deviations trigger discipline.
[P4.2] Trials do not abolish suggestibility; they constrain conduct. Licensing can carry that constraint into practice and forbid the private room where charisma becomes power.
[P4.3] A2: The eligible patients are people with chronic refractory depression, PTSD, or similar severe illness after adequate standard care, not currently psychotic, manic, or imminently suicidal: people with past crises, disability, stable medications, or complex comorbidity too messy for many trial criteria. Excluding active mania or imminent suicide is clinical triage, not trial eligibility as the only legal doorway [P2.5]. Licensed care can reassess after stabilization; a trial slot is fixed around washouts, travel, scarce sites, and narrow samples.
[P4.4] That double standard decides the clash. CON praises “predefined exclusions and stopping rules” [C2.8] in trials, then calls analogous screening fatal [C2.6]. It dismisses credentialing as mind-reading, registries as retrospective [C2.3], and revocation as too late for “the first patient” [C2.4]—while ignoring the controls protecting that patient. Each safeguard is rejected only by isolating it.
[P4.5] The comparison is not clairvoyance versus safety. It is permanent trial-only delay—no ordinary-practice evidence for patients trials least resemble—against a narrow licensed pathway with protocol fidelity, stopping rules, adverse-event duties, inspection, and outcome review. Those tools are not confined to randomized trials; licensing gives them force. The answer to commercial incentives is reporting, not a black market.
[P4.6] The proposition wins by promising neither zero risk nor universal access. It replaces a closed legal door with an audited one for patients losing time, function, and safety. Absolute caution makes practice-based evidence impossible: practice is never permitted.
