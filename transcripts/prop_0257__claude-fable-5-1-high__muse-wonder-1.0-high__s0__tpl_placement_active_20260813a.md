The result is platforms exiting markets, catalogues shrinking, and every purchase priced like insurance against eternity.
[C1.3] Transferability is even more destructive. A resold paperback is worn, local, and one-for-one. A resold digital file is perfect, instant, costless to copy, and global. [C1.4] Physical resale was tolerable because scarcity and decay preserved some incentive to buy new. Remove scarcity, and you remove the economics behind cheap pricing, deep discounts, bundles, and vast back catalogues.
[C1.5] Revocability also serves legitimate functions ownership would block. Platforms must remove infringing uploads, patch security flaws, comply with expiring upstream rights, and stop software that no longer runs safely. That Amazon Orwell case involved copies sold without proper rights; a seller unable to withdraw them would be forced to perpetuate a violation. Consumers benefit from this model's flexibility too: cloud libraries, re-downloads across devices, automatic updates, family sharing, and refunds are service features, not incidents of owning a file on a dead hard drive.
[C1.6] If "buy" misleads, require clearer disclosure, notice before shutdown, and refunds — proportionate fixes. Converting every purchase into perpetual, transferable property is a cure that kills the market it claims to protect.
