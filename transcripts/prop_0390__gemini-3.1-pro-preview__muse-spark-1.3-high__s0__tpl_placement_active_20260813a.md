[P1.1] The gaming industry currently treats serious cheating and severe harassment like localized weather events rather than systemic threats. When a player is permanently banned for deploying kernel-level cheats or engaging in sustained, vicious harassment in one game, they simply migrate to another platform. This fragmented approach fails communities. We must implement cross-ecosystem bans for serious offenders because protecting the vast majority of players is more important than preserving a frictionless revolving door for toxic actors.
[P1.2] First, cross-ecosystem bans provide the only meaningful deterrence. To a dedicated hacker or serial troll, a single-game ban is merely a minor inconvenience—a temporary roadblock easily bypassed by switching titles or creating a new free-to-play account. However, if the penalty for severe misconduct threatens a player’s access across major ecosystems like Xbox, Steam, or PlayStation, the cost of ruining games suddenly outweighs the thrill. By raising the stakes, we fundamentally alter the behavioral calculus of bad actors.
[P1.3] Second, communities deserve proactive protection. Currently, every game ecosystem must independently suffer a toxic player's abuse before taking action. Innocent players are forced to act as the industry’s unpaid moderators, enduring hate speech, doxxing, or game-ruining exploits until a new ban is finally issued. A shared ban architecture acts as a necessary quarantine, stopping known abusers before they can victimize a new community.
[P1.4] Finally, we must embrace the due-process trade-off. Critics will argue that cross-platform bans risk punishing false positives too harshly. But participating in online multiplayer ecosystems is a conditional service, not a fundamental human right. While basic appeal mechanisms should exist, the theoretical risk of a false positive cannot paralyze our response to undeniable, documented abuse.
[P1.5] For serious violations, the burden of safety must shift. We must prioritize the well-being and fair play of the 99% of users who abide by the rules over the absolute due process of the 1% who systematically destroy the experience for everyone else.
