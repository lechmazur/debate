[P1.1] Theatrical-first as the default is a gatekeeping habit, not a law of cinema. For almost every big title, studios still make the first legitimate viewing a trip to a building many people cannot enter, afford, or endure. That is a choice about who counts as the audience.
[P1.2] A theater is a terrible access device for a huge share of the public. Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers get one captioned screening if they are lucky. Blind and low-vision viewers rarely get live audio description. Autistic people, people with chronic illness, mobility limits, sensory overload, or caregiving duties cannot sit still in a crowded dark room they do not control.
[P1.3] Rural viewers, immunocompromised people, and parents of young children are not “lesser fans.” They are simply outside the ritual. Waiting months does not make the wait fair. It just withholds the work.
[P1.4] Home, done properly, is not a consolation prize. Accurate captions, audio description, pause, replay, volume control, adjustable contrast, and a quiet space often make the film more watchable than a multiplex ever will. The motion is not “kill theaters.” It is that exclusive windows should stop being the automatic first-class ticket for nearly every tentpole. Some films can still open big on a screen.
[P1.5] What should end is the reflex that prestige requires exclusion.
[P1.6] A movie is not more cinematic because a disabled person cannot see it for ninety days. Prestige that depends on rationing culture by body, schedule, and zip code is cheap prestige. Day-and-date release with robust home access treats cinema as a public art form instead of a pilgrimage. Studios already know people will pay to watch at home.
[P1.7] The remaining fight is whether that watch is first-class or an afterthought. First-class should not require stairs, a babysitter, and a healthy immune system.
