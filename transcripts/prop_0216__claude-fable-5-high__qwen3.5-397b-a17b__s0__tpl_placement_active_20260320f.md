[P1.1] The motion already concedes the hard part: yes, tighter land-use rules will cost some smallholders income and shave export growth. The question is whether that price buys something worth more. It does — three times over.
[P1.2] First, irreversibility. A lost export quarter can be recovered; a drained peat dome cannot. When peatland is cleared and burned for plantations, it releases carbon for decades and becomes near-permanent fire fuel. The 2015 Indonesian haze crisis hospitalized hundreds of thousands across the region and inflicted economic losses estimated in the tens of billions of dollars — dwarfing the marginal export revenue from the expansion that caused it.
[P1.3] Weighing a reversible, partial income loss against irreversible ecological and public-health damage is not a close call.
[P1.4] Second, the smallholder trade-off is smaller than it looks. The biggest gains available to smallholders come not from clearing new land but from closing the yield gap: smallholder plots typically produce far below what well-managed plantations achieve on the same acreage. Regulation that blocks frontier expansion while channeling support into replanting, better seedlings, and secure land titles raises incomes without raising deforestation. Unregulated expansion, by contrast, mostly enriches large concession-holders while smallholders absorb the haze, the land conflicts, and the flooding.
[P1.5] Third, market access. The export growth CON wants to protect is increasingly conditional on sustainability. The EU's deforestation regulation and corporate zero-deforestation commitments mean palm oil tied to forest clearing faces shrinking, discounted markets. Aggressive domestic regulation is how Malaysia and Indonesia keep their product premium and credible.
[P1.6] Weak regulation doesn't protect exports — it invites boycotts and lets foreign regulators write the rules instead.
[P1.7] So the real choice is not "growth versus regulation." It is short-term, narrowly distributed gains versus the region's air, water, climate resilience, and long-run market position. Governments exist precisely to make that trade when private incentives won't. They should make it now, aggressively, while there is still forest left to govern.
