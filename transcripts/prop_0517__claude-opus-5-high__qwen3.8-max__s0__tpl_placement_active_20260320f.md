[C1.2] Making an online certificate easier to recognize than that record treats a competence claim as more reliable than legal presence.
[C1.3] PRO’s audit argument assumes digital credentials are cheaper to trust. But a signed transcript is only as trustworthy as the issuer, assessment regime, and enforcement channel behind it. Cross-border online education includes accredited universities, boot camps, unregulated providers, and credential mills. [C1.4] They cannot easily audit whether a remote exam was taken by the named student, whether provider standards match local requirements, or whether a foreign accreditor has real power. When verification is cheap on paper but hard across borders, the easy lane becomes the fraud lane.
[C1.5] The danger is acute in professional work. PRO’s layering sounds reasonable, but if credential recognition is easier than residency, systems will pressure institutions to admit and hire on remote certificates before local checks are complete.
[C1.6] The equity appeal proves too much. If the nurse in Lagos cannot relocate, the answer is lawful, supervised pathways—not lowering online recognition below the residency floor. Easier credentialing risks creating a cheap remote labor pool with weak rights, while residency rules remain the baseline that protects workers, students, and the public.
