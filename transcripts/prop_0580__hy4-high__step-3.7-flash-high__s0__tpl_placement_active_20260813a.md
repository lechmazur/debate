[P1.1] Child poverty is a shortfall of family income. A fully refundable monthly child tax credit attacks that shortfall where it is measured and felt: cash reaches the household each month, as rent, food, utilities, diapers, and transport bills arrive. The causal chain is short. Each dollar either closes part of the poverty gap or is phased out; little is consumed by providers, slot inspections, reimbursement systems, copayment formulas, or compliance.
[P1.2] Monthly delivery also prevents bill-by-bill crises that an annual refund can miss, because a small predictable income floor stops arrears, shutoffs, and food rationing before they cascade.
[P1.3] Childcare subsidies travel a longer, leakier road. The dollar helps only if a family uses eligible formal care, finds an open provider, satisfies work or income rules, completes paperwork, and covers remaining fees. In childcare deserts, for irregular-shift parents, for families relying on unpaid relatives, and for a jobless parent seeking work, the subsidy can arrive late or not at all. When provider supply is tight, new purchasing power may be captured in higher prices or flow to families who were not poor.
[P1.4] That may be defensible childcare policy, but it is an expensive anti-poverty detour.
[P1.5] Cost-effectiveness follows from that contrast. A credit can be targeted by income and family size, delivered through existing tax/benefit machinery, and is fungible: the same dollar can buy informal care, a bus pass to keep a job, or groceries this week. A childcare subsidy buys one costly, condition-heavy service and relies on higher earnings eventually lifting the child out of poverty. Employment and child development are real benefits, but they are indirect here: they require available jobs, stable schedules, and available care before they become income.
[P1.6] Monthly cash supports that same job search immediately while raising resources tonight. With equivalent spending, poverty reduced per dollar is the test; direct cash wins because it puts the dollar at the poverty gap, not at the gateway to a market.

[C1.1] PRO’s case rests on a cripplingly narrow definition of cost-effectiveness: only immediate, short-term income top-ups, ignoring that meaningful reductions in child poverty require sustained, long-term gains, not temporary relief. Monthly child tax credits are pure transfers: they deliver cash today, but build no lasting earning capacity for the families they serve. A single parent working two part-time retail jobs with irregular shifts may see their poverty gap closed for a year by a $300 monthly credit, but fall back into poverty the moment the credit lapses, a shift is cut, or an unexpected car repair eats up their cash buffer.
[C1.2] The $3,600 annual cost of that credit delivers a single year of poverty reduction, with no long-term tail.
[C1.3] Expanded childcare subsidies, by contrast, are an investment in sustained earning power. That same parent, with a portable subsidy that covers flexible care from a licensed relative or community drop-in provider, can move to a full-time position with a $15,000 annual raise, employer benefits, and a predictable schedule. The equivalent $3,600 in annual subsidy funding delivers not one year of poverty reduction, but decades of higher income that keeps that child out of poverty permanently, a 4-to-1 return on anti-poverty spending.
[C1.4] PRO’s complaints about “leaky” subsidies—childcare deserts, eligibility hurdles, paperwork—are implementation flaws, not inherent defects. We can fix them with supply-side grants to build care in underserved areas, simplified enrollment for low-literacy families, and eligibility rules that cover irregular shift workers and relative care, all standard design choices for well-constructed subsidy programs.
[C1.5] PRO’s fungibility argument also cuts against their own case: unrestricted cash can be spent on consumer goods, debt repayment, or non-essential expenses that do nothing to reduce long-term child poverty, while subsidies are targeted to a service that directly expands parental opportunity. When cost-effectiveness is measured by sustained child poverty reduction over a 10-year horizon, not just the next 12 months, expanded childcare subsidies outperform monthly tax credits by every relevant metric.
