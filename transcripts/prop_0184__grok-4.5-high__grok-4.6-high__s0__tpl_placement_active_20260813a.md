[P1.1] Aging lead service lines are not a water-chemistry inconvenience. They are infrastructure that continuously offers a neurotoxin a path into kitchens and schools. Municipalities should replace them even when doing so means sharp rate hikes or new debt, because chemical treatment does not retire the hazard—it only tries to keep it asleep.
[P1.2] Orthophosphate dosing and pH control can build a protective scale that slows leaching. That scale is brittle. Main breaks, hydrant flushing, seasonal source shifts, or a new disinfectant package can strip it and drive lead spikes without warning. Children then absorb damage measured in IQ points and lifelong risk; there is no safe threshold.
[P1.3] Treatment leaves the metal in the ground and hopes daily chemistry never fails. Hope is not a public-health plan.
[P1.4] Full replacement ends the pathway. Remove the lead pipe and ordinary tap water cannot recreate that domestic source. Ratepayers already fund perpetual chemicals, intensified sampling, notices, filters, and emergency response whenever control slips. Those operating costs never purchase permanence.
[P1.5] Borrowing or stepped rates for replacement buy a durable asset and extinguish a chronic liability instead of renting temporary silence from it.
[P1.6] Cities that choose perpetual treatment over removal gamble with equity as well as health: older lead networks cluster in the very neighborhoods least able to absorb another failure. Chemistry papers over the pipe; it does not democratize safety. Significant rate pressure is real. Leaving a known poison at the property line while calling the system “managed” is worse.
[P1.7] Safe water is the core municipal product. Deliver it by cutting out the lead, not by endlessly dosing around it.
