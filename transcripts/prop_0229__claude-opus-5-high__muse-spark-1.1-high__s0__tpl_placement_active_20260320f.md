[P1.1] A universal fuel subsidy is a strange kind of welfare program: it pays you in proportion to how much fuel you burn. The household with two cars, a generator, and an air-conditioned villa collects a large share; the family that walks to work and cooks on a small canister collects a fraction. Across the Gulf and North Africa, the pattern is consistent — the richest fifth captures far more of the benefit in absolute terms than the poorest fifth. If a government designed a transfer that deliberately gave the wealthy the biggest cheque, it would be laughed out of parliament.
[P1.2] That is what these subsidies already do, only silently, through the pump.
[P1.3] So the real choice here is not subsidy versus nothing. It is the same money, spent one of two ways. Take the budget line and convert it into a per-household cash transfer, and the poorest are unambiguously better off — they receive more than the fuel price increase costs them, because they consumed less fuel to begin with. Iran's 2010 reform demonstrated the mechanics: near-universal cash payments deposited into bank accounts, and for the bottom deciles the transfer exceeded the loss.
[P1.4] The design is proven; the failures have been failures of sequencing and credibility, not of principle.
[P1.5] Which is exactly why five years matters. Overnight price shocks — the kind that triggered riots in Jordan and Nigeria — happen when governments raise prices before the transfer arrives. A phased schedule lets states build the registry, enroll households through national ID and mobile payments, pay the cash first, and let people see the deposit before they see the pump price move. Morocco and Egypt both showed that gradual, pre-announced glidepaths are survivable.
[P1.6] And the fiscal stakes are one-directional. Subsidy bills balloon precisely when oil spikes, crowding out schools, clinics, and water infrastructure at the worst moment. Cash transfers are budgetable, indexable, and can be scaled to the poor. Fuel subsidies also lock in the demand growth and smuggling that these states can least afford.
[P1.7] Same dirham, better delivered.
