[P1.1] Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease that drives diabetes, heart failure, kidney disease, joint destruction, and multiple cancers. Insurers already pay for all of it: the amputations, the dialysis, the bypasses, decades of insulin. What many refuse to cover is the one class of drugs that can actually reverse the underlying condition. That contradiction is what this motion fixes.
[P1.2] Three pillars.
[P1.3] First, parity. FDA authorization is the standard insurers apply to every other drug on the formulary — statins, antidepressants, blood-pressure medications, many treating conditions with behavioral components. Excluding anti-obesity medications alone isn't pharmacology; it's stigma codified as policy. And "clinically indicated" keeps the decision where it belongs: between patient and physician, not patient and actuary.
[P1.4] Second, the economics favor us. Obesity-related illness consumes a substantial share of U.S. health spending — commonly estimated in the hundreds of billions annually. Sustained double-digit weight loss averts or delays diabetes diagnoses, knee replacements, and cardiac events. Insurers currently fund the most expensive possible version of this disease: the late-stage one.
[P1.5] Coverage isn't new spending; it substitutes cheaper spending for costlier spending.
[P1.6] Third — the pillar that preempts the opposition's entire case — the motion has a brake built in. Value-based pricing and outcomes-based contracts mean insurers pay full price only when patients actually lose weight and keep it off, with rebates or refunds when they don't. This model already exists for costly specialty drugs. CON cannot cry runaway spending without explaining why payment tied to verified outcomes is somehow reckless.
[P1.7] The status quo is the worst of both worlds: insurers absorb catastrophic downstream costs while patients who can't pay out of pocket — disproportionately lower-income — go untreated, widening disparities. We don't tell a smoker we'll cover the lung cancer but not the cessation drug. We shouldn't tell a patient we'll cover the heart attack but not the medicine that prevents it. Coverage with accountability is no blank check; it ends an arbitrary carve-out and takes both medicine and math seriously.
