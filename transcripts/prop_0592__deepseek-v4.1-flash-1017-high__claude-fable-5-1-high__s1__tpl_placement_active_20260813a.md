[P1.1] Sanctions only work when they arrive before the aggressor consolidates gains. Unanimity makes the EU’s sanctions regime structurally slow, because a single government can turn any package into a hostage negotiation—not because it doubts the sanctions, but because it wants an unrelated concession. That is not sovereign protection; it is a choke point an aggressor can exploit.
[P1.2] The EU already uses qualified majority voting for trade, competition, and much of its external economic policy. Sanctions are economic statecraft, not a declaration of war. Requiring unanimity for them means the EU can negotiate a common market by majority but cannot impose a bank freeze or export ban without satisfying every capital. The result is visible: Russia sanctions have repeatedly been delayed and diluted by veto threats and exemption demands; Belarus sanctions were held up over an unrelated bilateral dispute.
[P1.3] Each delay teaches the next aggressor that Europe’s response can be bought or fractured.
[P1.4] QMV would change the bargaining before a crisis. A hostile government could still argue, publish reservations, seek exemptions; it could not unilaterally set the EU’s timetable. That produces faster first packages, more credible escalation, and fewer opportunities for divide-and-conquer. It also makes sanctions more predictable, which matters for markets and allies: partners can coordinate with a Europe that acts on a schedule rather than after another summit.
[P1.5] The objection will be legitimacy. But legitimacy does not require giving one member a permanent veto over twenty-six. QMV is the EU’s normal method for binding collective decisions, with thresholds and institutional checks. The real democratic deficit is a system where a government facing domestic pressure from an aggressor’s ally can block the collective will.
[P1.6] If Europe wants to punish aggression while it is happening, it must stop letting any single member sell the timetable.

[P2.1] The central error is [C1.1]’s premise that unanimity purchases enforcement. It does not. [P2.2] A government willing to quietly undermine enforcement can do that under unanimity too; the difference is that unanimity also lets it hollow out the rule before adoption. QMV separates the collective decision from bargaining over implementation. Outvoted states remain legally bound, and the EU can pair majority adoption with transition periods, compensation, and monitoring—measures that address cost without handing one capital the timetable.
[P2.3] [C1.3] concedes too much. Yes, a first Russia package arrived quickly; the test is whether decisive escalation follows while the aggressor is still adapting. [P2.4] QMV does not abolish those interests; it forces them to compete for exemptions inside a majority coalition rather than dictate the package. The choice is not “QMV versus ownership”; it is negotiating costs before adoption, with a deadline, versus letting the most reluctant state define what Europe can do.
[P2.5] And [C1.7]’s “unity is the sanction” confuses unanimity with credibility. A 27–0 package full of holes is not unity; it signals that one member can be bought. A clear double-majority decision, even with dissent recorded, tells an aggressor that courting the weakest capital will not stop Europe. On China or the Gulf, the same logic holds: if a broad majority is prepared to act, allowing two or three states to block does not preserve unity—it advertises exactly which governments an aggressor should pressure.
[P2.6] QMV raises the price of defection; unanimity subsidizes it.

[P4.3] A2: No serious claim is that QMV creates a European sanctions police within weeks.
