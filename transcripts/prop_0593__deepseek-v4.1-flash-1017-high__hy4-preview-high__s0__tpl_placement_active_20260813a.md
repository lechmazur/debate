[P1.1] Human-rights abuse in global supply chains survives on a simple asymmetry: the firm can trace the factory, the mine, the fishing vessel; the worker, the regulator, and the investor often cannot. The proposition attacks that asymmetry. Requiring large multinationals to publish standardized, audited due-diligence reports does not pretend a document is a remedy. It creates the information infrastructure without which remedy is impossible.
[P1.2] Voluntary codes fail for a predictable reason. They reward the firms with the least to hide and give the rest a vocabulary for saying nothing: “we are committed,” “we strive,” “we are working with suppliers.” Standardization makes those claims comparable across firms, sectors, and years. Audit makes them costly to fake. A company can produce a glossy sustainability page; it cannot as easily produce an assurance trail showing how it identified forced-labor risks, what it found, what it did, and what remains unresolved.
[P1.3] Financial auditing is imperfect, but no serious person concludes we should abolish audited accounts. We improve audit standards; we do not replace them with press releases.
[P1.4] The ex ante effect matters even more than the report. To publish a defensible map of suppliers, a firm must actually map them. To report risks, it must investigate them. To report remediation, it must create grievance channels and track outcomes.
[P1.5] Publicity then gives investors, buyers, regulators, unions, and affected communities something to act on. A brand that claims clean supply chains while hiding a subcontractor has more to lose when the gap is documented.
[P1.6] This is a targeted rule: large multinationals, which have the resources and leverage, not every small business. It complements criminal enforcement and trade policy; it does not replace them. The choice is not perfect accountability versus none. It is visible, comparable, contestable information versus a system in which the only people who know the truth are the firms with an incentive to conceal it.
[P1.7] Countries should require the light to be switched on.

[C1.2] Standardizing cocoa, cobalt, garments, and seafood forces messy realities into a lowest-common-denominator checklist—policies, training records, signed codes—that is auditable but weakly tied to whether a worker is free. [C1.3] PRO’s financial-audit analogy [P1.3] fails on mechanism, not sincerity. Accounts are audited through records the firm controls and reconciles; human rights depend on people who cannot safely speak and relationships an auditor cannot observe on a scheduled visit. Where audits cannot see, standardized, audited reports still manufacture a score. That false precision is worse than vagueness: investors and regulators mistake a clean assurance statement for a clean workplace, and brands gain a defensive shield—“we were audited”—while conditions persist.
[C1.4] The ex ante effect PRO praises [P1.4] can backfire. To produce a defensible map, a multinational’s cheapest option is often to terminate suppliers, shift to “safer” countries, or push compliance costs down the chain; workers in high-risk regions can lose orders rather than gain protection. [P1.6] Targeting large firms limits the defendant, not the supply chain. Countries should instead enforce existing labor and import law, ban goods made with forced labor, fix buyer purchasing practices, and fund independent worker grievance channels.

[P4.1] A1: The auditor does not have to accept the firm’s perimeter. A credible standard defines scope by the firm’s economic activity—purchase orders, payments, customs entries, product lines—and requires the auditor to test completeness, not merely inspect a list. If payments or orders trace to a supplier absent from the map, that is an audit exception or a qualified report, not a benign “unresolved gap.” Deliberate omission becomes a misstatement with legal and reputational exposure. A genuinely untraceable tier can be disclosed as a gap; it cannot be laundered into a clean bill of health.
[P4.2] A2: No disclosure rule by itself can force remediation spending; neither can an import ban or a hotline. The mechanism is that a clean report cannot certify “responsible withdrawal” unless the standard requires audited disclosure of exit rationale, notice, severance, remediation attempts, and worker outcomes. Termination without that trail yields a qualified report and a public, contestable record that regulators, buyers, and investors can penalize. CON’s alternative leaves the same exit invisible.
[P4.3] This is why PRO still wins. CON’s strongest objections—self-drawn maps, certified blind spots, paper compliance—are not arguments for darkness; they are arguments for exactly the independent testing and qualified opinions a standardized audit regime can mandate. Their alternatives require the address book, transaction evidence, and comparable risk data that only a disclosure duty generates at scale. A worker hotline can report a name; without a duty, the multinational can say it never knew that name.
[P4.4] And the “substitute for enforcement” charge fails: the motion complements bans and labor law; it does not replace them. Under CON, a firm can drop a high-risk supplier, hide what happened to its workers, and publish nothing. Under PRO, that decision is documented, audited, and actionable. The choice is not perfect justice versus imperfect paperwork.
[P4.5] It is accountability with evidence versus accountability by guesswork.
