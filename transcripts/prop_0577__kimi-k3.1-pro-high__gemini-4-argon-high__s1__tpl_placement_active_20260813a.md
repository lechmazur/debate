[P1.1] A wall can raise the price of a crossing; it cannot abolish the job waiting on the other side. The comparison is therefore not between openness and control. It is between adding more enforcement against persistent labour demand and creating lawful routes that satisfy part of that demand without clandestine crossings, smugglers, or undocumented work. Existing enforcement remains; the question is which expansion reduces irregular migration more effectively.
[P1.2] First, legal pathways attack the transaction that produces irregular movement. When farms, hotels, or care providers cannot obtain enough local labour, and workers have no realistic lawful route, both sides have incentives to use informal recruiters and smuggling networks. A screened seasonal visa lets the worker arrive openly, the employer hire legally, and the state know who entered, where they work, and when they must leave. This is not merely relabelling irregular migrants after arrival: a usable lawful route substitutes for the dangerous crossing and fraudulent job arrangement that would otherwise occur.
[P1.3] Second, seasonal and sectoral pathways can reduce settlement and repeat irregularity. Consider a harvest worker who expects employment again next year. If returning home means permanently losing access to that income, staying irregularly may seem safer. If compliance brings reliable re-entry, returning becomes rational.
[P1.4] Repeat participation, employer accountability, and worker protections make temporary work genuinely temporary. By contrast, making each crossing harder can discourage circular returns: people already across the border remain longer because leaving risks being unable to come back.
[P1.5] Third, additional border enforcement faces adaptation and diminishing returns. Smugglers change routes, raise fees, and take greater risks; higher apprehensions may reflect more attempts rather than less underlying migration. Enforcement also does little by itself for people who enter legally but work or remain irregularly. Demand-linked visas instead shrink the market on which smugglers and exploitative employers depend, allowing enforcement to concentrate on genuinely unlawful entrants and abusive recruiters.

Expanding seasonal visas to move workers directly past the border invites mass overstays while subsidizing new smuggling; border enforcement stops irregular migration before it begins.
