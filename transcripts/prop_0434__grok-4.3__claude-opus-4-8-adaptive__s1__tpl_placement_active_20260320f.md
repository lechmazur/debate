[P1.1] Neighbors facing chronic noise disturbances currently lose most disputes because their testimony is treated as anecdotal while violators exploit the absence of recorded data. Certified noise monitors close that gap by producing time-stamped, calibrated readings that directly match lease clauses or local ordinances on decibel limits and quiet hours. Once admitted, these logs shift the burden from the complainant to the source, shortening cases that now drag through repeated warnings and unverified denials.
[P1.2] Landlords and property managers already accept similar objective records for water leaks or pest complaints; noise should not remain the sole category where victims are required to tolerate harm until it escalates to violence or eviction. A renter who installs a certified device can demonstrate, for instance, that evening construction exceeded 55 dB for four consecutive nights, giving management concrete grounds for lease enforcement without needing multiple witnesses to risk retaliation.
[P1.3] Opponents claim such evidence invites frivolous filings or privacy intrusions, yet certification standards already filter out uncalibrated or manipulated recordings, and the devices measure only ambient sound pressure rather than conversations. The real privacy harm runs the other way: victims are forced to endure measurable interference or move, while the noise source retains the advantage of plausible deniability.
[P1.4] Expanding admissibility therefore deters repeat offenders, protects lease compliance, and aligns nuisance rules with the same evidentiary expectations applied to every other measurable disturbance in rental housing.

[C1.2] First, the attribution problem. A device sitting in your apartment records sound *pressure*, not *source*. It cannot tell the tribunal whether 55 dB came from the neighbor's party, the street below, the upstairs unit, or your own television. PRO's own example—"evening construction exceeded 55 dB for four consecutive nights"—quietly assumes we already know where the sound came from.
[C1.3] But that identification is the entire dispute. A reading that can't isolate the source isn't evidence; it's an authoritative-looking guess.
[C1.4] Second, nuisance has never been a pure number, and shouldn't be. The law asks whether interference is unreasonable given time, duration, character, and locality. A crying baby and a subwoofer can hit the same decibel. Handing decision-makers a calibrated figure invites them to substitute the easy metric for the hard judgment—convicting the loud-but-lawful and excusing the quiet-but-malicious.
[C1.5] That is worse justice dressed as better.
[C1.6] Third, "certified" describes the instrument, not the deployment. Calibration drifts, microphone placement near a wall or vent inflates readings, and a layperson optimizing for a complaint will place it to win. PRO compares this to water-leak records, but a leak doesn't move when a neighbor wants leverage. Sound does.
[C1.7] Finally, expanding admissibility doesn't end conflict—it arms it. The moment monitoring becomes the path to victory, every aggrieved neighbor installs one, and households start logging each other in a surveillance arms race. We would be encouraging people to wire their homes to build cases against the people on the other side of the wall.
[C1.8] Better evidence, yes. But this isn't better evidence. It's confident noise.

[P2.1] Certified monitors do not replace source identification; they supply the missing objective layer that current rules already demand in other disputes. Tribunals routinely combine readings with timestamps, witness logs, and building layout to attribute sound. A device that records sustained 55 dB spikes between 11 p.m. and 2 a.m. narrows the inquiry rather than ending it, shifting the conversation from “did it happen?” to “was it justified?”—exactly the judgment opponents claim to protect.
[P2.2] The claim that decibel data invites crude substitution ignores how nuisance standards already incorporate measurable thresholds. Lease clauses and local ordinances routinely set quiet-hour limits in decibels. Objective records prevent the present pattern in which violators simply deny the event occurred, forcing complainants to collect multiple neighbors willing to testify and risk retaliation. When a crying infant and a subwoofer reach identical peaks, the duration, time of night, and character of the sound remain available for evaluation; the monitor merely prevents the subwoofer user from pretending the sound never existed.
[P2.3] Deployment concerns are solvable through the same certification process already used for other evidence. Protocols can require documented placement away from walls, periodic calibration checks, and contemporaneous notes—standards property managers already apply to vibration or leak sensors. Sound may travel, yet so do water leaks; the comparison holds because both generate verifiable physical traces once the measurement method is controlled.
[P2.4] An arms race is unlikely once both sides know violations are documentable. Repeat offenders currently exploit the lack of records; reliable evidence raises the expected cost of continued disturbance and therefore deters the behavior that generates complaints in the first place. Allowing neighbors greater access to this evidence does not invent new conflict; it removes the evidentiary asymmetry that currently rewards the loudest party.
