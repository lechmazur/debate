[P1.1] A child in Lagos has sickle cell disease. The same disease can now be functionally cured with a one-time gene-editing therapy. That cure exists, is scientifically settled, and is priced in many markets above what the lifetime earnings of her parents will ever total. The question before us is whether states should have a structured, lawful tool to compel access to such essential gene-editing cures at affordable prices when companies refuse to license on reasonable terms.
[P1.2] The answer has to be yes, and the case rests on three facts no one disputes.
[P1.3] First, gene-editing therapies are categorically different from ordinary drugs. They are frequently single-administration cures for devastating, often inherited diseases. The old pricing logic, recouping R&D across years of repeat prescriptions, collapses when one infusion does the job forever. Companies respond by charging on a "value" basis calibrated against decades of avoided hospitalization.
[P1.4] The arithmetic routinely reaches seven figures per patient, which is precisely unaffordable for any public payer serving patients who, by definition, did not choose their disease.
[P1.5] Second, voluntary licensing and tiered pricing, however admirable in intent, repeatedly leave the worst-off patients behind. Coverage negotiations drag for years during which eligible patients die or deteriorate. Tiered prices are set by the patent holder, not by the country's ability to pay. A public-interest licensing pathway is the missing backstop that converts those negotiations from charity into enforceable entitlement.
[P1.6] Third, this pathway is not confiscation. It is how mature patent systems already work. Compulsory licensing exists in TRIPS, was endorsed in the Doha Declaration, was deployed during COVID-19, and operates with reasonable royalty payment. Innovation incentives are protected because the trigger is narrow, essential therapies, and because the patent holder still collects.
[P1.7] The pathway disciplines abuse of monopoly power; it does not abolish the monopoly.
[P1.8] The motion is the minimal, calibrated instrument for an otherwise unmanageable structural failure. The burden falls on the opposition to defend a status quo in which curable children go uncured.

[C1.1] Governments should not create a compulsory public-interest licensing pathway for essential gene-editing therapies. That tool would sacrifice tomorrow’s cures for a temporary political win on today’s prices. These therapies require years of high-risk science, specialized manufacturing platforms, and multi-billion-dollar capital that only recoverable monopoly returns can attract. Once any commercial success can be labeled “essential” and forced into an affordable royalty, the investment calculus collapses for every candidate still in the lab.
[C1.2] The economics are unforgiving. Unlike small-molecule drugs that recoup costs over years of repeat prescriptions, gene-editing products are one-shot interventions. Failure rates remain extreme. Investors already demand high returns to offset that risk.
[C1.3] Add the standing threat of state compulsion and capital simply migrates to less exposed fields. The patients who would have benefited from the next sickle-cell platform, the next muscular-dystrophy edit, or the next rare-metabolic fix never receive them because those products are never completed.
[C1.4] Practical barriers make the promise hollow. A patent license does not magically transfer autologous cell processing, viral-vector production, or quality-control know-how. Compulsion without genuine technology transfer yields either unsafe manufacturing capacity or years of litigation, not reliable, affordable doses. Voluntary licensing, advance-purchase commitments, and public co-investment already expand reach while keeping developers at the table; the compulsory pathway replaces that cooperation with exit.
[C1.5] Existing TRIPS-style tools were crafted for acute public-health emergencies and genericizable molecules. Turning them into a standing pathway for routine price fights on frontier biologics invites mission creep. Every high-cost therapy becomes a political target; every negotiation becomes a seizure threat. The result is not disciplined markets but chronic uncertainty that chills precisely the innovation the proposition claims to safeguard.
[C1.6] A rule that converts patents into temporary leases conditional on government-defined affordability does not deliver access. It rations progress. We reject the pathway.

Compulsory licenses on antibodies or vaccines have not emptied pipelines precisely because those products still generate returns over years of dosing or large treated populations. [C2.2] Label any success “essential” and force an affordable royalty, and capital migrates before the next rare-disease candidate ever reaches Phase I. Prior TRIPS use on older molecules is no evidence that frontier platforms survive the same threat.
[C2.3] The know-how objection is not a concession; it is decisive against the pathway. Voluntary deals keep the originator at the table, transferring vector processes and cell-handling know-how because upside remains. Compulsion turns the relationship adversarial. A government license does not summon missing closed manufacturing systems or trained personnel; it produces litigation, substandard batches, or developers who simply refuse to develop for markets that can later seize the reward.
[C2.4] A “negotiated with consequences” licensee cannot invent platforms that the threatened owner has already decided not to build.
[C2.5] Calling the tool narrow and royalty-bearing does not fix the economics or the politics. “Essential” is an elastic political word once a standing pathway exists.
