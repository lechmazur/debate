[P1.1] When a child with cystic fibrosis cannot get Trikafta because it costs more than a family earns in a decade, the patent system has stopped doing the job we granted it for. Patents are not natural rights; they are a policy bargain. Society grants a temporary monopoly in exchange for innovation that actually reaches people. When the price wall blocks access to a medicine people need to survive, the bargain has failed, and compulsory licensing is the correction mechanism already built into international law — TRIPS Article 31 and the Doha Declaration explicitly contemplate it.
[P1.2] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, the moral floor. A government's first duty is to its people's lives. If insulin, cancer drugs, or antiretrovirals are priced beyond reach, refusing to license generics is choosing patent-holder margins over citizens' survival. No serious theory of legitimacy survives that trade.
[P1.4] Second, a defined threshold disciplines the policy. The objection to compulsory licensing has always been "uncertainty chills investment." A pre-announced, rule-based affordability trigger — say, a multiple of median income or a share of per-capita health spending — flips that script. Companies know the rules in advance and price accordingly.
[P1.5] The threshold is not arbitrary seizure; it is a transparent backstop that pressures voluntary price negotiation before licensing is ever invoked. Most of the work happens in the shadow of the rule, not through actual issuance.
[P1.6] Third, the empirics favor us. Brazil and Thailand used compulsory licensing on HIV and heart medicines and expanded access dramatically without collapsing pharmaceutical R&D. India's generics industry supplies affordable medicines across the Global South. Innovation continues because the lucrative markets — wealthy insured populations, novel indications — remain profitable.
[P1.7] What compulsory licensing strips away is monopoly rent on desperate patients, not the return on genuine innovation.
[P1.8] The choice is simple: a world where patents bend when they would otherwise kill, or a world where price tags are allowed to function as death sentences. PRO defends the first.

[C1.1] The motion does not ask whether compulsory licensing should exist — it already does, as a discretionary last resort under TRIPS. The motion asks whether a price crossing a formula should automatically become grounds for breaking a patent, country by country, drug by drug. That mechanical trigger is what we reject, and it fails on three counts.
[C1.2] First, it destroys the medicines of tomorrow precisely where we need them most. A pre-announced affordability cap on essential medicines is a global price ceiling enforced by expropriation. Investors will respond rationally: shift capital away from drugs likely to be classified "essential" — antibiotics, tropical diseases, low-margin chronic therapies — toward cosmetic and lifestyle products that no threshold will ever touch. Notice the perversity: the rule punishes inventing exactly the medicines the world most needs.
[C1.3] Second, the trigger sabotages access in practice. Faced with automatic licensing, companies will simply not launch in threshold countries, delay registration, or withhold the regulatory data and manufacturing know-how generics need. A compulsory license on a complex biologic or a drug like Trikafta does not conjure a factory; without technology transfer, quality-assured supply, and cold chains, patients get a legal document, not a medicine.
[C1.4] Meanwhile the credible threat that makes voluntary licensing and tiered pricing work — tools that have actually delivered HIV drugs at scale through mechanisms like the Medicines Patent Pool — collapses, because companies facing automatic seizure have nothing to gain from negotiating.
[C1.5] Third, "a defined affordability threshold" is a fantasy of precision. Affordable to whom — the median household, the insured, the state payer? Governments will set thresholds to win headlines; firms will respond with launch boycotts and trade retaliation; patients sit in the crossfire.
[C1.6] The real access barriers are weak health systems, procurement failure, and underinvestment in insurance. The motion offers a slogan in place of those solutions — and trades a working safety valve for a doomsday device that fires automatically.
