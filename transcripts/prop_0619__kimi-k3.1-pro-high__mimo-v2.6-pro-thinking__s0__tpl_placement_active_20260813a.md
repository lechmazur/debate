[P1.1] When a campaign publishes someone’s home address, organizes thousands of abusive messages, and openly calls for confrontation, the resulting danger is not a mysterious tragedy. It is often visible in the platform’s own data before anyone is assaulted, fired, driven from home, or “swatted.” The question is whether the company best positioned to interrupt that escalation may simply watch.
[P1.2] Governments should answer no. A duty to mitigate foreseeable offline harm means a duty of reasonable care, not strict liability for every crime and not a mandate to suppress offensive opinions. Its trigger should be demanding: an identifiable coordinated campaign, warning signs available to the platform, and a credible risk of concrete offline injury. Its response should be proportionate: human review of escalating threats, friction on coordinated brigading, removal of doxxing, protection of targeted users, preservation of evidence, and urgent referral where necessary.
[P1.3] A platform that takes reasonable steps should not be punished because determined perpetrators nevertheless succeed.
[P1.4] This targets a gap that criminal law and individual moderation cannot fill. Police generally act after threats become crimes; victims see isolated messages, while platforms can see networks suddenly mobilizing, addresses circulating across accounts, and repeated reports converging on one target. Yet engagement incentives reward viral conflict, and moderation costs fall on platforms while offline costs fall on victims. Without legal obligation, caution remains optional precisely where intervention is cheapest and most effective.
[P1.5] The free-speech objection assumes the only alternatives are indifference or government censorship. That is false. This duty regulates negligent risk management, not viewpoints. It can expressly protect good-faith mistakes, require notice and appeal, exempt smaller services from burdens they cannot bear, and authorize courts to reject vague or disproportionate enforcement.
[P1.6] Existing duties already accommodate uncertainty in product safety, premises security, and other contexts; uncertainty justifies standards of reasonable care, not impunity.
[P1.7] The decisive comparison is who bears preventable risk. CON leaves targets begging for fragmented assistance while campaigns scale. PRO places a bounded obligation on the actor with visibility and leverage, before online coordination becomes offline injury.

[P5.1] The decisive question is not whether platforms can predict every tragedy. It is whether vigilance may remain optional when their own systems reveal organized intimidation escalating toward credible offline danger.
[P5.2] CON’s final “pincer” fails. A duty can add protection without demanding clairvoyance: direct rules address discrete content, while reasonable care addresses the pattern—converging reports, private information, and instructions to bombard or confront. Sometimes proportionate care means monitoring; sometimes it means restricting targeted contact or removing an address while leaving criticism intact. That is neither redundancy nor automatic protest suppression.
[P5.3] The deepest flaw in CON’s case is its one-way ledger. Speakers deserve protection from reckless over-removal, which is why compliance should require documented reasons, narrow and reversible measures, prompt restoration, and review of systematic suppression. But targets should not absorb preventable violence merely because platform judgment is imperfect. CON offers certainty for platforms and optional protection for victims; PRO requires care from the actor with visibility and leverage, without liability for reasonable mistakes or unsuccessful prevention.
[P5.4] Law can demand responsible action under uncertainty without demanding foresight. When coordinated danger is visible and intervention remains possible, government should make that responsibility real.
