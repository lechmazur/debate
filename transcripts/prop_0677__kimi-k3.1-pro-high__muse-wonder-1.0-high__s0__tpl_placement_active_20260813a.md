[P1.1] An applicant can be screened out before anyone reads their CV; an employee can be dismissed after a monitoring score brands them unproductive. If the underlying data are wrong—a mismatched identity, an unexplained employment gap, a broken activity metric—the person cannot even identify what to correct. The employer sees the process; the person bearing the consequences sees only “unsuccessful” or “terminated.”
[P1.2] This motion imposes a narrow procedural duty, not a ban on automation. When an automated system meaningfully influences hiring or firing, the affected person should receive clear, timely disclosure of that role and the kinds of factors involved. It need not require source code, model weights, or a predetermined outcome. “Meaningfully influenced” also excludes trivial administrative uses while capturing systems that rank, filter, score, or substantially shape a manager’s choice.
[P1.3] First, disclosure makes error correction and existing legal rights usable. Protections against discrimination or arbitrary dismissal are hollow if someone cannot discover that an automated assessment helped produce the decision. Nor does a manager’s formal approval solve the problem: a human can defer to a score without understanding it. The motion’s threshold correctly looks at actual influence, not the comforting fiction that a person clicked the final button.
[P1.4] Second, secrecy creates systematically bad incentives. Employers can validate systems, document their use, and review outcomes; applicants and workers usually cannot detect the system, let alone investigate it. Voluntary transparency is weakest precisely where errors or discriminatory effects would be most embarrassing. A legal baseline therefore protects responsible employers too, rather than making candor a competitive disadvantage.
[P1.5] The predictable objections confuse disclosure with surrendering trade secrets or imposing impossible explanations. Describing a system’s decision-making role and broad inputs reveals neither proprietary code nor every statistical interaction. The compliance burden is modest compared with the consequences of unemployment, and businesses using these systems are best positioned to know when they matter. If disclosure would let applicants game shallow criteria, employers should improve those criteria—not keep life-changing judgments invisible.

[P5.2] A notice that a productivity score materially influenced a dismissal may not identify the error. It does identify where to look. CON calls that mere suspicion, but informed suspicion is what allows existing complaints, record requests, and investigations to do their work. [P5.3] Nor does standard wording become useless merely because it is standard. “This ranking materially shaped your shortlist” communicates something “we may use AI” conceals. If an employer cannot say even that, the problem is its unexamined process, not the worker’s entitlement to notice.
[P5.4] This motion makes a deliberately modest claim: automation may assist judgment, but its consequential role must not remain invisible. CON demands a complete remedy or none. Workers should not be denied the doorway because it is not the whole building.
