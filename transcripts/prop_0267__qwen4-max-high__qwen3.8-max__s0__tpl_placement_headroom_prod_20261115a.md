[P1.1] Nightlife rarely dies at midnight because people want to go home. It dies because the kitchen closed at ten, the last cheap meal is gone, and the only option left is an expensive sit-down room or a taxi. That last hour is the hinge of a night out, and it is exactly the hour indoor hospitality zones cannot serve.
[P1.2] A food truck needs a vehicle, a permit and a pitch. A restaurant needs a lease, a fit-out, a liquor licence and an investor willing to bet on a district that is currently empty. When a city's nightlife is weak, capital will not move first; the cheapest, most mobile supply has to move first. Permits let small operators — often immigrant families testing a menu with their own savings — enter in weeks, and let a city find out which corners actually have demand before anyone pours concrete.
[P1.3] The alternative is not quiet streets. Crowds leaving clubs at two will still be hungry; without licensed vendors they get an unlicensed van, a kebab shop queue spilling into traffic, or nothing at all. A permitted truck gives that crowd a lit, staffed, orderly place to stand — and gives the city a lever: designated pitches, closing times, waste rules, noise limits, and a licence it can revoke tomorrow. Regulation is the answer to nuisance.
[P1.4] Scarcity is not.
[P1.5] So judge this on the motion's own terms: revival. Indoor zones are slow, capital-hungry and concentrated; if the bet fails, the city owns an empty district and higher rents. More late-night permits are fast, distributed and reversible. If the objection is noise or grease, the answer is a permit condition, not a refusal — because refusing the permit does not remove the appetite, it only removes the lawful way to meet it.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a useful supplement for an economic anchor. A revived nightlife economy needs places that can hold people safely for hours, in winter and summer, with toilets, seating, security, insurance, and predictable tax revenue. Indoor hospitality zones provide that backbone. A row of food trucks can feed a queue, but it cannot host a birthday dinner, a live set, a workplace gathering, or a family evening.
[C1.2] If cities replace indoor capacity with curbside permits, they trade durable jobs and premises for seasonal stalls that close when it rains or turns unsafe.
[C1.3] PRO says trucks are cheap and fast. That is precisely why they are fragile. Low capital entry means low resilience: vendors can vanish overnight, menus are narrow, and profits often leak outside local supply chains. Indoor venues, by contrast, sign leases, hire larger crews, buy from local distributors, and anchor adjacent spending for taxis, theatres, and bars.
[C1.4] A city that wants a nightlife economy needs operators with enough stake to maintain standards, cooperate with neighbours, and survive a bad quarter.
[C1.5] The harder problem is governance. Concentrated indoor zones let cities put infrastructure where it belongs: late transport, lighting, toilets, policing, waste collection, noise mitigation, and accessibility. Scattered late-night trucks disperse those burdens across residential blocks and narrow sidewalks. The result is predictable: grease traps, litter, idling engines, queue noise, blocked driveways, and complaints that force blanket restrictions.
[C1.6] Then the city spends more on enforcement than it gains in permit fees.
[C1.7] None of this means banning street food. It means refusing the substitution at the heart of the motion. Permits should complement indoor zones, not become the main revival strategy. If the goal is a nightlife economy that lasts, cities should lead with indoor hospitality districts and let food trucks fill gaps where they truly add value.
