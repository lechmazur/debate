[P1.1] AI dubbing does more than translate dialogue: it manufactures a performance in an actor’s own voice. That voice carries identity, reputation, and livelihood. The default should therefore be affirmative permission for the synthetic use, not silence extracted through a clause about “all media,” “distribution,” or “ancillary rights.”
[P1.2] The central distinction is between authorizing a project and surrendering control over a reusable personal instrument. An actor may gladly accept a role without expecting their voice to speak languages they do not know, deliver emphasis they never chose, or be redeployed in contexts they would reject. A performer’s accent, emotional texture, and vocal mannerisms are part of their professional craft. Treating those features as automatically captured by broad boilerplate mistakes access for consent.
[P1.3] This is not a hypothetical administrative concern. Imagine an actor who signs a standard distribution clause and later hears a synthetic version of themselves sounding fluent, intimate, menacing, or comic in ways they never performed. Even where the words are faithful translations, audiences may attribute the delivery to the actor. The reputational risk travels under the actor’s name, while the performer loses the practical ability to approve the result or negotiate its value.
[P1.4] Once models and dubbed versions circulate, withdrawing a signature is a weak remedy.
[P1.5] Opt-in consent solves the problem at the cheapest point: before synthesis. Producers can request a separate, explicit authorization describing the AI use, languages, territory, duration, and compensation. Performers who welcome the work can agree and bargain; producers remain free to contract. Those who decline can be dubbed by another voice performer or excluded from that synthetic use.
[P1.6] This changes the default, not the freedom to make deals.
[P1.7] The likely objection is efficiency: productions need scalable localization, and obtaining permissions adds friction. But friction is not injustice merely because it protects someone’s bargaining position. A clear consent request is trivial compared with producing and distributing a season of television. Convenience cannot establish entitlement to a person’s voice any more than it could establish entitlement to their face.

Neither describes how film and television actually work. Actors do not sign "boilerplate" in a vacuum. [C1.2] A clause granting dubbing and localization rights in exchange for a day rate, residuals, and replica compensation is consent. It is consent given once, with eyes open, at the moment the performer has leverage. Calling that "boilerplate" is a rhetorical move to make a bargained term look like a trick.
[C1.3] Consider what opt-in actually means on the ground. A series has forty speaking parts and ships to twenty territories. Opt-in gives each of those forty performers a veto, exercisable after the fact, over whether the show can be localized in a consistent way. The lead will say yes because she is paid handsomely.
[C1.4] The day player with two lines may be unreachable, deceased, or simply holding out. The result is not that his voice is protected; it is that a human dubber replaces him, which is exactly what happened before AI existed and exactly what PRO concedes is acceptable. Dubbing has never required the original actor's permission. A Spanish-speaking audience has heard Harrison Ford in another man's voice for decades without anyone claiming Ford's identity was violated.
[C1.5] AI dubbing is, if anything, more faithful to the performer than that tradition.
[C1.6] The harms PRO describes, menacing or intimate deliveries the actor never gave, are real, and they are already addressed: defamation, right-of-publicity law, and contract terms limiting use to translation of the actual performance. The remedy for a badly drafted clause is a better clause, negotiated collectively so the weakest performers get the same floor as the strongest. The remedy is not a per-use ritual that favors stars with lawyers and leaves everyone else out of the deal entirely.
