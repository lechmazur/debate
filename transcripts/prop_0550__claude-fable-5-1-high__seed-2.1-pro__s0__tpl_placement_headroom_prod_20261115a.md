[P1.1] Start with who actually receives the marginal dollar. Expanding college subsidies does not send more valedictorians to Stanford; they already go. It pulls in the student on the fence, the one with middling grades and little family support, the one most likely to leave after three semesters with debt and no degree. [P1.2] The famous "college premium" is an average over graduates. The marginal subsidized enrollee is disproportionately a non-completer, and non-completers earn little more than high school graduates while carrying loans. Subsidies buy a lottery ticket and hand the losers the bill.
[P1.3] Now picture the apprentice. At eighteen she is paid from day one, earns a nationally recognized credential by twenty-one, and has three years of wage growth, seniority, and employer references before the college student even enters the job market. Her training is shaped by an employer who needs the skill, so it matches real demand rather than a catalog of majors. She has zero debt, which means she can buy a house, start a business, or fund further education later, and many apprenticeship pathways feed directly into associate and bachelor's degrees paid by the employer.
[P1.4] Early earnings compound; early debt compounds the other way.
[P1.5] The comparison is also about leakage. [P1.6] Germany and Switzerland, with apprenticeship systems at scale, show youth unemployment far below their neighbors and strong mid-career earnings for non-university workers. Their lesson is simple: the path that pays you to learn, matched to someone who wants to hire you, beats the path that charges you to gamble.

An 18-year-old trained on a specific manufacturing line, a proprietary medical coding system, or a regional construction trade is slotted into a narrow, often non-portable skill set that peaks early and is vulnerable to automation, outsourcing, or industry shifts that can wipe out their primary earning asset with little warning.
[C1.2] By their mid-30s, those workers hit hard promotion ceilings because they lack the general, flexible critical thinking and broad quantitative skill base that four-year degrees build—the very skills that let workers move into management, switch sectors as the economy changes, and capture steep earnings growth through their 40s, 50s, and 60s when long-run earnings gaps are largest.
[C1.3] The PRO side’s complaint about college non-completers misrepresents what expanded subsidies do: unmet financial need is the single largest driver of dropout for low-income, marginal students. Targeted, expanded grants that cover tuition and basic living costs don’t just nudge students to enroll—they let them cut back on excessive part-time work, enroll full-time, and finish, unlocking the bachelor’s wage premium that grows, not shrinks, over a career. Claims of widespread subsidy leakage are overstated for student-centered aid, which flows directly to reducing unmet need rather than inflating institutional sticker prices.
[C1.4] Nor do the Germany and Switzerland examples hold up: those systems rely on rigid early tracking that permanently shunts low-income and immigrant youth out of professional pathways before they are old enough to choose, suppressing long-run mobility. The apprentice earns more at 20; the subsidized college graduate earns far more across a whole working life.
