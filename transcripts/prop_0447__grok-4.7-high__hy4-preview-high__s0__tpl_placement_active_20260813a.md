[C1.1] The motion asks the EU to tax a business structure rather than punish a proven abuse. PRO says control, profit, and avoidance meet when labor is routed through apps or subcontracting chains [P1.1]. But control and profit are ordinary rewards for organizing production; they are not proof that a social bill went unpaid. Specialization, flexibility, and risk transfer are legitimate reasons to contract out.
[C1.2] Where a worker is genuinely self-employed and prices in risk, no employer share is owed at all; a penalty attached merely to using a platform is arbitrary. Many large firms already pay full contributions on their own staff and their suppliers comply. A surcharge triggered merely by using a platform or a chain is a penalty on efficiency, not a remedy for evasion.
[C1.3] Nor will lead firms quietly absorb the cost. In competitive markets margins are thin, so a higher deemed contribution is shifted along the chain: lower prices to the micro-firm, fewer contracts for independents, or faster automation. The courier, cleaner, or engineer bears it in lost work or lower net pay. PRO is right that the micro-firm lacks compliance staff [P1.5]; making the large user pay a top-up does not give that firm capacity—it squeezes the little margin it has.
[C1.4] A construction principal facing a surcharge on every sub-layer will prefer bigger suppliers or fewer external bids, entrenching incumbents and shrinking opportunity for small specialists.
[C1.5] The cross-border case is also overstated. A uniform EU levy collides with national systems and benefit financing. If one country's rates are already high, the EU duty becomes a tax on location rather than a cure for non-payment. The direct route is enforcement: treat misclassified workers as employees, impose joint liability where the lead firm sets pay and conditions, require supply-chain transparency, and fund core protections through general taxation or portable worker accounts.
[C1.6] Punish unpaid contributions; do not tax the choice to contract.
