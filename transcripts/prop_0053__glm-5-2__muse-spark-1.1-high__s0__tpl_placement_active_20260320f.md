[P1.1] Cosmetic procedures are not lipstick. Botox, fillers, laser resurfacing, and surgical interventions carry real medical risks: infection, nerve damage, scarring, disfigurement, and in rare cases death. When a licensed clinic advertises these services, it operates under regulatory constraints designed to ensure informed consent. When an influencer posts a glowing video about their "glow-up" with a discount code, those constraints evaporate.
[P1.2] That gap matters most when the audience includes minors.
[P1.3] Young people are uniquely susceptible to this form of marketing. Influencers cultivate parasocial relationships that function like peer endorsements, and adolescents process social comparison with heightened emotional intensity. A teenager watching a creator they admire showcase filler results is not evaluating a medical decision—she is absorbing a social signal about what beauty and acceptance require. The disclosure buried in a bio or spoken at double speed does not change that dynamic.
[P1.4] The proposition targets audiences "likely to include minors" for a reason. Most lifestyle and beauty influencers have mixed-age audiences; pretending a platform dominated by teenagers is somehow adult-only is a fiction that benefits marketers, not public health. Age-gating and strengthened warnings acknowledge the obvious: when you cannot confirm the audience is adult, and the product is a medical procedure, the burden falls on the advertiser, not the child.
[P1.5] This is not a ban. It is a proportionate response. Stronger warnings ensure that risks are visible rather than buried beneath aesthetic filters and sponsored smiles. Age-gating limits direct exposure to promotional content for interventions that can be irreversible.
[P1.6] We already restrict alcohol and tobacco advertising to minors on the same logic—that certain products carry risks young people are not equipped to weigh. Cosmetic procedures belong in that category.
[P1.7] The counterargument will rest on free expression and consumer autonomy. But neither principle requires letting medical interventions be marketed to children through their favorite creators without meaningful guardrails.
