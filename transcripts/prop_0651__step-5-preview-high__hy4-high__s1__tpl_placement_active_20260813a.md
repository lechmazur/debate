[P1.1] English dominance is not a neutral convenience; it is an extractive filter that decides whose knowledge survives, whose culture is legible, and whose labor pays the cost of global communication.
[P1.2] In science, the English-only publication regime creates a permanent epistemic asymmetry. A researcher in Lagos, Hanoi, or Bogotá must translate not just words but entire conceptual frameworks to be recognized, while the latent categories of local inquiry—soil knowledge, plant taxonomy, clinical observation—are flattened into Anglophone taxonomies or dropped entirely. The result is not a global commons but a funnel: work becomes visible only after passing through English editorial norms, and knowledge that resists translation quietly disappears from the record.
[P1.3] The cultural damage compounds. When domains of prestige—science, business, the internet—migrate exclusively into English, local languages are pushed into the private and the nostalgic. Children lose fluency in the tongue that carries oral history, ritual, and ecological memory, and business discourse adopts Anglophone idioms that crowd out local idioms of negotiation, obligation, and trust. A language stripped of its high-status functions is not merely disadvantaged; it is being demoted from a living system to a heritage artifact.
[P1.4] The promise of efficiency does not rescue the arrangement. Efficiency is unevenly distributed: it is free for native speakers and expensive for everyone else, who spend years mastering a tongue and still lose publication speed and rhetorical nuance. More importantly, the gains are substitutable—translation tools, multilingual journals, local-language digital content can approximate convenience—whereas the losses are not. Extinct concepts, eroded fluency, and monocropped curricula cannot be recovered once the funnel has narrowed.
[P1.5] The question is whether one language should arbitrate global knowledge. It must not, because the cost is paid in the currency of human cognitive diversity.

[C1.2] The comparison is wrong too: PRO measures English against a fully funded multilingual utopia [P1.4]. The feasible alternative is fragmentation: narrower national systems, slower circulation, stronger local gatekeepers. Many narrow funnels [P1.2] would not liberate a researcher in Hanoi or Bogotá; they would demand more languages, more translation, and more luck to be read. Translation can flatten; no translation guarantees obscurity. [C1.4] Visibility is not purity, but purity without reach does not keep a system alive.
[C1.5] The efficiency objection reverses the mechanism. A common hub lowers the cost of multilingual access: one bridge to English connects a low-resource language to many others; pairwise translation needs roads everywhere, built last where resources are thinnest. Unequal fluency is real, but the remedy is subsidies, multilingual reviewing, translated abstracts, and training—not dismantling the bridge.

Bilingual schools, local media, courts, and families keep a language modern without abolishing English. Reciprocal exchange—concepts glossed, debated, and challenged in both directions—can preserve a system. [P4.2] A2: The feasible alternative is not a funded utopia but a distributed network: regional journals that review in local languages, paired translation budgets, and archives that index non-Anglophone sources as primary evidence. [P4.3] CON still confuses circulation with survival. A Vietnamese crop study cited in English journals may travel widely, but if the taxonomic reasoning behind it lives in Vietnamese field practice, citation counts do not keep that practice alive. [P4.4] The asymmetry is irreversible. Convenience lost by dethroning English is recoverable through training and translation; a language stripped of science, business, and the internet is demoted from a living system to a museum tongue. CON admits the tolls are unfair but offers patches—subsidies, abstracts, reviewing—that leave Anglophone institutions owning the editorial defaults, the citation indexes, and the prestige markers. Use by Nigerian or Indonesian scholars [C2.2] is not ownership; it is tenancy on someone else’s terms.
[P4.5] The motion asks whether dominance harms non-anglophone cultures and knowledge systems.
