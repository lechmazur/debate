[C1.2] PRO leans entirely on outcomes-based contracts to blunt this fiscal crisis [P1.4]. But that mechanism is an administrative illusion. First, commercial health plans face high enrollee churn—the average worker changes plans every two to three years. An employer or insurer footing thousands of dollars annually for chronic injections absorbs immediate, massive liabilities, while the theoretical downstream dividends—a heart attack averted a decade later—accrue entirely to a different insurer or to Medicare.
[C1.3] Second, outcomes contracts do not control aggregate budget exposure. Because modern anti-obesity medications reliably produce short-term weight reduction, manufacturers easily hit clinical benchmarks and trigger full reimbursement. Tying rebates to outcomes fails when the drug works in the short term, requires indefinite administration to prevent weight regain, and carries an exorbitant list price. When tens of millions qualify, paying a "fair" price per patient still bankrupts the risk pool.
[C1.4] Crucially, a statutory mandate destroys payers’ only genuine leverage: formulary exclusion. The moment the law forbids insurers from walking away, manufacturers have zero incentive to lower baseline prices. Proponents point to insulin and statins [P1.2], conveniently ignoring that statins are cheap generics and insulin’s price inflation was fueled by captive demand.
[C1.5] The resulting cost explosion will not fall on drugmakers; it will be passed directly to workers through spiked premiums, higher deductibles, and wage stagnation. Forcing everyday policyholders to subsidize lifelong, monopoly-priced drugs under an unproven contracting scheme is regressive and dangerous. True public health requires genuine price discipline, not a statutory blank check.
