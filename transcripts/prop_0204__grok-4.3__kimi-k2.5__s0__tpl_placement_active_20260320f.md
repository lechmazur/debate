[P1.1] National-security review should apply a presumption of blockage to acquisitions of critical-mineral assets by firms closely tied to rival states because these holdings grant lasting influence over the inputs essential to both military hardware and emerging technologies. Control over extraction and refining sites cannot be replicated overnight, so transferring them to entities aligned with potential adversaries creates permanent points of leverage during periods of heightened tension.
[P1.2] Rival governments routinely direct the commercial activities of their affiliated companies to advance geopolitical goals rather than pure market logic. This reality turns what might look like benign investment into a vector for embedding influence deep within another nation's supply chains, enabling everything from selective embargoes to demands for technology sharing under the threat of cutoff.
[P1.3] A presumptive rule places the onus on the acquiring party to show why the transaction poses no security hazard, which accounts for the difficulties in fully tracing ownership chains or predicting future policy shifts. Reactive case-by-case scrutiny after the fact often comes too late, once assets have been integrated and alternative sources have atrophied.
[P1.4] Those who oppose the presumption argue that it will reduce overall investment and slow innovation by limiting capital flows into mineral development. Yet this critique assumes that capital from rival states is interchangeable with allied or neutral sources, ignoring that the former carries attached risks of strategic capture that no amount of post-deal regulation can reliably neutralize.
[P1.5] By contrast, a default block encourages development of redundant capacity among trusted partners and spurs domestic exploration efforts that might otherwise be neglected in favor of cheaper foreign options. The result is a more robust foundation for technological sovereignty that protects against the kind of sudden shortages that have historically forced policy reversals under duress.
[P1.6] Ultimately, the cost of occasional missed opportunities in the minerals sector pales against the danger of systemic dependency, where critical industries grind to a halt precisely when resilience matters most.

[C1.1] A presumptive block does not protect supply chains; it strangles them. The world’s lithium, cobalt, and rare-earth projects require billions in upfront investment and technical expertise that Western capital markets alone cannot currently deploy at speed. A default prohibition would stall projects even in friendly jurisdictions—consider Australian or Canadian lithium mines that rely on Chinese offtake agreements and processing partnerships to reach bankability—creating exactly the shortages the PRO warns against while alternative financing remains years away.
[C1.2] The category "closely tied" is hopelessly elastic, inviting politicized expansion that chills legitimate commercial relationships. [P1.3] claims the presumption merely shifts the burden of proof, but in practice this means years of opaque review during which financing costs compound and competitors secure alternative sources. The security gain is speculative—potential future leverage that may never materialize—while the economic loss is immediate and measurable in abandoned projects and higher commodity prices.
[C1.3] Moreover, the presumption ignores the architecture of global mineral markets. Rival states dominate not merely extraction but processing capacity and refining technology. A unilateral default block invites retaliatory export controls on the refined materials we actually need, leaving domestic manufacturers with raw ore they cannot refine and spiraling input costs. This is not deterrence; it is mutually assured economic disruption.
[C1.4] Better tools exist. Conditional approvals with audit rights, strict governance requirements, and mandatory diversification clauses can neutralize coercion risks without severing the capital flows required to bring mines online. Security lies in velocity and redundancy, not in preemptive embargoes that treat every foreign investor as a prospective adversary. We should reject a mechanism that promises resilience while delivering stagnation.
