[P1.1] The precautionary principle sounds like humility, but as regulators actually apply it, it is a one-sided ledger: it counts every hypothetical risk of acting and assigns zero weight to the certain costs of not acting. That asymmetry is the heart of this debate. A rule that says "block until proven safe" doesn't eliminate risk—it silently transfers risk onto the people who would have been helped, and those victims never appear on any regulator's books.
[P1.2] Consider the invisible graveyard. When a drug approved too quickly harms patients, there are headlines, lawsuits, and named victims. When a life-saving therapy is delayed years by precautionary review, the people who die waiting are statistical ghosts—nobody is fired for their deaths. So the institutional incentive is always to over-block, and the errors compound in one direction.
[P1.3] This isn't a bug in implementation; it's baked into how the principle is commonly applied.
[P1.4] The historical record shows the pattern at scale. Precautionary fear stalled nuclear power for decades, so we burned coal instead—a fuel that demonstrably kills through air pollution and carbon emissions, orders of magnitude more dangerous than the reactors we refused to build. Europe's precautionary blockade of genetically modified crops delayed Golden Rice while vitamin A deficiency continued blinding and killing children, despite no credible evidence of GMO harm after decades of consumption. In each case, the "precaution" chose a familiar, ongoing harm over a novel, speculative one—and the familiar harm was worse.
[P1.5] Note what the motion requires: not that precaution never prevents harm, but that on net, as commonly applied, it blocks more benefit than it prevents harm. That comparison favors us because genuine catastrophes stopped by the principle are rare and often addressable by targeted, proportionate regulation—while the foregone benefits of cheap energy, faster cures, and abundant food are vast, continuous, and compounding.
[P1.6] Precaution that ignores the cost of caution isn't safety. It's superstition with paperwork.
