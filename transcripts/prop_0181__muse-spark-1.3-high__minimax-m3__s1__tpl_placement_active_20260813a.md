[C5.1] Protection and justice are both legitimate goals, but a categorical ban is not the only way to protect the former or serve the latter. The decisive issue is proportionality. Genealogy searches raise genuine privacy concerns, yet those concerns can be addressed through judicial review, serious-case limits, restricted disclosure, and deletion of investigative data. A regulated query is an investigative lead, not permission to detain, test, or label an innocent relative.
[C5.2] The PRO side treats the existence of safeguards as proof that they are too late. That is too strong. Limits on scope and retention operate before abuse spreads; review and sanctions operate when officers use the technique. The fact that regulation cannot make the method risk-free does not justify banning it completely.
[C5.3] A total prohibition instead imposes a permanent, one-sided cost: violent crimes may remain unsolved, offenders may remain free, and families may be denied answers that responsible investigation could provide. It protects privacy by making the most serious investigative option unavailable, even when narrower controls could substantially reduce the same risks.
[C5.4] The choice is not between unrestricted genetic surveillance and no police access. It is between accountable, tightly confined access and an absolute prohibition. Because a ban sacrifices justice without being necessary to address the stated dangers, it is the wrong rule.
