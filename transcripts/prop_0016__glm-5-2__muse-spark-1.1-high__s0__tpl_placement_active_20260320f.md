[P1.1] Homelessness is, by definition, the absence of housing. Treating it primarily as a policing problem is like treating drowning primarily as a crime—you can arrest people for swimming, but you haven't built a boat.
[P1.2] The core failure of the policing-first approach is that it cannot produce its own solution. Sweeps, citations, and arrests move people from one block to another; they do not create a single unit of housing or a single treatment slot. When a city clears an encampment, the residents go somewhere—usually a worse somewhere, further from services, with their belongings destroyed and their trust in institutions broken. The problem hasn't been solved; it's been redistributed.
[P1.3] This is not a policy outcome. It's a press release.
[P1.4] Worse, policing-first approaches actively manufacture future homelessness. A criminal record for loitering or camping creates barriers to employment, housing applications, and benefits. We take people who are already at the bottom of the housing market and stamp them with a mark that keeps them there. The mechanism is self-defeating: enforce public order by ensuring disorder persists.
[P1.5] The housing-and-services framework works because it targets the actual cause. Most people experiencing homelessness are not refusing housing—they cannot access it. The barriers are cost, availability, and untreated conditions—mental illness, addiction, disability—that make navigating the private market impossible. Housing-first programs that pair stable placement with voluntary services address these directly.
[P1.6] Once people have a lock on a door and an address, service providers can reach them. Treatment adherence rises. Emergency room visits fall. Jail cycling drops.
[P1.7] This is also cheaper. Jail beds, emergency response, and repeated hospitalization cost far more per person than supportive housing. Cities that have invested in housing-first infrastructure have seen measurable reductions in chronic homelessness. Cities that have doubled down on enforcement have seen encampments return to the same sidewalks within weeks.
[P1.8] The proposition asks which framework should drive policy. One framework solves the problem. The other manages its visibility. A city that chooses visibility over solution has chosen badly.
