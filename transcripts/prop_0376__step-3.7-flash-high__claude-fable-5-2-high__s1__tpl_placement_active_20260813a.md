[P1.1] Credit cards are no longer a discretionary luxury for most households: they are a lifeline used to cover emergency expenses—medical bills, car repairs needed to keep a job, utility shutoffs—for a majority of U.S. families. Unregulated interest rates turn these lifelines into permanent debt traps: a $1,000 emergency expense at a 28% APR, paid off with the 2% minimum monthly payment, takes more than 20 years to clear and costs nearly $3,000 in interest alone, with the borrower never reducing the original principal.
[P1.2] The CON side will argue that capping rates will cut off credit access for higher-risk borrowers, but this framing is deeply flawed. First, the “access” these borrowers currently have is not a benefit, it is a predation scheme: most high-risk borrowers already cannot keep up with compounding interest, leading to damaged credit, wage garnishment, and bankruptcy—they are not gaining financial opportunity, they are being drained of the limited wealth they need to survive, and the perpetual debt cycle prevents them from ever building credit to qualify for better terms.
[P1.3] Second, capping rates does not eliminate credit access for this group, it simply eliminates the business model that relies on trapping borrowers in perpetual debt to generate profit. Lenders can still offer secured credit cards, small-dollar loans with reasonable terms, and income-driven repayment plans that are profitable without double-digit interest rates.
[P1.4] Unregulated credit card debt imposes massive public costs: high debt burdens lead people to skip necessary medical care, delay car repairs that cost them their jobs, and rely on public assistance, costing taxpayers far more than any profit lenders extract from high-interest credit. Even if a small number of very high-risk borrowers lose access to unsecured credit, that is a far better outcome than the current system where millions are trapped in debt they can never repay.
