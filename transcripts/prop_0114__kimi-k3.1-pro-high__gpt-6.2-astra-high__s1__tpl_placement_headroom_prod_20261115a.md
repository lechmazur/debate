[P1.1] A woman who leaves paid work for seven years to keep her father safe does not merely lose seven years of wages. She loses contributions, entitlement, and sometimes a decent old age, while the state benefits from the care she supplied. Calling that arrangement “neutral” is a fiction: the pension system counts some necessary work and erases the rest.
[P1.2] Pension credits correct an injustice: insurance against old-age poverty should not evaporate because someone performs labor without an employer. This is not a gift for being related to somebody. It is deferred social insurance for verified, substantial care that displaces paid work or prevents institutional support. The caregiver still bears lost income and restricted choices; the credit simply stops society from charging her twice, first in the present and again in retirement.
[P1.3] The economic objection gets the direction backwards. Families already provide care that would otherwise require public services, paid aides, or avoidable hospital stays. Pension credits do not purchase all that care; they recognize its retirement consequences and make a public liability visible. It concentrates on caregivers, disproportionately women, and then leaves many of them more dependent on the state later.
[P1.4] A workable scheme can target sustained care for children or people with serious illness or disability, use existing care and benefit records plus attestation, impose sensible caps, and credit any caregiver regardless of sex. Fraud risks justify verification, not invisibility. Nor do credits excuse governments from funding paid leave and services. They answer a narrower, unavoidable question: when families still provide indispensable care, will pensions recognize it?
[P1.5] The alternative is to accept the labor, enjoy the savings, and abandon the worker at retirement. That is not fiscal prudence; it is an invisible subsidy extracted from the people least able to refuse.
