[C1.1] Compensated organ donation, even under the banner of strict regulation, turns a life‑saving act into a transaction that corrodes the very foundation of transplant medicine. The first casualty is altruism. Decades of evidence from blood and plasma donation show that introducing payment crowds out voluntary giving; when people can be paid, the moral impulse to donate for free weakens. A system that monetises kidneys risks shrinking the pool of unpaid donors, which currently supplies the majority of transplants in most nations.
[C1.2] Experience with paid plasma in the United States shows that even tightly regulated markets produce donor fatigue and health disparities.
[C1.3] The second, and more dangerous, casualty is the vulnerable. Regulation can set standards for screening and follow‑up, but it cannot erase the power imbalance between a desperate, low‑income individual and a well‑funded recipient or transplant centre. History from Iran, the only nation with a legal kidney market, reveals that even state‑supervised schemes produce coercion: reports describe donors pressured by family debt, inadequate post‑operative care, and a rise in black‑market activity alongside the official programme. The claim that legalisation eliminates trafficking is contradicted by data showing that legal markets often create new demand that fuels illegal channels.
[C1.4] Medical risk compounds the moral failure. Removing a kidney carries long‑term health consequences—hypertension, reduced renal reserve—that a one‑time payment cannot compensate. [C1.5] Finally, the proposition ignores viable alternatives.
