[P1.1] Child poverty is an income measurement. A child is poor because the household lacks money. The most cost-effective way to fix a shortage of money is to deliver money. Everything else in this debate is a detour around that fact.
[P1.2] Consider what each dollar has to do. A monthly child tax credit dollar goes from the Treasury to a family's bank account through infrastructure that already exists. Nearly all of it lands. A childcare subsidy dollar must first find a licensed provider with an open slot, survive an eligibility redetermination, pay the provider's rent and staff, and only then—if the parent secures a job whose earnings exceed the poverty line—does it register as a child lifted out of poverty.
[P1.3] Every link in that chain leaks. Cash has no chain.
[P1.4] Now consider who each dollar reaches. Childcare subsidies structurally skip the poorest children: infants whose parents can't find any care, kids in rural counties with no providers, children whose parents work nights or irregular shifts, families with a disabled parent, and anyone who doesn't complete the paperwork—which, in practice, is most eligible families. Cash reaches the stay-at-home parent in a childcare desert just as easily as the nurse in the suburbs. The deepest poverty is precisely where subsidies fail and cash succeeds.
[P1.5] We have a live test. In 2021, the United States sent monthly child credit payments to nearly every family. Child poverty fell to roughly its lowest recorded level—cut by something like a third or more—and food hardship dropped within weeks. When the payments stopped, poverty jumped right back.

Childcare subsidies create lasting change by enabling parental employment. Quebec’s $5/day childcare program increased maternal labor force participation by 5-8%, with the largest gains among low-income, single, and less-educated mothers—precisely the families most at risk of persistent poverty. These are sustained earnings that permanently lift families out of poverty, not temporary transfers.
[C1.2] Cost-effectiveness must account for long-term impact. Childcare delivers a double dividend: immediate poverty reduction through parental work, and lasting gains through early childhood development. Cash transfers produce no comparable multiplier effect on human capital.
[C1.3] Universality is the child tax credit’s fatal flaw for efficiency. Approximately 25% of credit dollars go to families earning over $75,000—wasteful spending for poverty reduction. Childcare subsidies are means-tested, ensuring every dollar serves families in genuine need.
[C1.4] The PRO overstates subsidy leakage. Provider capacity can be expanded through strategic investment, and administrative costs are modest compared to long-term benefits. Meanwhile, cash programs face their own inefficiencies: fraud, misuse, and potential work disincentives.
[C1.5] For equivalent spending, childcare subsidies produce more durable poverty reduction by addressing root causes—enabling parents to work and building children’s human capital—while cash offers only temporary relief. The motion’s claim that tax credits are more cost-effective is unsupported.
