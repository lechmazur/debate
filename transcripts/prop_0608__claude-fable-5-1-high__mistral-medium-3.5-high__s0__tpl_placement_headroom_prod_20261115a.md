[P1.1] "Net-zero" is a physical claim about the atmosphere, not a marketing adjective. When a company says it is net-zero, it is telling customers and investors that its activities add nothing to the carbon stock. Right now, that claim can be bought almost entirely with cheap credits, and the arithmetic rarely holds. A ton emitted from a smokestack is a certainty.
[P1.2] A ton "avoided" by paying a project not to cut a forest is a counterfactual: it depends on the forest being genuinely at risk, on it staying standing for a century, on nobody else selling the same ton, and on the deforestation not simply shifting next door. Years of investigations into avoided-deforestation and renewable-energy credits have found that a large portion delivered little or nothing. Offsetting a certain ton with a speculative one is not neutrality; it is accounting.
[P1.3] The deeper problem is substitution. When credits sell for a few dollars per ton and re-engineering a cement kiln or electrifying a truck fleet costs far more, a rational executive buys the credit. Unlimited offsetting therefore does not add to decarbonization; it replaces it. And in-value-chain cuts are the only ones that compound: every fleet electrified and every furnace converted pushes down the cost curve for the next company, as solar and batteries have shown.
[P1.4] Credits buy no learning.
[P1.5] A legal cap fixes the incentive. If roughly ninety percent of reductions must come from a company's own operations and supply chain, the capital flows where the physical change happens, and honest firms stop being undercut by rivals who bought their virtue at a discount. The cap also preserves a real role for high-quality removals: the genuinely hard residual in aviation, steel, and cement. That is why the proposal says "small share," not zero.
[P1.6] The question is simple: should the law let a company call itself net-zero while its own emissions barely move? It should not.

The proposal’s arbitrary cap would force companies to prioritize expensive in-value-chain cuts over cheaper external reductions, reducing total tons abated per dollar. A ton of CO2 avoided in Brazil is physically identical to one cut in Berlin; the atmosphere makes no distinction.
[C1.2] By ignoring this fundamental equivalence, the cap sacrifices economic efficiency for no environmental benefit.
[C1.3] For hard-to-abate sectors like aviation, steel, and cement, in-value-chain decarbonization remains technologically immature or prohibitively expensive, with no near-term solutions for many processes. The cap would make net-zero claims impossible for these industries, discouraging the very investment and innovation needed to develop breakthrough technologies. The ‘small share’ figure is arbitrary with no scientific or economic justification for the 10% threshold; it offers no principle for why this percentage is right rather than any other. Different industries have vastly different abatement cost curves; a one-size-fits-all legal limit is economic nonsense that would lock in higher costs and slower progress where reductions are hardest.
[C1.4] The voluntary carbon market has already mobilized billions of dollars and driven down the cost of abatement technologies through scale and learning-by-doing. Restricting demand now would strangle this market just as it begins to deliver. The genuine concerns about offset quality can be addressed through improved standards and verification, not through the blunt instrument of an arbitrary legal cap. Offsets are a vital bridge to a decarbonized economy; dismantling them now would be counterproductive.
