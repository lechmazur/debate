[P1.1] The question is who may turn a dead person's inner life into a product. Emails, voice notes, photographs, therapy messages, and private jokes are not inert data; they are residues of personality. A model that answers "I" as the deceased, in that voice and with those memories, creates new speech attributed to someone who never chose it.
[P1.2] Death does not erase that choice. Wills control property after death; bodies, reputations, and last wishes are governed by advance choices. If silence becomes permission for companies, consent is inverted: only people who found the right opt-out clause in time keep control of their digital identity. A right asserted from the grave is no right.
[P1.3] The harm is concrete. A grieving teenager asks a dead father's chatbot for advice and receives sentences stitched from private letters, old arguments, and voice messages. Being remembered is different from being scripted, updated, tested, and monetized. Families can split; friends whose intimate messages were swept into training never agreed.
[P1.4] Server possession should not settle that conflict.
[P1.5] Nor is this a ban on technology. The rule is clean: ask while the person is alive, get an explicit opt-in before death, proceed; no opt-in, no mimicry. Legal prohibition is needed because companies can design consent flows, but voluntary policies fail where the strongest claimant is absent and model training is hard to unwind. Silence, public posts, or a relative's grief are not a license.
[P1.6] Practical benefits do not outweigh this boundary. Memorial archives, recordings, and quotations remain available; the line is crossed when a firm builds an interactive proxy that speaks as the dead. Genuine chosen memorials survive an opt-in rule; opportunistic ones do not. The burden of one clear question belongs on the company seeking profit from identity, not on every person forced to police an afterlife.
[P1.7] If companies want someone's voice after death, they should ask while that person can answer.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like consent, but in practice it is a near-total ban disguised as a checkbox. Almost no one who has died explicitly opted in to AI mimicry — the technology and forms did not exist for them. Under this rule, families can never choose it later, historians can never build it, and companies must treat silence from 1920, 1980, or last year as a permanent veto. That does not restore control to the dead; it takes control from everyone living who knew, loved, or studies them.
[C1.2] After death, we do not normally require advance, medium-specific permission for every new use of a person's words. Executors publish diaries and letters, families donate photographs, filmmakers cast actors to play real people, and archives digitize voices — without proof the person opted in to that format. We trust estates and close relatives to balance dignity, memory, and public value, and intervene against fraud or exploitation. An interactive memorial should be governed the same way, not singled out because the medium is new.
[C1.3] The data is also rarely the deceased's alone. A father's messages live in his daughter's inbox; a teacher's lessons in students' recordings; a writer's drafts in an editor's files. Giving past silence absolute priority erases the legitimate interests of those co-creators and recipients, who may want a chatbot to preserve stories, teach children who never met a grandparent, or let witnesses answer future students' questions in their own words.
[C1.4] Abuse deserves law — clear labeling that this is a simulation, respect for any lifetime objection, next-of-kin authorization for private data, rights to shut it down, and bans on deceptive commercial endorsement. Those rules target deception and exploitation without outlawing consensual remembrance by the family itself.
[C1.5] Grief, history, and education are not corporate loopholes. They are reasons the decision should belong to living stewards under safeguards, not be foreclosed forever because a form was never signed.
