[P1.1] Religious exemptions from anti-discrimination law exist for a specific reason: to protect the integrity of religious practice itself—worship, sacraments, the selection of clergy, religious instruction. That protection is legitimate and worth preserving. But the moment a religious institution steps into the commercial marketplace—operating a hospital, running a university open to students of all faiths, managing a business serving the general public—it takes on a different role with different obligations.
[P1.2] The core issue is third-party harm. When a church selects its own ministers, the only people affected are voluntary participants in that faith community. But when a religiously affiliated hospital refuses to hire a qualified nurse because she is the wrong religion, or a religious university fires a staff member for marrying someone of the same sex, the people harmed are not voluntary participants in that religious community. They are employees and consumers in the secular economy who deserve the same legal protections as anyone else's employees and customers.
[P1.3] Broad commercial exemptions also create competitive distortions. A secular hospital must follow anti-discrimination rules. A religiously affiliated hospital across town, if exempt, gains a legal permission its competitor lacks—to fire, refuse to hire, or deny service on grounds the law otherwise prohibits. That is not religious freedom; it is a market advantage dressed up as one.
[P1.4] The line this proposition draws is both principled and workable. Core religious functions—clergy selection, worship, sacraments, doctrinal teaching—receive exemptions. Commercial activity that serves and affects the broader public does not. This does not force religious institutions to abandon their beliefs.
[P1.5] It simply says that when you choose to operate in the marketplace, you follow the same marketplace rules as everyone else. That is not hostility to religion—it is fairness to everyone else.
