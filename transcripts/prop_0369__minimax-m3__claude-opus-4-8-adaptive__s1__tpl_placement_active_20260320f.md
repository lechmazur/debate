[P1.1] The motion turns on a single behavioral fact: defaults are sticky. When a food-delivery app opens on a default of "deliver now, fastest option, tip suggested at twenty percent," most users accept that path. They don't comparison-shop the fee structure, they don't ask whether pickup across the street would save them six dollars, and they don't notice that the so-called "service fee" is really platform margin. The default is doing the work, not the user's deliberation.
[P1.2] That default is also a confession of interest. Apps make money on every layer of fulfillment: delivery fee, service fee, priority dispatch, surge pricing. They have every reason to keep the cheapest path hidden; they have no economic reason to surface it. A user who walks to the restaurant pays the restaurant the same, and pays the app nothing.
[P1.3] So the platform's incentive is to make delivery feel frictionless and pickup feel like an afterthought, even when pickup is the rational choice for the user, the restaurant, and the city.
[P1.4] This is not a market outcome, it is a market manipulation. The user thinks they are choosing; the architecture has already chosen for them. The proposition does not ban expensive options, restrict user freedom, or cap fees. It asks for one change: stop rigging the choice architecture.
[P1.5] Default to pickup. Default to the lowest-fee fulfillment path. Default to scheduled delivery when it is cheaper and greener. The user who genuinely wants the expedited option can still pick it with one tap.
[P1.6] Picture the concrete case: a worker on a lunch break, opening an app, seeing "delivery in twenty-five minutes" already selected. The same order, for pickup ten minutes away, costs half as much. The user never sees the comparison because the app never shows it. That hidden cost, repeated millions of times a day, is the harm.
[P1.7] And that is exactly what defaulting to the lower-fee path fixes.
