[P1.3] The result is not worker security—it is worker exclusion. You cannot protect people who never get hired in the first place.

[C1.1] The proposition offers India a trade: surrender binding employment protections now, receive universal social protection later. My objection is simple—the two halves are not equally deliverable, and that asymmetry is decisive.
[C1.2] Labor law liberalization is a single legislative act. Once retrenchment permission is gone, once fixed-term contracts can cycle indefinitely, the change is immediate and effectively irreversible—employers organize politically to defend it. Universal social protection is the opposite kind of project. It requires a tax base that reaches informal income, a registry that identifies hundreds of millions of workers who move between agriculture, construction, and petty trade, a claims bureaucracy, and sustained fiscal commitment across electoral cycles.
[C1.3] India's existing schemes—whether for rural employment guarantee or subsidized food—show how hard it is to deliver benefits cleanly at scale even with political will. Leakage, exclusion errors, delayed payments, and state-level variation are not hypothetical.
[C1.4] So the sequencing problem is the whole debate. PRO asks workers to give up the one protection they might actually enforce in court today in exchange for a benefit whose arrival date, adequacy, and portability depend on administrative capacity that does not yet exist. If the protection under-delivers—and it will, at least for years—workers are left with flexibility and nothing else. That is not a trade.
[C1.5] It is a demolition with a promissory note attached.
[C1.6] The Nordic "flexicurity" analogy smuggles in the very institutions it pretends are optional. Those systems pair easy dismissal with generous, well-administered benefits, near-universal union coverage, and active retraining. India has vast informality, fragmented governance, and weak worker organization. Copying the employer-facing half and promising the worker-facing half is exactly how these reforms fail worldwide.
[C1.7] Finally, look at who bears the transition risk. A garment worker in Tirupur cannot insure against the gap between enactment and delivery. Employers can price it. The proposition socializes the risk downward and privatizes the gains.
