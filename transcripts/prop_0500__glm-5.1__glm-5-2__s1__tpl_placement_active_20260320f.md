Road closures from pipe repairs and cable laying cost economies billions annually in lost productivity, diverted commerce, and pure human frustration. [C1.1] The proposition asks governments to pour vast sums into underground tunneling because surface disruption and land scarcity supposedly make it "increasingly necessary." That framing collapses on contact with financial reality. Boring costs are not a minor premium. Urban tunneling routinely runs several times the cost per kilometer of surface or elevated alternatives—sometimes an order of magnitude more.
[C1.2] No amount of hand-waving about "full lifecycle accounting" erases the fact that a tunnel consuming three billion dollars could have built five to ten surface projects serving five to ten times the population.
[C1.3] That is the core tradeoff the proposition hides. Every dollar spent underground is a dollar not spent expanding transit coverage, upgrading aging pipes across an entire city, or building protected bike lanes and bus rapid transit that deliver immediate benefits at a fraction of the cost. The opportunity cost is not theoretical. Cities that commit to mega-tunnel projects often end up with one impressive line and a depleted capital budget for a generation.
[C1.4] The resilience claim is selectively dangerous. Tunnels are indeed shielded from some surface weather. But they are uniquely vulnerable to flooding—and as recent events in cities worldwide have shown, a flooded subway or utility tunnel is not a minor inconvenience. It is a catastrophic, multi-month outage.
[C1.5] Underground infrastructure is harder to inspect, harder to repair, and harder to evacuate. When it fails, it fails completely.
[C1.6] The land-constraint argument is real but overstated. Dense cities still have wide arterials, rail corridors, and highway rights-of-way that can accommodate utilities and transit without deep boring. Elevated structures use minimal footprint and cost dramatically less. Surface disruption is manageable through scheduling, trenchless techniques for utilities, and phased construction.
[C1.7] These are incremental problems with incremental solutions.
[C1.8] The proposition demands the most expensive possible answer to problems that have cheaper, faster, more flexible alternatives. That is not vision. It is fiscal negligence dressed up as long-term thinking.
