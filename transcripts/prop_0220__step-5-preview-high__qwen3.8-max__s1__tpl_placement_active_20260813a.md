[P1.1] Long-run financial security is not built by subsidizing the people who already save; it is built by getting everyone else to save. Automatic enrollment with an opt-out does exactly that. Increasing tax deductions, by contrast, relies on active choice—something most households reliably fail to exercise when it comes to retirement.
[P1.2] The core problem is friction. To benefit from a larger deduction, a worker must notice the option, project future taxes, have spare cash, and adjust withholding or contributions before payday arrives. Every one of those steps loses people. Behavioral defaults remove that friction: when a worker is automatically enrolled, inertia works in favor of saving rather than against it.
[P1.3] Imagine a 25-year-old grocery clerk who has never opened a retirement account. Left to voluntary choice, she saves nothing. With an opt-out default, she contributes three percent of pay simply because doing nothing preserves the status quo. That is not paternalism; it is design that respects how humans actually make decisions.
[P1.4] Distributional effects make the gap wider. Tax deductions have little value to households in low brackets or with little tax liability, and they are capped in ways that matter most to higher earners. They reward contributions that would likely have happened anyway. Automatic enrollment, however, captures the non-savers—the low- and moderate-income workers whose retirement security is genuinely at risk.
[P1.5] It converts a minority habit into a baseline expectation.
[P1.6] Over decades, the difference compounds. A small, automatic contribution starting in one’s twenties builds substantial balances through continuity and market exposure. A deduction for a saver who was already contributing merely improves an existing balance. The proposition is not about which policy sounds more generous on a tax form; it is about which policy produces broader, more durable participation.
[P1.7] Automatic enrollment wins because it meets households where they are—busy, cash-strapped, and unlikely to opt in on their own.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes enrollment for security. Long-run financial security is measured by durable balances that replace income, not by the number of accounts created. Automatic enrollment may raise participation, but it mainly works only where employer payroll plans exist, and it often produces small default contributions that can be stopped or withdrawn. Increasing tax deductions, by contrast, rewards actual saving and lowers the cost of contributing across more work arrangements.
[C1.2] Defaults are often too weak to solve the real problem. Many automatic plans begin at low contribution rates, and inertia can trap workers at inadequate levels just as easily as it can start them saving. PRO’s grocery clerk illustrates the flaw: being enrolled at three percent does not mean she can afford to leave that money untouched. For households facing unstable pay, rent increases, medical bills, or high-interest debt, the opt-out is not a failure of design; it is a necessary escape valve.
[C1.3] If automatic saving pushes them into cash shortages, the result may be credit-card debt, plan loans, or early withdrawals—outcomes that damage long-run security.
[C1.4] Tax deductions operate where saving can be sustained: at the margin. When the after-tax cost of contributing falls, households with some capacity have a concrete reason to increase voluntary contributions. A worker who raises contributions because the tax incentive makes saving affordable can build a far larger balance than one parked at a token default. A deduction can also reach a freelancer or small-business worker who voluntarily opens a retirement account, while automatic enrollment does nothing for workers without an employer plan.
[C1.5] Because voluntary contributions are chosen to fit a household’s budget, they are less likely to be reversed under financial stress.
[C1.6] The proposition offers broad but shallow participation: more accounts, often low balances, and more liquidity risk. The better route to long-run security is deeper, affordable saving that produces larger balances and survives life’s emergencies.
