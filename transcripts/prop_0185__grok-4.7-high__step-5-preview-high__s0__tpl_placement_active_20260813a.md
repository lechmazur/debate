[P1.1] Every democracy faces the same fiscal trap. Spending is popular, taxes are not, and the costs of borrowing arrive after the next election. Legislators who restrain themselves lose to those who do not. Over time, that selection pressure produces chronic deficits, debt that compounds faster than the economy, and eventual crises in which adjustment is sudden, unfair, and economically destructive.
[P1.2] Leaving fiscal discipline to legislative discretion does not express democratic choice. It expresses a collective-action failure that no individual legislature can solve.
[P1.3] A constitutional balanced-budget requirement is the commitment device that failure demands. It changes the baseline from borrow-unless-we-choose-not-to to balance-unless-a-narrow-precommitted-exception-applies. Because amending a constitution is harder than passing a budget, the rule survives the political pressures that would repeal a statute the moment it bites. Politicians can still set priorities.
[P1.4] They simply cannot finance those priorities by quietly shifting the bill onto people who are not in the room.
[P1.5] The automatic-stabilizer exception keeps the rule from doing macroeconomic damage. When a recession hits, unemployment benefits rise and tax receipts fall without any new vote. Those movements cushion demand automatically. A well-designed provision lets them operate and requires the budget to balance only after they are accounted for, or over the cycle, not in every year of distress.
[P1.6] What it blocks is the abuse discretion invites: labeling permanent tax cuts or new entitlements as stimulus, rolling emergency spending forward year after year, and treating binding limits as suggestions.
[P1.7] Discretion has had decades to prove it can police itself. Debt ratios jump in bad times and fail to retreat in good ones. Constitutional balance with stabilizer exceptions does not pretend politics will become virtuous. It removes the easiest path to fiscal evasion and preserves the stabilizers that actually work.
[P1.8] That is the superior rule.

[C1.1] PRO describes a real fiscal trap, but constitutionalizing an outcome is the wrong cure. The decisive question is not whether deficits can be dangerous; it is whether a permanent constitutional rule produces more fiscal resilience than ordinary law under political accountability. It does not.
[C1.2] First, the stabilizer exception is the proposal’s pressure point. Automatic stabilizers do not appear on a ledger; balance requires estimates of potential output, trend revenue, and counterfactual spending. In a recession, a constitutional rule turns forecast disagreement into constitutional conflict. Courts or fiscal councils must decide whether a deficit is automatic or discretionary.
[C1.3] Either the exception is narrow and cuts arrive mid-recession—destroying demand and the tax base—or it is broad enough to let governments label permanent tax cuts and entitlements as stimulus, the abuse PRO concedes at [P1.6].
[C1.4] Second, constitutional constraints do not repeal politics; they relocate it. If debt is off-limits, officials borrow implicitly through guarantees, public enterprises, pension liabilities, tax expenditures, one-off asset sales and off-budget funds. Those channels are less transparent than an ordinary deficit and harder to correct. Balanced-budget rules have long coexisted with gimmicky timing and hidden liabilities.
[C1.5] Third, lock-in carries a democratic cost. Balance can be right in calm years and catastrophic after war, pandemic, demographic shock, or infrastructure emergency. Constitutions protect rights and allocate power; they are poor instruments for annual macroeconomic calibration.
[C1.6] Legislative discretion need not mean indiscipline. Independent fiscal councils, medium-term targets, multi-year appropriations and explicit emergency criteria can constrain governments without constitutional paralysis. Those rules remain strong when they bite but amendable when facts change. That is not weakness; it is governable adaptability.
[C1.7] Against a real threat, PRO offers a permanent rule whose recession behavior is either pro-cyclical or porous, and whose discipline can be evaded in darkness. Discretion, disciplined by transparent ordinary law, preserves the ability to act and correct mistakes without constitutional crisis. That is the better fiscal system.

[P2.7] Lock-in is not annual macroeconomic management. Legislatures still set rates and priorities. They lose only the power to bill the unborn. A pandemic expands stabilizers and tax shortfalls automatically; what discretion has failed to do is unwind the ratchet once the shock passes.
