[P1.1] Per public dollar, nuclear life‑extension and new‑build will cut emissions faster than an equivalent wind‑solar‑battery scale‑up between 2026 and 2036. The reason is simple: nuclear delivers baseload, high‑capacity‑factor power at a cost that is already sunk, so every dollar spent buys near‑continuous clean electricity. Extending the life of an existing reactor costs roughly $1–2 k per kilowatt and can add a decade of operation within two to three years. A comparable new‑build, even at $5–7 k/kW, still produces electricity at a levelized cost of $30–$40/MWh, far below the $50–$80/MWh that wind, solar and the required storage typically achieve when they must be paired to provide firm capacity.
[P1.2] Consider the numbers. Replacing 20 GW of coal with nuclear life‑extension would require about $20 billion in public subsidies and would eliminate roughly 150 MtCO₂ per year, a cost of $130 per tonne avoided. To achieve the same annual displacement with wind, solar and batteries you must install roughly 60 GW of wind, 80 GW of solar and 30 GW‑hours of storage, because capacity factors are 35 % for wind, 25 % for solar and storage must cover the remaining 40 % of demand.
[P1.3] The capital outlay for that mix, even at optimistic $1 k/kW for wind and $0.8 k/kW for solar, plus $300/kWh for storage, climbs to $45–55 billion—more than double the nuclear spend, while delivering the same emissions cut only after a longer build‑out and with higher curtailment losses.
[P1.4] Nuclear also avoids the hidden emissions of manufacturing, transporting and installing massive battery fleets, and it eliminates the need for backup gas peakers that wind‑solar systems still require. Because the marginal cost of running an existing reactor is near zero, each public dollar goes straight to displacing fossil generation, not to paying for storage or land acquisition. Over the 2026‑2036 window, that translates into a faster, cheaper pathway to the emissions reductions we need.
