[P1.1] Migrant workers already embedded in domestic industries have accumulated skills, relationships, and institutional knowledge that cannot be replaced overnight. Removing them forces employers to restart hiring, training, and compliance processes from scratch, raising unit labor costs and risking output shortfalls in sectors such as agriculture, logistics, and elder care. Long-term status converts these sunk investments into predictable workforce capacity rather than treating workers as interchangeable and expendable.
[P1.2] Stricter return enforcement, by contrast, generates recurring disruption. Employers facing repeated vacancies must either accept lower productivity during onboarding periods or bid up wages to attract scarce local applicants who often prove unavailable. The result is higher consumer prices and reduced competitiveness without shrinking overall labor demand.
[P1.3] Integrated workers also anchor secondary economic activity. Their continued presence sustains rental markets, school enrollment, and local tax bases that would shrink under mass removal. Shifting to permanent or multi-year status stabilizes those flows and reduces the fiscal drag of enforcement operations, detention, and litigation that accompany large-scale deportation efforts.
[P1.4] The practical alternative—prioritizing status—aligns legal recognition with labor-market facts already on the ground. It rewards compliance with workplace rules and community norms while limiting the administrative waste of cycling proven workers out and inexperienced replacements in.
