[P1.1] Every substandard home built today in a wealthy nation is an expensive future renovation in disguise. Housing lasts half a century or more; building new homes that already require future overhauls means knowingly pouring concrete around yesterday’s mistakes. Notice the precision of this mandate: net-zero-energy-ready does not force costly solar panels onto every roof tomorrow. It simply requires doing what is cheap while framing is open and punishingly expensive once drywall goes up: tight insulation, efficient windows, electric heating, and simple conduit for future solar or EVs.
[P1.2] First, building right once beats paying twice. During initial construction, sealing the envelope and installing an adequate electrical panel adds only a modest marginal cost, which shrinks rapidly as universal codes scale supply chains and standardize builder practices. Crucially, that upfront investment rolls into a long-term mortgage and pays families back from month one through drastically lower utility bills. Relying on later retrofits turns routine framing upgrades into invasive surgery: ripping open finished walls, scrapping heating systems early, rewiring panels, and financing the disruption through lump-sum savings or high-interest loans.
[P1.3] Second, only upfront standards solve the split-incentive trap and labor bottleneck. Developers do not pay the winter heating bills of the homes they sell, and landlords rarely pay their tenants' electricity costs. Without a universal baseline, builders default to outdated minimums to protect short-term margins, offloading decades of wasted energy and steep retrofit liabilities onto buyers, renters, and taxpayers. Worse, wealthy countries already face acute shortages of electricians and heating technicians.
[P1.4] Relying on retrofits for newly built homes squanders scarce tradespeople fixing avoidable mistakes instead of upgrading genuinely old housing stock.
[P1.5] Calling leaky, obsolete housing "affordable" confuses the sticker price with the true cost of living. Trapping households in volatile utility bills and inevitable renovation debt is a hidden tax. Wealthy countries should stop digging the hole deeper and build homes ready for the future the first time.
