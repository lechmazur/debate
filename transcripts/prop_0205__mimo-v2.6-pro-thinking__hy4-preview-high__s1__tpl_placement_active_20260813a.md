[P1.1] When you set up a new phone, the OS maker decides which wallet pays at checkout, which browser opens your links, and which AI answers your questions—before you've made a single choice. That is not a neutral default; it is a distribution advantage the platform gives itself, at the expense of every rival that must fight for a spot in settings menus most users never touch.
[P1.2] The mechanism is simple and well understood. On a dominant mobile OS, the provider controls both the marketplace and its own entrants. Self-preferencing in defaults converts that gatekeeping power into durable advantages in adjacent markets—payments, browsing, AI assistants—where the OS provider is not entitled to win. Defaults are sticky: most people stay with pre-selected apps not because they compared options but because switching is friction.
[P1.3] So the platform wins these adjacent contests on install-day inertia rather than on product quality. That is textbook foreclosure, and it is exactly what competition policy exists to stop.
[P1.4] The harms compound. Rival browsers and assistants never reach the scale to improve—fewer users means less data, less revenue, less iteration. Payment rivals get locked out of tap-to-pay entirely on some devices, so app makers pass on higher fees to consumers. And in AI especially, the OS owner can route the most valuable real estate—the assistant prompt that becomes the new search box—to its own model, letting it harvest queries its competitors never see.
[P1.5] Winner-take-more in one layer becomes winner-take-all across three.
[P1.6] Note what PRO does not demand: not that these companies stop making wallets or assistants, not that users be denied a genuine choice. The proposition bars preferencing in default settings—nothing more. That is a narrow, administrable rule. The platform can compete on the merits; it just cannot rig the starting line.
[P1.7] Anyone defending the status quo must explain why a company that already owns the track should also be allowed to design the starting positions.

The maker’s app occupies that slot because it is best integrated, not because rivals are banned. Remove the preference and the OS ships a worse product: a broken default, a random third party, or a clunky menu.
[C1.3] Ordinary users pay at checkout.
