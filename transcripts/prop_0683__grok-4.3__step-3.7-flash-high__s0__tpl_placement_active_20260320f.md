[P1.1] Social media platforms now serve as the primary infrastructure for speech, association, and commerce, yet they exercise this power without the procedural restraints that limit comparable institutions. Requiring notice, reasons, and a meaningful appeal before suspension or ban corrects the resulting imbalance. Users face sudden loss of audiences, revenue streams, and professional reputations with no guaranteed opportunity to contest the decision or even learn the precise rule violation.
[P1.2] Current voluntary policies leave enforcement to internal teams and algorithms whose criteria remain hidden and changeable. This structure invites selective application: identical conduct receives different treatment depending on the account’s visibility, politics, or external pressure. A legal due-process obligation forces platforms to articulate standards in advance and apply them consistently, reducing the scope for ad-hoc censorship while leaving room for genuine violations to be addressed.
[P1.3] The alternative—relying solely on market pressure or self-regulation—has proven unreliable. High-profile users can sometimes negotiate reinstatement, but ordinary individuals lack equivalent leverage. Mandated procedures close that gap without prohibiting moderation itself; they simply insist that the decision process be visible and reversible when errors occur. That minimal constraint prevents arbitrary deplatforming from functioning as an unchecked veto over participation in public life.
