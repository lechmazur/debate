[P1.1] Cosmetic-procedure marketing is not ordinary product promotion. It sells medical intervention by turning insecurity into demand, often through a trusted, aspirational personality. When that marketing reaches audiences likely to include minors, stronger warnings and age-gating are proportionate safeguards, not censorship.
[P1.2] Minors are especially vulnerable to this sales method. Their identities and body image are still developing; peer approval matters intensely; and they are less equipped to evaluate long-term risk against an immediate promise of beauty or status. Influencer advertising compounds that vulnerability because it resembles personal advice rather than a clinical sales pitch. A polished “before and after,” discount code, or cheerful recovery montage can conceal payment, editing, pain, complications, repeat procedures, and the possibility of disappointment.
[P1.3] The result is not merely an uninformed purchase. It can normalize the belief that ordinary adolescent features are defects requiring medical correction.
[P1.4] Stronger warnings directly correct that distorted presentation. They should be clear, prominent, and attached to the promotional content—not buried in a caption—stating that the post is advertising, that results vary, and that procedures carry medical and psychological risks. Such warnings preserve adult choice while ensuring that persuasion is not mistaken for neutral testimony.
[P1.5] Age-gating addresses the problem warnings cannot solve alone: some audiences should not be behaviorally targeted with this material in the first place. Platforms already classify content and advertising by age and audience profile. Requiring reasonable age controls where an influencer’s following is likely to include minors would reduce deliberate or foreseeable exposure. Perfection is not the standard.
[P1.6] Seatbelts do not prevent every injury; age checks need not catch every teenager to reduce harm.
[P1.7] The alternative leaves the most vulnerable viewers carrying all the risk while marketers capture all the benefit. Parents cannot supervise every personalized feed, and media literacy cannot neutralize an industry designed to make advertising feel intimate and authentic. These rules need not ban discussion of cosmetic procedures, patient experiences, or adult advertising. They simply impose greater care when commercial persuasion meets youth vulnerability, medical risk, and potentially lasting consequences.
