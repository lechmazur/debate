[P1.1] Copyright exists to secure rights in expression originated by human authors. An author makes the creative choices that give a work its original form. When a generative system produces the words, images, or melody and a person only supplies a prompt or picks a preferred result, that person has not authored the output. Protection should be refused unless the human can document substantial creative control over the final expression: proof that human judgment, not the model, fixed the choices that make the work original.
[P1.2] That rule follows from copyright’s purpose. Exclusive rights encourage people to create. A model needs no royalty to keep generating, and owning what it composes is a windfall, not an incentive. Extending protection lets firms stockpile claims over algorithmic recombinations, thickens disputes, and shrinks the space for human artists.
[P1.3] The public gets a monopoly without the creativity that monopoly was meant to buy.
[P1.4] Calling the model a tool does not erase the difference. A camera records the frame the photographer chose. A typewriter records the sentences the writer formed. A generative system, given a brief instruction, invents structure, diction, composition, and detail the user never decided.
[P1.5] Selecting among outputs or making cosmetic tweaks is not controlling expression. Substantial creative control means the human determines and revises the expressive core—arrangement, selection, specific language or visual decisions—so the finished work reflects those choices rather than model defaults.
[P1.6] Documentation makes the distinction enforceable. Without a record of the human’s interventions, every claimant can insist after the fact that control was substantial. Offices and courts would credit narratives instead of evidence. Requiring documentation of the creative steps taken protects genuine collaborators and stops mere prompting from masquerading as authorship.
[P1.7] Human direction that truly shapes the result remains protectable. Purely generated expression does not. Copyright should track creative control, not the mere act of pressing generate.

[C1.1] The proposition should fail because it replaces copyright’s ordinary question—has a human author fixed original expression, and was that expression copied?—with a process audit. [P1.1] treats authorship as if the human must have “fixed the choices that make the work original.” But a photographer does not fix every grain, reflection, or automatic correction; a digital artist does not specify every pixel produced by a filter, brush engine, or renderer. Those works are protected because human choices of subject, framing, parameters, selection, and revision are enough.
[C1.2] Generative surprise is not legal orphanhood.
[C1.3] The damaging word in the motion is “document.” American copyright has not generally made ownership depend on a certified diary of creativity. [P1.6] says records prevent after-the-fact stories, but records can be staged: prompts, screenshots, and version histories are easiest for big firms. A rule demanding “substantial creative control” in documented form would burden freelancers and small studios, whose work is iterative across chats, sketches, edits, clients, and tools. A copier could reproduce an AI-generated poster, then demand proof that the artist controlled each curve and leaf.
[C1.4] Documentation also mismatches the harm. The real issue is scope: human-authored selection, arrangement, wording, composition, and edits are protected; ideas, styles, facts, and independently created similarities are not. Overbroad assertions can be narrowed or refused without voiding an entire work because its creators lack the right paper trail. Existing limits—originality, independent creation, fair use, thin protection—address the “stockpile” fear in [P1.2] more directly than blanket evidentiary forfeiture.
[C1.5] PRO’s incentive story is backwards. Presses, cameras, synthesizers, and rendering engines need no royalties either, yet human works made with them are protected because people need incentives to design, select, refine, and distribute. If AI-generated outputs become unprotectable unless substantial control over the final expression is documented, the windfall goes to infringers and platforms, not the public. Copyright should examine human authorship and copying, not turn creation into a compliance file.
