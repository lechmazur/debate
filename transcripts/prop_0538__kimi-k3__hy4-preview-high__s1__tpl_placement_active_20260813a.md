[P1.1] For six decades, the arrangement has been simple: Africa ships out what it digs and grows, and buys back what others make from it. Cocoa leaves Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana as beans and returns as chocolate at ten times the price. Cobalt leaves the Congo and returns inside batteries no Congolese factory assembled. Crude leaves Nigeria and returns as petrol Nigeria pays to import.
[P1.2] This is not trade; it is a relay in which Africa runs the hardest leg and hands off the profit. The motion asks us to stop defending that relay.
[P1.3] First, the money. The value of a commodity sits overwhelmingly in processing, branding, and manufacturing — the stages that happen abroad. The lion's share of the world's cocoa is grown in West Africa, yet West Africa captures only a small fraction of the global chocolate industry's revenue. Every container of raw exports is a container of exported jobs, exported tax base, exported skills.
[P1.4] Second, the fragility. Commodity prices swing violently, and economies built on one or two exports swing with them — budgets collapse, currencies crater, debt spirals, all on decisions made in London or Shanghai. Manufacturing and diversified regional trade smooth those shocks; they compound capability instead of renting out geology.
[P1.5] Third, the market already exists. Over a billion African consumers import food, fuel, fertilizer, and pharmaceuticals from outside the continent — often buying at a distance what a neighbor produces next door. The African Continental Free Trade Area finally gives that demand a continental address. Intra-African trade is disproportionately in manufactured and processed goods, so building it and building manufacturing are the same project, not competing ones.
[P1.6] Expect the other side to praise comparative advantage and existing export revenue. But comparative advantage is not a life sentence — it described South Korea's rice paddies before it described its shipyards. No country has ever grown rich selling raw materials to its former rulers and buying back the finished goods. The question tonight is not whether Africa trades; it is whether Africa keeps trading on terms written [clipped]

[C1.1] The motion’s story is seductive: stop shipping beans and ore, start shipping chocolate and batteries to our neighbors. But the sequencing is backwards. Africa cannot prioritize manufacturing and intra-regional trade over raw-material exports, because those exports are what pay for the machines, technology, and imports that any factory needs. Industry is not conjured from resentment; it is bought with foreign exchange.
[C1.2] Consider what actually builds a factory. The needed machines, turbines, and fertilizer are not made in Accra or Kinshasa; they are imported and paid for in dollars. Those dollars come overwhelmingly from cocoa, oil, copper, and cobalt sold to whoever pays best—often the very former colonial powers and distant markets the motion would deprioritize. If Nigeria stops prioritizing crude exports, it does not magically get Dangote-style refining; it loses the dollars to buy the equipment refining needs.
[C1.3] PRO cites South Korea’s rice paddies becoming shipyards. But South Korea did not industrialize by refusing to sell rice or by concentrating on trade with poor neighbors. It used primary exports to import capital goods, then conquered rich markets in America and Europe. Scale, hard currency, and competitive pressure came from global demand, not from a neighborhood of low-income consumers.
[C1.4] Intra-African trade is small, fragmented, and choked by bad roads; it cannot substitute for the scale of the global market.
[C1.5] The 'relay' metaphor hides a basic fact: the cocoa Ghana sells today funds tomorrow’s schools and clinics. Botswana did not get rich by rejecting diamond exports to London; it used the revenue to build stability. Comparative advantage is not a life sentence, but it is the only ladder we have. The priority is to keep climbing it by earning from raw materials while gradually investing in power, logistics, and skills—not to saw off the rung that is currently holding the economy up.

[C2.1] The motion’s supporters claim they never proposed sawing off the ladder—merely pointing revenue and policy at manufacturing "rather than renting out more geology." But that is the saw. Prioritizing factories and regional markets over raw exports means allocating scarce foreign exchange, credit, and roads to industry first, while the mines and oilfields that earn hard currency get the remainder. [C2.2] The rung is being taxed.
[C2.3] Turn the foreign-exchange argument around, as they invite, and it breaks. Processing multiplies earnings only if someone buys the processed good. Rich markets tax raw inputs lightly but raise duties on processed goods: cocoa and hides enter cheaply, while chocolate and leather hit tariff walls. African neighbors cannot absorb the volume; a low-income regional market cannot replace European and Asian demand at scale.
[C2.4] Force premature processing and you do not multiply foreign exchange; you lose the buyers with hard currency, while the AfCFTA consumer cannot yet pay world prices. The engine stalls at the tariff wall.
[C2.5] South Korea convicts them, too. Korea moved from rice to shipyards by using primary export earnings—and American market access—to import capital goods, then selling manufactures to rich markets, not poor neighbors. Los Angeles mattered because that is where scale and hard currency lived. The motion reverses this: deprioritize distant rich markets for a fragmented continent that cannot pay.
[C2.6] That is not Korea’s lesson; it is a blueprint for uncompetitive factories.
[C2.7] The volatility charge mistakes the disease for the cure. The failed experiment is import substitution: protected factories selling to small markets, collapsing when foreign exchange ran out. From idle refineries to dormant mills, that history came from prioritizing manufacturing before power, skills, and logistics existed. Raw exports are industry’s financier, not its enemy.
[C2.8] Keep the rung, tax it well, and build the ladder—do not jump from the top.
