[P1.1] Secondary schools should split these jobs cleanly. AI tutoring belongs where practice has a checkable target. It should not be the main source of writing feedback, because writing is judged by purpose, voice, and growth, not by a single correct output.
[P1.2] Mastery practice is repetitive and convergent. A student factoring quadratics, conjugating verbs, or retrieving dates needs many attempts, fast correction, and a hint that does not wait for the next class. An AI tutor can supply that volume without exhausting the teacher. The success condition is visible: the step is right, the term is recalled, the procedure holds.
[P1.3] Used that way, AI frees class time for work machines cannot do.
[P1.4] Writing feedback fails that test. A paragraph can be clean and still evade the question, flatten a real experience into cliché, or borrow a voice the student does not own. The useful comment is often “cut the second sentence; the image is the argument,” or “you have not answered the counterclaim a reader will raise.” That judgment depends on the assignment’s audience, earlier drafts, and what this writer is ready to hear. A model has none of that history.
[P1.5] It has patterns, and patterns reward safe, average prose.
[P1.6] Make AI the main reader and students learn to write for the model. They chase praise, accept rewrites they cannot explain, and treat rubric language as the point of the essay. Teachers then mark a product they did not watch being made and lose the signal of who cannot yet sustain an idea across a page. Students without an adult editor at home are likeliest to mistake those comments for instruction.
[P1.7] Grammar flags can sit at the edge. The main source has to remain the teacher, who can compare this draft to last month’s, refuse polished emptiness, and teach revision on purpose. Mastery drills scale. Judgment does not.

[C1.1] The proposition's split sounds tidy, but it would leave most secondary students with less feedback, not better feedback. Writing is not one indivisible act of judgment. It is a stack of skills: a clear thesis, topic sentences, evidence, transitions, grammar, sentence variety, citation, counterargument, revision. Many of those have checkable targets, and all improve with rapid iteration.
[C1.2] AI can be the main source of feedback on those layers—main, not sole—while teachers handle the rare, high-stakes judgments about voice, context, and growth.
[C1.3] Consider a class of thirty. A teacher can give one round of comments per essay every few weeks. That is not enough. Writing improves through multiple drafts, and most students need feedback before the next deadline, not after.
[C1.4] An AI tutor with the assignment, rubric, previous draft, and teacher's priorities can respond within minutes: "your second paragraph changes claim; your evidence does not support it; here are two ways to revise." The student revises, gets another response, and learns revision as a habit. The teacher then reviews the pattern of AI comments, spot-checks the drafts, and intervenes where the writer is stuck. That is a better use of human judgment, not an abdication of it.
[C1.5] PRO says the model lacks history and rewards safe prose. A model given no context does. A model given the assignment, exemplars, and draft history can do much better, and the teacher can audit and correct it. The alternative is not a skilled adult editor for every student.
[C1.6] For students without one at home, the choice is AI feedback or almost none. They are likeliest to be harmed by the proposition's ban.
[C1.7] The real question is not whether AI replaces the teacher's final judgment. It is whether AI can run the feedback cycle that actually produces writers. It can. The teacher remains the editor-in-chief.
[C1.8] The proposition confuses main source with only source and protects a bottleneck.
