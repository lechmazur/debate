[P1.1] Major-city transit is shared civic infrastructure, not a vending-machine service. We pay collectively for sidewalks, streetlights, and libraries because universal access creates spillovers no individual customer will buy. An open bus or train seat is capacity already being produced; filling it is a cleaner, less congesting, more connected use of the city's investment. Charging at the door taxes the very behavior the city wants.
[P1.2] Broad-based taxation is the matching finance. The beneficiaries are not only passengers: employers get a wider labor pool, shops get customers, drivers get less traffic, owners get accessible property, and all residents get cleaner air and a city that can move during emergencies or income shocks. The tax must replace the fare line and protect service, not hollow it out. A fare loads the cost onto the person boarding, often the least able to pay; a citywide tax base with ability-to-pay features spreads the bill across those who gain.
[P1.3] Fare removal also makes the system better. It ends broken card readers, fare disputes, evasion policing, discount bureaucracy, and boarding delays; it gives universal access without forcing poor riders to prove eligibility. Permanence matters because households and employers make durable choices around a default they trust. A temporary pilot asks people to reorganize life around a perk that may vanish; a permanent right to ride lets short trips, job searches, clinic visits, and off-peak errands become ordinary.
[P1.4] So modest initial ridership gains are not a defeat; they expose the poverty of the ridership-only metric. The first-month turnstile count misses lower enforcement costs, less stigma, simpler boarding, income security, and the long-run shift from calculating each ride to using the network naturally. Defending fares protects a revenue stream and a price signal; the better comparison is with the larger social return: a dense city where access to work, school, care, and public life does not depend on coins at the door.

[C1.1] Fare-free transit would blow a hole in city budgets that broad taxation cannot responsibly fill. Major transit systems rely on fares for a substantial portion of operating costs; eliminating them would require billions in new taxes annually for large systems. The PRO admits initial ridership gains may be modest, meaning cities would sacrifice a major revenue stream for minimal immediate benefit. This isn’t a civic investment—it’s a fiscal gamble that threatens service cuts, deferred maintenance, or tax hikes on other essential services like education and healthcare.
[C1.2] Broad-based taxes are not the fair alternative they claim. Cities often rely on regressive sales or property taxes that, when raised to replace fare revenue, hit low-income residents harder proportionally than current fares. Fare-free transit funded this way could perversely shift costs from riders to non-riders, including the working poor who don’t use the system but would pay through higher taxes on groceries or rent.
[C1.3] The PRO’s vision of frictionless access ignores hard capacity constraints. Even modest ridership growth would strain already crowded peak-hour services, degrading reliability for essential workers who cannot afford delays. Because low-income workers often have less flexibility in their schedules, they would bear the brunt of overcrowding. Permanent fare removal eliminates the price signal that currently manages demand, risking chronic overcrowding that makes the system less useful for everyone.
[C1.4] The comparison to sidewalks fails fundamentally: sidewalks don’t require massive, recurring operational subsidies, and their capacity isn’t limited by peak demand. Moreover, administrative savings from eliminating fare collection are minimal—a small fraction of farebox revenue—while the lost fares represent a large share of operating budgets. Once fares are eliminated permanently, reversing the policy becomes politically toxic even if it proves unsustainable.
[C1.5] The better policy is targeted subsidies: means-tested free or reduced fares for low-income riders address equity concerns directly while preserving the revenue base that maintains service for all. Permanent, universal fare elimination is a blunt instrument that endangers service quality, city finances, and ultimately the very riders it purports to help.
