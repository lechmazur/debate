[P1.1] Mass environmental destruction is the defining atrocity of our era, yet it remains invisible to international criminal law. Genocide protects human groups, war crimes constrain conduct in armed conflict, but international law contains no crime of deliberate, large-scale ecosystem destruction in peacetime. That is the gap ecocide fills.
[P1.2] Existing treaties regulate environmental harm, but they rely on state consent, voluntary compliance, and civil liability. They do not stigmatize ecological devastation as a crime of international concern. When a government or corporation drains a wetland that sustains regional water supplies, or clear-fells an entire rainforest ecosystem, the victims are left without criminal recourse. Environmental devastation often crosses borders, affecting downstream communities and global climate systems, yet no prosecutor can currently charge it.
[P1.3] Ecocide would provide that recourse and treat such destruction as a violation of the international legal order, not merely a policy failure.
[P1.4] Some argue this threatens sovereignty or development. That is backward. Sovereignty is a responsibility, not a license to destroy shared natural systems. International criminal law already targets illegal economic activity—trafficking, slavery, toxic dumping—without hindering legitimate growth.
[P1.5] A crime of ecocide would apply only to conduct so severe it is comparable to existing atrocity crimes, not to ordinary industrial activity. The threshold would be high, but the conduct it targets is extraordinary.
[P1.6] Recognition would also reshape incentives. Corporations and states calculate environmental damage as an externality, a cost to be weighed against profit or growth. A binding international crime raises the moral and legal cost, forcing precautionary measures and accountability. It sends a signal that mass ecological destruction is not merely regrettable but intolerable.
[P1.7] Deterrence is impossible without a named crime, and prevention is cheaper than restitution.
[P1.8] Countries should recognize ecocide as an international crime because the current framework fails the victims of planetary harm. We do not wait for atrocities to become worse before naming them. The law should protect the living systems human survival depends upon.

[C1.3] The chill effect would be devastating. Indonesia’s palm oil sector, which lifted millions from poverty, might collapse under ecocide threats. [C1.7] Finally, intent and causation are nearly impossible to prove. Environmental harm stems from complex, long-term actions by many actors. But international criminal law already prosecutes "widespread or systematic attack" and "serious" violations in war—terms requiring judicial calibration, not mathematical precision. Ecocide can be defined by severity, scale, and unlawfulness, exactly like other atrocity crimes.
[P2.3] Boundary cases do not eliminate the category; they give courts work to do. The current alternative is worse: no boundary at all, because the conduct is simply not a crime.
[P2.4] Second, politicization. CON warns of selective enforcement, yet the status quo guarantees zero enforcement. If the ICC is imperfect, the answer is to strengthen accountability, not to shield governments and corporations. The Montreal Protocol succeeded because the ozone problem was narrow and substitutes were clear; climate and ecosystem destruction lack a single substitute and demand legal constraint, not polite cooperation.
[P2.5] Paris is voluntary by design—which is why states can pledge and fail without consequence.
[P2.6] Third, the chill effect. CON frames investor fear as harm, but fear of liability is deterrence working. We do not repeal homicide law because it chills violence. Indonesia’s palm oil and Ethiopia’s dam involve ecological transformation demanding scrutiny, not exemption.
[P2.7] If such projects destroy ecosystems essential to life, criminal exposure is not an injustice; it is overdue. CON’s "economic colonialism" argument inverts reality: wealthy operators externalize ecological ruin onto poorer communities, who lack any criminal remedy. Ecocide gives them protection, not penury.
[P2.8] Finally, causation. Criminal law routinely assigns responsibility for diffuse, complex harm—corporate fraud and environmental tort handle long chains of causation. Recklessness, not specific intent, can anchor ecocide, just as it anchors other serious offenses.
[P2.9] The status quo is not neutral. It licenses devastation without stigma. CON offers cooperation that has failed and liability that pays damages but never calls destruction what it is. Recognition names the crime, raises the cost, and protects the living systems we depend on.
