[P1.1] The immigration court's core failure is not haste. It is delay. When a case sits for years, people stop receiving mail after they move, witnesses leave, memories fade, and counsel become unreachable. Nonappearance is what happens when a hearing is so far away that ordinary life cannot stay frozen.
[P1.2] Years of waiting also teach respondents that the process is not real until it suddenly is. Faster processing through more judges and remote appearances attacks that failure at the source.
[P1.3] Expanded staffing does not mean shorter hearings. It means the same deliberative hearing, earlier. A judge not drowning in a multi-year docket can read the file, grant a continuance when evidence is missing, and distinguish a strong claim from a weak one. More judges mean more hours of judging, not fewer minutes per case.
[P1.4] Delay forces stale paper and rewards whoever can still find a lawyer after the market has moved on.
[P1.5] Remote hearings raise appearance, not just throughput. A farmworker hundreds of miles from court, a detained parent, a respondent who cannot miss another day of work—these people lose in absentia because the courthouse is inaccessible, not because they lack a claim. Video appearance lowers that barrier. Counsel can cover more calendars.
[P1.6] Interpreters can be assigned without travel bottlenecks. Connectivity problems are real, but they are solvable logistics, unlike a years-long wait that ends in a missed hearing that cannot be undone. Fairness is the meritorious defense actually being heard.
[P1.7] Wrongful removal is already happening under delay. People with valid relief are ordered removed in absentia, then face a colder record if they reopen. Others wait so long that conditions change, documents expire, and the correct outcome becomes guesswork. More staff and remote access reduce those silent errors more than they create new ones.
[P1.8] The alternative is not careful justice. It is justice that never arrives, then arrives too late.

The metric is not how quickly a case moves, but whether the outcome is more likely correct.
[C1.2] Faster processing with more judges and video links makes removal easier to administer, not fairness easier to achieve.
[C1.3] Expanded staffing first. More judges sound neutral, but in removal court, added capacity is capacity to move cases against people who usually have no lawyer [P1.3]. If the bottleneck were crowded calendars, earlier hearings would help. The real bottleneck is proof: foreign documents, country-conditions evidence, and an attorney who finally takes the case after months.
[C1.4] Speed freezes the record at its thinnest, when the government's file is complete and the respondent's is empty. Extra judges and DHS counsel convert that imbalance into faster orders.
[C1.5] Remote hearings worsen the trade. PRO is right that video raises appearance; a farmworker or detainee can be counted present [P1.5]. But presence is not participation. A traumatized asylum seeker appearing by phone or poor video cannot easily review exhibits, confer privately with counsel, or read an interpreter's nuance.
[C1.6] The hearing may be logged as attended and the case as heard, yet the person is removed because distant credibility determinations are made on a degraded record. That is nominal compliance purchased with real unfairness.
[C1.7] Finally, the downsides are asymmetrical. A missed or delayed hearing can sometimes be fixed; a wrongful removal often cannot be undone, especially when the person is quickly deported and counsel is gone. If these reforms add some appearances while causing even a modest rise in erroneous removals, they fail the motion's own balance. Speed here does not deliver careful justice; it delivers a faster assembly line in the government's favor.
