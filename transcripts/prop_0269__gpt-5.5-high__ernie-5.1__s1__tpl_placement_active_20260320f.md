[P1.1] The basic rule should be simple: counterfeit risk belongs with the party best able to prevent, detect, and spread it. In clothing resale, that party is the platform, not the individual buyer. A buyer sees a few photos, a seller description, and maybe a rating. The platform sees seller history, pricing patterns, repeat complaints, shipping data, category risk, payment flows, and millions of comparable listings.
[P1.2] If anyone can build scalable authentication systems and flag suspicious goods before harm occurs, it is the marketplace that profits from the transaction.
[P1.3] “Authenticate more items” does not mean every five-dollar T-shirt gets hand-inspected. It means risk-based expansion: more branded goods, luxury categories, high-value sneakers, suspicious sellers, repeat-return products, and listings whose price or photos trigger concern. Platforms can combine photo screening, seller verification, expert review for high-risk items, and post-sale guarantees. Buyers cannot replicate that.
[P1.4] Telling the buyer to “do your own research” is hollow when the entire business model is to make strangers trust each other through the platform.
[P1.5] Absorbing more risk is also the right incentive structure. If platforms can keep fees while disclaiming responsibility, they have too little reason to police counterfeit volume aggressively. They benefit from more listings and completed sales, while the buyer bears the loss, the hassle, and the evidentiary burden after the fact.
[P1.6] But if the platform must refund buyers, guarantee authenticity, or eat part of the loss when its marketplace fails, it has a direct financial reason to improve screening and remove bad sellers earlier.
[P1.7] The cost objection is backwards. Counterfeits already impose costs; the question is whether those costs are hidden in random buyer losses or managed systematically by the platform. It also helps honest sellers, because buyers pay more confidently when they trust the marketplace.
[P1.8] Resale depends on trust, and trust cannot rest mainly on the least informed person in the transaction.

[C1.2] Buyers are already the party best equipped to manage counterfeit risk. They can inspect photos, compare price to retail, read seller history, and use third‑party authentication services that charge a few dollars per check. That is a regressive cost increase, not a fairness improvement.
[C1.3] The incentive structure the proposition relies on is flawed. Platforms earn revenue per transaction; adding a mandatory authentication step reduces transaction volume and margin. Rather than policing sellers, platforms will either outsource verification to buyers or raise fees, both of which dilute the intended consumer protection. In practice, the few niche sites that do full authentication (e.g., The RealReal) survive because they charge premium prices and serve a luxury segment; mainstream marketplaces cannot replicate that model without destroying their price advantage.
[C1.4] Counterfeit incidents, while damaging, represent a small fraction of total sales. Making platforms absorb that risk creates moral hazard: sellers may feel freer to list fakes, knowing the platform will eat the loss. A system where buyers bear the risk keeps sellers accountable and preserves the low‑cost, high‑volume model that makes resale attractive.
