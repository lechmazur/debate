[P1.1] Religious life is not only private conviction. Communities build schools and workplaces so faith can shape ordinary days. Dress is among the oldest, mildest tools for that: a kippah in a Jewish school, modest clothing at a Christian charity, a headscarf expected of those who chose a Muslim academy. These marks say the institution is not a neutral shell.
[P1.2] It has a character.
[P1.3] That character deserves some room, not a blank check. The proposition limits the room to cases where participation is genuinely voluntary and alternatives are realistic. A parent who picks a religious school over a public one, or an applicant who accepts a faith-based job knowing the dress norm, is choosing, not being conscripted. When the local school is the only school, or the employer is the only employer, the condition fails and the expectation should not bind.
[P1.4] The safeguards are not decoration. They are the boundary between association and coercion.
[P1.5] Two goods follow. Pluralism survives only if minority institutions can look and live differently; a single dress regime for every school and workplace does not liberate, it homogenizes. Conscience survives because no one is forced to wear a symbol they reject and no one is stranded without a real exit. The total ban and the unlimited exemption both fail that test.
[P1.6] A conditioned room does not.
[P1.7] Power imbalances and internal dissent are serious. Voluntariness must mean informed entry, a real chance to leave without ruin, and no punishment for conscientious objection where the role does not require the symbol. But abolishing the room treats every religious school as a public utility and every faith employer as a secular firm that happens to pray. Shared practice, not merely private opinion, is what these institutions exist to protect.
[P1.8] Tight limits, not erasure, keep that practice free. Strip the room away, and faith may be believed but never visibly shared by those who freely joined.

[C1.1] The proposition is wrong at the exact point it looks most careful. “Genuinely voluntary” is doing enormous work, because a dress rule is not an invitation; it is a condition attached to something people need: schooling for their children, a paycheck, a career. A teacher who needs the job and a family that needs the local religious school may sign the contract or application, but that signature is a bargain under pressure, not proof of free consent. The test asks whether alternatives remain realistic, yet alternatives in a rural county, a specialized field, or a tight labor market are not realistic just because a map shows another employer or another school.
[C1.2] Moving, losing seniority, commuting hours, or accepting a secular setting can be a price so high that calling it an exit is a fiction.
[C1.3] The safeguards also fail those least able to use them. Children do not choose a school in any meaningful sense; they live with their parents’ choice. Staff with families, mortgages, visas, or student loans face ruinous costs. Conscientious objectors inside a religious community may face social penalties that never show up in a formal record.
[C1.4] The condition therefore becomes a license: institutions satisfy it formally, while pressure persists informally, and the burden falls on the individual to prove coercion.
[C1.5] Nor is religious character genuinely at stake. A school can be Jewish, Christian, or Muslim through curriculum, calendar, prayer, and communal life. An employer can close for holy days and permit religious dress. What it should not do is charge admission in the form of mandated attire.
[C1.6] The question is not whether faith may be visible; it is whether institutions may make visibility compulsory. That is the toll booth this proposition legitimizes.
