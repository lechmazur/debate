[P1.1] Diplomatic recognition is not a neutral weather report noting who holds the palace. It is a legal bank account: it unlocks a UN seat, treaty-making power, sovereign immunity, IMF loans, arms sales, and the right to sell oil and minerals as the lawful owner. To hand that account to anyone who can seize territory by force is to turn international order into a prize for thuggery.
[P1.2] The territorial-control rule says effectiveness is enough. If the Taliban can hold Kabul through floggings and bans on girls' schools, if a junta in Myanmar can hold Naypyidaw by shooting protesters, if a group like ISIS holds a city by beheading dissidents — then they qualify. That standard rewards precisely what international law was created to prevent. It tells every general: crush dissent hard enough, hold the capital long enough, and the world will sign over legitimacy.
[P1.3] Making recognition contingent on minimum democratic and human-rights standards — no genocide, no apartheid, no systematic political murder, a basic path for people to contest power — does not demand Sweden everywhere. It sets a floor beneath which we refuse to certify a gang as a government. That floor matters for three reasons.
[P1.4] First, it protects populations from their own captors. A regime that cannot meet minimal standards cannot plausibly claim to represent its people.
[P1.5] Second, it creates leverage. States crave recognition because it pays. Conditioning that payment gives the international community its only non-military tool to say torture has a cost.
[P1.6] Third, it aligns law with its own purpose. The UN Charter begins with "We the Peoples," not "We the effective warlords."
[P1.7] We can still talk, deliver aid, negotiate ceasefires without recognizing. Contact does not require coronation. A world that recognizes on control alone has no answer to why control should ever be restrained.
