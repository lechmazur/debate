[P1.1] Picture a 38-year-old seeing an ad: freeze your eggs now, take control, come back when ready. She spends well over ten thousand dollars for medication, monitoring, and retrieval, plus yearly storage, endures weeks of hormones and surgery, and banks a small number of eggs believing she has bought time, roughly the same security a 30-year-old buys.
[P1.2] She has not. Biology is brutally age-graded. Eggs frozen in the early thirties have a far higher chance per egg of ever becoming a baby than eggs frozen near forty, and far fewer are typically needed for a reasonable cumulative chance. Live birth is also far lower than thaw survival or fertilization, the rosier numbers ads like to feature.
[P1.3] Clinics have age-stratified outcomes; patients often do not. When advertising leads with empowerment and postponement while pushing age curves, cycle-to-baby math, and wide uncertainty into fine print, it converts a medical gamble into a lifestyle accessory.
[P1.4] Prominence is the whole ballgame. A vague “results vary, talk to your doctor” buried beneath glowing imagery does not correct the headline impression. People anchor on what is large, simple, and hopeful, especially on an emotional, time-pressured decision. A fair rule would require what reasonable patients need where they will actually see it: realistic live-birth chances by age at freezing, tied to plausible egg numbers, with uncertainty shown as a range, not a promise.
[P1.5] This is ordinary consumer protection, not paternalism. We require lenders to headline APR, not hide it, precisely because optimism sells. The cost here is worse than money: false reassurance encourages waiting, and waiting itself destroys the very fertility being insured. Competition on hope will not fix this; only a prominence mandate will.
[P1.6] Requiring truth up front expands freedom instead of limiting it.

[C1.1] Fertility clinic ads should not be forced to headline age-based live-birth rates, because no such rate is both realistic and portable enough to put in an ad without misleading the very women it claims to protect.
[C1.2] A woman's chance does not turn on age at freezing alone. It turns on how many mature eggs she actually retrieves — unknowable before stimulation — sperm quality years later, embryo development, uterine health, and her clinic's lab. A 38-year-old who banks twenty eggs and one who banks six share an age bracket and almost nothing else. A mandated curve takes a population average built from those differences and presents it, big and first, as if it were her prognosis.
[C1.3] That is not clarity; it is false precision with official backing.
[C1.4] Prominence makes the distortion harder to correct. If the number is national, it understates good clinics and flatters weak ones. If clinic-specific, older groups often have small, dated cohorts who have returned to thaw, so the honest range is so wide it communicates almost nothing while looking scientific. Readers will anchor anyway: some read the top as a promise, others the bottom as futility, and skip the consultation where reserve testing could give a personal estimate.
[C1.5] That consultation, which already precedes treatment, is the right place for this conversation. An ad is an invitation to inquire, not informed consent. We can and should punish genuinely deceptive claims — 'insurance,' 'stop the clock,' guaranteed-baby imagery. But moving from policing falsehood to prescribing a headline statistic confuses marketing with medicine and will deter precisely the older patients for whom freezing, however imperfect, may still beat using even older eggs later.
[C1.6] Freezing does preserve a relative advantage: younger eggs over older eggs. Headlining a low absolute average hides that comparison, scares women out of learning their own numbers, and replaces one simplistic slogan with a simplistic number. Reject the mandate.

[C2.6] Ban “insurance,” ban guarantees, punish cherry-picked thaw rates. Do not force every ad to lead with a contested population model patients cannot apply to themselves until after the workup the ad is meant to prompt.
