[P1.1] Noncompete clauses, once reserved for a tiny handful of senior executives with access to core corporate trade secrets, are now routinely applied to workers across every income bracket, from fast-food shift supervisors to hairstylists to entry-level lab technicians, many of whom hold no confidential information that could meaningfully harm a former employer. Restricting these clauses for nearly all workers, with no broad employer exceptions, is the only way to stop employers from weaponizing contract fine print to suppress wages, stifle mobility, and block workers from building their own livelihoods.
[P1.2] When workers cannot leave for a competing firm that offers better pay, safer conditions, or more aligned work, employers face no competitive pressure to bid up compensation or treat staff well. Broad, cross-industry labor research consistently links widespread noncompete use to stagnant wage growth, reduced new business formation, and even slower industry innovation, as skilled workers are blocked from moving to roles where their talents would be more productive. Broad employer carveouts are not a reasonable balance; they are a loophole that will swallow the rule entirely, as employers will reflexively classify nearly every role as requiring access to sensitive information to escape restriction.
[P1.3] We already see this dynamic in states with weak noncompete rules, where even hourly frontline staff sign contracts barring them from similar work within a 30-mile radius for a year or more after leaving. Existing trade secret law already provides strong, targeted remedies when a worker actually steals or misuses proprietary information like client lists or unreleased product designs, so there is no need for blanket noncompete exceptions that trap ordinary workers in jobs they cannot afford to leave. Those exceptions do not protect legitimate business interests; they only protect employers who would rather lock in cheap, immobile labor than compete fairly for talent.
