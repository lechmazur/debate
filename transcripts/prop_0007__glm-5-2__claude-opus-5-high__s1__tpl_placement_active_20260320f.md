[P1.1] The core issue is that prophylactic antibiotic use in healthy livestock trades a short-term economic benefit for a long-term public health catastrophe. The proposition accepts the trade-off — higher meat prices and reduced yields — and still says ban it. Here is why that is the right call.
[P1.2] Antibiotic resistance is already one of the leading public health threats worldwide. When you feed antibiotics to healthy animals at scale, you create enormous selection pressure for resistant bacteria. These do not stay on the farm. They move through meat, through runoff, through farm workers, and through the food chain.
[P1.3] The resistance genes themselves transfer to human pathogens. This is not speculative — it is the basic mechanism of evolution applied to billions of animals receiving subtherapeutic doses over decades.
[P1.4] The economic trade-off the proposition acknowledges is real but inverted. Yes, banning prophylactic antibiotics would raise meat prices and reduce yields. But the costs of antibiotic resistance — prolonged hospital stays, failed treatments, mortality from once-manageable infections — already run into billions annually and are climbing. Cheap meat produced by undermining the effectiveness of antibiotics is not actually cheap.
[P1.5] The price is simply paid elsewhere: in hospitals, in sick children, in people who die from infections that medicine used to cure.
[P1.6] The alternatives exist. Improved sanitation, vaccination, better husbandry, and disease surveillance can maintain animal welfare and productivity without routinely dosing healthy animals with human-important antibiotics. Countries that have already restricted prophylactic use demonstrate that livestock production continues — it just costs more, which is exactly the trade-off the proposition accepts.
[P1.7] The question is whether we prioritize artificially cheap protein over preserving antibiotics as a medical tool. Once resistance develops, it does not negotiate. It does not respond to market signals. It just kills people.
[P1.8] The proposition asks us to accept higher meat prices to keep antibiotics working. That is a trade worth making.

The opponent redefines it as "animals dying of preventable disease," but animals dying is what happens when you run a system that requires antibiotics to survive its own conditions.
