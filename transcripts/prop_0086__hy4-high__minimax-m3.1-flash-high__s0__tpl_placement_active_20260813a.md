[P1.1] The line for hospitals and care providers is not kindness versus technology; it is support versus substitution. Robots can prompt walks, play music, or give midnight reminders. But replacing most human companionship changes the older adult’s world: people are no longer expected to notice, remember, and answer; simulated attention is declared good enough.
[P1.2] Companionship in care is not mere entertainment. It is how distress becomes visible. An aide sitting briefly may notice a resident is quieter than usual, hiding pain, struggling with dentures, or frightened by a changed routine. A conversational device can register words; it cannot carry the responsibility to interpret silence, check the body, call a nurse, or tell family that something is wrong.
[P1.3] A screen can fill time. It cannot be a witness.
[P1.4] The loss is also moral. Older adults in institutions often lose familiar roles, homes, privacy, and control. Human company restores standing: someone asks what they want, knows the old joke, respects a refusal, and can be held accountable. A synthetic companion may be endlessly patient, but its patience has no loyalty behind it and no duty to repair a mistake.
[P1.5] When most contact is synthetic, dignity becomes mood management.
[P1.6] Staffing pressure makes substitution more dangerous. The claim that a robot is better than no company treats scarcity as fate and lets an empty schedule look solved: administrators get a cheap metric, residents get a cheerful interface, and the public stops seeing missing staff. That is how a temporary emergency hardens into a permanent standard of care. Those least able to object—people with dementia, speech problems, depression, or no vocal family—are easiest to park with machines.
[P1.7] Keep AI at the edges: reminders, games, monitoring, optional comfort. Do not make it the main companion. When care is tight, protect a floor of human presence; do not redefine abandonment as innovation.
