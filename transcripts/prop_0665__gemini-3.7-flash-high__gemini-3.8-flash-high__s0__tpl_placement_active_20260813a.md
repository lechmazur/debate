[P1.1] Severe inherited mitochondrial diseases are among the most devastating conditions known to medicine. Disorders like Leigh syndrome and MELAS strip children of basic neurological and muscular function, condemning them to progressive organ failure, severe disability, and premature death. Because mutated mitochondrial DNA is transmitted maternally, affected families are trapped in a multi-generational cycle of profound loss and trauma.
[P1.2] Mitochondrial replacement therapy (MRT) offers a decisive way to break this cycle. By replacing defective mitochondrial DNA with healthy donor mitochondria, MRT enables at-risk mothers to have healthy children who share their nuclear genome. Public health systems have a clear moral and fiscal obligation to fund this intervention.
[P1.3] First, funding MRT fulfills the core mission of public health: high-impact prevention. The lifelong clinical management of a single patient with severe mitochondrial disease requires continuous, specialized care—ranging from mechanical ventilation and anti-epileptic therapies to frequent intensive care admissions. These catastrophic burdens are ultimately absorbed by public healthcare budgets. Funding MRT is a one-time preventive measure that eliminates fatal pathology at its root, yielding significant long-term savings while averting human suffering.
[P1.4] Second, public funding guarantees health equity. Without state coverage, MRT becomes an exclusive privilege reserved for the wealthy. Low- and middle-income families would face an agonizing dilemma: risk passing on a fatal illness, abandon biological parenthood, or incur crushing private debt. Public health systems exist precisely to prevent access to life-saving reproductive medicine from being dictated by wealth.
[P1.5] Third, the bioethical calculation strongly favors intervention. Mitochondrial DNA functions as the cell’s energy generator; it does not determine physical appearance, personality, or cognitive identity. MRT is not genetic enhancement; it is a cellular transplant aimed exclusively at preventing lethal disease.
[P1.6] When public medicine possesses the tools to spare children from agonizing, fatal conditions before they begin, refusing to fund that care is indefensible. Public health systems should fund MRT.
