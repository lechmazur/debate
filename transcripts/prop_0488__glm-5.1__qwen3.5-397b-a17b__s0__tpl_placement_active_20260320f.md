[P1.2] First, concentration narrows the genetic base. When a handful of firms decide which seed varieties reach commercial scale, they rationally favor a narrow set of high-yielding, chemically dependent traits. The result is vast monocultures sitting on slim genetic foundations. A single emerging pest or climate shift can cascade across continents because the same handful of genotypes dominate everywhere.

[P1.5] That inverted incentive directly corrodes resilience.
[P1.6] Third, extreme market power lets these firms capture regulation. They can shape intellectual property rules, seed certification standards, and pesticide approvals in ways that entrench their position and shrink the space for public-sector breeding, farmer-saved seed, and regional seed cooperatives. [P1.7] Breaking up these firms won't magically solve every agricultural problem, but it cracks open the monoculture of decision-making. Public breeding programs regain relevance.
[P1.8] Farmers reclaim meaningful choice.
[P1.9] The proposition asks whether governments should act to protect food-system resilience.
