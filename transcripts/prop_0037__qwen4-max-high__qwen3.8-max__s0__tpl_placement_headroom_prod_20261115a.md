[P1.1] Congestion pricing rations a scarce good. A ban changes what the good is. Pricing's honest objective is to make traffic flow — its success metric is vehicle speed. London has charged drivers for two decades; its centre is still car-dominated, with buses queuing behind the toll-payers.
[P1.2] A price is a dial you tune to preserve the system. A ban redesigns the system.
[P1.3] Some harms cannot be priced, only redistributed. A charge does not remove the turning conflict at a junction; it decides which incomes get to create it. A child hit by a driver, an asthma attack at the school gate, a pavement surrendered to parking — these are physical facts, not fees. Zero is a different category from cheap.
[P1.4] Space is the real prize. Only exclusion lets a city convert lanes into tram tracks, trees, loading bays, wider pavements, seating — durable investments that make the alternative genuinely better. Pricing leaves the asphalt intact and the bus stuck behind whoever could afford the toll. You cannot redesign a street around a vehicle you still invite in.
[P1.5] Expect the claim that bans are regressive. Invert it. A charge is a permission slip for the wealthy and a penalty for the cleaner driving at five in the morning because no bus runs. A ban with exemptions — disabled badge holders, residents, emergency services, deliveries and trades in fixed windows — allocates access by need rather than by bid.
[P1.6] Ghent, Oslo, Ljubljana and Pontevedra pushed cars out and their centres filled with people, not boarded-up windows. Most arrivals already come by foot, bike or transit; what a ban mainly removes is through-traffic using someone else's neighbourhood as a shortcut.
[P1.7] The question is not whether driving should cost more, but whether the centre belongs to the people in it or the cars passing through.

[C1.1] The central flaw in the proposition is the word “entirely.” A total ban treats a complex system as if one rule can replace millions of different trips. But central districts contain hospitals, clinics, disabled drivers, night-shift workers, plumbers, florists, restaurants receiving deliveries, and residents who cannot carry heavy goods on transit. A ban does not eliminate these needs; it converts them into permits, exemptions, enforcement cameras, and litigation. The result is not a clean public realm but a bureaucracy that decides which trips count as legitimate.
[C1.2] Congestion pricing is stronger because it is adjustable, less intrusive, and capable of funding the alternatives. If traffic is excessive at 8 a.m., raise the charge; if retail access suffers, lower it or create evening exemptions; if buses are slow, dedicate the revenue to lanes and service. A ban cannot be tuned. It displaces pressure.
[C1.3] Through-traffic may move to ring roads and bordering neighborhoods, shifting emissions and danger to communities with less political power. Meanwhile, essential access still occurs, now under a maze of exceptions.
[C1.4] The equity objection cuts against the ban more sharply. A well-designed price can include resident discounts, disability exemptions, low-income credits, and free transit transfers. It asks drivers to pay for the congestion they impose while preserving choice. A categorical prohibition gives access to those who live inside the district, own no car, or can absorb complex rules, while penalizing people on the margins: a home-care nurse with equipment, a parent dropping a child at a specialist clinic, a small builder whose tools cannot ride a bus.
[C1.5] Pricing can also be paired with physical changes—bus lanes, wider pavements, loading windows, bicycle streets—without pretending that every private vehicle must vanish. That incremental coalition is realistic and reversible. A ban is brittle, divisive, and likely to create worse pollution on the periphery.
