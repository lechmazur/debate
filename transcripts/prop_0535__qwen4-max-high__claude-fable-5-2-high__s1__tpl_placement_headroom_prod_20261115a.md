[P1.1] An advance directive is not a forecast of future feelings. It is a decision about what may be done to a body that can no longer decide. When someone writes "if I no longer recognize my children, let pneumonia take me," they are exercising the only authority they will ever have over that moment. The question is whether that authority survives the disease it was written for.
[P1.2] It must, because dementia destroys precisely the capacities that would justify revising it. We override a competent person's earlier choice when they reconsider. Dementia offers no such reconsideration. It removes memory of the commitment, the ability to compare states, and often the awareness that anything has been lost.
[P1.3] Contentment in that condition is not a verdict on a life; it is frequently adaptation to a shrinking world. Treating a smile as a repeal mistakes the absence of objection for the presence of consent.
[P1.4] Consider the woman who spent years watching her father die by inches and wrote her instructions in grief and clarity. Later she is pleasant, cooperative, and fed by hand. Nothing about that tells us she now values surviving in the state she explicitly refused. Under the opposing default, her decades of judgment are cancelled by a mood she cannot contextualize — and cancelled by whoever is standing nearest the bed.
[P1.5] That is the real alternative: not the patient's voice, but an interpreter's. Families carry exhaustion and guilt; institutions carry costs and beds. The directive exists because those pressures peak exactly when the patient is weakest.
[P1.6] Strongly binding is not blind. Clear evidence of genuinely changed values — sustained, documented, not mere acquiescence — releases the instruction. Comfort and care never stop. The burden stays where it belongs: on those who would override her, not on her to keep re-earning her own words.

[C1.1] Look at the escape hatch PRO offers: "clear evidence of genuinely changed values." Then look at [P1.2]: dementia destroys the capacities needed to revise. Put those together and the exception can never be met. A patient who cannot articulate values cannot produce clear evidence of changing them.
[C1.2] For anyone the disease has silenced, "unless" means never.
[C1.3] That matters because the person in the bed is not an empty body awaiting instructions. She has interests even when she lacks "values": she enjoys music, lights up at her grandchild, reaches for the spoon, is frightened when she can't breathe. PRO's own example — pleasant, cooperative, eating — is a woman with a life going well by her own present lights. The motion says treatable pneumonia should kill her, because her evident wish to go on is not a philosophy.
[C1.4] Second, the directive is weaker evidence than PRO pretends. It was written by someone who had never lived the condition. Healthy people predict that disability will be unbearable; those living with it routinely report otherwise. Watching a father decline tells you what it was like to watch, not what it was like to be him.
[C1.5] Calling contentment "adaptation" concedes the point: adaptation is exactly what the author failed to foresee.
[C1.6] Third, the errors are not symmetrical. Treat wrongly, and you can stop tomorrow. Withhold wrongly, and there is no tomorrow.
[C1.7] And PRO's interpreter problem cuts against them. "No longer recognizes my children" needs interpreting too — by the same tired family, the same institution counting costs and beds. Those pressures push toward enforcing a refusal, not resisting it.
[C1.8] The better rule: directives are weighty evidence, decisive when the patient is suffering or indifferent, but answerable to the living person's present welfare. A document should guide her care, not outrank her.
