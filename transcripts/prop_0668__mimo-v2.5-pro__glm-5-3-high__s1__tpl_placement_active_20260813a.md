[P1.1] When a government exports Pegasus-style spyware to a regime that routinely jails journalists, it is not providing a neutral tool. It is handing over a weapon calibrated against its own people. The core question here is simple: are democracies obligated to stop profiting from foreseeable repression, or can they hide behind the fiction that every tool is neutral and every buyer is benign?
[P1.2] The answer is clear, and the principle is straightforward. Advanced surveillance technologies—spyware, phone interception systems, mass facial recognition platforms—are force multipliers for political control. In the hands of a government that respects due process and press freedom, they face meaningful legal constraints. In the hands of a regime that treats dissent as criminality, those same tools become instruments of domestic terror.
[P1.3] The technology does not change; the context of its use does. And we know what that context looks like in practice. Governments with documented records of torturing dissidents, disappearing activists, and crushing independent media have repeatedly turned imported surveillance capabilities against precisely those targets.
[P1.4] The "legitimate policing uses" defense collapses under scrutiny. When a regime's definition of crime includes organizing protests, writing critical articles, or messaging opposition groups, then policing *is* repression. There is no clean separation between the two. We have seen opposition figures and human rights lawyers targeted with commercially exported spyware—tools that were supposedly sold for lawful intercept purposes.
[P1.5] The gap between official justification and actual use is not an aberration. It is the predictable outcome of selling powerful surveillance to governments that have every incentive to weaponize it.
[P1.6] My opponent will argue that a ban punishes legitimate security needs and drives trade to less scrupulous suppliers. But this gets the moral calculus backwards. A government does not earn the right to import repression-enabling technology simply by claiming it fights crime. And "someone else will sell it" has never been a valid ethical defense for complicity—it is the logic of every arms dealer in history.
