[P1.1] The Scottish Highlands have roughly a million red deer and not a single animal that hunts them. The result is a landscape people mistake for wilderness: bare hills where a forest should be, saplings browsed to stumps, taxpayers funding endless culls that never catch up. That is what "no predators" costs. It is a cost too, just one that never appears on a farmer's invoice.
[P1.2] The case rests on three claims.
[P1.3] First, degraded ecosystems are not repaired by anything else. Deer and boar populations without predators do not stabilise; they boom, strip regeneration, spread disease, and crash. Human culling is expensive, patchy, and politically fragile. Predators work every night for free, target the weak and sick, and change prey behaviour so that riverbanks and young woodland get a chance to recover.
[P1.4] Where wolves and lynx have returned in Yellowstone and across continental Europe, forestry damage, crop loss, and deer-vehicle collisions decline, and those benefits accrue disproportionately to rural people.
[P1.5] Second, the harm to farmers is real but small and insurable. Across Europe, wolf predation is a tiny fraction of livestock deaths compared with disease, weather, and dogs. Compensation, guardian animals, and fencing reduce losses further and are already standard where wolves live. A problem you can compensate is not a problem that justifies a veto.
[P1.6] Third, and decisively: objection is not the same as legitimate veto. Every public good, from wind farms to hospitals to reservoirs, is opposed by someone nearby. We answer objection with consultation, mitigation, and money, not by abandoning the project. Ecosystems are national and intergenerational assets; a few thousand landholders cannot own the decision to keep them broken.
[P1.7] The motion says "even when they object," and the honest answer is yes: their objection obliges us to design reintroduction carefully and compensate generously. It does not oblige us to do nothing.
[P1.8] The choice is not predators versus peace. It is predators with compensation versus a slow, expensive, permanent collapse that everyone pays for.
