[C1.1] The proposition errs by demanding that universities complete physical investments before addressing digital gaps that already affect daily student life. Today's campuses include growing numbers of commuter, non-traditional, and geographically dispersed learners who cannot rely on being physically present for clubs or events. Digital wellness platforms meet these students on their terms, offering low-pressure ways to form connections without the logistics of travel or scheduling conflicts that physical spaces impose. This approach recognizes that not every student experiences the campus as a central hub.
[C1.2] This sequencing fails to account for accessibility. Common spaces and recurring in-person activities often exclude students with health conditions, family obligations, or part-time status, creating a community that serves only the resident core. Expanding digital tools concurrently or even ahead allows every enrolled student, regardless of location or circumstance, to participate in wellness initiatives from the outset, reducing the isolation that the motion claims to solve. Equity demands that universities serve the full spectrum of their population rather than assuming a traditional on-campus model that no longer describes most enrollees.
[C1.3] Physical funding also ties up resources in ways that limit flexibility. Renovating a lounge or supporting a club requires dedicated space and maintenance that cannot easily adapt to changing student demographics or crises that restrict gatherings. Digital platforms, once built, can update rapidly and reach across barriers like distance or time zones, delivering engagement where physical efforts plateau. With finite budgets, allocating first to adaptable technology avoids the sunk costs of underutilized buildings during shifts in attendance patterns.
[C1.4] Furthermore, the motion overlooks the potential for digital engagement to feed into stronger in-person communities. Students who first connect online often transition to attending physical events with greater ease, using digital as an on-ramp rather than a competitor. Prioritizing the physical delays this process for those who need initial digital support to even consider showing up, ultimately weakening overall campus cohesion by leaving potential participants behind.
