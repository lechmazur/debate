[P1.1] Routine flaring and heavy methane leakage are not unavoidable costs of producing energy; they are choices to burn or vent a usable resource because doing so is cheaper than capturing it. The proposition targets routine practices and leakage above strict thresholds, not emergency safety releases. That distinction matters: governments can still protect workers and system integrity while ending the deliberate disposal of gas for convenience.
[P1.2] The strongest reason to support the ban is timing. Methane is far more effective than carbon dioxide at trapping heat in its first decades in the atmosphere. Because methane decays faster than CO2, cutting it is one of the few climate levers that can slow warming in the near term, not just after 2050. Routine flaring also turns potential fuel into carbon dioxide, particulates, and wasted energy.
[P1.3] Leakage above strict thresholds means operators are losing product, often from valves, pneumatic controllers, tanks, and pipelines that can be detected and repaired with ordinary industry tools. If a company cannot operate without routinely leaking or burning gas, the operation is not efficient; it is externalizing cost onto the public.
[P1.4] The opposition will say this threatens short-term domestic supply. But the supply at issue is usually marginal associated gas from oil production or wasted gas that was never being delivered to households. A ban forces capture, reinjection, use on site, or shutdown of the dirtiest wells. That may reduce some cheap production, but it improves long-term energy governance by preventing lock-in to wasteful infrastructure and encouraging storage, electrification, and cleaner gas supply.
[P1.5] Short-term convenience is not a license to impose long-term climate damage, health harms, and wasted resources on future citizens.
[P1.6] Strict thresholds make the duty enforceable: measure, verify, repair, and penalize. Governments should prefer rules that price harm honestly over rules that let pollution continue because it is convenient. For that reason, the ban is justified even when it trims short-term supply.

[C1.1] Cutting methane is a good idea. That is not what this motion asks. It asks whether governments should reach for the bluntest possible tool — an outright ban above a fixed threshold — and accept losses to domestic energy supply as an acceptable price. They shouldn't, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the ban is the wrong instrument. Methane emissions are notoriously hard to measure; estimates from satellites, flyovers, and ground sensors routinely diverge widely. A "strict threshold" backed by shutdown authority means livelihoods and supply hinge on contested measurements. Escalating fees, capture mandates with realistic timelines, and pipeline-access investment achieve the same repairs PRO describes — valves, controllers, tanks — without handing regulators a shut-off switch calibrated to numbers nobody can verify precisely.
[C1.3] If leaks are cheap to fix, as PRO insists, a fee fixes them. The ban's only added feature is the supply destruction.
[C1.4] Second, the supply losses are not abstract. Shut in marginal wells and associated-gas oil production, and prices rise across the system. Energy costs are regressive: the family choosing between heating and groceries pays for this policy, not the operator. And in places like Nigeria or Iraq, where flaring persists precisely because gathering infrastructure doesn't exist, a ban means shutting down the revenue base of poor states — or, more realistically, means the law is ignored and enforcement becomes theater.
[C1.5] Third, the climate math backfires. Gas removed from domestic supply doesn't vanish from demand. It's replaced by imported LNG — with its own leakage across liquefaction and shipping — or by coal, which is worse on every metric. A ban that shifts production abroad while raising prices at home is how climate policy generates backlash that kills better policy.
[C1.6] PRO must defend not methane reduction, which everyone wants, but this specific trade: a rigid ban, contested measurement, and deliberate supply sacrifice, when flexible tools deliver the cuts without the casualties. That trade fails.

[P2.2] CON says measurement is contested. All pollution rules face measurement limits, but they do not require perfect certainty to work. Leak detection, optical gas imaging, continuous sensors, and audits already let operators and regulators find large leaks. Where exact flow is disputed, enforcement can rely on presumptive standards, repair deadlines, escalating penalties, and loss of the right to vent.
[P2.3] The problem with a fee-only alternative is simple: if the fee is lower than capture costs, operators treat it as a tax on doing business. A ban removes the option to pay for the privilege of wasting public resources.
[P2.4] On supply, CON assumes the lost gas is essential household fuel. Much routine flaring is associated gas from oil operations, never destined for consumers. Requiring capture, reinjection, or local use can actually add usable energy over time. Where marginal wells shut in, the supply hit is the price of refusing to subsidize pollution.
[P2.5] If governments worry about regress, they can direct rebates or efficiency support to vulnerable households—without preserving a right to leak. In places lacking gathering lines, a fee is even easier to evade; a bright-line ban can condition permits, finance, and market access on capture.
[P2.6] Finally, the coal-replacement argument proves too much. If every methane rule is abandoned because markets might respond imperfectly, we lock in methane’s near-term warming power indefinitely. Strict domestic standards also create leverage for cleaner imports and supply chains. The real risk is not that a ban is too strong; it is that flexible fees remain too weak where enforcement is political, budgets are thin, and waste is profitable.
