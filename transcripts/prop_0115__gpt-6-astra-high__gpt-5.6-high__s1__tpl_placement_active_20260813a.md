[P1.1] Buy-now-pay-later changes when you pay, not whether you owe. A provider finances a purchase and acquires a claim on your future income. That is credit. Placing the agreement beside a checkout button does not make its consequences resemble choosing delivery speed.
[P1.2] Regulation should follow the financial obligation, not the interface.
[P1.3] The central risk is fragmented borrowing. One installment plan may be manageable; several plans across different providers can create overlapping demands on the same paycheck. A shopper sees a small payment attached to each purchase, while the household must meet the combined obligation. Even interest-free credit can produce missed payments, overdrafts, or collection pressure.
[P1.4] Zero interest is a valuable feature, but it is not a substitute for responsible lending.
[P1.5] That is why buy-now-pay-later should carry the core protections associated with credit cards: clear, standardized disclosure of repayment schedules and potential charges; proportionate affordability assessments; meaningful rights when goods are defective, undelivered, or refunded; and enforceable limits on abusive fees and collection practices. Providers should face financial oversight and complaint-resolution obligations, rather than being treated primarily as checkout software. If a purchase goes wrong, the consumer needs rights against the financing arrangement, not merely a suggestion to contact the merchant.
[P1.6] “Much more like credit cards” does not mean mechanically copying every rule for revolving balances onto a short installment loan. Checks can scale with exposure, and disclosures can reflect the product’s actual structure. Keep the simplicity and interest-free options; reject the regulatory shortcut. Compliance has costs, but leaving preventable debt distress with consumers does not eliminate costs—it shifts them.
[P1.7] The strongest test is whether convenience survives when borrowers understand their obligations and receive enforceable protection. A useful payment product should pass that test. If its appeal depends on making borrowing feel unlike borrowing, that is precisely the reason to regulate it as credit rather than preserve the illusion at checkout.
