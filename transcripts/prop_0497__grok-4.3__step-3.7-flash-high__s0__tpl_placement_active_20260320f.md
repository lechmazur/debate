[P1.1] Cultural significance now resides as much in executable code as in printed pages or film reels. Games, apps, and digital artworks function as the dominant expressive forms of recent decades, yet they disappear once publishers abandon servers, drop backward compatibility, or dissolve. Legal deposit places a permanent public copy beyond that commercial lifecycle, exactly as it has protected novels and motion pictures for generations.
[P1.2] Without compulsion, preservation depends on the shifting priorities of private owners. Companies routinely delete or encrypt legacy assets to cut storage costs; operating systems and hardware move on, rendering the remaining copies unusable. A mandated national archive removes this single point of failure and creates a stable reference collection for historians, designers, and the public.
[P1.3] The mechanism already works for other media. Deposit libraries receive copies, verify integrity, and maintain access protocols. Extending the same requirement to significant software adds no new conceptual burden while closing an obvious gap in the record. Technical standards for bit-preservation and emulation are mature; the missing element is legal obligation.
[P1.4] Arguments that focus solely on cost or complexity overlook the asymmetry: the expense of occasional deposits is modest compared with the irreversible loss of entire cultural strata. Once the original files are gone, no amount of later funding recovers them. Mandating deposit therefore secures the baseline condition for any future scholarship rather than leaving heritage to market whim.

[C1.1] PRO’s core error is treating software as a static cultural artifact identical to books or films, when it is in fact a functional, rights-encumbered product tied to proprietary ecosystems that existing deposit frameworks were never designed to handle. First, the workable definition of a "depositable copy" collapses for most modern software. Legal deposit for traditional media captures the complete, self-contained work as distributed to the public.
[C1.2] But contemporary games, apps, and digital art regularly rely on external infrastructure the publisher never distributes: live service backends for MMOs, DRM authentication servers, cloud processing for generative tools, and day-one patches that fix core functionality. A deposited offline copy of a 2024 live service game or a cloud-based AI art tool is a useless husk, unable to function as the cultural artifact it is meant to preserve. Even older software requires obsolete operating systems, drivers, and hardware that impose extraordinary ongoing maintenance costs on archives, a burden PRO dismisses as "modest" without justification.
[C1.3] Second, mandatory deposit imposes an uncompensated taking of intellectual property. Unlike books and films, where the deposited copy is identical to the consumer product, software is distributed under license, not sale, with strict prohibitions on redistribution. Rights holders own underlying source code, art assets, and proprietary algorithms as trade secrets and copyrighted works. Forcing them to hand over full, unredacted copies to government archives without fair compensation violates IP rights in nearly every democratic jurisdiction, and creates massive risks of state-sponsored IP theft.
[C1.4] The U.S. Copyright Office already recognizes this: it only requires partial, redacted source code deposits for software, explicitly declining to mandate full copies due to trade secret concerns. Third, PRO’s "culturally significant" threshold is unworkably vague. Unlike the low, objective bar for traditional media deposit, this subjective filter invites bureaucratic and political interference: governments can exclude works critical of their policies, or prioritize corporate blockbusters over grassroots independent digital art that holds deep cultural value for marginalized communities.

The alternative—permanent disappearance once a company folds or a server shuts down—erases the cultural record without ever compensating creators or the public. Mandating deposit therefore assigns the minimal necessary copy to a non-commercial steward rather than leaving the entire work to market deletion.
[P2.3] Finally, the charge of subjective selection ignores that every existing deposit regime already filters by cultural significance. Film archives decide which productions merit permanent retention; extending that judgment to games and apps does not invent new political risk. The real asymmetry remains: without compulsion, entire strata of recent expression vanish regardless of significance, while the deposit system at least creates the factual baseline on which future judgment can operate.
