[P1.1] Sex work policy faces a fundamental tension: protecting people selling sex from immediate violence while preventing the market expansion that fuels trafficking and coercion. Both extremes fail half this test. Full prohibition treats the seller as an offender, handing violent buyers and pimps ultimate leverage: the victim’s fear of police. Conversely, a fully legal market normalizes and expands buyer demand far beyond what voluntary labor can supply, while giving exploiters lawful storefronts behind which coercion hides in plain sight.
[P1.2] Decriminalizing the sale of sex while criminalizing its purchase resolves this dilemma by matching legal liability to real-world power. Removing penalties on sellers immediately strips predators of state-backed blackmail. Picture a seller threatened by an aggressive client: under prohibition, calling police invites her own arrest; under asymmetric decriminalization, the law stands unambiguously on her side. Equally vital, she avoids a criminal record, the exact barrier that locks people out of mainstream housing, banking, and employment, trapping them in survival sex.
[P1.3] Simultaneously, criminalizing the buyer targets the engine of commercial exploitation: demand. Unlike sellers, who disproportionately enter the trade through poverty, homelessness, addiction, or outright force, buyers exercise discretionary spending power. Because ordinary buyers fear arrest and public exposure, buyer-side penalties meaningfully deter purchases and shrink industry revenue. When easy profits dry up, trafficking networks lose their incentive to recruit and transport vulnerable people, whereas an open legal market acts as a magnet for coerced supply to satisfy booming, state-sanctioned demand.
[P1.4] Safety cannot mean sacrificing assaulted sellers to satisfy prohibitionists, nor sacrificing trafficked women and youth to sustain an unrestrained commercial industry. Asymmetric decriminalization shields those selling sex today, keeps pathways out of the trade open, and starves predatory networks of tomorrow’s profits, placing legal risk squarely on the party with the power to walk away.
