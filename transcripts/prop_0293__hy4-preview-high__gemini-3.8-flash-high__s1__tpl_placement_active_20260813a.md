[P1.1] Civic space is a statement. A statue on a city hall lawn or a town square is not a neutral artifact; it is the community saying, with stone and taxpayer maintenance, that this person is worthy of honor. When the person honored is primarily associated with slavery, colonial domination, or mass repression, keeping that statue in a place of civic honor is not history. It is a continuing claim that their crimes do not disqualify them from our shared praise.
[P1.2] Relocation is the honest alternative. Move the monument to a museum or a contextualized site and it stops being a civic endorsement and becomes evidence. In a museum, the statue can stand beside the voices of the enslaved, the colonized, and the executed. The original monument is preserved; what changes is the message.
[P1.3] The street corner says, "Honor this leader." The museum says, "Understand what this leader did, and who paid for it."
[P1.4] The usual warning is that removal erases the past. Relocation does the opposite. Destroying a statue would lose an object; relocating it keeps the object while ending the honor. The larger distortion is leaving a Confederate general on a courthouse lawn, where the monument silently repeats the Lost Cause claim that treason in defense of slavery was noble, or leaving a slave trader like Edward Colston in a Bristol plaza, where his wealth is detached from the human beings he trafficked.
[P1.5] Contextualization repairs that lie.
[P1.6] Every democracy curates its public honors; it chooses holidays, names, and monuments. To keep honoring such figures in common spaces tells victims' descendants that the community still prizes the perpetrator's public standing over their own equal dignity. We can preserve the past without worshiping it. Remove these statues from places of civic honor, place them where their full record is taught, and stop asking the public to walk past a celebration of oppression.

[C1.2] First, removal weakens historical accountability. When a controversial statue remains in the town square—stripped of its unearned pedestal, contextualized with plaques detailing its subject's atrocities, or countered by new monuments honoring the oppressed—it forces the community to confront its actual history every single day. Moving that statue behind museum walls transforms an urgent public reckoning into an optional exhibition that most citizens will never visit. Banishment allows a city to wash its hands and pretend it was always enlightened.
[C1.3] True accountability demands leaving the physical evidence where it stood, radically reinterpreted in situ, so the public square records both the injustice and the society's triumph over it.

From George Washington and Thomas Jefferson to Winston Churchill and foundational nation-builders worldwide, pivotal leaders are entangled with slavery, imperial conquest, or severe repression. Once civic belonging demands removing anyone tied to these sins, the threshold becomes an ideological weapon, driving endless cycles of iconoclasm where each generation purges whatever fails contemporary moral consensus.
[C1.5] A mature democracy does not run from its past by packing it into crates. It layers its history, confronting sins where they occurred rather than cleansing them from sight. We must reject the illusion of moral purity through removal and choose the harder, more honest work of transforming our public spaces without erasing their scars.
