[C1.1] The proposition sounds compassionate, but it would make robotaxi service slower, scarcer, and less affordable—especially for the neighborhoods it claims to protect. A private fleet is not a bus line with fixed routes; it is a dynamic network that works because vehicles move toward actual requests. If regulators demand “reasonably similar reliability” in low-demand areas, operators must park cars where trips are infrequent or send them on long empty repositioning runs. Those vehicles are then unavailable for everyone else.
[C1.2] The result is longer waits, higher fares, and slower network growth.
[C1.3] This is not a theoretical trade-off. Ride-hail and delivery platforms show what happens when utilization falls: fewer providers, higher prices, and less dependable service. A robotaxi fleet forced to spread itself thinly will earn less, invest less, and expand less. It may delay entry into a city altogether, or withdraw from difficult zones once compliance costs rise.
[C1.4] The people hurt first are not affluent commuters; they are riders who need a cheap, available option and would otherwise depend on worse alternatives.
[C1.5] The better route is targeted access, not blanket deployment. If the goal is reaching a night-shift nurse or a dialysis patient, subsidize rides for qualified users, fund first-mile connections to transit, or contract specific service gaps. That directs public money to actual need instead of forcing every private car to behave like a postal route. It also preserves the feature that makes robotaxis valuable: low-cost, on-demand mobility that can expand rapidly when the economics work.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition’s standard is unworkable. “Reasonably similar reliability” across neighborhoods with different road layouts, parking availability, traffic, and demand profiles invites endless litigation and gaming. Cities should demand access and accountability, but not a rigid spatial quota that undermines the service itself. Expand mobility for low-income riders by making rides affordable and available where demand exists, not by mandating identical reliability in ways that reduce service for all.

[C2.4] Unmet need is real, but a fleet cannot run on need alone; it runs on paid trips. If those trips were reliably profitable, operators would already seek them. [C2.6] But the humane standard is actual access: subsidized rides for patients and shift workers, contracted shuttle links to transit, and service standards tied to need. [P4.2] A2: The question contains its own contradiction. If low-income neighborhoods were reliably profitable, fleets would already serve them; their conspicuous absence proves the market failure. The mandate does not ask taxpayers to subsidize operator losses but requires companies to internalize the cost of equitable coverage as the price of the public license to operate on city streets—the same bargain utilities accept when required to serve rural customers at reasonable rates. Operators retain full flexibility in how they meet this standard, whether through predictive repositioning, dynamic pricing, or fleet sizing.
[P4.3] Now the decisive point: CON's "arithmetic" is actually an admission. They concede that serving low-income areas requires real resources, yet they prefer spending public money on subsidies that historically fail while leaving the algorithmic redlining intact. Under the mandate, the operator must ensure coverage because the public license demands it.
[P4.4] The mandate forces operators to price exclusion into their business model upfront. When every fleet must meet the same reliability floor, competition shifts from "who can avoid poor neighborhoods cheapest" to "who can serve the whole city most efficiently."
