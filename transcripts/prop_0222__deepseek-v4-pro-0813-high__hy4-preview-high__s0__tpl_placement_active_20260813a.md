[P1.1] The punitive model is failing on its own terms: it criminalizes a health crisis, and the result is more death and more crime.
[P1.2] Decriminalizing possession of small amounts does not legalize trafficking or large-scale supply. It means a person caught with a small bag is routed toward a health or civil response instead of arrest and a criminal record. That distinction is the engine of both benefits.
[P1.3] First, overdose deaths fall. The threat of arrest drives use into isolation: locked bathrooms, empty lots, places where nobody calls 911. It makes people delay or hide when someone stops breathing, and makes them reluctant to carry naloxone or use supervised services because those could be treated as evidence. Remove the arrest threat from simple possession, and the safer behavior becomes rational: staying near help, calling emergency services, accepting clean equipment, testing a supply, entering treatment without confessing to a crime.
[P1.4] Portugal’s long experience with decriminalization shows that moving people out of the criminal system and into health services did not remove all drug harms, but it replaced a punitive spiral with a survivable path.
[P1.5] Second, downstream crime falls. A possession conviction is itself a downstream crime generator: it blocks jobs, housing, and benefits, the stabilizers that make repeat offending less likely. It consumes police, court, and jail capacity that could target violent crime and trafficking. When possession is diverted to treatment, the property crime and street disorder driven by untreated addiction decline as people get help.
[P1.6] Fewer low-level possession cases cycling through courts also means fewer people get a record that pushes them into illegal income.
[P1.7] The deterrence objection is weak. If handcuffs deterred addiction, the places with the most possession arrests would have the lowest addiction and overdose rates. They generally have the opposite. What saves lives is making help safer than hiding.
