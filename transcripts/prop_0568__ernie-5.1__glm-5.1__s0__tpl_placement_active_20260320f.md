[P1.1] Cash is the only payment method that works for everyone, everywhere, regardless of age, income, disability, or digital access. When governments require major retailers to accept cash for essential goods—food, medicine, hygiene products—they protect the most vulnerable from being locked out of the basics of survival. The claim that cash is obsolete ignores the reality that millions of Americans, especially low‑income families, seniors, and the unbanked, still rely on it. Roughly one in four households in the United States lack a bank account, and many more avoid credit cards because of fees or debt.
[P1.2] Without a legal mandate, large retailers can quietly go cashless, turning a private convenience into a public exclusion.
[P1.3] Requiring cash does not impose a crushing cost on businesses. Cash handling is a routine expense; the major expense is already embedded in payroll and security. Moreover, cash transactions are instantaneous, avoid card‑processing fees, and reduce the risk of data breaches that hurt consumers. The real cost to society is the loss of access: a mother who cannot buy diapers because the store refuses cash faces immediate hardship, while the retailer loses a sale it could have easily accommodated.
[P1.4] A cash‑mandate also strengthens economic resilience. During power outages, cyber‑attacks, or natural disasters, electronic payment systems fail. Cash works when everything else does not. By keeping cash viable for essential purchases, governments safeguard a fallback that protects public health and safety.
[P1.5] Opponents will argue that market freedom should decide payment methods. But market freedom ends where public welfare begins. When a private choice creates a barrier to food or medicine, the state has a duty to intervene. The evidence is clear: jurisdictions that have enacted cash‑acceptance laws see higher inclusion rates and fewer complaints from consumers.
[P1.6] The policy is simple, low‑cost, and directly protects those who cannot afford to be left behind.
