[C2.1] The opponent's accountability argument collapses on contact with reality. Yes, historians attach their names to textbooks, but they answer to university departments, peer review boards, and professional associations—not to the parents whose children must live with the curriculum. A school board member can be recalled; a tenured professor who mischaracterizes a war cannot be. You can vote against a politician who lies about history.
[C2.2] You cannot vote against a scholarly consensus that decides your community's suffering doesn't count as genocide, or that colonialism's 'infrastructural development' cancels out its famines. That is not accountability. That is insulation.
[C2.3] Their algebra analogy is the tell. We don't let voters decide whether two plus two equals four because arithmetic has right answers. But history is not arithmetic. It is the selection and interpretation of evidence through moral frameworks that shift across generations.
[C2.4] When scholars 'state where disagreement exists,' they are not being neutral—they are deciding which disagreements deserve classroom space and which are too dangerous to teach. They are determining whose trauma counts as history and whose gets filed under 'complexity.' That is a political choice, made by people who overwhelmingly share one ideological outlook. Calling it expertise doesn't change the fact that it's a monopoly.
[C2.5] And the correction argument is backwards. Yes, children graduate before the next election. But when a community finally organizes to fix a distorted textbook, they can vote, they can protest, they can win. When they disagree with the scholarly consensus, they face decades of academic gatekeeping, citation cartels, and professional blacklisting.
[C2.6] The ballot box offers a reset button. The academy offers a life sentence. You cannot recall a historian. You cannot primary a peer review board.
[C2.7] We do not need guardians deciding which narratives are 'confirmed' and which are 'contested.' We need schools that reflect the actual moral disagreements of the community, not the current fashion of the history department. That is what democracy looks like—messy, contested, and answerable to the people it serves.
