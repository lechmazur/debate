[P1.1] Public transit is a public good, not a commodity. Making it permanently fare-free in major cities advances equity, efficiency, and environmental goals simultaneously. The equity case is strongest: fare barriers hit low-income residents hardest, forcing impossible choices between mobility and basic needs. Where fares have been eliminated, low-income ridership consistently rises, proving cost is a primary obstacle to access.
[P1.2] Operationally, fare collection is costly and inefficient. Typical systems spend a significant portion of fare revenue on collection itself—money better spent on service improvements. Removing fares simplifies operations, speeds boarding, and eliminates enforcement conflicts, reducing delays and improving reliability.
[P1.3] Environmentally, even modest ridership gains reduce car dependency. Each new transit trip replaces polluting vehicle miles, improving air quality and public health. The long-term cultural shift towards transit as a default option is more valuable than immediate metrics.
[P1.4] Funding through broad-based taxation is fair because the benefits—cleaner air, less congestion, stronger local economies—accrue citywide. Everyone pays, everyone benefits. The alternative perpetuates a regressive system where those who can least afford it bear a disproportionate burden.
[P1.5] Initial ridership gains may be modest, but that’s not the metric that matters. The value lies in removing barriers, reducing inequality, and redefining urban mobility as a right. Cities like Tallinn have shown fare-free systems work at scale. The question is whether we treat transit as a public service or a business.
[P1.6] It must be the former.

[C1.1] Public service does not mean zero price for every ride. This motion demands permanence before proof: abolish fares forever, fund them through broad taxes, and accept modest ridership gains. That is not bold transit policy; it is a fiscal commitment insulated from feedback.
[C1.2] Modest gains are the point. If zero fare does not substantially pull people out of cars, the fare was not the binding barrier. In major cities, the barriers are frequency, coverage, transfers, safety, night service, and land use. A night-shift nurse whose bus comes every forty minutes is not made mobile by a free fare.
[C1.3] She pays taxes for a downtown rider's free trip, though that rider already had good options.
[C1.4] Universality weakens the equity claim in [P1.1]. “Free for everyone” subsidizes tourists, affluent commuters, airport trips, and people who would have paid. Targeted low-income passes, fare caps, free transfers, and free rides for children or benefit recipients remove the price barrier while preserving revenue from those who can pay. Broad taxation is not automatically progressive: sales taxes, fees, and property-tax pass-throughs can hit renters and non-riders, including residents of transit deserts.
[C1.5] The operational case is overclaimed too. Collection costs justify modern payment and all-door boarding, not deleting dedicated revenue for drivers, maintenance, and service hours. No fare does not end rationing; it moves rationing to crowding, pass-ups, deferred repairs, and budget fights. Small-city examples cannot answer for megacity metros, commuter rail, tourists, and cross-boundary flows.
[C1.6] Use the next dollar where it produces mobility: more buses, bus lanes, elevators, shelters, safer stations, late-night routes, and targeted affordability. If a city proves that fare-free service durably shifts car trips to transit and that it can fund quality service, it can revisit free fares. “Permanently” and “even if gains are modest” forbid that discipline. The proposal buys a slogan while starving the improvements that make transit genuinely accessible.

Targeted subsidies have administrative edges, but imperfect targeting is cheaper than buying the whole farebox. Automatic enrollment, fare caps, and free transfers can remove the cash barrier with less fiscal exposure, without pretending bureaucracy vanishes.
[C2.2] P2.2 also collapses arithmetically. If collection consumes 10-15% of fare revenue, most fare revenue remains for service and maintenance. Modern payment and all-door boarding address that cost directly. Deleting fares deletes the larger share too.
[C2.3] P2.3 spends the “savings” on capacity expansions, but one budget must now replace lost fares, maintain service, add capacity, and absorb growth. That spends one dollar several times. Otherwise “rationing” becomes crowding, pass-ups, repairs, and budget fights.
[C2.4] The tax argument shifts from mobility to diffuse benefits. Clean air and less congestion justify funding transit, not a zero price at the turnstile. P2.4’s nurse still waits forty minutes for a late-night bus; a tourist’s tax contribution does not make the route frequent. Broad taxation is not automatically proportional: renters, consumers, non-riders, and transit-desert residents can pay through sales taxes, fees, and pass-throughs.
[C2.5] Rising assessments near stations may reward property owners more than the low-income rider this policy claims to help.
[C2.6] Permanence is the final inversion. P2.6 treats insulation from pilots as a cultural mechanism; it is insulation from evidence. If the gains are modest, permanence blocks the city from redirecting money to frequency, coverage, safety, and late-night service. The alternative is targeted affordability and better service, not the status quo.
[C2.7] A right to mobility should be measured by whether people can actually get where they need to go, not by whether the turnstile is removed forever.

It turns transit from a discretionary service into essential infrastructure, like roads or water systems. The “failures” they cite are not arguments against fare-free—they’re symptoms of a system still treated as optional.
[P4.5] Under permanent fare-free, crowding justifies expansion, underfunding becomes a political scandal, and the virtuous cycle of investment and ridership begins. Unlike CON’s piecemeal approach, this delivers systemic change: targeted measures keep transit as welfare; permanent fare-free elevates it to its rightful place as the foundation of urban life.
