[P1.2] Start with the arithmetic. A pension system that pays benefits for twenty years when average retirement lasted fifteen has a structural gap no rate hike fully closes, because taxes fall on a shrinking base of workers per retiree. Raising payroll contributions to plug that gap means every hire costs more, hours get cut, and low-margin, labor-intensive work—care, hospitality, cleaning—contracts first. Those are exactly the jobs that fund the system.
[P1.3] Taxing work harder to fund retirement gradually shrinks the work that pays for retirement.
[P1.4] Linking the retirement age to life expectancy does the opposite: it aligns the promise with the reality that made it affordable, so legislatures stop lurching between crisis hikes and denial. Firms and households can plan decades ahead because the rule, not a political fight, adjusts the schedule.
[P1.5] This is also the fairer intergenerational bargain. A worker entering the labor market today should not face higher payroll taxes for a benefit formula set when her grandparents retired at 65 and died a few years after. [P1.6] The obvious objection—that some jobs break bodies before 67—deserves an answer, and the answer is design, not retreat: disability pathways, hardship exemptions, minimum pensions for long careers starting young, and earlier access for arduous occupations. Carve-outs cost far less than an across-the-board payroll tax increase, and they target the people who actually cannot work longer.
[P1.7] Raising the age is not cruelty. It is the only lever that keeps the promise solvent while leaving work cheaper to hire.
