[P1.1] Comparison shopping works only if the comparison-page number is the number a family will actually pay. A parent buying four seats is not buying four abstract tickets; they are buying normal luggage and, especially with young children, seats together. A base fare omitting baggage and seating is not a cheaper equivalent. It is a different product quoted as if comparable.
[P1.2] The carrier that unbundles most can look cheapest, win the click, and reveal the real bill only after the family has invested time and trust.
[P1.3] Two adults and two children may compare a $180-per-person fare with a $200-per-person fare. If the first adds checked-bag and seat-selection charges for each traveler, the apparently cheaper flight can become the more expensive trip. The family might still choose it knowingly. The injury is earlier: the comparison screen has steered them by a false ranking.
[P1.4] That is not consumer choice; it is choice after the price signal has been manipulated.
[P1.5] The requirement is modest and market-making: show the full practical cost before options are compared, using party size, itinerary, ordinary baggage, and the cost of seating the family together where that charge is imposed. If preferences vary, use a clear default and let users adjust it. Airlines could still sell bare-bones fares, charge for bags, and price premium seats. They simply could not use omitted necessities to look cheaper than an all-in rival.
[P1.6] Complexity is not a defense. Airlines and booking sites already know fare rules, bag rules, and seat inventory well enough to charge at checkout. If they can compute those fees near payment, they can show them when the customer is deciding which flight is cheaper. Complexity is why disclosure must arrive early: families lack time to rerun every search, and drip pricing punishes transparent firms.
[P1.7] Put the burden on the seller controlling the fee schedule, not on the parent juggling passengers, luggage, seating, and a checkout surprise.
