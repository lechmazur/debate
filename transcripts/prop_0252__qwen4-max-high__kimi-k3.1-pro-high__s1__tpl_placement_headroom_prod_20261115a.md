[P1.1] An unpaid internship is a job with the wages deleted and a euphemism attached. If someone spends six months drafting briefs, writing copy, or cleaning data that an organisation sells, that is productive labour, and the default rule of a decent labour market is that productive labour is paid. The motion already grants the honest exception: genuinely educational placements, supervised by an institution, where the trainee is the primary beneficiary. Everything else should meet a presumption of employment, with the employer proving otherwise.
[P1.2] That exception matters, because the strongest objection to restriction — "you'll destroy young people's opportunities" — usually describes something the motion protects. Clinical rotations, student-teaching, a semester of supervised lab work: untouched. What gets restricted is the media firm running its social media through a rotating cast of graduates, or the ministerial office staffed by people whose parents pay their rent. Those roles were never training.
[P1.3] They were staffing, financed by families instead of the budget.
[P1.4] Unpaid work does not create opportunity; it rations it. Two equally talented graduates apply; only the one who can live rent-free in an expensive city can accept. The filter is not aptitude but household wealth, and it compounds, because the unpaid stint becomes the prerequisite for the paid job.
[P1.5] Meanwhile the employer has no reason to create an entry-level position when the same work arrives free. Free labour at the bottom suppresses paid work at the bottom.
[P1.6] The question for the other side is simple: name the benefit that requires non-payment. Experience is not payment. Exposure is not payment. If the work is worth doing, it is worth a wage; if the learning is real, the carve-out already covers it.
[P1.7] Anything left over is someone else's profit margin wearing a lanyard.

[C1.1] PRO’s own examples expose the flaw. A student teacher manages a classroom; a clinical student performs useful work; a lab student may clean data. Learning and productive labour are not opposites. Yet the motion heavily restricts placements that fail a contestable “primary beneficiary” test or lack academic sponsorship.
[C1.2] That is not a clean rule against exploitation; it is protection for formal providers and uncertainty for everyone else.
[C1.3] Consider a graduate learning grant-writing at a small charity, or a career-changer mastering lighting at a local theatre. The coaching is genuine, the commitment limited, and no vacancy exists. Without a university partner or compliance staff, the safest response to heavy penalties is cancellation. Formal education is worthwhile, but making institutional recognition the gateway to workplace learning excludes precisely those outside established networks.
[C1.4] Nor does prohibition conjure wages. An exploitative firm may pay; a cash-poor organisation simply withdraws. Large employers can fund polished paid schemes; smaller charities, theatres, local papers, and startups cancel. Wealthy applicants still obtain shadowing through family contacts, while others lose a structured route in.
[C1.5] A policy sold as attacking privilege can make access more private, not more equal.
[C1.6] The better dividing line is conduct, not institutional status. Enforce wage law where interns displace employees; require defined supervision, limited hours and duration, expense reimbursement, and accessible complaints. Those measures punish bogus jobs without abolishing short, voluntary placements whose educational value is real but uncredentialed.
[C1.7] PRO asks what benefit requires non-payment. The answer is coaching where no paid job exists and no institution will sponsor one. Heavy restriction removes that option for everyone to stop abuses targeted enforcement can address. It sacrifices legitimate routes into work when the sensible task is to police exploitation, not to give universities and large employers a monopoly on opportunity.

[C5.1] The decisive point is not whether payment is preferable. It is. It is whether that preference justifies forcing workplace learning to prove its innocence under heavy penalties. It does not.
[C5.2] PRO’s final checklist concedes the workable rule: capped hours, named supervision, no lone responsibility for recurring functions. Those are conduct standards, enforceable against bogus staffing without presuming that every other placement is employment. Recurring staff work, staff cover and recruitment are visible evidence; enforcement need not divine an imaginary hiring decision. Reversed burdens therefore add uncertainty precisely where coaching is genuine but informal, not protection where exploitation is already visible.
[C5.3] Against that cost, PRO offers only a possibility: cancelled placements become wages. Sometimes they will; sometimes nothing will exist. The wealthy applicant retains family shadowing, while the local learner loses a structured Saturday route. Calling that progress counts an imaginary salary and ignores an actual opportunity.
[C5.4] So reject the false choice between employer immunity and heavy restriction. Punish fake jobs, require wages for displacement, reimburse expenses and expand paid access. But do not make genuine coaching prove its innocence under a contestable exemption. Targeted enforcement removes exploitation; heavy restriction can remove the opportunity and call that equality.
