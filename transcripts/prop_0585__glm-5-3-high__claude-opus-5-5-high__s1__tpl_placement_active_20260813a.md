[P1.1] The question is which investment buys more reliability and more decarbonization per dollar. Transmission and market integration win on both counts, and the gap isn't close.
[P1.2] Start with physics. The single largest driver of grid stress is geographic mismatch: wind and solar surge in one region while demand peaks in another, hours or days before or after. Transmission converts that mismatch from a storage problem into a scheduling problem. When Texas freezes or a heat dome settles over one state, electrons flow a thousand miles in milliseconds.
[P1.3] Batteries, by contrast, must be pre-positioned, pre-charged, and sized in advance for a load they may see a handful of hours per year. Long-duration storage is insurance against rare, extended lulls; transmission is infrastructure that delivers value every single day through ordinary arbitrage, congestion relief, and access to the cheapest clean energy on the continent.
[P1.4] The cost asymmetry is stark. Transmission is a mature technology with declining unit costs and a multi-decade asset life—fifty to a hundred years. Long-duration storage is, at grid scale, still largely a technology bet. Pumped hydro works but is geographically constrained and slow to permit.
[P1.5] Hydrogen, thermal, and multi-day batteries remain expensive, with round-trip efficiencies that squander a third to three-quarters of the energy stored. Subsidizing them heavily now means paying premium prices for immature chemistry while cheap, proven abatement sits trapped behind interconnection queues.
[P1.6] Consider what market integration actually does: it aggregates demand across millions of customers whose peaks don't coincide, aggregates supply across diverse weather systems, and lets reserves be shared rather than duplicated in every balancing area. That aggregation shrinks the storage requirement itself. A continentally connected grid needs far less storage per megawatt of clean energy than isolated islands do—meaning transmission doesn't compete with storage; it reduces the storage bill.
[P1.7] The proposition says "primarily." Storage has a role. But the foundation comes first: build the arteries, integrate the markets, and the storage that remains necessary becomes smaller, cheaper, and better targeted.
