[P1.1] Compulsory turnout is not a demand that citizens choose a candidate; it is a modest requirement that they show up and mark a ballot, with penalties scaled to income and waived for hardship. In stable democracies, where elections are free, secret, and administratively competent, that requirement is justified because the central democratic promise is government accountable to the whole public, not to the subset that happens to vote. The real contest is between democracy as rule by the people and democracy as rule by whoever happens to participate.
[P1.2] Voluntary turnout rarely produces a representative sample. It is systematically skewed toward the older, wealthier, more partisan, and more mobilized. When elections are decided by 40 or 50 percent of eligible voters, winners can claim mandates while entire neighborhoods, age cohorts, and income groups are absent from the deciding electorate. Politicians then rationally cater to likely voters: homeowners over renters, regulars over the precariously employed, activists over the exhausted middle.
[P1.3] The result is not merely low participation; it is policy distortion.
[P1.4] A proportional civil penalty fixes the incentive problem without coercing belief. Like jury duty or paying taxes, voting is a shared civic obligation. The state may ask everyone to bear a small, fair burden because the benefit—legitimate, representative decisions—depends on universal participation. A fine tied to income, with community service or hardship exemptions, respects proportionality and avoids punishing the poor.
[P1.5] Stable democracies are exactly the right cases. They have reliable rolls, secret ballots, independent courts, and enough trust to enforce turnout without turning it into repression. Weak regimes could abuse such power; mature democracies can use it to strengthen consent. If the objection is liberty, the liberty burden is minimal: appear, receive a ballot, and you may still leave it blank.
[P1.6] What is not minimal is letting an unrepresentative minority choose governments for everyone else.
