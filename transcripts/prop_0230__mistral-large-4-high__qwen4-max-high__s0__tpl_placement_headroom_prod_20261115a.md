[P1.1] Europe’s security architecture rests on a dangerous illusion: that professional armies alone can defend a continent of half a billion people. Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine shattered that complacency, exposing hollow stockpiles, shrunken reserves, and societies unprepared for sacrifice. Mandatory national service is not a nostalgic gesture; it is a structural necessity for deterrence and a powerful engine of social cohesion.
[P1.2] On defense, conscription creates the deep reserve that modern warfare demands. Finland maintains a wartime strength of roughly 280,000 from a population of 5.5 million precisely because nearly every male citizen trains and serves. Switzerland’s militia model keeps hundreds of thousands ready. When Lithuania reintroduced conscription in 2015 and Latvia followed recently, they did not merely add uniforms; they built societal resilience—citizens who know first aid, evacuation routes, and how to resist occupation.
[P1.3] A population that has served is harder to intimidate and easier to mobilize in a crisis. Professional soldiers cannot hold every border; armed, trained citizens can defend the homeland against hybrid threats too.
[P1.4] On cohesion, mandatory service forces interaction across the fractures that social media and segregated schooling have widened. A teenager from a wealthy Paris suburb and one from a rural Romanian village, spending months training, cooking, and standing watch together, develop shared vocabulary and loyalty that no diversity seminar can manufacture. France’s expanded Service National Universel attempts exactly this, and Germany is openly debating a similar model to rebuild civic trust across class lines.
[P1.5] Critics will cite cost and individual liberty, but the alternative is strategic irrelevance and fraying societies. Europe must choose between comfortable weakness and resilient solidarity. Mandatory national service delivers both deterrence and the social glue that democracies need to survive the next crisis. The time for half-measures has passed.
