[P1.1] Pay secrecy doesn't protect businesses; it protects bias. When large employers withhold pay bands and promotion rates, they don't preserve some neutral managerial tool—they maintain an information asymmetry that consistently disadvantages workers, particularly women and minorities, while insulating discriminatory patterns from scrutiny.
[P1.2] The core trade-off here is clear: some managerial flexibility diminishes, but what that flexibility often enables is precisely the problem. Without transparency, a manager can pay two people doing the same job dramatically different amounts, and neither employee has the information to question it. Promotion rates hidden by department become impossible to audit for bias. The "flexibility" defenders invoke is, in practice, the freedom to make opaque, unaccountable decisions that cluster into systemic disparities.
[P1.3] Consider what transparency actually produces. Colorado's pay transparency requirements didn't collapse businesses—they compressed unjustified pay gaps. When workers see the range for their role, they can negotiate from knowledge rather than guesswork. When promotion rates by department become visible, patterns of exclusion become visible too.
[P1.4] A company where women are promoted at half the rate of men in comparable roles shouldn't discover this through a lawsuit; it should discover it through routine disclosure.
[P1.5] The argument that this burdens employers gets the burden calculus backward. Workers currently carry the burden of navigating careers blind, accepting offers without context, and discovering years later that colleagues with identical credentials earn significantly more. Disclosure shifts the cost of information to the party best positioned to provide it—the employer.
[P1.6] Managerial discretion isn't vanishing; it's being constrained within visible boundaries. Managers can still pay for performance within bands. They can still promote based on merit. What they lose is the ability to hide arbitrary decisions behind institutional silence.
[P1.7] Pay transparency treats compensation as what it is: a matter of public concern for workers and for markets that only function when participants have information. The modest constraint on flexibility is not just justified—it's necessary.

[C1.1] Mandated disclosure sounds like sunlight. In practice, it delivers fog, compression, and a worse deal for the workers it claims to champion.
[C1.2] Start with what mandates actually produce. When employers are forced to post pay bands, they respond rationally: bands balloon. New York City's law produced postings with ranges like $90,000 to $250,000—technically compliant, informationally worthless. Companies also retitle roles, fragment job categories, and restructure ladders so that "promotion rates by role" become statistically meaningless—a department with four people in a role will show wild swings every year that say nothing about fairness.
[C1.3] A mandate cannot compel honesty; it can only compel paperwork, and paperwork gets gamed.
[C1.4] Second, the measured effects of transparency cut against workers. Research on pay disclosure regimes—including studies of Canadian and Scandinavian transparency rules—has repeatedly found that the dominant effect is wage compression achieved by holding down the top, not lifting the bottom. Employers facing public bands stop making aggressive retention offers, stop rewarding off-cycle excellence, and anchor everyone to the midpoint, because every exception becomes a grievance. The star engineer who would have gotten a 20% counteroffer to stay now gets a shrug.
[C1.5] Overall wage growth slows. That is not a hypothetical cost of lost flexibility; it is the predictable mechanism.
[C1.6] Third, this is the wrong tool for the stated problem. Discrimination is real, but it is already illegal, and the remedy is targeted enforcement: pay-equity audits, agency investigations, litigation discovery. Blanket public disclosure punishes every employer—and chills legitimate, individualized pay decisions—to catch the minority that discriminates, while sophisticated discriminators simply hide inside compliant, gamed bands.
[C1.7] Finally, in small teams, role-level disclosure effectively publishes individual salaries, stripping employees of their own privacy without consent.
[C1.8] The choice is not transparency versus secrecy. It is between honest, targeted accountability and a compulsory ritual that compresses wages, manufactures misleading statistics, and trades real flexibility for fake clarity.
