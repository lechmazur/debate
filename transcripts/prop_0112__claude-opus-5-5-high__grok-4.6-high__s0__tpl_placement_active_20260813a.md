[P1.1] Basic medical coverage has one job: be there when you get sick. Employer-based insurance fails that test by design, because it ties your health care to the one thing illness is most likely to take away, which is your ability to work.
[P1.2] Picture a warehouse worker diagnosed with cancer. Treatment makes full shifts impossible. She loses the job, and with it her insurance, at exactly the moment she needs it most. That is not a glitch; it is the architecture.
[P1.3] Work status is a terrible proxy for medical need, and it often runs opposite to need. The sick, the disabled, workers laid off in a recession, and caregivers who quit to look after a parent all lose coverage. Single payer covers her because she is a resident, not because she clocked in. The basic floor should follow the person, not the paycheck.
[P1.4] Second, tying coverage to jobs distorts the labor market. People stay in jobs they hate because leaving means losing coverage for a spouse with diabetes. Would-be founders never start businesses. Workers bargain timidly because the employer effectively holds their family's health care.
[P1.5] Small firms lose talent to giants that can afford rich plans. Single payer frees workers to move and frees businesses from moonlighting as insurance administrators.
[P1.6] Third, single payer delivers the same floor for less. One payer means one rulebook and one claims system instead of armies of billing staff fighting dozens of insurers over codes. It also brings real bargaining power over drug and hospital prices. The United States, the flagship employer-based system, spends far more per person than Canada, Taiwan or the UK, yet leaves tens of millions uninsured or underinsured.
[P1.7] CON will say single payer means higher taxes. But workers already pay: employer premiums come out of wages they would otherwise take home. The real question is not whether we pay. It is whether we keep paying for coverage that vanishes the moment we need it.
