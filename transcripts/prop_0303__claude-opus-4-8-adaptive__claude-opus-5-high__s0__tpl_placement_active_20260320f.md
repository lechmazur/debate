[P1.1] The claim isn't that talent doesn't matter. It's that we systematically overstate how much a modern meritocracy has replaced inherited advantage—and the people who benefit most are the least likely to see it. Let me set the terms of the debate around three mechanisms.
[P1.2] First, the pipeline is rigged long before the interview. By the time a student sits an elite admissions test, roughly two decades of unequal investment have already happened: tutoring, stable housing, private schools or well-funded public ones, summers spent on enrichment rather than earning rent money. When a wealthy child and a poor child both score well, we call it merit.
[P1.3] But merit measured at age eighteen is largely a readout of resources spent from age zero. The starting line is the story.
[P1.4] Second, elite gatekeeping rewards cultural fluency disguised as excellence. Selective admissions and prestige hiring lean on "well-roundedness," unpaid internships, the right extracurriculars, polished interview manner, and networks that convert a family connection into a foot in the door. [P1.5] Third—and this is decisive—the meritocratic story is self-serving. Once you clear these hurdles, the most comfortable belief is that you did it alone. That's why successful people underreport their advantages: not from dishonesty, but because attributing success to effort feels better and justifies the rewards. The gap between how much privilege actually operates and how much winners admit is the exact space this motion occupies.
[P1.6] The burden here is asymmetric. CON must show that pathways have genuinely become fair enough that class background is no longer a heavy thumb on the scale. I only need to show that privilege still reproduces itself more than the winners acknowledge.
