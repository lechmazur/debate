[P1.1] Blanket pet bans exist to mitigate property damage and nuisance, but they are a blunt, overinclusive tool that imposes unnecessary costs on both tenants and property owners by treating every pet and pet owner as an identical high risk, when the overwhelming majority of pet-owning tenants cause no more long-term property wear than non-pet owners. Standardized, dedicated damage insurance or secured deposits eliminate the core risk bans are designed to address, guaranteeing immediately accessible, ring-fenced funds to cover any pet-related harm without drawing on generic security deposits or forcing landlords to chase former tenants for repayment after move-out.
[P1.2] Unlike ad-hoc, inconsistent pet policies that vary building to building, a uniform standardized requirement eliminates guesswork for landlords: no risk of tenants carrying worthless, low-limit plans, no arbitrary rejection of well-behaved animals based on unfounded breed or size stereotypes unlinked to actual damage risk, and no incentive for tenants to sneak unvetted pets into units to avoid bans—a common practice that leaves landlords with zero advance notice, zero set-aside damage funds, and no ability to set clear conduct expectations for animals on the property.
[P1.3] Nor do bans stop noise or nuisance: tenants who sneak pets in have no reason to comply with animal conduct rules, since admitting the animal exists already puts them at eviction risk. When pets are registered openly, landlords can apply the same nuisance rules they already use for loud parties or unruly guests. This model also delivers tangible financial benefits to building owners: pet-friendly units with guaranteed damage coverage draw from a large, underserved tenant pool that stays significantly longer to avoid the hassle of seeking pet-permitted housing, cutting costly turnover expenses that routinely exceed the cost of rare, isolated pet damage.
[P1.4] This approach delivers better risk protection than blanket bans, while expanding fair access for responsible tenants.

[P2.1] The core flaw in the opposition’s case is that it judges a default pet-permitted policy against an imaginary world where blanket bans actually eliminate pet-related nuisance, allergen exposure, and damage—when every property manager knows bans do no such thing. Tenants who want pets do not vanish when a building posts a no-pet rule; they hide their animals, leaving no registration on file, no proof of vaccination or flea prevention, no dedicated damage funds on reserve, and no ability to hold owners accountable for barking, hallway incidents, or allergen spread before problems escalate.
[P2.2] When the opposition complains that nuisance enforcement is slow, they ignore that enforcement against unregistered pets is infinitely slower: tenants can lie, delay, and drag out disputes for months by denying the animal exists at all, with no pre-existing paper trail to cut through that friction. Open registration means no factual fight over whether a dog is even present, so landlords can resolve barking or hallway conflicts faster, not slower. Right now, residents with severe allergies already share spaces with unregistered, unvetted pets in buildings that advertise as pet-free, with zero notice or mitigation protocols in place.
[P2.3] The claim that insurance and deposits cannot cover cross-unit damage misrepresents what standardized pet damage coverage is designed for: unlike capped generic security deposits, dedicated pet policies explicitly cover gradual damage including odor remediation and subfloor repair, rather than only acute, obvious harm. Nor does a default rule eliminate pet-free housing; it simply ends the automatic, one-size-fits-all ban that currently locks responsible, long-term tenants out of units while failing to deliver the quiet, allergen-free living environment opponents claim to protect.
[P2.4] Pet-free buildings will still exist for residents who need them, but they will be a deliberate choice, not a lazy default that fails on its own terms.
