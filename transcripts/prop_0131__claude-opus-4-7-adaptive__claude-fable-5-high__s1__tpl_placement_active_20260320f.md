[P1.2] My case rests on three claims.
[P1.3] First, the alternative is more expensive, not less. When informal care collapses — and it collapses constantly, through burnout, divorce, illness, or simple exhaustion — the relative ends up in a hospital bed or a nursing facility at many times the cost of supporting the caregiver at home. Paying family carers a real wage is not new spending; it's substitution for spending governments are already on the hook for, delivered by the person the patient actually trusts.
[P1.4] Second, generosity changes behavior at the margin. A token stipend keeps people trapped: too little to live on, too much to ignore. A genuine wage — with payroll contributions, respite hours, and training — lets a caregiver stay attached to the labor market, accrue a pension, and continue caring sustainably instead of crashing out at year three. Countries and regions that have moved toward proper carer payments, like parts of Scandinavia and some U.S.
[P1.5] Medicaid self-direction programs, show that paid family caregiving is workable, auditable, and popular.
[P1.6] Third, the demographic math is merciless. Populations are aging while birth rates fall. The pool of available unpaid relatives is shrinking per elder every year. A policy that "assumes unpaid care will continue to absorb the burden" is assuming something arithmetic forbids.
[P1.7] The question isn't whether society will pay for this care. It's whether we keep pretending it's free.

[C1.2] Start with the fiscal trap PRO glosses over: the woodwork effect. The overwhelming majority of family care happens today without payment, motivated by love and obligation. The moment government offers a "genuine wage," it must pay not just the marginal caregiver who would otherwise quit, but every existing caregiver who comes out of the woodwork to claim it. That isn't substitution — it's paying full price for something currently happening anyway, on a scale of millions of households.
[C1.3] The savings PRO promises evaporate, and the money that could have funded home-care services, respite, and adult day programs is consumed by transfers that change nothing about who receives care.
[C1.4] Second, generous wages don't liberate caregivers — they entrench them. Pay a daughter a state salary to leave her job and bathe her mother, and you have converted her into a low-wage care worker with no colleagues, no career ladder, no promotion, and no exit. The gendered "hidden tax" PRO laments gets formalized, not abolished. Women's labor-force attachment falls; the policy subsidizes precisely the pattern PRO claims to oppose.
[C1.5] The genuinely pro-caregiver agenda is professional home care, paid leave, and respite — services that let relatives remain daughters, sons, and workers rather than become employees of their parents' decline.
[C1.6] Third, accountability. Professional care is trained, supervised, and inspectable. Cash flowing into private homes is not. Programs paying family members have repeatedly struggled with fraud, with payments continuing after care stops, and — most darkly — with creating financial incentives to isolate an elder and gatekeep their benefits.
[C1.7] And PRO's demographic point cuts against them: if relatives per elder are shrinking, the answer cannot be doubling down on the family model. It's building a professional workforce that scales. Spend the money there.
