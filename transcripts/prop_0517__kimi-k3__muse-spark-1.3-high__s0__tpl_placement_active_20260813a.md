[P1.1] Every gatekeeper should ask two questions: can this person do the work, and do they pose a risk? Credentials — degrees, licenses, verified assessments — answer both. Residency answers neither; it records where someone sat, not what they can do. This motion asks only that friction follow function: the process proving capability should run smoother than the process proving geography.
[P1.2] Start with purpose. A hospital hiring a foreign-trained nurse is protected by her exam results and supervised hours, not by the decade she spent in Manila. Regimes that make residency the easy path and credentials the obstacle course invert the logic of safety itself — and the costs are concrete: wards stay short-staffed while qualified people wait on paperwork that proves nothing about competence. Easing credential recognition aligns the gate with what the gate is for.
[P1.3] Next, verifiability. A digitally issued credential can be confirmed with its issuer in minutes — signed, time-stamped, tamper-evident. Residency rests on leases, utility bills, entry stamps, and discretionary interviews: easy to fabricate, slow to check. Sound policy makes the more verifiable thing easier, and every advance in digital verification widens that asymmetry.
[P1.4] Finally, fairness. Residency-first pathways reward those who could already afford the visa fees, the relocation, the years in-country — mobility capital, not merit. Easier credential recognition lets a self-taught developer in Lagos or a pharmacist in Bogotá compete on knowledge rather than birthplace, converting opportunity from an accident of geography into a function of ability.
[P1.5] The predictable objection is fraud — diploma mills, fake certificates. But the answer to fraud is verification, and verification is precisely what digital credential registries do best. Faking an address is far easier than forging a cryptographically signed record from a real institution.
[P1.6] The burden on CON is stark: defend a world where proof of where you sat moves faster than proof of what you can do. That world wastes talent, weakens safety, and entrenches privilege. We should build the other one.
