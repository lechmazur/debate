[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane, but it mistakes what government owes people. It does not owe every settlement permanent economic viability; it owes people opportunity, security, and a real choice about where to build a life. Cities grow because they let workers specialize, firms find suppliers, and ideas cross paths. That is not a policy trick, and it is not undone by scattering more subsidies across the map.
[C1.2] The PRO case says the playing field is tilted. Fine—then fix the tilt: reform housing supply, price congestion, stop metro-only tax deals. But that does not justify a permanent rural-viability program. “Do more to keep smaller towns economically viable” means place-based subsidies, protected local employers, infrastructure sized for shrinking populations, and incentives for firms to locate where the market would not. Those policies have a familiar record: they can slow decline for a while, but they rarely create self-sustaining economies.
[C1.3] Too often they subsidize landowners, incumbent firms, and politically connected projects while the young and ambitious still leave.
[C1.4] The fairness argument cuts the other way. If a town’s hospital, school, and employer are collapsing, the humane response is not to trap a seventeen-year-old there with a subsidy and a slogan. It is to make moving less punishing and staying less isolated: portable benefits, housing in productive regions, broadband, transport links, and targeted help for those who cannot move. That helps people wherever they go.
[C1.5] A policy obsessed with keeping every region “viable” spreads public money thinly and asks taxpayers in successful cities to underwrite declining property values indefinitely.
[C1.6] PRO says we need not choose between cities and towns. But resources are finite. Every dollar spent preserving an unviable local economy is a dollar not spent on the people who live there. Governments should manage concentration—housing, infrastructure, congestion—not pretend they can repeal it town by town.
