[P1.1] Historically, communities gathered at weddings to help launch a new household. Today, wedding culture demands the exact opposite: couples are pressured to drain their savings, delay buying a home, or take on debt just to host a one-day luxury gala for everyone else. When a cultural ritual shifts from supporting a marriage to cannibalizing its financial foundation, the obligation to follow that script evaporates.
[P1.2] Look at what meeting "traditional expectations" actually requires in today's market. Attach the word "wedding" to a venue, a meal, or flowers, and the price instantly multiplies. The expected baseline is no longer a modest reception in a local hall; it has escalated into a five-figure production with formal catering, open bars, professional videography, and expensive destination pre-wedding trips. Pressuring young couples—who already face steep housing and living costs—to bankroll this inflated industry out of family duty actively harms their future.
[P1.3] Moreover, what gets enforced as sacred tradition is increasingly just performative spectacle. Driven by relentless commercialization and social media, the modern wedding has morphed from a communal vow into a curated content shoot. Couples are expected to stage elaborate wedding-party proposals, police guest aesthetics, and manufacture picture-perfect moments for an online audience. The anxiety of directing a flawless show routinely eclipses the actual commitment, turning partners into stressed event producers on a day meant to celebrate their union.
[P1.4] Eliminating the obligation to conform does not stop anyone who genuinely wants and can afford a grand wedding from having one. It simply removes the guilt and familial coercion weaponized against couples who choose a courthouse ceremony, an elopement, or a simple backyard dinner instead. A couple’s real duty is to the stability of their marriage, not the entertainment of extended relatives. Because modern wedding culture trades substance for expensive theater, couples owe those expectations almost nothing.

The obligation to include them is not guilt-tripping; it is the reciprocal cost of being woven into a support network.

[C1.4] PRO also mistakes ritual for performance. Symbolism is not superficiality. The wedding is a vow made visible, not theater for its own sake, and it is not fake because phones are present.
[C1.5] The problem is not ceremony; it is commercialization, and the remedy is to strip away the vendor markup, not to abandon the form.
[C1.6] Couples should absolutely resist debt and coercion. But the motion asks for something far more radical: little obligation at all. That answer isolates rather than liberates. The better path is to honor the communal core of the wedding while rejecting the expensive theater layered on top.
[C1.7] What couples owe their communities is not a spectacle, but acknowledgment. Tradition is not the enemy of marriage; treating marriage as a transaction is.
