[P1.1] Sign up for many services today takes one tap and a stored-card confirmation. Canceling them can take a treasure hunt: hidden settings pages, chatbots that pretend not to understand, mandatory retention chats, phone lines open only while you are at work, or even mailed letters. That gap is not accidental user-experience drift; it is engineered friction, and it turns ordinary forgetting into recurring revenue. It is a business model: keep the clicks that acquire you, hide the clicks that release you.
[P1.2] This is a real consumer-welfare harm, not annoyance. Consumer welfare is not only the monthly price. It also includes the ability to understand terms, act on preferences, and exit. When firms make enrollment instant but exit costly, they charge a second, undisclosed price: time, attention, phone minutes, and autonomy.
[P1.3] A free trial that auto-converts is fine only if escape is genuinely available. When it is buried, the “consent” to keep paying is hollow.
[P1.4] Look at the incentive. A firm confident in its value can make leaving easy and still retain customers; friction is what a company buys when it expects many users to stay only because leaving is a hassle. That is why cancellation mandates work: not to punish business, but to force firms to compete on service rather than on obscurity.
[P1.5] Regulators should therefore require symmetry: cancellation must be as easy as sign-up. If enrollment is two clicks online, cancellation must be two clicks online, in the same channel, without mandatory upsells, without deceptive delays, and with immediate confirmation. This does not ban subscriptions or trials. It simply removes the subsidy that dark patterns extract from consumer inattention.
[P1.6] The beneficiaries are everyone whose attention is scarce—shift workers, caregivers, older users. Subscription fatigue is not imaginary; it is the monthly ritual of auditing bank statements for “subscriptions” you cannot remember approving. Easy cancellation restores real choice, reinstates market discipline, and lets subscriptions compete on merit.

[C1.1] The dark-pattern problem is real enough to police, but “cancellation must be as easy as sign-up” is the wrong rule: vague, overbroad, and likely to harm the same consumers it claims to protect.
[C1.2] First, sign-up and cancellation are not mirror images. Signing up is an affirmative choice to start a relationship and pay; cancelling ends access, sometimes deletes data, forfeits grandfathered pricing, or breaks a bundle. A rational process may confirm identity, warn about consequences, or offer a pause. The PRO motion [P1.5] treats any such step as illegitimate friction.
[C1.3] But a confirmation screen is not a dark pattern; it is protection against accidental cancellation, especially for family plans, business accounts, or services tied to devices and warranties.
[C1.4] Second, “as easy as sign-up” is unenforceable in practice. Firms can satisfy it by making sign-up harder—adding steps, forcing phone verification, or requiring in-app purchase. The rule then targets the wrong side. Or they can implement one-click cancel and quietly degrade support, so users who cancel by mistake have no easy way back.
[C1.5] The mandate invites compliance theater, not genuine consumer control.
[C1.6] Third, the consumer-welfare case is weaker than PRO’s vivid examples suggest. Subscription fatigue is often the cost of convenient, low-friction trials; many cancellations already occur through app stores, card issuers, virtual cards, and chargeback rights. Where a firm uses deception—hidden charges, fake retention agents—existing fraud and unfair-practice law can strike directly. A blanket parity rule instead raises compliance costs for every gym, newspaper, software tool, and small SaaS provider.
[C1.7] Those costs return as higher prices, fewer cheap trials, and less willingness to serve marginal customers. The people who lose most are low-income users who benefit from low entry prices.
[C1.8] Regulators should punish deception, not mandate a simplistic symmetry. Make cancellation clear, yes; make it legally identical to sign-up, no. That would sacrifice consumer choice for regulatory sloganeering.
