[P1.1] Global supply chains are built on an architecture of deliberate opacity. When forced labor extracts minerals, child labor harvests crops, or sweatshops collapse, multinational corporations reliably retreat behind plausible deniability. They claim ignorance because the abuse occurred two or three tiers removed from their direct contracts. Decades of voluntary corporate social responsibility have proven that when firms grade their own homework, they produce public relations, not accountability.
[P1.2] Mandating standardized, audited human-rights reports destroys that deniability.
[P1.3] First, standardization replaces corporate spin with comparability. Currently, companies cherry-pick harmless metrics—touting community philanthropy while concealing bonded labor in their supply base. A uniform standard forces every enterprise to disclose identical, material indicators of risk, arming consumers, regulators, and investors with the objective data needed to reward genuine reform and punish exploitation.
[P1.4] Second, independent auditing provides the missing teeth. An unverified report is merely advertising. Rigorous third-party audits pierce multi-tiered supplier opacity, verifying whether safety protocols, wage standards, and prohibitions on child labor genuinely exist on factory floors and farms. When falsifying reports brings legal and financial exposure, boards will finally treat labor abuses as operational liabilities rather than PR inconveniences.
[P1.5] Finally, this mandate corrects a toxic market distortion. Today, ethical firms that invest heavily to purge abuse from their supply chains are undercut by rivals that intentionally exploit sweatshops hidden in regulatory blind spots. Universal disclosure levels the playing field, making human rights compliance a basic cost of entry rather than a competitive disadvantage.
[P1.6] Large multinationals possess the capital, sophisticated logistics, and commercial leverage to police their networks. If their profits cross borders, their basic human obligations must follow. Sunlight cannot remain optional.

If a uniform standard demands zero child labor, the rational supplier response is not remediation; it is falsifying age records, moving underage workers to undeclared home-based work, or routing orders through unmonitored workshops. The published report can improve while abuse becomes harder to see. Comparability is useful only if the data reflect the factory floor.
[C1.4] The mandate also creates a liability trap. Once the public report becomes raw material for litigation and campaigns, boards treat high-risk suppliers as legal threats rather than partners to improve. [C1.5] A tool meant to protect people at the bottom pushes them out of the regulated economy.
[C1.6] The leveling-the-field claim ignores how the burden falls. Multinationals can fund compliance departments; suppliers cannot absorb many overlapping, conflicting national standards. Countries will not adopt identical definitions. The likely result is a trade barrier dressed as ethics, favoring formal factories while excluding poorer suppliers.
[C1.7] Paper accountability is cheap for headquarters and costly for the workers it claims to protect. The better target is independent inspection and enforcement, not a global reporting ritual.
