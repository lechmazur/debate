[P1.1] Astronauts are the most expensive, fragile, and limited scientific instruments we can send into space. Keeping them alive demands air, water, food, radiation shielding, pressurized hulls, abort rockets, rescue plans, and a return trip — mass and money that produce no data about a planet, a moon, or a star. A robot pays none of that tax. It can travel one-way, endure cold, radiation, and vacuum for decades, work without sleep, and be lost without a national tragedy.
[P1.2] That is why the science-per-dollar gap is not marginal; it is structural.
[P1.3] The record proves it. Robotic probes have visited every planet, landed on Mars, Titan, and asteroids, flown through comet tails, and left the solar system. Orbiting telescopes have mapped the cosmic microwave background, found thousands of exoplanets, and looked back to the first galaxies. By contrast, crewed flight for half a century has largely circled Earth in low orbit, and its signature research question is how spaceflight harms the crew itself.
[P1.4] That is circular science: spending billions to study the problem created by sending people in the first place.
[P1.5] The opportunity cost is decisive. A single flagship crewed program to return to the Moon or attempt Mars consumes funding that could sustain an entire fleet — ocean probes to Europa and Enceladus where life might exist, a Venus mission, sample returns, a new generation of telescopes to image Earth-like worlds and search their atmospheres.
[P1.6] Instead of one high-risk destination chosen because humans can survive there, agencies could explore ten places chosen because the science matters there.
[P1.7] This gap will widen. Sensors, autonomy, and miniaturization improve every year; human physiology does not. Shifting most exploration funding to robots and telescopes is not abandoning ambition — it is buying vastly more discovery, in more places, for longer, without risking lives to do work machines already do better.

The Apollo astronauts, working on the Moon for a few days each, made observations, chose samples with judgment, and adapted to surprises in ways no rover has matched. On Mars, our most capable rovers cover in months what a geologist could traverse in an afternoon — and the rovers cannot drill, bend, climb, or change the plan when the interesting rock is over the ridge.
[C1.3] Robots are magnificent at surveying; humans are what you send when the questions get hard.
[C1.4] Second, the ledger is rigged. My opponent counts every dollar of crewed infrastructure as pure overhead. [C1.5] The "entire fleet" that supposedly replaces one crewed program has never materialized when tested, because flagship costs expand to fill whatever budget exists. The bottleneck isn't astronauts; it's flagship mission culture.
[C1.6] Third, and decisive: political sustainability. Space science budgets are not handed down on stone tablets; they are voted. [C1.7] Gut crewed programs, and the science budget doesn't double; it withers into a line item with no constituency.

[P2.6] Discovery inspires, not only departure.
[P2.7] A steady stream of wonders from many worlds is the strongest constituency science can build — exactly what robots supply and crewed-first budgets postpone.

[C2.1] My opponent says Apollo proves their point because astronauts only worked a few days. But that comparison is rigged in their favor. Robots survive for years precisely because they work in slow motion — throttled by bandwidth, light-delay, and cautious autonomy. A Mars rover takes days to decide what a field scientist decides in a glance, because a human twenty light-minutes away cannot drive it.
[C2.2] Duration is not capability; it is the price of absence.
[C2.3] On infrastructure, they say robots merely need "reliable uncrewed launch" — as if it falls from the sky. Heavy-lift capability has historically been developed on the back of the largest customers, and crewed programs are those customers. Strip them out, and you don't get a leaner launch market; you get a thinner one, with fewer suppliers and higher per-flight costs for every science payload. The flagship observatory my opponent defends still has to ride something.
[C2.4] Their strongest concession is hiding in plain sight: "a leaner crewed program can retain symbolism and skills." Leaner means incapable. We watched this movie after Apollo ended — the promised dividend of cheap science never arrived; budgets sagged, the industrial base scattered, and rebuilding human capability took a generation. You cannot mothball a craft like a telescope and restart it when convenient.
[C2.5] And yes, a rover landing or a deep-field image commands attention — for a news cycle. Then it fades, because there is no one to follow, no risk, no story. The black-hole photograph trended for a week; astronauts have been inspiring schoolchildren for sixty continuous years. Sustained political coalitions are built on sustained narrative, not press releases.
[C2.6] The real choice is not fleet versus footprint. It is a living, resilient exploration enterprise versus a science directorate that inherits a shrinking budget with no constituency defending it. That is what "most" actually means in practice — and it is why the motion fails.

[P4.1] A1: The dividend was never actually tried. When Apollo ended, the money did not shift to robots inside exploration; it largely left NASA as total spending fell. When the Shuttle retired, its savings were recycled into the next generation of crewed vehicles and commercial crew development. A cut followed by an exit, and a retirement followed by a replacement, are not a reallocation.
[P4.2] And even then, the enduring exploration occurred: Viking on Mars, Voyager through the outer planets, and later orbiters and telescopes — while crewed flight remained in low Earth orbit studying itself.
[P4.3] A2: What survives is regular low-Earth-orbit crew rotation bought as a commercial service, plus a modest station and life-support testbed. That keeps rendezvous and docking, spacewalks, crew medicine, and closed-loop life support in routine use, and keeps astronauts visibly flying. What does not survive is a government-owned flagship campaign to the Moon or Mars whose vehicles, landers, and abort architecture set the entire budget. Buying transport as a service rather than building a destination empire is how the skill base persists without swallowing exploration.
[P4.4] Those answers expose the trap in the opposing case. For them, any crewed program small enough to save serious money is by definition too small to count, so crewed flight must remain the budgetary center forever. That is not an argument; it is a veto. It would lock agencies into paying the life-support tax on every major ambition, indefinitely, on the speculation that voters will otherwise abandon space entirely.
[P4.5] But voters have repeatedly rallied to discoveries, and skills are maintained by flying routinely in orbit, not by funding the most expensive possible destination. The choice remains stark: preserve a real but right-sized human presence, and spend most exploration money where it buys years of work across many worlds — or let the needs of a few crew members dictate which planets, moons, and questions we can afford to ask. Only the first option maximizes science without abandoning humans in space.
