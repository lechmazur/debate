[P1.1] A filter that turns on before a child ever chooses it is not a feature; it is a lesson. It teaches that the natural face is a draft to be corrected. The rule is narrow but decisive: social platforms and beauty apps should not apply face-slimming, skin-smoothing, or age-shifting filters to minors by default.
[P1.2] Defaults matter because most people take the path of least resistance. When a camera opens already smoothing skin, slimming a jaw, or shaving years off a face, the unaltered image becomes the deviation. For an adult, that is a nuisance; for a minor, it is a norm under construction. Adolescence is precisely when appearance, peer feedback, and identity are being welded together.
[P1.3] A twelve-year-old does not merely see a playful effect; she sees the version of herself the platform considers presentable. Repeated daily, the mirror becomes an accusation: my real face is the problem, and the app is the solution.
[P1.4] The concrete harm is not speculative. Clinicians have described patients bringing in filtered selfies as the face they want. When a minor's baseline selfie is already narrowed, poreless, and younger, ordinary skin, baby fat, and asymmetry start to look like defects. That is how a convenience feature can feed body dissatisfaction and, in vulnerable users, dysmorphic thinking.
[P1.5] Age-shifting filters are worse: they sexualize or infantilize a child's own image before she has the standing to consent to it.
[P1.6] The other side will say minors can opt out, parents can supervise, and filters are playful. But opt-out is exactly the point. A default for a minor is not neutral availability; it makes the alteration the presumptive truth. We are not banning filters.
[P1.7] We say the natural face should be the start, and the altered face should require a deliberate choice. If a platform can autoplay sound, it can also auto-respect a minor's face. The default should be honest, and the choice should be the child's.
