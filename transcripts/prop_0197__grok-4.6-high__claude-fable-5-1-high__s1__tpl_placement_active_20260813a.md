[P1.1] Orbital space is a thin, shared commons. Kinetic anti-satellite tests shatter a satellite into thousands of fragments that remain in orbit for years or decades. Those pieces do not stay still. They collide, spawn more fragments, and can lock entire altitude bands into a debris cascade no one can sweep up.
[P1.2] That is the irreversible harm this treaty exists to stop.
[P1.3] We have already seen the mechanism. A kinetic intercept turns one object into a persistent cloud. Debris from the 2007 Chinese test still forces avoidance maneuvers. Later Indian and Russian intercepts added more trackable wreckage and countless smaller pieces that sensors cannot catalog well.
[P1.4] Each demonstration lasts minutes; the hazard lasts generations. Operators already burn fuel dodging known objects. The untrackable debris still kills satellites.
[P1.5] A test ban is the high-leverage rule, not a demand that states abandon every counterspace option. It targets the specific act that injects the largest, longest-lived debris fields into orbits everyone uses. Ground radars and optical networks already detect explosions and new catalogs of objects. A debris-generating intercept is hard to hide, which makes the prohibition verifiable rather than aspirational.
[P1.6] Without a treaty the incentives run backward. Proving a kill is politically tempting. Each state that tests poisons the commons for itself as well as its rivals. Unilateral restraint is unstable because the first defector captures a prestige gain while everyone else inherits the wreckage.
[P1.7] A binding ban converts that race into a shared constraint.
[P1.8] Waiting is not free. GPS timing, weather forecasting, disaster warning, broadband, and reconnaissance all ride in the same crowded shells. Once a cascade takes hold, those services degrade together. Ban the tests now, while the environment is still usable.
[P1.9] The alternative is to keep proving weapons in the one domain where the wreckage never falls away in time.

Weapons tests are a minority contributor, and the problem is growing fastest from thousands of new commercial satellites crowding the same shells.
[C1.3] A treaty that fixates on tests while saying nothing about fragmentation generally is a fire code that regulates candles and ignores the wiring.
[C1.4] Second, "all" is the wrong word. Not every kinetic test poisons orbit for generations. The 2008 American intercept of a failing satellite occurred so low that the fragments reentered within months. India's 2019 test was likewise designed so most debris decayed quickly.

[C1.6] Third, the treaty binds only those who would already abstain. A voluntary moratorium has spread among Western states since 2022 without any treaty. [C1.7] We detected 2007 and 2021 perfectly well. Nothing followed.
[C1.8] Finally, banning tests does not ban weapons. An untested interceptor still exists, and a state that never tested is more likely to fire in a crisis without knowing where its debris will go. The treaty regulates the demonstration and leaves the danger intact.
