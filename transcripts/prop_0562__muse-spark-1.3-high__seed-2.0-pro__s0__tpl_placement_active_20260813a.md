[P1.1] A teenager caught with a small bag of drugs and a shoplifting charge should not sit weeks in a cage because she lacks five hundred dollars while a richer person charged with the same offense sleeps at home. That is exactly what cash bail for nonviolent offenses does: it turns poverty into punishment before any conviction.
[P1.2] This violates the core promise of innocent until proven guilty. Pretrial detention for a theft, drug possession, or driving offense triggers a cascade: lost jobs, missed rent, lost custody, and crushing pressure to plead guilty just to get out, guilty or not. The conviction that follows then shadows employment and housing for years. Wealth buys freedom; poverty buys a record.
[P1.3] Public safety does not require this cruelty. By definition we are talking about most nonviolent offenses, not assaults or armed robbery. For shoplifting, fraud, drug possession, judges can still detain the rare person who poses a genuine flight risk or threat. For everyone else, supervised release and simple court reminders work.
[P1.4] A text, a phone call, transportation help, and periodic check-ins address the main reason people miss court: forgetfulness, confusion, and poverty, not criminal masterminding.
[P1.5] That approach is both more effective and cheaper. Housing someone in jail costs taxpayers far more per day than supervision, while doing nothing to make them more likely to appear. Jurisdictions that cut back on cash bail, such as New Jersey and Washington, D.C., have kept court appearance and public safety largely intact without a wealth test at the jailhouse door.
[P1.6] Equal justice cannot coexist with a price tag on pretrial freedom for low-level charges. Replace cash with supervision and reminders, and courts can protect the community, preserve the presumption of innocence, and stop jailing poverty.

[C1.4] But five hundred dollars that you get back if you appear is also not punishment. It is the lightest, fairest incentive that actually works.
[C1.5] Worse, this motion writes out victims entirely. [C1.6] Pro cites New Jersey, but omits what the data actually showed: after cash bail was rolled back, failure to appear rates rose sharply for nonviolent offenses. [P2.4] The claim that everyone just gets it back is fiction for the poor.

[P2.6] What about victims, the clerk facing repeat theft? Cash protects her least. A defendant with money pays and can steal again that afternoon. Supervision can forbid entering the store, require check-ins, escalate to detention for violations.

[P2.8] And if some missed court dates rose after reform, weigh what fell: weeks in jail before conviction, lost jobs, coerced guilty pleas to go home.
