[P1.1] Frontier AI should not enter the European market simply because its developer believes it has complied with a rulebook. It should enter only after an independent regulator authorizes deployment against published safety standards. The decisive question is who bears uncertainty before release: the company creating the risk, or everyone exposed to it.
[P1.2] General-purpose models undermine regulation organized principally around intended uses. The same model can tutor students, write software, or assist malicious cyber activity. Its developer cannot identify every downstream deployment, while individual deployers cannot reliably assess the underlying system. That makes the frontier model itself an essential regulatory checkpoint—not just whichever application attracts scrutiny later.
[P1.3] Mandatory licensing would cover frontier general-purpose models under clear capability and training-scale criteria. Before EU deployment, developers would submit independent evaluations, security measures, and a credible incident-response plan. Authorization could impose access restrictions, monitoring, and reassessment after material upgrades. Existing risk-based rules can impose advance duties; licensing adds the crucial requirement that an accountable public body affirmatively approve market entry.
[P1.4] Risk assessment informs that decision rather than substituting for permission.
[P1.5] This matters because some mistakes cannot simply be recalled. Once powerful model weights are widely distributed, withdrawing a product listing does not withdraw the capability. For hosted models, deployment can spread vulnerabilities across many downstream services before enforcement catches up. Licensing cannot guarantee safety, but it can require testing and mitigations before exposure, refuse inadequately supported releases, and suspend authorized access when conditions are breached.
[P1.6] A later fine cannot perform that preventive function.
[P1.7] The strongest objection is that licensing could entrench incumbents. That argues for published criteria, review deadlines, proportionate fees, accessible evaluation facilities, and appeal rights—not automatic market access. Apply the same requirements to domestic and foreign providers, without exempting dangerous capabilities merely because their developer is small. Competition should reward efficient compliance, not willingness to externalize risk.
[P1.8] For frontier AI, permission before deployment is a more defensible default than enforcement after diffusion.
