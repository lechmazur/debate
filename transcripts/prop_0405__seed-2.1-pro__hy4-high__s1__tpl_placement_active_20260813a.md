[P1.1] Shared e-bike and scooter companies do not merely rent out neutral property—they design the operating rules for every vehicle in their fleet. They choose deployment density, set geofence boundaries, program speed governors, control app warnings for restricted zones, and decide how aggressively to block use of their devices on sidewalks and crowded pedestrian corridors. Right now, those safeguards are consistently weak: devices routinely unlock and operate at full speed on sidewalks, and companies dump hundreds of vehicles in dense downtown areas with barely token enforcement of no-ride rules.
[P1.2] When a rider hits a pedestrian—an older person out for a walk, a kid stepping off a curb, a worker heading to a bus stop—current liability almost always falls solely on the individual rider. Those riders often have no relevant insurance, may leave the scene before exchanging information, and rarely have the resources to cover serious medical bills or lost wages from a crash. The injured pedestrian is left covering those costs alone, while the company that profited from the ride, and had the technical ability to prevent the illegal use in the first place, faces zero meaningful consequences.
[P1.3] Assigning greater legal responsibility for these injuries does not punish companies for acts outside their control—it aligns their incentives with public safety. If they bear a clear share of liability for crashes on sidewalks and in clearly restricted zones, they will actually invest in accurate geofencing, slower default speeds in pedestrian-heavy areas, stiffer account penalties for rule-breaking riders, and more thoughtful fleet deployment instead of treating pedestrian injury as an acceptable external cost of doing business. No other party is better positioned to prevent these harms at scale, and no standard is fairer than holding the entity that controls the system and profits from it accountable when its predictable, preventable failures hurt vulnerable road users.

[C1.1] Liability should follow fault and immediate control, not the deepest balance sheet after a crash. A shared e-bike or scooter company is responsible for its own negligence: defective brakes, misleading apps, broken geofences. But when a rider rides onto a sidewalk or into a restricted plaza, the decisive cause is that rider’s unlawful decision. A car-rental agency is not turned into an insurer because a customer drives on a footpath. “Profited from the ride” is not causation.
[C1.2] PRO’s control list [P1.1] confuses influence with guarantee. Firms can warn, throttle, suspend accounts, and draw geofences; they cannot make a person heed every signal, avoid carrying a scooter past a boundary, or stop weaving along a curb where GPS drifts. Even clearly restricted zones are public rules defined and policed by cities, with blurry edges around parks, campuses, construction, and transit plazas. Predictable misuse is not preventable misuse at reasonable cost; the rider at the handlebars remains best able to avoid the collision.
[C1.3] Greater liability would not make that curb decision safer; it would move lawyers into the design room. Because a single crash can trigger expensive litigation, firms would answer with blunt exclusion zones, fewer devices near downtown and transit, higher prices, and withdrawal from less profitable areas. They would document compliance more than prevent the split-second choice to ride illegally. And if the platform becomes the dependable payer, the rider who broke the rule has one less reason to worry.
[C1.4] Pedestrian compensation is PRO’s strongest point, but the remedy is misplaced. Existing fault rules can reach a company whose own defect or deception caused harm; cities can enforce sidewalk bans; deposits, identity checks, and rider insurance can make the violator answer. The motion instead adds a status-based surcharge because a fleet owner has assets. That shifts the bill from the lawbreaker to the service.
[C1.5] Hold companies to their own duties, not to every rider’s violation.

[P2.1] The car-rental analogy at the heart of CON’s case collapses the second you look at actual control. A rental car company hands over keys for days at a time, has no real-time ability to slow or stop a vehicle that swerves onto a sidewalk, and operates in a system built around mandatory driver insurance and clear registration. Shared micromobility firms unlock each device individually, can throttle or cut power by location in seconds, choose how many devices to flood onto pedestrian-heavy blocks, and currently face no reason to build those safeguards well because sidewalk crashes cost them almost nothing.
[P2.2] CON confuses the impossibility of perfect enforcement with the absence of a clear duty to act reasonably. Arguments about GPS drift and riders ignoring warnings deliberately ignore that this motion applies to clearly restricted zones—main sidewalks, marked pedestrian plazas, unambiguous no-ride corridors—not the blurry edge of a construction site. Right now, companies do not even try to enforce those clear zones consistently; there is no reason to treat their current half-baked geofencing as the technical ceiling when liability gives them a direct reason to improve it.
[P2.3] A device automatically limited to walking speed on a sidewalk cannot inflict life-altering injury no matter what careless split-second choice a rider makes.
[P2.4] Warnings about blunt exclusion zones, higher prices, and eroded rider accountability are speculative distractions. The status quo already lets companies externalize pedestrian injury costs onto victims and public emergency rooms—those costs do not disappear, they are just paid by the wrong people. Shared responsibility does not erase rider penalties; it simply stops firms from treating preventable sidewalk crashes as an acceptable cost of chasing maximum ride volume. Riders already face minimal effective accountability when they flee scenes or lack assets to cover medical bills, so CON’s proposed fixes of city enforcement and rider insurance have already failed the people who need protection most.
