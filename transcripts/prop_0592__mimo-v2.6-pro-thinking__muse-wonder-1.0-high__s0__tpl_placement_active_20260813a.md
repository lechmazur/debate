[C1.2] An outvoted government does not become enthusiastic. Aggressors do not need a formal veto if they get a formal leak — one coastline, one financial centre, one refinery that quietly looks away. Unanimity is the price of airtight enforcement; QMV buys a press release and sells the loopholes.
[C1.3] It also misdiagnoses delay. Europe was not slow on Russia because officials were counting votes. It was slow because cutting oil, gas, payments and shipping meant finding alternative supplies and compensating exposed industries. A qualified majority would face the same economic facts, only now states bearing the heaviest costs — landlocked energy importers, maritime economies, neighbours of the aggressor — could be outvoted by states bearing far less.
[C1.4] That is not solidarity; it is majorities spending minorities' security.
[C1.5] Sanctions can impoverish regions, close industries and invite retaliation, from energy cut-offs to cyberattacks and border pressure. [C1.6] QMV would not end bargaining; it would relocate it to assembling blocking minorities, with less incentive to answer the final objection. Unanimity forces Europe to solve the hardest problem before acting. A measure imposed over a fundamental objection may pass sooner, but it will leak sooner and persuade no one. Unanimous endurance beats majority theatre.

When one capital holds the package hostage, the price of its signature lands in the legal text — pipeline carve-outs, longer transitions, sectoral escape clauses. That is not a reluctant enforcer quietly looking away; that is a loophole with a citation.
[P2.3] The holdout signs cheerfully and then enforces the diluted deal it negotiated. Against Russia, oil measures were reshaped around the loudest holdout's supply problem, and each carve-out became an evasion map. Enforcement gaps under QMV can be policed by the Commission and the courts; a designed exemption is legal.
[P2.4] Second, the cost argument has the sign backwards. Under unanimity the decisive veto belongs to the state with the most to gain from blocking — a gas contract, a port, leverage over an unrelated file — whatever its actual exposure. Under the Lisbon threshold, blocking requires at least four states and more than a third of EU population. Genuinely exposed states — neighbours, energy importers, maritime economies — can still stop a reckless package if their objection is broad and serious.
[P2.5] What they cannot do is ransom Europe alone for a side payment.
[P2.6] Third, yes, bargaining relocates. That is the improvement. An aggressor today needs one capital: cheap to buy, easy to hide. Under QMV it needs four governments defending its position in public.
[P2.7] That raises the price of protection and exposes the protector.
[P2.8] Unanimity does not find alternative supplies faster either. It lets one government delay while the costs fall on everyone else, then monetize the delay. Compensation for exposed regions is a budget problem; the veto is not compensation, it is a veto.

[C4.3] A2: Unanimity does not declare every veto noble. It holds that severe sanctions, with retaliation and concentrated economic harm, should not be imposed on a member without consent. [C4.5] The choice is a visible concession before action, exchanged for committed enforcement, versus a faster vote followed by invisible resistance. Sanctions Europe owns together will outlast sanctions some members were ordered to enforce — and aggressors exploit exactly that difference.
