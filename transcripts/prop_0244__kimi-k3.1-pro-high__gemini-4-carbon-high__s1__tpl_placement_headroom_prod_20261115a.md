[P1.1] Body cameras were sold as more than another evidence-gathering tool. They were supposed to make misconduct harder to hide, complaints easier to resolve, discipline more consistent, and public trust stronger. The test is that promise, not whether footage occasionally helps either side. A technology can produce real benefits and still fall well short of the accountability revolution advocates predicted.
[P1.2] The basic problem is that recording is only the first link in a long chain: capture, preservation, review, investigation, discipline, and institutional reform. Cameras strengthen one link while leaving the others in the hands of the same institutions whose conduct needs scrutiny. Policies commonly give officers discretion over activation, allow gaps or obstructed views, and leave departments substantial control over review and release. “No footage” can therefore mean no accountability, while “footage exists” does not mean anyone with power will act on it.
[P1.3] Second, video is evidence, not a verdict. It can be incomplete, ambiguous, or interpreted through existing legal standards that give wide latitude to split-second police judgments. Supervisors, prosecutors, and review bodies still decide whether force was policy-compliant and whether misconduct deserves consequences. When those decision-makers rarely impose sanctions, adding a lens does not change their incentives.
[P1.4] A clear recording followed by a mild reprimand may improve documentation while leaving the underlying accountability deficit intact.
[P1.5] Finally, early optimism rested on deterrence: people would behave better because they were watched. But broad deployment made the limits visible. Cameras became routine equipment, unevenly implemented across forces and encounters, rather than independent oversight. They can document an abused person without giving that person timely access, an effective remedy, or protection against retaliation.
[P1.6] So the decisive comparison is not “cameras versus darkness.” It is transformative accountability promised versus selective evidence delivered. On that comparison, the advocates’ expectations were not met.
