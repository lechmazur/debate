[P1.1] The current peer review system is built on a remarkable assumption: that the most consequential gatekeeping of scientific knowledge should be done by people who are unpaid, uncredited, and unaccountable. We ask senior researchers to donate hours of expert labor to journals that then sell their work back to libraries for profit. And we are surprised that reviews are rushed, inconsistent, and sometimes hostile. The proposition offers two concrete fixes, pay reviewers and publish their reports, and together they target the precise failures of the status quo.
[P1.2] Start with payment. Reviewing a manuscript well takes hours. A careful read of methods, statistical checks, and a constructive review can consume an entire workday. Yet most reviewers receive nothing, not even a fee that would buy lunch.
[P1.3] When the marginal cost of a sloppy review to the reviewer is zero, we should expect, and we get, sloppiness. Compensated review changes that. It signals that the work is real labor, not a favor. It also expands the pool: right now, only tenured faculty with light teaching loads can afford to review.
[P1.4] Payment lets working scientists, postdocs, and industry researchers participate, broadening expertise and reducing the clique dynamics that plague closed fields.
[P1.5] Published reports solve a different problem: accountability without exposure. Anonymous review lets bad behavior hide. Reviewers can be dismissive, biased, or factually wrong, and the author never knows who said it or why. Publishing reports, even while keeping reviewer identity confidential where appropriate, creates a public record.
[P1.6] Bad reviews become visible to editors, future authors, and eventually historians of science. Good reviewers build a reputation. Retaliation fears are real but solvable; journals already mediate this in practice, and published reports need not include names.
[P1.7] Pay plus publication makes review a profession rather than a favor. That is what "would work better" demands.

[C1.1] The proposition treats peer review as a market failure that can be fixed with money and transparency. In reality, the very features it wants to change—anonymity and volunteer labor—are what keep review honest and rigorous.
[C1.2] First, anonymity is not a bug; it is the core safeguard that lets reviewers say “this method is flawed” or “the claim is overstated” without fear of professional retaliation. If reports are published, even without names, the stylistic fingerprints, the specific criticisms, and the timing will allow authors to guess who wrote them. In small fields this guesswork is almost certain, and the chilling effect is immediate. Studies of open‑review experiments show a measurable drop in the willingness to raise negative points once reviewers know their words will be public.
[C1.3] The result is softer, less useful reviews—exactly the opposite of “working better.”
[C1.4] Second, paying reviewers creates a new class of incentives. Money ties the reviewer’s income to the journal’s volume. Journals that pay per review will attract reviewers who chase fees, not those who chase truth. This invites strategic behavior: reviewers may inflate the length of reports, avoid tough critiques that could jeopardize future invitations, or even favor papers from authors who are likely to cite them.
[C1.5] The current volunteer system, while imperfect, aligns the reviewer’s motive with reputation: a good review enhances standing, a bad one harms it. Paying severs that link and replaces it with a transactional one that rewards compliance, not rigor.
[C1.6] Third, the financial logic collapses. Most journals already operate on razor‑thin margins; adding reviewer fees would either raise subscription costs dramatically or shift the burden to authors, turning peer review into another pay‑to‑publish layer. The net effect would be higher barriers for researchers in low‑funded institutions, narrowing the reviewer pool rather than broadening it.
[C1.7] Thus, the proposition’s two fixes each undermine the very qualities—candor, independence, and cost‑effectiveness—that make peer review functional. Keeping reviewers unpaid and anonymous is not a relic; it is a design choice that works, and tampering with it would make the system worse, not better.
