It is solved fastest by satellites detecting broad features, surface vessels using multibeam sonar, and autonomous vehicles surveying close to the bottom for long periods. A crewed submersible is almost designed not to map: it carries a few people for a few hours, moves slowly across a tiny area, and still needs an expensive support ship overhead.
[C1.2] Comparable to space exploration makes that mismatch fiscally enormous. It would commit space-scale resources to the costliest, riskiest method of reaching the seafloor. Those burdens reduce payload, bottom time, and sites visited.
[C1.3] At parity, crewed exploration would not multiply ocean knowledge; it would crowd out the robotic mappers, sensor networks, and laboratories that can deliver that knowledge at scale.

[C1.5] The space analogy therefore backfires. Robotic probes, telescopes, and landers have delivered the bulk of space science, while crewed flight has consumed outsized funding and risk. [C1.6] They should not tie that urgent work to carrying passengers to the bottom at space-programme prices. The seabed needs coverage and evidence, not occupants.
