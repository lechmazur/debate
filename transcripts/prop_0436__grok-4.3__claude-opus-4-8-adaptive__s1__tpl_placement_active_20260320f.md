[P1.1] When delivery and reservation platforms push sponsored restaurants to the top of every search, users face a rigged display that hides cheaper independent options behind extra taps and filters. This design converts neutral discovery tools into paid placement engines, where visibility depends on fees rather than distance or price.
[P1.2] The practical effect is straightforward: diners default to the first results shown, which are the ones that paid to appear. Cheaper local spots drop down the list or require manual sorting by price, a step most users skip when deciding quickly on a phone. The outcome is higher average meal costs without any improvement in food quality or speed.
[P1.3] Independent restaurants operate with smaller marketing budgets and cannot match the bidding power of chains. As sponsored listings expand, these smaller businesses lose foot traffic and order volume, accelerating closures in neighborhoods that once offered varied affordable choices. The market does not correct for this because the platforms control the default ranking users actually see.
[P1.4] Treating heavy sponsorship as harmless advertising misses the asymmetry. Traditional ads sit beside results; here the paid entries replace or crowd the most relevant organic ones. Users lose the ability to compare genuine nearby prices without deliberate extra effort the interface discourages.
[P1.5] Requiring clear separation between sponsored and organic listings, ranked first by proximity and price, restores the function apps claim to provide. Without that limit, the platforms extract ongoing rents from restaurants and diners alike while narrowing real choice.
