[P1.1] The case comes down to one variable: lead time. Deflecting an asteroid is physics we already understand. A kinetic impactor nudging a rock by a few millimeters per second, years before arrival, turns a direct hit into a clean miss. But that only works if the years exist.
[P1.2] Treat planetary defense as a research niche and you guarantee that when a real object is found, the capability exists on slides, not on a launch pad.
[P1.3] Consider what "research niche" actually produces. The DART test was a genuine success, but it took the better part of a decade from concept to collision, with no deadline forcing the pace. Now imagine the same engineering compressed into eighteen months because a 150-meter object has a credible impact solution for 2041. Spacecraft procurement does not compress on command.
[P1.4] The asteroid does not negotiate.
[P1.5] Second, we are flying mostly blind. Chelyabinsk in 2013 injured well over a thousand people with a roughly 20-meter object nobody saw coming because it approached from the sunward side. We have catalogued the great majority of kilometer-class objects, but only a minority of the city-killing class in the 140-meter range. The threat is not hypothetical; the inventory is incomplete.
[P1.6] Permanent infrastructure means a dedicated space-based survey telescope operating continuously, not one that flies when a budget line survives.
[P1.7] Third, the economics are those of a fire department, not a lottery ticket. The annual probability of a damaging impact is low. The probability over a century is not. Standing capability costs a rounding error against defense spending: a survey observatory, a small number of impactor buses kept in reserve, and a standing international decision protocol.
[P1.8] Compare that to the alternative—discovering the object and then convening committees about whose rocket to build.
[P1.9] The real question is not whether an impact is likely this year. It is whether, on the day the warning comes, we want the answer to be "we can launch" or "we published a paper on it."
