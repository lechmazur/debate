[P1.1] A home in a tight market is not just an asset. It is the scarce doorway to stability, schooling, and wealth — and large institutional investors are buying that doorway before local families can reach it.
[P1.2] This is not equal competition. A family brings a mortgage, an appraisal, an inspection, and years of savings for a down payment. A large fund brings cash, no contingencies, a closing in a week, and an algorithm bidding on dozens of houses at once. Sellers rationally take certainty.
[P1.3] Repeated across neighborhoods in suburban Atlanta, Phoenix, or Charlotte, starter homes vanish, investor bids reset prices, and appraisals for the next family ratchet upward. In a tight market, every house taken at the margin reprices the ones left.
[P1.4] The harm compounds after the sale. An owner-occupied home builds equity for the household living in it and anchors people to schools and neighbors. The same home as a rental security does the opposite: rent and appreciation flow to distant shareholders, tenants deal with corporate, fee-driven management, and a would-be buyer becomes a permanent renter of the house she tried to buy. We are allocating not just houses, but who gets to build wealth from scarce land.
[P1.5] This restriction is narrow. It does not ban renting, apartments, or new construction. Large investors can still build homes, finance multifamily housing, or buy where local buyers are not being crowded out — activities that add supply. What cities stop is only the subtraction: bulk buying of existing single-family homes where vacancy is low and local bids are losing to cash scale.
[P1.6] If supply is the crisis, let capital solve it by creating homes, not by outbidding the people who need to live in them.

[P4.1] A1: Nothing guarantees one particular house — a standard no housing rule could meet — but the restriction delivers homes cumulatively because the next bidder is not the fund in miniature. A flipper must preserve a resale margin, then returns the house to the for-sale market. A local landlord has finite credit and cannot cash-bid above appraisal across dozens of listings, month after month, to harvest rent and appreciation for a portfolio. Remove that permanent, systematic top bidder and prices stop being set by securitized rental yield; they fall back toward offers a financed owner-occupant can close.
[P4.2] A2: The line is control, not paperwork. A city can define “large” by beneficial ownership in its market — say, an investor and affiliates controlling dozens of single-family homes — require disclosure of the parent, funder, and manager at purchase, and aggregate LLCs under common control. Shells created by one platform still share one owner, one acquisition strategy, and one property manager, so they count as one buyer. A neighbor with two rentals is not an institutional platform, and borderline cases do not make that distinction unworkable.
[P4.3] Notice what this does to CON’s trade-off. If the next buyer is a flipper, the home re-enters ownership; if a small landlord, it often remains rented, undercutting claims of a rental collapse, but without the scale that repeatedly outbids families and locks whole starter-home blocks into permanent tenancy. Either way, cities keep directing unrestricted institutional capital toward building new homes, where it adds doors instead of competing for the scarce doors already standing. In tight markets, that marginal shift — from distant portfolio ownership toward attainable local ownership — is exactly the public purpose worth defending.
[P4.4] Ownership is not a label on the same tenancy; it is equity, stability, and control that renting back the same house does not replace.

Prices and bidding pressure ease toward buyers who will live in the home, build equity from it, and stay.
