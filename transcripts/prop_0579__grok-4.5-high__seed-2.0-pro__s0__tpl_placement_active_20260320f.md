[P1.1] A national digital ID option would cut fraud and expand service access far more than it expands surveillance or breach risk. Today's paper-based and fragmented systems are porous: stolen numbers fuel tax and benefit scams, fake physical cards open accounts, and remote, disabled, or undocumented residents lose hours or opportunities simply proving identity. A voluntary cryptographic credential—user-controlled, selectively disclosed, reusable across agencies—raises the cost of impersonation while letting people unlock services from a phone.
[P1.2] Fraud falls because verification becomes cryptographic rather than visual or form-filling. Impostors cannot easily mint multiple synthetic lives when each person holds one strong, revocable token; anomaly detection becomes faster and cleaner. Banks and agencies stop hoarding photocopies that themselves become breach targets. Access rises for the same reason: remote, portable proof replaces travel, notaries, and institutional gatekeeping.
[P1.3] A rural resident or someone with limited mobility can claim healthcare support, open basic banking, or complete government filings without physical presence or endless paper loops. That inclusion gain is immediate and tangible.
[P1.4] Surveillance and data-breach fears, while genuine, are smaller in net impact. An option leaves exit and non-use intact. Design choices—minimal attributes revealed per use, purpose binding, offline capability, user wallets rather than one government super-database—prevent authentication from becoming continuous tracking. Breach surface shrinks when data is minimized and encrypted under user control, unlike the scattered legacy vaults that already leak.
[P1.5] Analog systems already fail both privacy and security; done right, digital reduces the practical harms while delivering measurable drops in fraud loss and exclusion.
[P1.6] The comparison favors the proposition: fewer identity-theft victims, broader legitimate reach to services, and privacy tools that keep added risk smaller than the benefits realized.
