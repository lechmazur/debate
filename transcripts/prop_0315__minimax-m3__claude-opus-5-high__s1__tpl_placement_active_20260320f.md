[P1.1] Every season, a single referee's whistle—or a single replay booth's hesitation—rewrites a game, a player's season, sometimes a career. The leagues that employ these officials then ask fans, athletes, and broadcasters to simply accept the call. The proposition asks a modest question: should the public be told more than the score? Yes, and the case for fuller disclosure is overwhelming.
[P1.2] First, the moral case. Leagues operate as quasi-public institutions. They claim sovereign authority over rules, schedule, and outcomes that shape billions in commerce and entire cultural rituals. With that authority comes an obligation to explain, not just enforce.
[P1.3] A tax authority that audited you without telling you the rule would be illegitimate; a league that overturns a goal with "it was inconclusive" is no different.
[P1.4] Second, the trust case. Transparency is the cheapest trust-building tool leagues own. When a referee explains a handball live, with the camera showing the contact, fans grumble and move on. When the same call is buried in a vague report, the grumble becomes "the league has it in for us."
[P1.5] Opacity doesn't prevent criticism; it manufactures it.
[P1.6] Third, the competitive case. Transparent officiating teaches players faster and lets coaches adjust honestly. The NFL's mic'd officials and the NHL's situation-room announcements show the model works without any collapse in authority. Where pilots black-box their cockpits after a near miss, leagues refuse to black-box rulings that swing championships.
[P1.7] The counterargument—that disclosure would undermine referee authority, slow the game, or expose officials to harassment—fails on contact. [P1.8] Referees don't need secrecy to do their job. They need clarity. Leagues that treat fans as adults rather than customers will earn the legitimacy they currently purchase with marketing. Endorse the proposition.
