[P1.1] For crores of Indians, a phone is not a luxury. It is a bank account, a classroom, a shopfront. When the battery swells or the screen cracks, the present system offers a cruel choice: travel to a distant authorised centre, pay nearly the price of a new device, wait weeks — or discard and replace. A national right-to-repair law requiring access to parts, tools and documentation ends that exploitation at its root.
[P1.2] India already has the answer in its galis and bazaars: lakhs of skilled independent repairers who can fix a handset in an hour for a fraction of the company quote. But manufacturers are deliberately locking them out — withholding schematics, restricting genuine spares, using software pairing that rejects even an identical part, gluing batteries so opening destroys the device. This is not innovation; it is a monopoly on fixing. A national law levels the field, keeps repair money in local economies, and brings affordable service to small towns and villages where authorised centres barely exist.
[P1.3] The environmental stakes are enormous. India discards vast quantities of electronics each year, much of it dismantled unsafely or burned. Extending the life of phones and laptops by even a year or two directly cuts e-waste, saves foreign exchange on imports, and conserves scarce materials. Repair is the most practical circular economy.
[P1.4] Ownership must mean something. Paying full price should not leave you a tenant of the manufacturer. Fears about safety and piracy can be handled with reasonable limits and genuine parts, just as with automobiles. What cannot be defended is secrecy as a business model.
[P1.5] Give every Indian buyer and every local technician the right to fix what they own.

[P2.1] Opposition’s entire case requires inventing a law no one proposed. The motion demands access to parts, tools and documentation. It does not ban compatible parts, does not require a licence to open a phone, does not criminalise the bazaar technician. [C1.2] claims he will be “criminalised overnight” for using an affordable screen, but where is that ban written?
[P2.2] Nowhere. A right to buy a genuine battery at a fair price is an added option, not a mandate to buy it. He can keep doing exactly what he does today — only now the manufacturer cannot starve him of a schematic or software-unlock him out.
[P2.3] Second, the certification trap is pure fiction. Requiring companies to publish manuals and sell spares is not the same as requiring repairers to register for training. No one needs permission to read a manual. If anything, today’s system is the closed licence raj: only the authorised centre gets the code, the paired part, the diagnostic tool.
[P2.4] This law breaks that raj open.
[P2.5] The grey-market objection gets the economics backwards. Most second-hand phones were once new phones. When schematics, board diagrams and genuine spares circulate for the models officially sold here, that knowledge and stock spill across counters to fix identical parallel-imported or handed-down units too. Denying the whole country manuals because some devices came informally is like refusing roads because some cars lack papers.
[P2.6] And the price scare ignores who pays today. The poor first-time buyer already pays — when a cracked display means replacing the phone, when a dead battery means weeks without UPI or classes. Maintaining a parts inventory and posting a PDF is trivial beside marketing and warranty networks firms already run. Automobiles live under exactly such parts obligations, and Maruti did not become unaffordable.
[P2.7] This does not entrench monopoly; it ends secrecy as monopoly.
