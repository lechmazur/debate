[P1.1] A carbon border adjustment wins this debate on one word in the motion: "global." Domestic subsidies and regulations, however well-designed, change behavior inside one country. A border adjustment changes the economics of carbon for every exporter who wants access to the largest consumer market on earth. That difference compounds over five years.
[P1.2] Start with leakage. U.S. regulation of steel, cement, aluminum, and chemicals raises domestic production costs, which shifts production to jurisdictions with dirtier grids and no carbon price. Emissions don't fall; they migrate. The Inflation Reduction Act's clean manufacturing incentives help, but they can't touch a ton of CO2 from a blast furnace in a country with no climate policy.
[P1.3] A border adjustment can: it prices that ton at the port of entry.
[P1.4] Second, leverage. When Europe moved toward its carbon border mechanism, major trading partners who had resisted carbon pricing for years suddenly began designing their own systems, because a domestic carbon price lets exporters keep the revenue at home instead of paying it to Brussels. The same logic applies to the American market. Within five years, a U.S. mechanism plausibly pulls several large emitting economies toward carbon pricing that a decade of diplomacy and no amount of domestic subsidy ever accomplished.
[P1.5] That is genuine global emissions reduction, achieved through market access rather than treaty negotiation.
[P1.6] Third, speed and durability. Subsidy programs ramp slowly—permits, interconnection queues, supply chains. Regulations invite litigation and reversal. A border adjustment, once set, acts immediately and automatically on every import transaction.
[P1.7] It also generates revenue that can cushion domestic industry and accelerate the clean transition, making the policy self-reinforcing rather than budget-dependent.
[P1.8] The subsidy-only path asks: how fast can America decarbonize alone? The border adjustment asks: how fast can America's market power decarbonize its trading partners too? Over five years, the second question is the one that determines global emissions, and only one side of this debate is even asking it.
