[P1.1] A rifle jams; a person is still responsible. That is the arrangement every law of war rests on: somewhere, a human being chose to fire, and can be praised, court-martialed, or hanged for it. A weapon that selects and engages targets without meaningful human control breaks that arrangement, and nothing in the current design pipeline repairs it.
[P1.2] Start with accountability. If an autonomous system strafes a wedding convoy, who stands trial? Not the machine. Not the operator, who chose nothing about that engagement.
[P1.3] Not the commander, who lawfully deployed a system certified as reliable. Not the programmer, who never met the victims and cannot foresee every battlefield. The result is a lethal act with no author — an outcome that isn't a bug in the technology, it is the technology working as designed. Distributed responsibility means no responsibility.
[P1.4] Second, the judgments we're delegating aren't pattern-matching problems. Distinction and proportionality require reading intent from context: is that farmer carrying a rifle to hunt or to ambush, is the man crawling toward the treeline surrendering or flanking, is this particular hospital's destruction worth this particular target. Machine learning systems are brittle exactly where battlefields are messy — novel scenes, adversarial deception, degraded sensors. And unlike a pilot who hesitates, a system that misclassifies once will misclassify identically at machine speed, ten thousand times.
[P1.5] Third, speed is the real danger. Autonomous systems interacting with adversary autonomous systems produce the military equivalent of a flash crash — except you cannot unwind the trades. Two forces each delegating engagement decisions to algorithms neither side can inspect creates escalation faster than any human can intervene. Nuclear-armed states cannot afford war that begins in milliseconds.
[P1.6] Fourth, these weapons are cheap. Small drones and open-source targeting software mean this proliferates to militias and cartels, not just great powers. A ban's window closes once the arsenals exist.
[P1.7] Humans have always drawn lines around means of war we refuse to normalize: poison gas, blinding lasers, landmines. Delegating the decision to kill belongs on that list.
