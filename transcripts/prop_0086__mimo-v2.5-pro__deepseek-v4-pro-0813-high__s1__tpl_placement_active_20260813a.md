[C1.2] Companionship is not only existential witness. It includes regular conversation, memory prompts, orientation, games, songs, and consistent response. A well-designed companion can provide these 24/7 without fatigue. For a dementia patient repeating the same question fifteen times, calm, familiar repetition is immediate relief and reduced agitation, not an empty simulation.
[C1.3] The proposition’s perfectionism withholds real relief. It says AI cannot offer genuine recognition, but many older adults know the robot is not human and still benefit. Robotic pets reduce anxiety and invite touch and speech, much as animals do. We do not withhold a wheelchair because it is not legs; we should not withhold an interactive companion because it is not a person.
[C1.4] The slippery-slope concern runs backwards. Under staffing, humans are wasted on routine loneliness checks and repeated questions. Offloading those to AI frees caregivers for high-value human contact: difficult conversations, feeding, crisis comfort. Blocking AI spreads staff thinner and leaves residents less accompanied, not more.
[C1.5] The humane test is reduced suffering, not a purity test for the source of companionship. Under tight staffing, most companionship should be supplied by AI or robotics because that is the only reliable way to deliver consistent engagement at scale. The result is not abandonment; it is fewer hours of silence and more human attention where it matters most.
