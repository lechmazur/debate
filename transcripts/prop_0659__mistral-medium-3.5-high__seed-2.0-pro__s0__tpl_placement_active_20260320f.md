[P1.1] Emotion-recognition systems in hiring and evaluation are a direct threat to fairness, privacy, and human dignity. They rest on the scientifically shaky foundation that facial expressions or speech patterns universally reveal inner emotional states—a claim that ignores cultural diversity and individual differences. In practice, these systems amplify discrimination: trained on non-representative datasets, they misclassify emotions of women and minorities at far higher rates, turning hiring and promotion processes into minefields of hidden bias. A qualified candidate might be rejected for not smiling "enough" during an interview, while an employee could be disciplined for "negative" expressions during a personal moment, with no meaningful way to appeal.
[P1.2] This isn’t merely inaccurate; it’s a radical invasion of psychological privacy, forcing workers to have their most personal reactions continuously monitored and judged in contexts where emotions are irrelevant to job performance. Unlike objective performance metrics, emotional assessments lack clear, contestable standards, making them easy tools for subjective bias disguised as impartial data. The chilling effect is immediate and corrosive: employees suppress legitimate emotions, fearing retribution for any display of frustration, sadness, or even deep concentration, creating toxic environments where authenticity is punished and mental health suffers.
[P1.3] Voluntary guidelines cannot address this. The economic incentives for employers to adopt these cheap, "data-driven" shortcuts are overwhelming, while the harms—systemic discrimination, psychological distress, wrongful terminations—are severe and often irreversible for those affected. Many jurisdictions already prohibit polygraphs in employment for precisely these reasons; emotion recognition is the 21st-century equivalent, and just as unethical. Without prohibition, we risk normalizing psychological surveillance in the workplace, eroding worker rights and embedding inequality under a deceptive veneer of technological progress.
