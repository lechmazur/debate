[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies confuse cheap fuel with help for the poor. They discount every litre for the SUV and second car as well as the nurse’s commute. The test is not whether petrol looks affordable; it is whether vulnerable families can meet essential needs without the state bankrupting itself or freezing the energy market.
[P1.2] Targeting converts waste into protection. Fuel consumption generally rises with income, so universality directs much of the budget to households least in need. That money can instead support low-income families, pensioners and people whose work or location makes fuel costs unavoidable. Cash can reflect need; a pump discount rewards whoever burns most.
[P1.3] Fiscal space matters too: an unaffordable subsidy crowds out health, education and transport, or ends in an abrupt crisis that hurts the poor most.
[P1.4] Transfers protect people without protecting waste. Under a subsidy, more help requires more fuel consumption. With cash, a household can buy food, pay rent or take the bus, and keep savings from using less energy. Honest prices preserve incentives to conserve and invest; income support preserves dignity.
[P1.5] Broad price controls instead conceal scarcity, discourage supply and maintenance, and risk queues, rationing and black markets. A controlled price is little comfort when fuel is unavailable.
[P1.6] This is a phase-out, not a cliff edge. Enrol and pay recipients before prices rise; sequence increases predictably; use simple categorical and offline enrolment where digital records are weak; and adjust support as essential costs change. Exclusion errors need correction, but they cannot justify leakage to the affluent by design. Compensation can also reflect higher food and transport costs, based on household need rather than past petrol purchases.
[P1.7] The choice is not between helping everyone and abandoning the vulnerable. It is between an indiscriminate discount that ties protection to fuel consumption, and direct support that follows poverty. Replace the subsidy, not the social contract.

[C1.2] Household cash transfers cannot contain cascading supply-chain inflation. Worse, once fiscal pressures return, governments routinely freeze or under-index cash payouts, leaving families with eroded stipends and permanently higher food and transit bills.

Enrolling every vulnerable household before prices rise [P1.6] assumes bureaucratic capacity subsidy-reliant states rarely possess. Categorical or offline workarounds [P1.6] still miss the vast "missing middle": street vendors, delivery riders, and peri-urban workers who fall outside rigid poverty rolls yet cannot survive doubled transport and food costs.
[C1.4] Discounting an affluent driver’s tank is a fiscal inefficiency, easily clawed back through steep levies on luxury vehicles and large engines without starving schools or clinics [P1.3]. Excluding working families from affordable food and mobility is a social catastrophe.
[C1.5] Nor do "honest prices" gently spur conservation [P1.4]. Where public transit is scarce, essential travel is inelastic; a nurse [P1.1] without a bus route cannot burn less fuel, she cuts groceries. Meanwhile, broad price controls need not mean empty pumps [P1.5]. Regulated margin caps, cost-plus ceilings, and price-smoothing bands cover legitimate supply costs while insulating entire supply chains from global shocks, instantly and with zero exclusion error.
[C1.6] Replacing broad price stability with targeted cash trades guaranteed, economy-wide protection for a fragile bureaucratic promise, unleashing inflation, exclusion, and unrest that fracture the social contract [P1.7] rather than saving it.
