[P1.1] Shipping an artifact home and calling the debt paid treats culture as freight. Colonial powers did not merely remove objects; they concentrated the laboratories, archives, endowments, and specialist training that keep those objects legible and durable. Restitution that stops at the crate leaves the receiving country with the work and the former power with the means to care for it. That is not completion.
[P1.2] It is an unfinished transfer.
[P1.3] Conservation is not a local afterthought. Wood, textile, ivory, and corroding metal need controlled environments and conservators trained on those materials. Those skills clustered where the collections and budgets sat for generations. Provenance research is skewed the same way: expedition diaries, dealer records, and museum files remain overwhelmingly in the capitals that organized the taking.
[P1.4] A country can own an object and still be locked out of its history unless that paper trail is funded, digitized, and shared on the recipient's terms.
[P1.5] Museum capacity completes the same duty. A museum that cannot store, study, exhibit, and teach with returned works has received a symbol, not a cultural resource. Funding galleries, training, and research posts—time-limited, and governed by the recipient—turns restitution from ceremony into public use. It is not a permanent subsidy or a veto.
[P1.6] It returns resources colonial extraction made scarce.
[P1.7] Ship and withdraw sounds like respect for sovereignty. In practice the object often leaves conditions the sender would never accept for its own collection. If it then deteriorates, the loss is blamed on the recipient while the sender keeps the credit of having returned it. That is evasion.
[P1.8] Former colonial powers should fund conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity so the object survives, its history is recoverable, and the holding institution can actually use it. Restitution is repair, not logistics.

[C1.1] Calling shipment “culture as freight” [P1.1] sounds humane, but it smuggles in a dangerous premise: ownership by the receiving country is not real until the former colonizer certifies the museum around it. Restitution undoes an unlawful transfer. It has a determinate end: title, possession, relevant records, and authority pass to the rightful owner. The proposition replaces that endpoint with an “unfinished transfer” [P1.2] whose budget, experts, and standards are set in the old capital.
[C1.2] That is not decolonization; it is receivership with better language.
[C1.3] Those conservation, provenance, and capacity needs are real, but they do not prove this duty. Conservation can be handled through recipient-led planning, handover conditions, or negotiated cooperation. Provenance files in imperial archives should be copied, digitized, and surrendered as part of the return; the answer is not to pay those institutions to remain gatekeepers of another country’s history [P1.4]. Museum capacity is broader: buildings, salaries, security, and access are national cultural policy.
[C1.4] If every returned object triggers a duty for former powers to build capacity, there is no limiting principle short of general reparations—and the proposal makes artifact shipments the delivery mechanism for it.
[C1.5] Even recipient-governed, time-limited funding is practical paternalism. A donor funding climate systems, training posts, and research agendas can demand audits, approved consultants, procurement rules, and publicity. It can delay a return while assessing “readiness,” or tie conservation money to loans. It rewards incapacity.
[C1.6] Worse, it imposes one museum model: some returned works belong in community custody, ceremony, restricted keeping, or local care, not a gallery built to the sender’s standards [P1.5].
[C1.7] Assistance may be welcome when requested, on recipient terms. But saying restitution is incomplete unless former powers finance those programs converts a finite duty to return stolen property into open-ended leverage. Repair is not mere logistics, and it is not permanent tutelage. It ends at restored authority; then the recipient decides whether the former power is invited back.

Calling that receivership confuses who decides with who owes.

Care is owed for the materials sent back; provenance work concerns the files of those takings; capacity concerns the places that will hold them. A hospital or a general reparations ledger is not triggered by a crate. [C1.4] inflates a bounded repair into an unlimited one so the bounded repair can be refused.
[P2.3] Copying the archives, as urged, is necessary and still incomplete. Files without people trained to read them leave interpretation in the old capital. Funding that research on the recipient's side ends gatekeeping; surrendering paper while expertise stays behind merely renames it. Handover conditions and negotiated cooperation are already conceded [C1.3].
[P2.4] If those conditions fund care, the duty is admitted and only the form is disputed. If they do not, conditions are just a slower shipment.
[P2.5] Recipient governance answers the paternalism charge. The recipient writes the plan: gallery, shrine, community store, or restricted keeping. A textile worn in ceremony still needs people who can keep it between uses. Funding local methods is not imposing a vitrine.
[P2.6] Strings—readiness tests, loan conditions, consultant capture—are contract defects. If every risk of strings voids the duty, the sender's cheapest string, nothing after shipment, becomes the only lawful one.
[P2.7] Assistance only when requested [C1.7] sounds like sovereignty and works as an exit. The former power keeps the laboratories and the credit; the recipient inherits the object and the blame if it fails. Restitution is complete when the holder can keep the work alive, recover its history, and use it as they choose, not when the shipper has cleared the dock.

[C2.1] “What does not pass, unless someone pays” [P2.1] names a cost, not a debtor. The wrong was taking, keeping, and controlling objects and records; the remedy is return, disclosure, and authority. A returning museum must surrender the object’s file, conservation history, and relevant provenance records. It does not thereby have to fund a conservator, a research post, or a gallery.
[C2.2] Need is not title. Responsibility follows authority; later deterioration does not make restitution incomplete.
[C2.3] The “object-linked” limit [P2.2] still has no end. Objects endure; so do their costs: humidity control, salaries, insurance, training, and research. “Keep the work alive, recover its history, and use it as they choose” [P2.7] is not a bounded repair but a permanent outcome guarantee. A ceremonial textile needs care between ceremonies forever. Time-limited grants just postpone the question: who decides when capacity is enough?
[C2.4] Usually the funder. A crate may not trigger a hospital, but it triggers a conservator, storage, security, and audits of use—cultural policy by another door.
[C2.5] Administration is not a “contract defect” [P2.6]. Public money brings eligible costs, procurement rules, audits, and proof of results. Recipient governance does not erase this: the recipient writes the plan, the former capital holds the purse, and local authority becomes a grant application. The vitrine is not the danger; the reporting line is.
[C2.6] Strip out conditions and the claim is an unconditional transfer—general reparations under an artifact label. Keep conditions and the dependency returns. “Nothing after shipment” is not a string; it is the boundary of the wrong.
[C2.7] Handover cooperation is not this duty. Returning papers and sharing expertise transfer control; financing institutions is a new agreement. Sovereignty includes the right to say: send the object, send the records, and stop. The proposition calls that an exit because it measures completion by guaranteed outcomes rather than restored authority.
[C2.8] A duty to underwrite another country’s cultural future is not repair; it is empire by invoice.
