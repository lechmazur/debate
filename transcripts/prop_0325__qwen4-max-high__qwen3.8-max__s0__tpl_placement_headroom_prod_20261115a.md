[C1.1] The proposition mistakes the bundle for a wall when it is often the roof. Sports broadcasting is not a vending machine for one team’s feed; it is a financing system for an entire competition. Rights fees are paid because broadcasters can sell audiences across many matches, many clubs, and many afternoons. That pooled value funds fixtures that would otherwise look unattractive: mid-table games, women’s leagues, lower divisions, Olympic sports, and the production trucks, camera crews, and commentators who make even a single team pass feel professional.
[C1.2] PRO says marginal streaming cost is near zero. True, but the cost that matters is not the next byte; it is the rights, production, and guaranteed revenue that precede it. If broadcasters carve competitions into team passes, they do not simply add a cheaper door. They split the risk.
[C1.3] The predictable bundle revenue becomes volatile, dependent on which clubs are fashionable, healthy, or winning. Broadcasters respond by raising bundle prices for superfans and charging more for marquee events, while small-club supporters face thinner coverage or pay-per-view pricing because their niche is no longer subsidised by the giant clubs.
[C1.4] This is the trap in PRO’s “ordinary price discrimination.” In sports, fandom is not a neat individual preference; it is network value. A league is only compelling because rivalries, standings, and narratives are built across clubs. Manchester United’s global appeal is partly built by Liverpool, Arsenal, and the rest. Selling team-by-team treats the ecosystem as a catalogue and risks weakening the shared product.
[C1.5] The better answer to affordability is not forced unbundling but targeted discounts, mobile tiers, delayed access, and anti-piracy enforcement that preserves cross-subsidy. Cheaper does not automatically mean fairer if it fragments the sport. That is not a bargain; it is deferred scarcity.
