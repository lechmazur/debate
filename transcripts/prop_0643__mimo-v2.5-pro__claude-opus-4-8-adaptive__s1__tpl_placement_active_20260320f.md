[P1.1] The modern corporation concentrates enormous power in the hands of a board that answers primarily to shareholders. Workers—who bear the daily consequences of strategic decisions, who possess irreplaceable ground-level knowledge, and whose livelihoods depend on the firm's long-term health—are systematically locked out of the room where those decisions are made. Requiring large public companies to reserve board seats for worker-elected representatives isn't a radical experiment. It's a correction of a structural deficiency that damages workers, firms, and the broader economy.
[P1.2] Consider what workers bring that current boards often lack. A director chosen from a slate of retired executives and financial professionals sees spreadsheets and market projections. A worker-elected representative sees the safety shortcuts that will eventually trigger a scandal, the morale collapse that will drive turnover, the operational inefficiency that no consulting report will capture. This isn't sentimentality—it's information.
[P1.3] Boards make better decisions when they have access to perspectives that currently require layers of filtering to reach them.
[P1.4] The empirical track record supports this. Germany's co-determination system, which reserves up to half of supervisory board seats for employee representatives, operates in one of the world's most competitive economies. German firms have not collapsed under the weight of worker governance. Instead, they tend to exhibit longer investment horizons, more stable employment, and stronger apprenticeship systems.
[P1.5] The same basic structure operates across Scandinavia and the Netherlands. These aren't cautionary tales—they're models of how industrial democracies manage capitalism more effectively than we do.
[P1.6] The standard objection—that workers lack the expertise to govern—patronizes millions of professionals and ignores how much of board expertise is performative anyway. Workers don't need to audit financial statements themselves; they need a voice to challenge the assumptions that go unchallenged when every director shares the same class background and incentive structure.
[P1.7] What this proposal really addresses is a power imbalance. Shareholders already have a board. Management already has a board. Workers deserve one too.
