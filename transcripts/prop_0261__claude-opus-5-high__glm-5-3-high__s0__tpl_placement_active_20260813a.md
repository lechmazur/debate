[P1.1] A man dies leaving two libraries. The paperbacks on his shelf go to his daughter. The nine hundred ebooks he paid full price for — often the same titles, at the same or higher price — vanish when the account is closed. Nothing about that difference is principled.
[P1.2] It is an accident of which button he clicked.
[P1.3] Start with the money, because that is what makes this a matter of law and not of taste. Platforms sell these goods under a button labelled "Buy," at prices set against permanent ownership, with a checkout that looks exactly like a purchase. Then, in a clickwrap agreement no one reads and no one can negotiate, they redefine the transaction as a personal, non-transferable licence that expires with the buyer. That is a term extracted by adhesion, not agreed by bargaining.
[P1.4] Inheritance is the default rule for everything a person buys with their own money; a boilerplate clause should not be able to quietly repeal succession law for the fastest-growing category of household property.
[P1.5] And it is growing. For a rising share of people, most of what they own — books, films, music, games, decades of accumulated purchases — exists only as licences. If those all die with the holder, we are not preserving a narrow exception; we are engineering the mass, automatic destruction of family estates, transferring value back to sellers who were already paid in full.
[P1.6] The usual objection is that transfer breaks the economics. It does not, if the guarantee is written the way any sensible statute would write it: one estate, one transfer, no duplication. The heir inherits the entitlement; the decedent's access is extinguished. Nothing is copied.
[P1.7] The publisher sold one copy and, at the end, one copy still exists. That is precisely the bargain physical books have honoured for centuries without collapsing publishing.
[P1.8] So the question is simple: when someone pays purchase prices for a permanent good, does their family inherit it, or does the seller silently get it back? Law should answer the first way.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to force something into inheritance law that was never sold as property in the first place. That is the whole problem.
[C1.2] When you buy a paperback, you buy an object, and the object can be passed on. When you license an ebook, you pay for access to a service on stated terms — terms that say personal, non-transferable, non-surviving. Heirs inherit what the deceased actually held. They held a licence.
[C1.3] Inheriting the licence means inheriting its limits, exactly as an heir inherits a gym membership that ends, a subscription that lapses, a leased car that goes back to the dealer. Nobody thinks the estate should get a free car because the decedent drove one for three years.
[C1.4] My opponent's real complaint is with the licensing model itself, and that case proves too much. If non-transferable licences must survive death because the buyer clicked "Buy," then they must also be transferable on resale — the logic is identical. And the moment zero-marginal-cost digital goods become freely transferable, the price of first sale collapses. Physical resale worked because books degrade, shipping costs money, and copies are scarce.
[C1.5] A digital file transfers instantly, infinitely, at zero cost. A robust "one estate, one transfer" chain is functionally a resale market with extra steps. Publishers would not absorb that loss; they would reprice everything as subscriptions — and consumers, including heirs, would be worse off. The current model funds cheap access precisely by limiting transferability.
[C1.6] Note also the drafting incoherence: "next of kin" is not even how inheritance works. Property passes by will or intestacy through estates, not automatically to the nearest relative. A statute keyed to next of kin would override testation and create instant disputes among children, spouses, and stepfamilies over who gets the Steam library.
[C1.7] The remedy for misleading "Buy" buttons is honest labelling and consumer protection law — punish the deception at the point of sale. It is not to conscript succession law into converting every licence into perpetual, inheritable property, with the costs quietly loaded onto [clipped]
