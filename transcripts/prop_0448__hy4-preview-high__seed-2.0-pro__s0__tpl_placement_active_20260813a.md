[P1.1] To claim asylum in the EU today, a person usually must first break the law: pay a smuggler, cross a border without permission, and survive the journey. That is not a flaw in European policy; it is its operating system. A unified humanitarian visa would replace this deadly lottery with a legal door. People fleeing war or persecution could register at an EU office outside the bloc, be screened, and if their claim is credible travel lawfully to have it examined.
[P1.2] The motion targets the true pull factor—the absence of any legal route—not the promise of protection. That is the central case for the motion.
[P1.3] Start with control. Opponents equate legal routes with lost sovereignty; that is backwards. Under a common visa, applications go to embassies or processing centres, not beaches. Fingerprints, security checks and interviews happen before travel, so states know who is coming and why.
[P1.4] Contrast that with boats arriving unannounced and officials scrambling to identify rescued people. The visa does not abolish borders; it makes them governable.
[P1.5] A unified scheme also fixes Europe's fairness problem. Today a few frontline states carry an EU-wide obligation while others free-ride. If asylum is a common status, access to it should be common too. One visa lets the EU allocate responsibility by capacity, reducing secondary movements and the lottery where fate depends on which state an applicant reached first.
[P1.6] Legal pathways also work against smugglers, not for them. Every safe, scheduled passage is one a trafficker cannot sell. It saves the vast cost of interception, detention and rescue, and lets reception be planned, not improvised. Europe can honour the Refugee Convention by offering a lawful way to seek protection, and return those found not to need it.
[P1.7] The status quo chooses shipwrecks and smugglers over law and life; a unified humanitarian visa chooses both compassion and control.
