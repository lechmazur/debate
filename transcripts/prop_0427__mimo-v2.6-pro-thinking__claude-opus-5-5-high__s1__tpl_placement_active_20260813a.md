[P1.1] Loneliness is not a deficit of information. A teenager who knows, in the abstract, that endless scrolling distorts belonging is not thereby invited anywhere tonight. Anti-loneliness work succeeds when it changes who a young person actually sees, week after week — teammates, bandmates, a coach who notices when they stop turning up. That is what public funding buys, and it is why funded sports, arts, and clubs outrank literacy campaigns on both loneliness and violence.
[P1.2] Three mechanisms, all structural rather than didactic.
[P1.3] First, dose. A funded program produces recurring face-to-face contact with the same peers and adults, hours a week, across seasons. A literacy curriculum delivers a module, an assembly, an online lesson. Knowledge of media hygiene decays fast; friendships and mentors accumulate.
[P1.4] Second, the alternative to isolation has to be a place to go. Lonely, unsupervised hours are where grievance, gang recruitment, and impulsive violence find their footing. A free evening basketball league or a school choir occupies those hours with structure, adult supervision, and a shared project. A literacy campaign leaves the hour empty and instructs the kid to log off into it.
[P1.5] The empty hour is where prevention has to happen.
[P1.6] Third, status. Youth violence is often about respect, recognition, and belonging — the same needs that pull kids toward gangs and extremist circles. Sports and arts supply status honestly: the defender who never quits, the trumpet section, the one who painted the set. No slide deck offers that.
[P1.7] Note the word "alone." Even the strongest advocate for media literacy must concede that literacy alone cannot create a club, a coach, or a team. And the comparative record is unflattering: literacy moves knowledge, modestly and briefly, while effects on wellbeing and behavior stay inconsistent. Programs create belonging; literacy only describes it.
[P1.8] Fund the teams. Teach the literacy too — but bet the anti-loneliness and anti-violence budget on places where young people actually meet.
