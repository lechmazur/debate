[P1.1] Dating apps exist first to help users form meaningful connections, not to hold their attention for its own sake. The narrow, targeted limits called for here apply only when swipe mechanics and paid boosts actively erode match quality and user trust—they do not ban core functionality outright, they just pull back on design choices that trade those two non-negotiable user goods for marginal extra screen time and short-term revenue. Unlimited, frictionless swiping tuned to maximize session length actively degrades match quality by turning profile evaluation into a rapid, low-stakes reflex.
[P1.2] When users are pushed to swipe at the pace engagement algorithms reward, they do not weigh stated values, common interests, or conversational cues; they make split-second judgments based on a lead photo, then move on. That dynamic creates cascading failure: matches are unthoughtful, conversations fizzle, ghosting becomes the default, and users leave interactions feeling like the process is rigged to waste their time. Paid visibility boosts supercharge that trust collapse. When users learn profiles appear in their queue not because they align with stated preferences, past swipe patterns, or likely compatibility, but because someone paid to cut the line, they stop believing the app’s core matching function works at all.
[P1.3] They assume every standout profile is a paid placement, every match is a fluke, and the app has no incentive to actually help them meet someone. These limits are not anti-platform. Short-term engagement metrics look good on a quarterly report, but the long-term health of a dating app depends on users actually having good experiences, not burning out in a frustrated cycle of endless, unrewarded swiping that leads nowhere. Chasing clicks at the cost of the very reason people open the app is a self-defeating business model, and basic guardrails to prevent that tradeoff serve both users and the platforms themselves.

[P2.1] CON’s core misdirection is framing this as a call for heavy-handed, perfect compatibility filters, when the proposal only curbs features when they demonstrably erode match quality and trust—a standard no more unworkable than basic consumer protection rules for every other digital service. You do not need to perfectly predict romantic chemistry to recognize that interface designs tuned to keep users swiping for hours produce decision fatigue, reflexive photo-only judgments, and burnout rather than thoughtful sampling. CON claims fast, unlimited swiping helps users in small or niche pools find matches, but that gets the dynamic backwards: when swiping becomes a mindless, rapid reflex, rare compatible profiles get buried under split-second, low-effort judgments, not surfaced.
[P2.2] Engagement-optimized swiping does not expand choice for marginalized or geographically isolated daters—it trains users to tune out entirely before they can read the details that signal shared values, identity, or common ground. On paid boosts, CON says no one is forced to match, but that ignores the core trust harm: once users believe queues are ordered by willingness to pay rather than compatibility, they approach every profile with cynicism, swiping left even on organic, well-matched profiles out of the assumption they are just paid placements.
[P2.3] That wastes both the boost buyer’s money and the free user’s chance at a real connection. Finally, CON’s market correction argument collapses because every app faces the same short-term incentive to juice session length even when it burns users out; churn lets platforms chase new sign-ups faster than they alienate old ones, so competition alone will not fix the race to the bottom. These guardrails do not give apps cover to sell swipes back—they block that exact extractive move, requiring design to prioritize real matches over endless scrolling, for paying and free users alike.
