[P1.1] Elite universities present themselves as the great equalizers of our time, but the reality is far more cynical. They are, in essence, the modern equivalents of medieval guilds—exclusive clubs that exist primarily to launder inherited privilege into the currency of 'merit.' These institutions reproduce class hierarchy more effectively than they promote mobility, and our policy frameworks must stop treating them as engines of fairness and start recognizing them for what they are: institutions of inheritance.
[P1.2] Look at the admissions process. While test scores and grades provide a facade of objectivity, the path to an elite diploma is paved with advantages available only to the wealthy. Legacy preferences, donor development, and "holistic" reviews that reward expensive extracurriculars create a system where the children of the top percentile are vastly overrepresented compared to their equally talented peers from lower-income backgrounds. This is not a ladder; it is a velvet rope designed to keep the circle exclusive.
[P1.3] Even more telling is what happens after graduation. Elite universities serve as powerful networking hubs that facilitate "opportunity hoarding." They do not create potential; they concentrate it. By funneling their graduates into high-status roles in finance, law, and consulting, they ensure that the upper class remains a closed circuit.
[P1.4] Real mobility requires breaking down barriers for the disadvantaged. Instead, these schools build higher walls for the privileged.
[P1.5] Because these institutions function as engines of inheritance, they deserve none of the policy deference granted to actual public goods. Tax exemptions and preferential funding are predicated on the idea that these schools serve the commonweal. They do not. They serve their own.
[P1.6] Policy should treat them as what they truly are: highly effective mechanisms for passing down status from one generation to the next.

The remedy for hoarded opportunity is more admits, not fewer privileges. Blaming elite universities for the inequality they select from is blaming the ladder for the height of the wall.
[C1.4] Meanwhile the inheritance story is collapsing. [C1.5] Follow PRO's policy to its end. Reclassify these schools as inheritance machines, cut their privileges, and the first casualty is the aid budget that buys the poor student's seat. The alternative to mobility by degree is not equality but mobility by family network — inheritance in its purest form. The strongest escalator from the bottom to the top we possess deserves to be run faster — more seats, more transfers, more outreach — not rebranded as part of the wall.
