[P1.1] The fundamental purpose of the minimum wage is to protect workers from exploitation and ensure a basic standard of living. Gig-economy platforms circumvent this by defining labor solely as the minutes spent actively completing a task, ignoring the hours workers spend logged into the app waiting for an assignment. This proposition corrects that loophole by requiring platforms to compensate workers for active waiting time.
[P1.2] When a driver or delivery worker is logged in and waiting, they are economically captive. They cannot take a second job, attend a class, or meaningfully disconnect. They are holding themselves at the platform’s disposal to ensure the algorithm has enough liquidity to guarantee fast pickup times for consumers. The platform profits from this standby availability, yet forces the worker to absorb the financial risk of slow periods.
[P1.3] If orders drop, the worker sits in their car burning gas and time, earning zero dollars per hour, while the platform benefits from their mere presence keeping the network viable.
[P1.4] By excluding waiting time, platforms artificially suppress labor costs and shift market risk onto the worker. They secure a massive, on-demand workforce without paying for the idle time required to maintain it. This drives effective hourly earnings well below the legal minimum wage once total logged hours are factored in.
[P1.5] Including active waiting time simply aligns gig work with the economic reality of traditional standby rules. If a company requires a worker to be available on their proprietary app, ready to accept dispatches at a moment's notice, that time is worked time. We must close the algorithmic loophole that allows tech companies to dictate a worker's availability while only paying for the profitable fractions of an hour.

Minimum wage compliance must track productive, platform-directed activity, not self-selected availability.
[C1.6] Forcing platforms to subsidize uncontrolled downtime does not elevate gig workers; it prices them out, replaces autonomy with scheduling, and sacrifices the on-demand economy to a regulatory model it cannot sustain.
