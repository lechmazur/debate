[P1.1] A rejected asylum claim is not a paper transaction. It is someone returned to persecution, a family separated, a life rerouted by an error no one can inspect. Automated decision-making already operates in immigration and asylum screening - scoring risk, triaging applications, detecting fraud, classifying nationalities. The question is not whether these systems will exist, but whether they will be answerable to the people they govern.
[P1.2] I defend a global minimum standard for algorithmic transparency because three failures converge without one.
[P1.3] **First, the accountability gap.** When a human officer denies a visa, a name sits on the decision, a rationale can be tested, an appeal is possible. When a proprietary model returns a score, responsibility diffuses into code and contract. Asylum law has recognized that the right to appeal presupposes the right to understand the case against you. Algorithmic opacity erodes this without ever revoking it.
[P1.4] **Second, harm stays invisible.** Training-data skew against certain regions, calibration errors that under-score applicants from conflict zones, national-origin bias - none surfaces without disclosure. Documented cases in visa screening and border classification show the pattern: harm accumulates quietly because no one is required to look.
[P1.5] **Third, a race-to-the-bottom risk.** Algorithms are sold internationally. Vendors pitch the same opaque system to many governments, competing on cost and speed, not explainability. Only a shared floor prevents the least accountable system from becoming the default export.
[P1.6] The objection that transparency burdens national security misunderstands the proposal. A minimum standard does not require publishing source code. It requires disclosure that an algorithmic tool is in use, a plain-language description of its function, an avenue to challenge its output, and independent audit access. Sovereignty objections ignore that a floor constrains only the worst abuses, not architecture.
[P1.7] Trade-secrets objections miss the cure - safe harbors and third-party audits already operate in finance and medical AI.
[P1.8] A global minimum standard is the smallest intervention that preserves the oldest promise of asylum law: that a decision against you can be met with reasons you can answer.
