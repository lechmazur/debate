[P1.1] Access to high-quality internet is not an optional convenience; it has become the central gateway to employment, education, health care, banking, and public administration. A person without reliable broadband today can be locked out of job applications, remote schooling, telehealth appointments, tax filing, and emergency information. When a utility becomes necessary for ordinary participation in society, the state has an obligation to guarantee it—and to give that guarantee legal force. Water and electricity were not always treated as universal services; they became so because life without them became untenable.
[P1.2] Internet has crossed the same threshold.
[P1.3] The market alone will not deliver universal high-quality access. Providers rationally invest first in dense, affluent areas, leaving rural, low-income, and marginalized communities with slower, costlier, or no service. That is not a temporary glitch; it is the normal logic of private network investment. Universal service obligations exist precisely because infrastructure with high fixed costs and huge social spillovers is underprovided when left to commercial choice.
[P1.4] Electrification required enforceable buildout and affordability rules; broadband requires the same.
[P1.5] A merely aspirational policy fails. Treating access as a legally enforceable universal service means residents can demand a defined minimum standard—reliable, low-latency service capable of two-way video, remote work, and learning—at a reasonable cost. Regulators can impose quality, continuity, and affordability conditions, and people have remedies when those standards are not met. Otherwise, access remains a subsidy or a promise, easily cut or ignored by the next administration.
[P1.6] High-quality does not mean luxury. It means the baseline needed to function in a modern economy: stable enough for telehealth, school, work, and civic participation, not a bare signal. The standard can be updated as technology changes, just as safety and reliability standards evolve for water and electricity.
[P1.7] But the legal duty must exist, or geography and income will continue to decide who can participate. That is why internet should sit alongside water and electricity as an enforceable universal service.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane until you ask three practical questions: enforceable against whom, at what standard, and at whose expense? On each, the utility model collapses.
[C1.2] First, the analogy fails on its own terms. Water and electricity are stable commodities: a liter is a liter, a volt is a volt, and the standard barely changes across decades. "High-quality internet" is a moving target that redefines itself every few years. Legally enforceable standards must be written down, litigated, and defended in court—which means the mandated baseline will always lag the technology.
[C1.3] Worse, once a definition carries legal liability, providers optimize to the letter of the rule rather than to actual innovation. We would freeze in law what we most need to keep fluid.
[C1.4] Second, enforceability has a price the poor will pay. If the duty falls on private providers, they must build unprofitable lines and offer regulated rates—costs recovered from every other subscriber, a regressive tax on urban low-income users to wire remote second homes. If the duty falls on the state, an enforceable right means anyone can sue when their video call stutters. Courts cannot lay fiber; they can only order money moved—typically away from schools and clinics in countries where even water and electricity are not yet universal.
[C1.5] A justiciable right to broadband in such places is symbolic law that consumes real litigation resources.
[C1.6] Third, the mandate targets the wrong problem. In most developed countries the binding constraint is no longer availability but adoption: affordability, devices, and digital skills. Targeted subsidies, voucher programs, competition policy, and rapidly improving satellite and wireless options are closing gaps faster than any legal architecture could—and they adapt without statutory rewrites.
[C1.7] The choice is not universal access versus neglect. It is flexible, targeted expansion versus a rigid legal regime that ossifies standards, distorts investment, and diverts public money into courtrooms. Expand access, yes. Enshrine an enforceable utility right, no.

[P2.1] The moving-target objection attacks a strawman. Utility regulation does not require a permanent, frozen standard; it sets a performance floor and updates it. Drinking-water rules have been tightened repeatedly, and electricity reliability standards have changed as grids and safety knowledge improved. An enforceable broadband duty can do the same: define quality by function—low latency, sufficient upload, and reliability for two-way work, school, and health care—and let regulators revise the benchmark as technology advances.
[P2.2] Law sets the floor; providers remain free to exceed it. [C1.2] confuses enforceability with technological stagnation.
[P2.3] The cost argument is weaker still. Under the status quo, private providers avoid unprofitable rural and low-income areas, so excluded people pay with lost jobs, education, telehealth, and civic access. A universal service framework can fund expansion progressively through public procurement, buildout obligations, and cross-subsidies; that is a distributive choice, not a flaw in the legal model. And enforcement need not mean a “stuttering video call” lawsuit.
[P2.4] Courts and regulators can order providers to meet coverage and quality benchmarks, or require agencies to administer funds properly. The legal duty is what gives those remedies force.
[P2.5] Finally, the claim that adoption is the real problem concedes too much. Affordability and reliability are part of the universal-service obligation. Vouchers and discretionary programs are weaker without an enforceable baseline; they can be cut, narrowed, or captured. Satellite and wireless services can help, but they do not consistently deliver the low-latency, high-capacity connection the motion protects.
[P2.6] If targeted expansions are genuinely working, binding them into law locks in that success instead of leaving it to the next budget cycle. The alternative is not flexibility; it is a promise that geography and income can still break.
