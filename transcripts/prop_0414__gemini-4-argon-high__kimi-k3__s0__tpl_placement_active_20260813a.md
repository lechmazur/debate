[P1.1] In overcrowded cities, a tourist tax is not an arbitrary levy; it is a fee meant to offset a specific, destructive footprint. When millions of visitors descend on cities like Venice, Barcelona, or Amsterdam, they do not strain every municipal department equally. They attack three exact systems: they hollow out resident housing via short-term rentals, pack subways and buses, and leave mountains of trash in public spaces. Earmarking tourist taxes primarily for housing, transit, and sanitation ensures the revenue repairs the exact damage visitors create.
[P1.2] Absorbing these funds into general budgets breaks that link and invites political capture. In practice, "fiscal flexibility" is often a polite label for a slush fund. When tourist taxes enter the general pot, politicians routinely use them to plug unrelated deficits or—most perversely—subsidize tourism marketing boards and convention centers that attract even more crowds. The result is a regressive trap: working residents pay the hidden cost of higher rents, longer commutes, and dirtier streets, while city hall treats their declining quality of life as a cash cow.
[P1.3] Earmarking also aligns revenue with reality. While tourism fluctuates, transit and sanitation burdens scale directly with visitor volume; when crowds surge, earmarked funds automatically rise to pay for extra trains and street sweepers. Meanwhile, boom-year surpluses can capitalize affordable housing trusts, permanently insulating locals from the speculative spike of vacation rentals.
[P1.4] Finally, earmarking is necessary to preserve the social license of tourism itself. Resident backlash against overtourism is boiling over because locals endure the daily misery of congestion while the tax revenue vanishes into an opaque bureaucracy. Residents should not have to fight at annual budget hearings just to offset the wear-and-tear on their own neighborhoods. Locking this revenue primarily into housing, transit, and sanitation turns an extractive industry into a fair, visible civic bargain.

[C1.1] Nobody disputes that tourists impose costs. The question is whether locking the revenue into three statutory silos fixes those costs better than a budget that can see the whole city. The motion's engine is the word "primarily" — a lockbox. The lockbox fails four ways.
[C1.2] First, money is fungible, so earmarks change labels, not totals. A city hall inclined to cut transit can fund it with tourist taxes and quietly withdraw the general money it spent before. This is not speculation: state lotteries earmarked for education famously produced little net gain in school spending, because legislatures shifted other dollars out. The iron link between tax and repair is an accounting fiction.
[C1.3] Second, earmarks bind hardest at the worst moments. Tourist revenue is pro-cyclical; it vanishes exactly when crises hit. When the pandemic emptied hotels, American cities whose hotel taxes were legally dedicated to convention centers and stadiums kept servicing those bonds while emergency needs exploded — and the lockbox forbade moving the money. A general budget pivots; an earmark cannot.
[C1.4] Third, the footprint premise is rigged. Tourists burden not only housing, transit, and sanitation but policing, emergency medicine, water, heritage sites, public health. Either you earmark for everything — absurd — or someone must judge priorities year to year. That someone is the budget process.
[C1.5] And the scale is wrong: a per-night fee raises modest sums, while housing shortages run to the billions. Promising a hotel tax will "insulate locals" from the housing market overpromises so badly it breeds the very cynicism PRO fears.
[C1.6] Fourth, capture runs the other way. Dedicated funds are precisely how tourism marketing boards and convention bureaus locked in their subsidies — statutory earmarks guaranteed their money regardless of merit. Annual budgets are debated in public and can cut failures; earmarked trusts become unaccountable empires.
[C1.7] Tax the tourists, certainly. Then let elected governments spend it where the damage — all of it — actually is.
