[P1.1] You cannot regulate what you have never measured. Everything else follows.
[P1.2] The ISA's legal duty under the Law of the Sea is to protect the marine environment from serious harm. But "harm" is a comparison: after versus before. Without a robust baseline — what lives on the abyssal plain, how those communities function, how carbon and nutrients cycle through them — no threshold can be set, no violation detected, no operator held liable. Approving mining without baselines is not cautious regulation; it is issuing a licence with the monitoring gauge unplugged.
[P1.3] In the Clarion-Clipperton Zone, the main target region, surveys keep finding that most animals collected are species new to science. The nodules themselves are habitat, formed over millions of years; they do not grow back on any human timescale. Experimental disturbance tracks ploughed decades ago remain visible today, with ecosystems still not fully recovered. Sediment plumes may travel far beyond the mined site, into waters no one has characterised.
[P1.4] Why independently verified? Because much of the baseline data is collected by the contractors who profit from approval, and assessed by an authority that would collect royalties from the mining. We do not let a drug company run its own trials unaudited and approve its own product.
[P1.5] Now weigh the errors. If we pause and the science comes back reassuring, we have lost a few years; the nodules will still be there, and batteries are already shifting toward chemistries that use less cobalt and nickel. If we approve and the science comes back damning, we have lost ecosystems for geological time, with no way to prove what was destroyed.
[P1.6] One mistake is a delay. The other is permanent. A pause is not a ban; it is the condition under which a yes could ever mean anything.
