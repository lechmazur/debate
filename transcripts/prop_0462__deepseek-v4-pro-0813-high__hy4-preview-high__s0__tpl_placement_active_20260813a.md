[P1.1] Math tracking is sold as matching instruction to ability, but in practice it sorts by advantage. Placement into a lower track is sticky: students rarely jump into the honors sequence, and placement reflects parent advocacy, earlier enrichment, teacher expectations, class, and race as much as mathematical thinking. The result is not a meritocracy but an inherited hierarchy. Heterogeneous classes disrupt that hierarchy instead of scheduling it.
[P1.2] The usual objection is that advanced students will be bored. That assumes a mixed-ability class must move at one slow pace. It does not. A well-supported heterogeneous class can use low-floor, high-ceiling tasks: every student enters the same problem, but stronger students go deeper through proof, generalization, multiple solution methods, or contest-style extensions.
[P1.3] Intensive tutoring blocks make this possible. Struggling students receive pre-teaching and re-teaching, while advanced students receive compacted extension challenges. The classroom is mixed; the support is differentiated. Rigor does not require permanent tracking.
[P1.4] In fact, tracking narrows the advanced pipeline too early. Students who develop later or lack private enrichment are shut out before they can show growth. Intensive tutoring targets actual skill gaps, which is the real bottleneck for most learners. Schools that shift resources from sorting into tutoring can raise both floor and ceiling because they stop confusing past preparation with future potential.
[P1.5] Labeling children into a “low” track also does direct harm: lower expectations, weaker curriculum, stigma, and less experienced teachers often follow the same label. Replacing most tracked sections with heterogeneous classes plus intensive tutoring removes that self-fulfilling prophecy and makes high expectations the default. Excellence and equity are not opposed here; the equitable structure is the stronger engine for broad mathematical excellence.
