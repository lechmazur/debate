[P1.1] The real question tonight is not whether judicial independence matters — it does — but whether lifetime tenure is the *only* way to secure it. Fixed terms, designed correctly, strengthen democratic legitimacy far more than they erode judicial independence. I'll make three moves.
[P1.2] First, lifetime tenure quietly hollows out democratic legitimacy. When justices serve for decades, vacancies become national emergencies, and the timing of a single death or retirement can swing constitutional law for a generation. That is not democratic accountability — it is a lottery with constitutional stakes. Fixed terms convert that lottery into a predictable rhythm.
[P1.3] The public sees when a seat will turn over, can judge a justice's record, and can weigh in through elections that actually mean something. Compare the United States, where a single recent vacancy reshaped rights for millions, with Germany, where Federal Constitutional Court justices serve twelve-year, non-renewable terms and the court's authority has only deepened.
[P1.4] Second, the supposed damage to independence is mostly a category error. Independence is a structural property — salary protection, removal only for cause, jurisdictional respect — not a synonym for "never face accountability." A justice who knows the term ends on a fixed date, and cannot be reappointed, has *less* reason to curry favor, not more. Lifetime tenure actually creates a perverse incentive at the margins: near the end, justices face enormous pressure because one vacancy can swing the court for decades.
[P1.5] Fixed terms drain that pressure out of the system.
[P1.6] Third, the comparison the motion demands is asymmetric. The democratic-legitimacy gains are large and visible: predictable turnover, reduced stakes-manufacturing, public ownership of the judiciary. The independence costs, with proper design, are small and containable. The balance favors reform.
[P1.7] The choice is not between independence and democracy. It is between a system where one side wins the lottery and a system where the public owns the court. Fixed terms are the latter.
