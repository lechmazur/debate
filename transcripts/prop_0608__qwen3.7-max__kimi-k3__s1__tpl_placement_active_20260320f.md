[P1.1] Corporate "net-zero" claims have devolved into a license to pollute. By relying heavily on cheap, unverified carbon offsets—like paying to protect forests that were never in danger of being logged—companies buy an accounting illusion while their physical emissions continue to rise. We need legal limits on offsets because the voluntary market has repeatedly failed to guarantee additionality, permanence, and accurate baselines. When offsets are unlimited, they become a cheap loophole that actively delays urgent climate action.
[P1.2] Mandating that the vast majority of reductions occur in-value-chain forces companies to do the hard work of actual decarbonization. This means retrofitting factories, electrifying corporate fleets, and fundamentally redesigning global supply chains. When a corporation is legally required to cut its own direct and indirect emissions, it is forced to invest heavily in green technology and long-term operational efficiency. This drives the systemic industrial transformation required to stabilize the climate, rather than just shifting capital to a distant, easily reversible forestry project that might burn down a decade later.
[P1.3] This proposition does not ban offsets; it restricts them to a small, legally capped share. A limited role for offsets is appropriate for the final, technologically unavoidable fraction of a company's footprint, or for funding high-quality, durable carbon removal. But if we allow offsets to cover the bulk of a corporate footprint, we actively crowd out the capital and innovation needed for real, physical mitigation.
[P1.4] Without strict legal guardrails, "net-zero" remains a marketing fiction that accelerates greenwashing. Capping offsets ensures that when a corporation claims to be net-zero, it has actually transformed its physical operations, not just its balance sheet. We must legally require companies to clean up their own messes before they are allowed to pay to clean up someone else's.

[C1.5] Third, the cap strangles two things climate policy desperately needs. Offset demand is the principal revenue stream scaling engineered carbon removal—direct air capture that must get cheap by mid-century and only gets cheap with buyers today. Cap that demand, and the forests don't get protected better; they get logged.
[C1.6] Finally, "in-value-chain" is a fuzzier boundary than PRO admits.
