[P1.2] That is the absurdity at the centre of this debate: a right that can only be reached through a crime.
[P1.3] Picture a Syrian widow in Beirut with two children. She has documents, a case, a cousin in Germany. There is no counter she can walk up to. Her only route is a smuggler charging thousands per head for a night crossing in an overloaded boat.
[P1.4] If she survives, she becomes a "problem" at Lampedusa. If she doesn't, she becomes a statistic. A humanitarian visa gives her a third option: apply at a consulate, be screened, wait for an answer.
[P1.5] Notice what that changes. The decision moves from the smuggler to the state. Today, who reaches Europe is determined by who can pay $5,000 and swim; under a visa scheme, it is determined by European officials assessing claims with files, interviews, and security checks done calmly in advance, rather than in a chaotic reception centre after arrival. That is more control, not less.
[P1.6] It is also cheaper: processing an application in Amman costs a fraction of sea rescue, detention, and appeal.
[P1.7] Unified matters as much as visa. Italy's church-run humanitarian corridors and various ad hoc national schemes have shown the mechanism works at small scale — but a patchwork lets member states free-ride on each other and produces incoherent, arbitrary access. A single EU instrument allows shared quotas, common criteria, and a distribution agreed in advance instead of improvised during a crisis.
[P1.8] CON will call this open borders. It is the opposite. A visa is a filter with a refusal stamp attached. The question is not whether Europe screens asylum seekers, but whether it screens them before the drowning or after.
