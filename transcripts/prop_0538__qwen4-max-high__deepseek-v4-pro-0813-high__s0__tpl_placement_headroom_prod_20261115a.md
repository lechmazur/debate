[P1.1] Cocoa leaves Ghana as beans and returns as chocolate. The farmer keeps a sliver of the shelf price; the roaster, the brand, the logistics firm and the tax authority abroad keep the rest. That gap between what a commodity is worth in the ground and what it is worth finished is the whole question here. Prioritizing intra-African trade and manufacturing means moving to the side of the ledger where value, skills and tax revenue accumulate.
[P1.2] Raw-material dependence is not merely less profitable; it is structurally disempowering. A country exporting unprocessed copper or crude is a price-taker in markets it cannot influence, whose buyers are concentrated and whose prices swing with someone else's business cycle. Budgets built on that swing get slashed exactly when citizens need them most.
[P1.3] Meanwhile the mine employs a few thousand people, while the millions of young Africans entering the labour market every year need work that only processing, assembly and the services around manufacturing can supply.
[P1.4] Regional trade is the realistic first rung. Nobody asks Lagos or Nairobi to out-compete established Asian exporters on day one. But soap, textiles, cement, processed food, generic medicines and simple machinery can be sold to neighbours, in markets forgiving enough to climb the learning curve. That is how late industrializers started: nearby demand, then export discipline.
[P1.5] Colonial infrastructure was built from mine to port, not city to city. The continent's low internal trade is an inherited design, not a verdict of economics.
[P1.6] So when CON says comparative advantage counsels patience, remember that today's advantage was manufactured by yesterday's extraction, and static efficiency is the argument for staying poor politely. The motion says prioritize, not prohibit. Keep exporting; stop treating the raw-material lane as destiny.

[C1.1] The motion asks African governments to shift scarce capital and political attention away from the sectors that currently generate foreign exchange, tax revenue, and jobs linked to global demand. That ordering is dangerous. Commodity exports are not the disease; they are the cash flow that buys imported machinery, medicines, fertilizer, and debt service. Removing priority from them before replacement industries are competitive would export the current account stability first and manufacture a crisis second.
[C1.2] The regional market is not a waiting abundance. Intra-African trade is low partly because many neighboring economies export similar primary products, consumer incomes are small, and transport, electricity, and border costs are high. A "first rung" only exists where demand can reward a factory at profitable scale. For many processed goods, the realistic buyer remains the distant rich market, not the under-sized regional one.
[C1.3] The value-added argument is also too quick. Local processing can capture margin only when energy, skills, packaging, and quality control are cheap enough to leave a profit. If they are not, turning cocoa into chocolate or copper into wire destroys value rather than capturing it. The cocoa farmer may gain more from a well-taxed, well-governed export chain than from a subsidized factory that cannot sell affordable chocolate.
[C1.4] This does not mean accepting colonial infrastructure as destiny. It means sequencing matters. Successful late industrializers typically earned from exports first—often raw or low-wage goods—then used those earnings to upgrade infrastructure and skills. The better priority is to improve the terms, taxation, local inputs, and governance around existing exports while cutting internal trade costs.
[C1.5] Manufacture where it is profitable, not because a slogan says regional production is morally superior. The export lane need not be destiny, but it is currently the engine; parking it first would stall the very transition the motion claims to want.
