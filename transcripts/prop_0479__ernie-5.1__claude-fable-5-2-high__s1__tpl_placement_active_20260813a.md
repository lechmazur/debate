[P1.1] When a government must decide whether to impose a carbon tax, it currently leans on a handful of economists and agency analysts. Those experts bring training, but they also bring institutional blinders: they share the same data pipelines, face the same political incentives, and have little personal cost for being wrong. The result is a narrow, often overconfident forecast that rarely captures the dispersed knowledge sitting in firms, NGOs, and ordinary citizens.
[P1.2] Subsidized prediction markets invert that dynamic. By offering modest financial rewards for correct forecasts, they attract anyone who holds a piece of relevant information—traders in energy markets who see price signals before analysts, local entrepreneurs who sense consumer reaction, academics who model climate feedback. The market price itself becomes a probability estimate, continuously updated as new data arrives. History shows this works: the Iowa Electronic Markets have out‑performed poll aggregators on election outcomes for decades; DARPA’s forecasting tournaments repeatedly found that crowd‑sourced market predictions beat the best expert panels on geopolitical and scientific questions.
[P1.3] Consider the early COVID‑19 response: market participants who tracked supply‑chain data forecasted vaccine distribution bottlenecks weeks before official projections, offering an early warning that expert models missed.
[P1.4] A subsidy solves the liquidity problem. Pure markets with tiny stakes under‑price information; a government‑funded pool guarantees enough participation to generate reliable signals while keeping the cost modest—far cheaper than commissioning endless expert studies. Crucially, the market output is transparent and auditable: policymakers can point to a price rather than a vague consensus, making accountability concrete.
[P1.5] Expert panels will always have a role, but they should not dominate. The proposition is not to replace all analysis, but to make prediction‑market results a formal, weighted input alongside traditional forecasts. Doing so sharpens decision‑making, curbs groupthink, and lets governments tap the collective intelligence that no single committee can match.
