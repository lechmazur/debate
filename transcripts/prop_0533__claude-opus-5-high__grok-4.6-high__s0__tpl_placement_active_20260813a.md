[P1.1] The Blue Nile falls from the Ethiopian highlands and supplies the great majority of the Nile's water. Ethiopia has contributed that water for millennia and drawn almost nothing from it, while a colonial-era allocation system — negotiated in 1929 between Britain and Egypt, and again in 1959 between Egypt and Sudan — divided the entire river between two downstream states without Ethiopia in the room. No principle of international law binds a country to a treaty it never signed, concluded by an empire that did not govern it.
[P1.2] That is the starting point, and it decides most of this debate.
[P1.3] Notice what the proposition actually turns on: whether Egypt's *objection* is enough to stop construction and filling. That is a demand for a veto, not for consultation. And a veto has no expiration date. Negotiations over GERD have run for more than a decade; every technical gap has been followed by a new demand.
[P1.4] If "Egypt objects" is a stopping condition, Ethiopia's development schedule belongs permanently to Cairo, and any future Ethiopian irrigation or power project dies in the same corridor.
[P1.5] Then consider what the dam actually does. GERD is a hydroelectric project. Water spins turbines and continues downstream; it is not consumed. Storage behind the dam in a deep highland gorge loses far less to evaporation than shallow desert reservoirs, and a regulated flow reduces the flood-and-drought whiplash Sudan has lived with for generations.
[P1.6] The realistic dispute is about the *pace* of filling during dry years — a scheduling question, manageable by coordination — not about Egypt's water disappearing.
[P1.7] Weigh the stakes concretely. Tens of millions of Ethiopians still cook over biomass and study without electric light. The claim on the other side is that their access to power should wait indefinitely on the consent of a neighbor whose own Aswan High Dam was built without asking anyone upstream.
[P1.8] International water law asks for equitable and reasonable use and good-faith harm mitigation. It does not hand any state ownership of a river that begins in someone else's mountains.
