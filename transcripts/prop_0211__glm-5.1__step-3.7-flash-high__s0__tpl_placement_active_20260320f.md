[P1.1] Preventive obesity drugs like GLP-1 agonists represent a fiscal watershed. When a health system reimburses broadly—meaning open access at population scale—unit price becomes destiny. A drug costing thousands per patient per year, prescribed to tens of millions, does not merely strain budgets; it cannibalizes them. Every dollar overpaid forwegovy is a dollar unavailable for dialysis, maternity care, or mental health.
[P1.2] Outcome-based pricing and spending caps are not punishments for manufacturers; they are the logical extension of what preventive medicine claims to be. Prevention promises downstream savings—fewer cardiac events, less diabetes, reduced joint replacements. If manufacturers believe their drugs deliver those savings, they should accept contracts tied to measured outcomes: reduced cardiovascular events, documented weight thresholds maintained, hospitalization rates falling. Pay-for-performance is standard in hospital reimbursement; pharmaceutical corporations deserve no exemption.
[P1.3] Without these safeguards, the incentive structure is perverse. Manufacturers market aggressively to the widest possible population, charge whatever the market will bear, and externalize all risk onto taxpayers and patients who face formulary restrictions or higher premiums. The manufacturer profits whether the drug prevents disease in a given patient or simply adds cost. Outcome-based pricing re-aligns incentives: manufacturers who deliver genuine health improvements earn full reimbursement; those whose products underperform share the downside.
[P1.4] Spending caps serve as a backstop. Even effective drugs can become fiscally ruinous at population scale if unit costs remain unchecked. Caps let health systems plan, budget, and honor commitments to all patients—not just those for whom a blockbuster drug is prescribed.
[P1.5] The resolution says "broadly only when"—it does not deny individual coverage through exceptions pathways. It attaches conditions to opening the reimbursement floodgates. That distinction is critical. Broad coverage of expensive preventive drugs without fiscal discipline is not compassionate; it is a promise that will be broken when the money runs out.

[C1.1] The proposition’s proposed pricing tools are unworkable, counterproductive, and will harm patients far more than they protect health system budgets. Outcome-based pricing is functionally impossible to implement fairly for chronic obesity drugs, and PRO’s comparison to standard hospital pay-for-performance is a false equivalence. Hospital reimbursements tie payment to discrete, measurable episodes fully controlled by the provider, while obesity is a chronic condition shaped by social determinants, patient adherence, and baseline comorbidities—factors entirely outside a manufacturer’s control. Clinical success for these medications is not measured by hitting arbitrary weight thresholds alone: a patient with obesity and pre-existing knee damage who loses 8% of their body weight may see enough reduction in joint pain to resume regular exercise, cutting their long-term risk of diabetes and joint replacement, even if they never hit the 15% cut-off many plans would use to define “success.” Manufacturers would be penalized for results they cannot possibly influence.
[C1.2] Worse, measuring downstream outcomes like reduced cardiac events takes 5–10 years, long after budget cycles have closed, so plans would still face massive short-term overruns while waiting for data to justify reimbursement. Spending caps are equally destructive. They create a hard cliff: once a health plan hits its predetermined cap for obesity drugs, it cuts off access for all patients, even those responding well enough to avoid far costlier downstream care. A patient whose obesity-driven sleep apnea has resolved, slashing their heart failure risk by 60%, would lose coverage the moment the cap is hit, leading to weight regain, sleep apnea return, and a $50,000+ hospitalization that costs the system far more than the drug itself.
[C1.3] Finally, these tools will not deliver the cost savings PRO promises. Manufacturers will either refuse to participate in outcome-based contracts entirely, eliminating broad access for patients, or bake the risk of penalties and cap restrictions into 30–40% higher upfront list prices, leaving health systems no better off than if they negotiated standard flat rebates—the proven, widely used tool to control costs for high-cost specialty drugs without restricting patient access or creating perverse incentives.

[P2.6] CON's "proven" rebate alternative doesn't tie payment to health outcomes at all; it pays for volume regardless of effectiveness.
