[P1.1] Museums should return looted colonial-era artifacts because their continued possession depends on a colonial acquisition that lacked the consent of the people from whom the objects were taken. Better preservation in a foreign institution does not repair that original wrong. It may reduce deterioration, but legal title is not the same as moral legitimacy: museums cannot convert seizure into stewardship merely by protecting an object well.
[P1.2] The preservation argument also ignores the kind of preservation that matters. An artifact can remain physically intact while being culturally dispossessed. Sacred objects, royal regalia, and ancestral remains may have little meaning apart from the communities whose histories, ceremonies, and political authority they embody. The Parthenon sculptures, for example, are not merely decorative fragments; their separation from Greece deprives them of their civic and historical context.
[P1.3] Restitution allows origin-country institutions to interpret artifacts for their own publics rather than presenting them through the narratives of former colonial powers.
[P1.4] Return is not synonymous with neglect. Originating countries should be responsible for conservation, and international partnerships can provide funding, training, climate-controlled storage, and shared research. Museums may also retain long-term custodial roles or lend objects back under agreed conservation regimes. A carefully managed transfer can therefore preserve the artifact and restore the community’s relationship with it.
[P1.5] The objection that foreign museums possess superior facilities mistakes concentrated wealth for an entitlement to keep other people’s property.
[P1.6] There is a legitimate concern that sudden transfers could damage fragile objects. But that calls for enforceable care standards, transparent preparation, and international oversight—not permanent ownership by the party that committed the initial looting. The relevant comparison is not simply “foreign museum versus local museum”; it is colonial ownership, which generated the dispute, versus reparative ownership that can include professional conservation support.
[P1.7] Justice is not an optional alternative to preservation. The strongest institution is the one that can preserve an artifact while allowing the people whose history it embodies to exercise authority over it. Return recognizes that authority and opens restoration to begin.

[C1.1] An artifact destroyed by unstable storage, civil conflict, corrupt diversion, or underfunded conservation is not “culturally restored” to anyone—it is gone forever, for the origin community and the entire world. Preservation is not a trivial colonial talking point to be dismissed as a post-hoc justification for theft; it is the precondition for any of the cultural, historical, and spiritual value PRO invokes to exist at all. Framing permanent, irreversible loss of an object as an acceptable cost of symbolic justice is hollow, performative restitution that serves no living community, least of all the descendants of the people who created it.
[C1.2] PRO dismisses superior foreign preservation as a colonial entitlement, but that ignores a hard reality: mandatory one-way return often gambles irreplaceable artifacts on institutions that lack consistent power for climate control, trained conservation staff, security against re-looting, reliable public access, or insulation from political factions that may seize sacred objects to prop up nationalist regimes unaccountable to the communities that made them. Many returned objects have ended up locked in unmonitored basements, damaged by improper handling, or stolen again to feed black market antiquities trades, with no recourse for the communities the policy was meant to help.
[C1.3] Co-curation, long-term rotating loans, joint research, and dedicated community and diaspora access programs deliver the contextual authority and connection PRO demands without risking an object’s permanent destruction. Global museums also make these artifacts accessible to far more people, including diaspora communities scattered by the very colonial displacements PRO decries. There is no justice in handing a community a crumbling, lost, or stolen artifact just to check a box for colonial atonement, especially when many origin groups prioritize guaranteed access and long-term preservation over unilateral title transfers that put their shared heritage at irreversible risk.
