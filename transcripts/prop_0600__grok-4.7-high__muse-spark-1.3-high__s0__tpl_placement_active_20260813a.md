[P1.1] The nearshoring moment is Latin America's clearest chance in decades to escape commodity dependence. Manufacturing and services are moving out of Asian supply chains toward the Western Hemisphere because of proximity to U.S. demand, existing agreements, and geopolitical friction with China. Passive openness will capture only warehouses and screwdriver plants. A deliberate nearshoring industrial policy can pull in supplier ecosystems, engineering work, and export platforms that raise productivity for a generation.
[P1.2] That policy will increase protectionist measures and fiscal burdens. Local-content requirements, temporary tariffs on competing imports, and public money for power, ports, skills, and investment incentives impose real costs and invite rent-seeking. Those costs are the price of building what markets have failed to build on their own. Without conditioned protection and catalytic spending, foreign plants arrive, import their components, and leave thin local linkages.
[P1.3] Protection used as a time-limited bridge—tied to export performance, local supplier development, and sunset clauses—is different from permanent shelter for inefficiency.
[P1.4] The fiscal case is comparative, not absolute. A government that forgoes the outlay does not get a free lunch; it gets reliance on volatile commodity revenues, informal employment, and thin tax bases. Targeted spending that anchors an auto, electronics, or medical-device cluster can formalize jobs and widen the revenue base that later finances the debt. Earlier industrializers treated similar burdens as the entry fee to diversified manufacturing.
[P1.5] Latin America can design narrower, more accountable versions without pretending that fiscal purity will somehow produce the same result.
[P1.6] The status quo has already run the experiment of openness plus restraint. It delivered episodic growth without the industrial depth that stabilizes employment and trade balances. Missing this relocation window is not neutrality; it is a choice to remain a commodity periphery while others lock in production. Prioritizing nearshoring-focused industrial policy, even with more protection and higher near-term fiscal cost, is the better path.

[C1.1] Nearshoring hype is a bad excuse for expensive, inward-looking policy. Latin American governments are being asked to subsidize foreign multinationals and tax their own consumers in order to chase investment that can leave as quickly as it arrived.
[C1.2] The fiscal burden is not an entry fee; it is a direct threat to development. Most of the region borrows in costly currency, pays high interest, and already struggles to fund schools, hospitals, courts and basic roads. Diverting scarce budgets into tax holidays, free industrial parks, cheap energy for anchor investors and subsidized credit means less for the productivity foundations everyone else needs. When debt rises to pay multinationals that already have capital, borrowing costs rise for the domestic firms that create most jobs.
[C1.3] Protection makes it worse. Local-content mandates, temporary tariffs and forced supplier programs do not build ecosystems; they raise prices for inputs, punish efficient domestic producers, and reward lobbying. Latin America has lived this movie: protected auto markets produced among the world's most expensive cars for consumers while productivity lagged, and sheltered firms learned to capture ministries rather than conquer export markets. Sunset clauses seldom sunset.
[C1.4] And for what? Nearshoring locates decisions in Washington and in boardrooms abroad, not in Bogota, Lima or San Salvador. Favored access can vanish with one change in U.S. tariff rules, labor complaints or election cycle, leaving behind empty parks and fiscal holes. Trading commodity dependence for dependence on U.S. trade preferences is not diversification.
[C1.5] Countries that want more manufacturing should do the unglamorous work: reliable power, ports, courts that enforce contracts, simpler taxes, skills and open competition. A race among neighbors to offer larger exemptions only transfers rents to mobile capital while leaving domestic small firms to pay full tariffs and full taxes. That attracts investment without taxing the poor to pay the rich.
