[C1.1] The proposition asks us to enforce a one-sided legal right to donor identifying information even if it erodes regulated donation supply, writing off predictable harms to donors, intended parents, and future donor-conceived people themselves to prioritize a single interest above all else. First, the supply tradeoff the proposition dismisses as secondary is no trivial inconvenience to prospective parents. When regulated donation supply drops sharply, the people who lose out first are future donor-conceived children who are never conceived at all.
[C1.2] They have no ability to exercise a right to identity information if the policy creating that right prevents them from existing in the first place. Worse, shrinking legal supply does not end donor conception—it pushes arrangements to unvetted, informal online markets with no genetic disease screening, no guardrails to prevent accidental incest, no clear boundaries for donors or parents, leaving every party worse off.
[C1.3] Second, retroactively voiding explicit anonymity promises is a basic breach of fair dealing. Donors contributed genetic material on the clear condition they would not face unplanned contact, disruption to their existing families, or legal entanglements decades later. The claim this right is “narrow” is hollow: identifying information is the threshold for unwanted outreach, paternity claims, disruption to a donor’s spouse and children who had no part in the arrangement, and even future inheritance disputes. We do not need to release names to meet legitimate medical needs: mandatory, updated anonymous medical history registries can give donor-conceived people critical health information without breaking prior commitments.
[C1.4] The adoption analogy fails: adoption involves relinquishing care for an existing child, not a conditional gamete donation made on a promise of non-identification. The DNA test point is equally flimsy: the fact that some people can track relatives through consumer databases does not justify legally forcing every donor into the open, any more than the existence of lockpicks justifies abolishing front door locks.
