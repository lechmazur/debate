[P1.1] A public university that runs a degree program most students never finish is not educating students — it is collecting tuition and Pell Grants on their way out the door. That is the heart of this debate. When completion is the metric by which a program is judged "low," we are not theorizing about prestige or tradition; we are acknowledging an outcome already measured. Shifting resources away from those programs toward shorter vocational and technical pathways is not a hostile act against higher education.
[P1.2] It is the minimum act of stewardship a public institution owes to its students and its taxpayers.
[P1.3] Consider the typical picture. A four-year university program in, say, general studies or certain humanities tracks posts a six-year completion rate in the twenties or thirties. Students leave with debt, no credential, and a transcript that does not transfer cleanly. Meanwhile, a one- or two-year technical pathway in healthcare support, skilled trades, or IT typically finishes a much larger share of its enrollees, costs a fraction as much, and places graduates into jobs that pay a family-supporting wage within months.
[P1.4] The resources tied up in the under-finishing program are not a neutral inheritance. They are an active choice to keep subsidizing failure.
[P1.5] The counter-position must defend that choice. It must argue that pouring public money into seats that rarely produce graduates is justified by something other than the graduates themselves — by research spillovers, by institutional prestige, by vague claims about long-term earnings. Those are real benefits in some cases. They are not free, and they are not a defense of every program with a poor completion rate.
[P1.6] The proposition is narrow and surgical: shift resources from low-completion programs toward pathways that actually carry students through to a credential and a job. On any honest accounting of student welfare, fiscal responsibility, and public mission, that direction is the one a public university is obligated to take.

[C1.1] Public universities exist to expand capability and produce knowledge, not to run a completion factory. Treating unfinished degrees as pure waste is the error that drives this proposition. Students who leave early still carry usable skills, partial credits, and clearer direction into jobs or later study; many return or transfer. Labeling those trajectories “collecting tuition on the way out” erases real gains and the uneven preparation of the very students public campuses are obligated to serve.
[C1.2] The call to strip resources from low-completion programs and pour them into shorter vocational tracks confuses institutional roles. Technical pathways already deliver strong results in community colleges and dedicated institutes. Public universities are different creatures: they house research, broad curricula, and the flexible structures that let first-generation students discover interests, switch fields, and build analytical habits that outlast any single job. Defunding the programs that perform that work does not create better wages; it narrows opportunity for the students least able to afford a second try elsewhere.
[C1.3] Completion rates alone are a blunt instrument. They punish departments that enroll part-timers, working parents, and high-risk students rather than coddle only those already likely to finish. Better advising, support, and curricular redesign are real remedies. Abandoning degree capacity is not.
[C1.4] Public money buys more than a diploma on schedule; it buys scientific literacy, civic competence, and the long-horizon talent pool that vocational certificates cannot replace. Stewardship means improving the harder mission, not surrendering it for shorter, safer pathways.

[C2.2] Nor does the proposition merely shift “seats.” [P2.3] Low-completion undergraduate programs sit inside the same departments that teach the high-finishers, staff the core curriculum, and sustain the intellectual culture public universities exist to protect. [C2.3] PRO’s attempt to rebrand the blunt instrument [P2.5] still fails. Persistently low rates frequently track exactly the open-admission mission of public campuses: enrolling students whose prior preparation makes six-year graduation harder. Declaring that those departments must now clear a higher bar than community-college pathways is not stewardship; it is an invitation to cream-skim. Advising reforms take time; abandoning the programs after years of underfunding support is the real sunk-cost error [P2.6].
[C2.4] The clash is therefore not “why fund non-graduates” but whether public universities should forfeit their distinct, harder role—producing adaptable, scientifically literate citizens and long-horizon talent—so that more students can be pushed into short credentials other institutions already deliver better. Completing a narrow pathway faster is not a higher public good when it comes at the price of opportunity to discover what that pathway should even be.
