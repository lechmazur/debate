[P1.1] Water is the one input to human life with no substitute. Everything else in an economy can be re-sourced, re-priced, or done without. That asymmetry is why the ordinary presumption in favor of markets breaks down here, and why a categorical rule beats case-by-case permitting.
[P1.2] Start with what commercial bottling actually is. The company does not create the water. It does not purify a river or recharge an aquifer. It takes water that fell as rain into a shared basin, or water a city's ratepayers spent a century building infrastructure to capture, and adds a plastic bottle and a label.
[P1.3] The value captured is enormous; the value added is packaging. Meanwhile the extraction fee is often a nominal permit charge. That is not a market transaction. That is a transfer of a public asset to a private balance sheet.
[P1.4] Second, the harm is geographically concentrated in a way regulation handles badly. Bottling is a small share of national water use, which is exactly the argument bottlers make — and exactly why it slips through. The withdrawals land on one spring, one creek, one shallow aquifer, and the neighbors with wells discover the problem years later, when drawdown is already baked in. We have watched this play out in Michigan, in California's Inland Empire, in Maharashtra.
[P1.5] Long-term permits get issued in wet years and litigated in dry ones, while the company holds a contractual right and the town holds a lawsuit.
[P1.6] Third, private ownership of the source corrupts the incentive to fix public water. A firm selling bottles profits when people distrust the tap. Flint is the clean illustration: the crisis was solved with pallets of donated bottles while the pipes stayed broken. A drinking-water system funded by public money and defended by public accountability cannot share a revenue model with an industry that monetizes its failure.
[P1.7] Ban the ownership, the trading, and the extraction. Regulate use of water — but do not sell the source.

[C1.1] The case for a ban treats a visible bottle as if it were the aquifer. It is not. Commercial bottling is a tiny, metered slice of withdrawals. Agriculture, power plants, and leaky city pipes move far more water, usually with less monitoring.
[C1.2] A categorical ban on private ownership, trade, and extraction for bottling is not a water policy. It is a prohibition on the one use the public can see. If the extraction fee is too low, raise it. That is a pricing error, not a reason to confiscate the source.
[C1.3] Water's lack of a substitute is why basins need hard caps, not why one commercial use must be erased. Price the water, cap the basin, require proof a well will not dry the creek or the neighbor, and make the permit seasonal and revocable in drought. Those are ordinary tools. Michigan, the Inland Empire, and Maharashtra show cheap permits issued in wet years and litigated later.
[C1.4] That is bad government, not proof a firm cannot operate under a hard volumetric limit.
[C1.5] Worse, the motion bans trading. Tradable rights are how arid regions pay farmers to leave water in-stream or send it to a city. Kill trade and you lock low-value use in place. A ban on trade is a ban on paying people to use less.
[C1.6] Ownership bans go further: they take springs on private land, groundwater under private parcels, and surplus municipal water already purchased in good faith.
[C1.7] Flint cuts against the ban. Municipal failure put lead in the tap. Bottles were the backup, not the cause. People in disasters, boil-water orders, and towns with aging plants need packaged water whether or not a corporation owns a spring.
[C1.8] Outlaw the legitimate supplier and you get shortages and grey markets — and not one extra pipe replaced.
[C1.9] Keep the public trust. Cap the aquifer. Do not ban the bottle.
