[P1.1] Water is a fundamental human right, not a private commodity for corporate exploitation. The United Nations has explicitly recognized access to clean water as essential to human life and dignity. Yet private corporations continue to extract municipal water sources at almost no cost, bottle it, and sell it back to the very same communities at exorbitant markups. This practice becomes particularly egregious during droughts, when corporate extraction continues unabated while local residents face severe water restrictions and rationing.
[P1.2] The structural conflict is irreconcilable. Municipal water systems are designed to serve entire communities at cost, ensuring universal access and sustainable management. Bottled water corporations, by contrast, exist to maximize shareholder returns. In California, Nestlé paid just $524 annually to extract 27 million gallons from the San Bernardino National Forest.
[P1.3] In Michigan, Nestlé paid only $200 to extract millions of gallons while Flint residents lacked clean tap water. When water becomes a private asset, profit motives inevitably drive unsustainable extraction, price gouging during shortages, and even the creation of artificial scarcity to drive up margins. Aquifers that take centuries to replenish are treated as disposable under this model.
[P1.4] The economic exploitation is breathtaking. Municipal tap water costs less than one cent per gallon to provide. Bottled water, often sourced from the same public waters, sells for up to ten dollars per gallon—a markup of one thousand times or more. This represents a massive and ongoing transfer of wealth from communities to corporations for a resource that should belong to everyone.
[P1.5] The irony peaks during droughts: residents are fined for overuse while corporations extract under contracts that prioritize profit over conservation.
[P1.6] Without a comprehensive ban, communities remain permanently vulnerable. They become hostages to corporate pricing power, see drought responses undermined by commercial extraction, and surrender democratic control over a life-essential resource. Governments must reclaim water as a public good. The status quo is a fundamental misallocation—transforming a human right into a privatized profit center.

[C1.1] The motion treats every commercial water contract as a Nestlé-style scandal. That is its fatal overbreadth. The real problem is not that a corporation touches water; it is that governments write and enforce bad permits. The California and Michigan examples are permit-pricing failures, not proof that prohibition is the only cure.
[C1.2] A public utility can charge royalties, cap withdrawals, require metering, impose drought triggers, mandate public-trust review, and cancel contracts for breach. Those tools target the abuse while preserving the benefit.
[C1.3] Bottled water is also infrastructure of last resort. When a boil-water notice hits, when a storm knocks out treatment plants, when a household cannot trust its tap, sealed bottled water is a public-health tool. A ban on corporate extraction and trading can make that supply harder to secure precisely when it matters. Municipalities often lack capital and logistics; partnering with a bottler can finance treatment, provide standby capacity, and distribute emergency stocks.
[C1.4] The choice is not “corporate profit or public water.” It is “regulated partnership or prohibition.”
[C1.5] The ban also leaks. If corporations cannot contract for municipal sources and aquifers, extraction migrates to private land and less transparent jurisdictions. That is worse for aquifer protection, because the public loses visibility and leverage. And banning ownership or trading is not a conservation rule; it is expropriation with compensation fights and years of litigation, while the water still flows.
[C1.6] The markup outrage confuses water with the product. Bottled water’s price includes treatment, packaging, transport, retail, and convenience; tap water’s one-cent figure excludes the pipes, pumps, and billing that make it available. A human right to water means universal access and affordability, not a blanket prohibition on every commercial use. Governments should govern water—price it, meter it, cap it, and enforce the public trust.
[C1.7] The motion substitutes a slogan for stewardship.
