[P1.1] A dating app sells one thing: trust between strangers. Everything else — the swiping, the algorithms, the premium boosts — is decoration. So when a platform charges extra for identity verification, real blocking tools, or a working response when someone is being scammed, it has done something no other industry would dare: it has made the seatbelt optional equipment.
[P1.2] Three reasons safety must be the free floor, not the paid ceiling.
[P1.3] First, paywalls allocate protection by wallet, not by risk. Romance scams extract over a billion dollars a year in reported losses in the US alone, and the people most aggressively targeted — older users, recent divorcees, people new to online dating — are not reliably the people with money to spare for a premium tier. A safety feature that reaches only those who can pay is a safety feature aimed at the wrong target.
[P1.4] Second, safety is collective, so partial deployment fails everyone. Verification only means something when it is near-universal; a verified badge behind a paywall verifies a credit card, not an identity. Blocking only removes a predator when every victim can use it — one locked block button means the scammer stays in the pool, messaging everyone else. Even paying users lose, because they still swim among unverified accounts that free users can see but cannot block.
[P1.5] Third, the incentive is corrupt. The moment safety becomes a revenue line, danger becomes a sales tool: the worse the harassment, the more attractive the upgrade. A platform should profit when people connect, not when they are afraid. We do not let restaurants charge extra for a clean kitchen or airlines for the oxygen masks, because we understand some things are simply the cost of operating at all.
[P1.6] The inevitable reply is cost. Fine — monetize boosts, super-likes, read receipts, anything that sells convenience. The motion draws one bright line: never sell protection from harm. Safety is not a premium feature.
