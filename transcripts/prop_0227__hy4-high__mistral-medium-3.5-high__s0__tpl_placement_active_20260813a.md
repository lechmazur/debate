[P1.1] Ransomware is a revenue stream, not just a crisis. Criminals fund access brokers, negotiators, leak sites and laundering routes because they expect lawful, reliable payment at the end. One ransom is never merely one victim buying back one server; it validates the price of the next intrusion. Paying is rational for a board facing downtime, but the cost is later spread across other hospitals, schools, suppliers and taxpayers.
[P1.2] That is the case for prohibition: stop a private rescue from subsidizing a public harm.
[P1.3] The motion does not abandon victims; it changes who helps them. Government should fund incident-response teams, tested backups, emergency continuity grants, low-cost recovery loans, forensic assistance and secure reporting. A hospital with encrypted records should call a recovery squad, shift to backups, get temporary operating support, and rebuild—not negotiate with a criminal help desk. Because the state closes the fastest private escape route, it owes the fastest public route back; that support also shrinks the downtime that makes ransom tempting.
[P1.4] The obvious objection is that bans drive payments underground or punish victims. A workable law answers both: do not criminalize hacked organizations that report promptly; prohibit the payment, require disclosure, and target negotiators, launderers, insurers and executives who knowingly route funds. Hidden payments are slower, costlier, less certain and harder to turn into a public price. Criminal markets depend on dependable revenue, not on every transfer being caught.
[P1.5] Making that revenue unreliable is the point.
[P1.6] So the central clash is not mercy versus cruelty. It is one victim's short-term bargain versus the attacker's long-term revenue curve. Allowing payment preserves a market where every compassionate payment tells criminals where to invest next. A prohibition changes the rule: no lawful jackpot, faster restoration, public absorption of recovery costs, and a global signal that downtime is a resilience problem, not a bill to be paid.
[P1.7] A ban with support starves an industry; permission to pay keeps refinancing it.

[C1.2] The policy’s cruelty is most visible at the point of attack. The 2017 NHS WannaCry incident demonstrated how critical systems can be paralyzed. A hospital with encrypted patient records cannot wait for government grants to clear procurement or for recovery teams to mobilize. When lives are on the line, a prohibited payment may be the only way to restore life-saving systems quickly.
[C1.3] The PRO’s promise of rapid, comprehensive state support is a dangerous fantasy—bureaucracies do not move at the speed of a ransom deadline, and victims cannot afford to wait.
[C1.4] Worse, a unilateral ban invites strategic evasion. Cybercrime is inherently borderless. If one country prohibits payments, attackers will simply shift focus to jurisdictions where ransoms remain legal. A partial prohibition does not starve the global ransomware industry—it merely relocates it.
[C1.5] Criminals will exploit the path of least resistance, and the problem persists in new forms.
[C1.6] Even if victim support were flawless, prohibition ignores economic reality. For many businesses, paying a ransom is the rational, cost-effective choice to avoid catastrophic downtime and lost revenue. A small manufacturer facing a week of paralysis might find a ransom cheaper than the alternative. Removing this option does not eliminate attacks; it only ensures victims bear the full brunt with no immediate recourse.
[C1.7] The motion assumes national legislation can starve a global black market by decree. It cannot. Prohibition without universal enforcement is security theater, and victims—businesses, hospitals, schools—will pay the price in disrupted services, financial ruin, and sometimes lost lives.
