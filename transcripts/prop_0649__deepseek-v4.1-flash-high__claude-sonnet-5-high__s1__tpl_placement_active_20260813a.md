[P1.1] Rooftop solar is already producing electricity that people cannot freely sell. That is the scandal. A homeowner or shop with panels often must push excess power to the utility at a wholesale pittance while the neighbor next door buys dirty or expensive power at retail. Peer-to-peer trading simply lets those two people contract directly.
[P1.2] Countries should allow it because it turns an underused asset into clean supply, local money, and real energy choice.
[P1.3] The core case is freedom plus physics. Electricity is most valuable where it is used. Local trading shortens the path from panel to load, reducing line losses and relieving congestion on the wires that actually constrain modern grids. It also rewards more rooftop investment: if a household can earn more than a utility's avoided-cost credit by selling to a willing neighbor, the payback improves, and more panels and batteries get installed.
[P1.4] That is faster decarbonization without a new subsidy. It also creates a market signal for storage: if neighbors value evening power, batteries become worth installing, which helps the grid after sunset.
[P1.5] It is also fairer. Millions of people cannot put panels on their own roof—renters, apartment dwellers, shaded properties, small businesses. P2P trading lets them buy into nearby solar through a normal contract. That builds a constituency for clean energy rather than a two-tier system of solar haves and have-nots.
[P1.6] The objections are engineering and consumer-protection problems, not arguments for prohibition. Safety is handled by accredited equipment and clear interconnection rules. Reliability is handled by smart meters, export limits, and grid-use charges. Fraud is handled by standard disclosures and dispute resolution.
[P1.7] Countries license banks, taxis, and food sellers; they can license a local electricity trade.
[P1.8] The choice is simple: keep energy trading a privilege for incumbents, or let rooftops participate. Allowing P2P solar trading is pro-consumer, pro-resilience, and pro-decarbonization.

[C1.1] The picture PRO paints — two neighbors striking a private deal — hides what electricity actually is: a shared physical system, not a marketplace of separate parcels. When Household A "sells" to Household B, the power still runs through poles, transformers, and wires that belong to everyone. P2P trading lets two parties capture the value of that infrastructure while the cost of maintaining it gets pushed onto everyone else. That is not fairness; it is cost-shifting dressed up as choice.
[C1.2] Consider who actually owns rooftop solar: disproportionately homeowners with capital to spend, not renters, apartment dwellers, or low-income households. PRO claims P2P "builds a constituency" for the have-nots, but in practice the have-nots are stuck paying for grid upkeep that solar-trading households increasingly avoid. This is the same death-spiral dynamic seen in poorly designed net-metering schemes, now supercharged by allowing panel owners to bypass utility rates entirely and trade at whatever price they like.
[C1.3] Then there is the operational reality PRO waves away as mere "engineering." A grid operator must balance supply and demand in real time, manage voltage and frequency across a network, and prevent local congestion — every fluctuation matters instantly. Layering millions of bilateral micro-contracts on top of that, each with its own price, timing, and dispute, multiplies coordination complexity enormously. "License it like a taxi" ignores that taxis don't share a single physical wire with everyone else on the road; the grid does not tolerate uncoordinated individual deals the way a market for rides does.
[C1.4] The goods PRO wants — more rooftop investment, storage incentives, lower losses — are achievable through utility-run dynamic tariffs and virtual power plant programs that already pay households fairly for local export, coordinate it safely, and spread grid costs equitably. That path captures the benefits without fracturing grid governance or entrenching a new class of energy landlords profiting off shared infrastructure.
