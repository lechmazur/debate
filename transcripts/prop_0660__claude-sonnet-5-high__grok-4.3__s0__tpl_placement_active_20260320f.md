[P1.1] Right-to-disconnect laws rest on a simple, testable mechanism: knowledge work has no natural stopping point. Unlike a factory line that shuts off at the whistle, email and Slack never sleep, and without a legal backstop, the default behavior of firms is to colonize every hour of an employee's life with the expectation of availability. The law's function is not to make people work less—it's to make the boundary explicit and enforceable so that individual workers aren't each forced to unilaterally defy an unspoken norm of always-on responsiveness.
[P1.2] That structural fix is where the welfare gains come from, and they are large and durable, while the productivity losses CON will point to are smaller, temporary, and often illusory.
[P1.3] Start with welfare. Chronic after-hours connectivity is strongly linked to burnout, sleep disruption, and anxiety—not because employees object to occasional urgent requests, but because the mere expectation of being reachable produces a state of low-grade vigilance that never switches off. France's implementation since 2017, and similar frameworks in Spain, Italy, and parts of Latin America, target exactly this: not banning work, but banning the ambient pressure to always be on call. Removing that ambient pressure recovers cognitive bandwidth, restores sleep quality, and reduces the attrition and absenteeism that quietly bleed knowledge firms dry.
[P1.4] Now the productivity side, where CON's intuitive story is weaker than it looks. Knowledge work productivity is not a linear function of hours logged; it's a function of focus, creativity, and judgment, all of which degrade under chronic fatigue. A rested, boundaried workforce produces higher-quality output per hour, catches fewer costly errors, and turns over less—each of which is a productivity gain hiding inside what looks like a welfare policy. Firms that resisted after-hours culture, forced or not, have often reported no measurable output decline and better retention, which is itself a competitive productivity advantage in fields where hiring and training knowledge workers is expensive.
[P1.5] The real comparison, then, is not "welfare versus productivity." It's whether short-run flexibility losses outweigh long-run gains in quality, retention, and sustainable output—and they don't.

Individual workers can't unilaterally opt out of an always-on culture without career risk, which is why the fix has to be collective and legal, not left to private bargaining. And "compressed total output that eventually shows up in slower salary growth" is asserted, not shown—CON offers no mechanism linking rested, lower-turnover teams to shrinking headcount.
[P2.4] C1.4's competitiveness claim needs actual evidence it doesn't have.
