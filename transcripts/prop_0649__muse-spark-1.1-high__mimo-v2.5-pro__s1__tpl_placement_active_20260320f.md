[C1.2] Here's the core problem PRO glosses over: the grid is not just a wire. It's a system that must balance supply and demand in real time across millions of nodes, maintain frequency within tight tolerances, and guarantee power availability at 2 AM on a cloudy January night. When House A sells directly to House B at 15 cents, who maintains the backup generation that B still needs after sunset? Who pays for the distribution upgrades when local voltage swings wildly as clouds roll across a neighborhood of solar traders?
[C1.3] The answer, under P2P, is increasingly "everyone who didn't get a seat at the table"—renters, apartment dwellers, households without the capital to install panels. They remain on the traditional tariff, but the fixed costs of grid maintenance don't shrink just because privileged participants started trading around them. Those costs get socialized upward.
[C1.4] This isn't hypothetical. Every jurisdiction that has seriously experimented with distributed solar at scale—Germany, Australia, California—has discovered that high rooftop penetration without careful coordination creates the "utility death spiral" that PRO dismisses as scaremongering. Fixed grid costs migrate to fewer non-solar customers, raising their bills, which pushes the inequity problem deeper. P2P trading accelerates exactly this dynamic by making rooftop solar more profitable for those who already have it.
[C1.5] PRO promises competition and access. What it delivers, in practice, is a regulatory nightmare: thousands of bilateral contracts, consumer protection gaps, liability questions when trades fail, and a grid operator forced to manage chaos it didn't authorize. Countries should not allow peer-to-peer rooftop trading because the grid is a shared commons, and carving out a private market within it undermines reliability and equity for everyone who can't participate.
