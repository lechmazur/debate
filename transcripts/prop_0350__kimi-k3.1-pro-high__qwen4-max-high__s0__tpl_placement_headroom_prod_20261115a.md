[P1.1] Obesity policy should be judged by whether it changes daily exposure at population scale, not by whether it gives people another reason to try harder. Food choices are repeated, rapid, and constrained. When the cheapest calories are heavily promoted, when checkout aisles and delivery apps push impulse purchases, and when children encounter advertising before they can evaluate it, “personal choice” is already engineered. Policy should target that engineering, not merely consumer willpower.
[P1.2] That is the decisive advantage of food-environment and marketing policies: they alter the default for everyone, even for people who never read a label. Healthier school and hospital procurement, limits on child-directed junk-food marketing, restrictions on predatory price promotions, and reformulation or placement rules work during thousands of ordinary decisions. Campaigns instead ask each person to notice, remember, afford, and apply advice while commerce pushes the other way. Their messages expire; the environment remains.
[P1.3] This is also the fairer approach. Campaign knowledge is easiest to convert into behavior when someone has money, time, nearby healthy retailers, and control over household food. Without those conditions, “eat better” can become an instruction to swim upstream, followed by blame when the current wins. Children make the point sharply: informing parents is useful, but it does not make relentless child-targeted advertising a fair contest.
[P1.4] CON will invoke responsibility and freedom. But restricting manipulative promotion or improving defaults does not ban adults from buying food; it reduces commercial interference with choice. Nor does PRO require abolishing education. The motion concerns focus: information can support structural policy, but should not substitute for it.
[P1.5] The comparison is therefore between exhorting millions of individuals to overcome their surroundings and changing the surroundings once so healthier choices become easier, normal, and less relentlessly contested. Only the second matches the scale, duration, and causes of the problem.
