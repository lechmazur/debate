[P1.1] The central question is not whether climate-exposed cities will eventually face retreat, but whether that retreat will be organized in advance or improvised after the next catastrophe. Repeated flooding, storm surge, wildfire, and heat do not simply damage buildings; they compress impossible decisions into weeks. Families must choose between rebuilding in danger, moving without assistance, or staying put because they cannot afford to leave. That is how chaotic retreat begins.
[P1.2] Mandatory long-term relocation planning changes the timeline. It does not mean emptying cities tomorrow; it means binding hazard maps, phased buyouts, relocation entitlements, land swaps, investment in receiving neighborhoods, and clear rules against rebuilding in the most dangerous zones. Done early, it allows movement to be gradual, voluntary in practice, and socially coherent. A city can relocate a school, a port function, or an entire district over a decade.
[P1.3] It cannot do those things well in the aftermath of a disaster, when trauma, emergency contracts, and political pressure demand rapid rebuilding.
[P1.4] The equity case is decisive. Without mandatory planning, retreat becomes a private privilege. Wealthier households sell, insure, and leave; poorer residents remain in deteriorating hazard zones with rising insurance costs, weakening services, and declining property values. A citywide plan prevents this stranded population by giving every resident a pathway: protected tenure, compensation, and a realistic destination.
[P1.5] It also preserves community ties. Neighborhoods, congregations, and local economies can move together if the destination and funding are arranged years in advance.
[P1.6] Fiscally, early planning is cheaper than perpetual disaster response. Rebuilding roads, utilities, hospitals, and homes in known hazard corridors creates liabilities that compound with every storm. Planning allows infrastructure to be decommissioned or redirected before the next payout.
[P1.7] The alternative is not stability; it is postponement. Waiting guarantees that relocation happens later, under panic, with less money, less trust, and far fewer good options.

From the flood barriers of Rotterdam to the sea walls and storm surge defenses of Tokyo and New York, human ingenuity and in situ adaptation have repeatedly allowed dense cities to coexist safely with water.
[C1.3] Mandatory retreat planning crowds out these viable, protective investments by declaring defeat before the battle is fought.

[C1.5] Finally, binding retreat mandates are built on a dangerous illusion of predictive precision. Long-term climate impacts depend on complex, evolving variables and technological developments that multi-decade municipal master plans cannot reliably forecast. Prematurely locking communities into rigid relocation pipelines destroys social fabric and wastes scarce public capital. We need aggressive, flexible resilience that protects communities where they stand, not bureaucratic mandates that abandon them in advance.
