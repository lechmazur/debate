[P1.1] When animal models are inadequate, the choice is not between infecting people and not infecting people. It is between infecting a few with their informed consent under intensive monitoring, and infecting many without consent through delayed protection and failed products.
[P1.2] That is the moral core of controlled human infection studies. A small group of healthy adults volunteers, is given a carefully manufactured and dose-escalated challenge strain, housed in biocontainment, monitored daily, and when needed given curative therapy that reliably clears the pathogen. What we learn in weeks would otherwise take years of field trials with thousands of participants in endemic settings, each exposed to wild infection with all its chaos and no guaranteed treatment.
[P1.3] This is not hypothetical. Modern vaccines for cholera, typhoid and malaria advanced precisely because challenge models let us screen candidates, identify immune correlates, and discard losers before scaling to large populations. Influenza challenge work taught us how shedding and immunity actually behave, not how a mouse approximates it.
[P1.4] Ethical objections are serious because deliberate infection sounds transgressive. But our ethics already accept deliberate, non-therapeutic bodily risk when three conditions hold: informed, competent consent; risk minimized to proportionate public benefit; and no less risky way to get the knowledge. CHIS satisfy this. Healthy volunteer risk is comparable to live kidney donation or early-phase oncology dosing, both permitted.
[P1.5] The difference is CHIS risk is often lower, transient, and reversible with rescue.
[P1.6] Prohibiting broader use does not protect the vulnerable. It forces us to test unoptimized vaccines in field trials that enroll children in places with weak care, to wait for natural waves to teach us what a challenge unit could teach in a fortnight, and to accept ignorance about diseases where animals simply do not get sick like humans. Regulators should therefore permit them more broadly under rigorous oversight, precisely because animal models are inadequate and ethics demands better evidence, not less.

[C1.1] The proposition asks regulators to expand a powerful and dangerous tool. PRO's opening frames this as a binary: a few consented volunteers, or thousands exposed in field trials. That framing is wrong, and conceding it loses the debate before it begins.
[C1.2] "Animal models are inadequate" is a license to do almost anything. PRO cites cholera, typhoid, malaria, and influenza — mature models refined over decades. But the same logic green-lights challenge studies for pathogens where long-term sequelae are poorly understood, where rescue therapy is uncertain, or where the immune response itself drives pathology. The trigger condition is loose; the oversight is not.
[C1.3] The alternative to CHIS is not "infect thousands in field trials." It is better science: improved organoids, human-challenge-sparing correlates of protection, immunobridging, adaptive Phase 2 designs, and yes, better-resourced animal models. PRO assumes we must pick between two doors. We can build a third.
[C1.4] Informed consent under deliberate infection is weaker than PRO admits. Volunteers are typically young, healthy, and — in commercial CHIS programs — compensated. The transactional structure creates pressures ordinary clinical consent was never designed to absorb. Historical abuses at Willowbrook and elsewhere were not anomalies.
[C1.5] They were what happens when researchers internalize the very frame PRO just offered: small controlled harm justified by large diffuse benefit.
[C1.6] "Rescue therapy reliably clears the pathogen" is empirically false for many proposed CHIS targets. We lack curative regimens for SARS-CoV-2, for several malaria strains in non-immune hosts, and for respiratory viruses now under study. PRO licenses experiments on the assumption medicine can undo them. It often cannot.
[C1.7] Broader permission is not cautious expansion. It is normalization of a practice whose safeguards lag behind its ambitions. Regulators should hold the line until the trigger condition is tight, the alternatives are honestly exhausted, and the rescue claim is something better than hope.
