[P2.1] The definitional objection attacks a caricature. A disclosure rule need not ask sponsors to label a trial simply “negative.” It can objectively require posting every completed or terminated trial relevant to the product, including methods, prespecified endpoints, adverse events, and aggregate findings. A failed primary endpoint alongside a promising secondary endpoint is not a reason for secrecy; it is exactly the mixed result clinicians need to assess. Comprehensive disclosure prevents the reclassification and spin CON fears.
[P2.2] Nor does early termination create an escape hatch. Terminated studies should trigger disclosure of why they stopped and whatever aggregate data were accrued. Once a trial is registered, abandoning it cannot erase its existence. That design changes the incentive from “bury weak evidence” to “either complete the study or publicly explain why you did not.” Patients’ contributions therefore produce knowledge rather than the void CON describes.
[P2.3] The proportionality argument also relies on an implausibly rigid implementation. Making disclosure a condition of access does not require permanently banning a medicine over an accidental late filing. Regulators can provide clear deadlines, notice, and an opportunity to cure. But if a sponsor persistently refuses to reveal evidence material to safety or efficacy, withholding access is entirely proportionate: the country cannot responsibly authorize sales while the evidentiary record remains deliberately incomplete.
[P2.4] Small developers deserve simple templates and support, not a lower standard of truth for their patients.
[P2.5] Existing disclosure systems are not an argument against this rule. They show that registries and standardized reporting are feasible. Posting results before commercialization need not delay scientific review; any delay caused by refusing to upload already-collected findings is controlled by the sponsor.
[P2.6] The decisive comparison is between a manageable compliance duty and distorted medical decisions affecting every later patient. CON protects speed to market even when the evidence guiding that market is selectively incomplete. Access to medicine is valuable only when clinicians can accurately judge what that medicine does.
