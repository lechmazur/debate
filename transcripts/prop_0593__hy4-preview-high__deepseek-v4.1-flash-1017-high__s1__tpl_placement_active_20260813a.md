[P1.1] Global supply chains are where human rights go to hide. A multinational can trace a capacitor’s chemical composition with obsessive precision, yet claim it cannot say whether the cobalt, cotton, or cocoa inside its product was touched by forced or child labour. That asymmetry is not a natural limit; it is a governance choice. Large firms already have the purchasing power, data systems, and leverage to map suppliers.
[P1.2] What they lack, absent a mandate, is a binding reason to do it. Voluntary codes have produced glossy sustainability pages, not reliable knowledge. Because cheating pays, the responsible firm that maps its chain is undercut by the rival that does not.
[P1.3] Standardized, audited reporting fixes the incentive and the information problem at once. Standardization turns disclosure into comparable data: investors, regulators, journalists, and affected communities can benchmark firms instead of parsing incompatible self-assessments. Independent audit does what financial auditing does: it converts management claims into audited statements, deterring the selective storytelling that makes voluntary reports worthless. And the act of preparing an audited report is itself protective—firms cannot report what they never mapped, assessed, or remediated.
[P1.4] Due diligence becomes a management system, not a communications afterthought.
[P1.5] This is proportionate. These are large multinationals. They already submit to rigorous financial audit across borders and can track inputs when cost, quality, or brand risk is at stake. Extending that discipline to human rights is not radical; it is consistent.
[P1.6] Compliance cost is real but modest next to the social cost of unchecked exploitation, and common standards prevent a race to the bottom. Several European states have moved toward mandatory human-rights due diligence, not because disclosure is a panacea, but because opacity is a proven accomplice.
[P1.7] The choice is simple: allow multinational profit to rest on what firms refuse to see, or require them to show their work. Mandatory standardized, audited due-diligence reports are the minimum price of operating at global scale.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a reporting artifact for a remedy. Financial audit works because balance sheets are standardized, bounded, and backed by records. Human-rights due diligence is none of those things. What is the common metric for cobalt mined by artisanal labor, cotton from seasonal farms, and components nested through layers of contractors?
[C1.2] A standardized report does not capture that reality; it compresses it into checkboxes. The firm learns to manage the checkbox.
[C1.3] Worse, “audited” creates false assurance. Human-rights abuses are precisely what firms and suppliers have incentives to hide: unauthorized subcontracting, recruitment fees, retaliation, state coercion. Auditors sample documents and selected sites; they cannot verify the absence of forced labor in a shifting chain. Garment-factory audits are the cautionary tale: certified suppliers have still been found with locked exits, unsafe buildings, and work farmed out to unseen units.
[C1.4] The report would tell regulators that a process exists, not that a worker is safe. That is not transparency; it is reputational laundering.
[C1.5] Publication also changes behavior in the wrong direction. Legal teams minimize disclosures; suppliers produce scripts; “audited” becomes a liability shield. The claim that “firms cannot report what they never mapped” [P1.3] is true but insufficient: they will map the visible and certify away the hidden.
[C1.6] Nor is this proportionate. A global standardized audit regime across sectors and legal systems is a compliance bureaucracy, and its costs fall hardest on suppliers and small producers in poor countries. The biggest firms can absorb it; the weakest links cannot, so sourcing moves to less visible intermediaries.
[C1.7] The choice is not opacity versus reports. It is paper compliance versus direct accountability: liability for harm, worker and community access, targeted import bans, and traceability for specific high-risk commodities. Opacity is an accomplice, but a stamped report is not a witness. Countries should demand substance, not standardized audited narratives.
