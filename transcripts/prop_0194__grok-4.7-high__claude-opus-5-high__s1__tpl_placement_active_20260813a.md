[P1.1] Democracies answer the living and systematically shortchange everyone who comes after them. Electoral cycles reward benefits that arrive before the next vote and costs that can be postponed—emissions locked in for centuries, debt that finances consumption rather than assets, aquifers drawn down, nuclear waste left unresolved. Future people have no ballot, no lobby, and no remedy once the damage compounds. That is not a side effect of bad leaders.
[P1.2] It is what institutions produce when they are accountable only to people alive today.
[P1.3] A future-generations commissioner with binding veto power over a narrow set of major long-horizon decisions corrects that bias without displacing democracy. The veto should trigger only when a choice is hard to reverse, its principal effects fall after current voters are gone, and the downside is catastrophic or compounding: a carbon budget that commits the country to multi-century warming, borrowing that leaves no matching public asset, or approval of wastes hazardous for millennia. Ordinary tax rates, social programs, and annual budgets remain with elected governments.
[P1.4] Binding power is essential. Advisory councils already exist and are waved aside the moment restraint is politically costly. A veto forces the present majority to redesign or abandon a decision that would treat the unborn as an externality. Long, non-renewable terms, published statutory tests for what counts as major and long-horizon, and open impact assessments keep the office from becoming a general policy veto.
[P1.5] Legislatures keep every other lever and can amend the commissioner’s mandate through ordinary constitutional process.
[P1.6] Self-restraint has already been tried. Climate deadlines slip, maintenance is deferred, and debt rises in good years as well as bad. Limited binding authority for those who cannot yet speak is how a democracy stops raiding the future it claims to serve.

[C1.1] Future people have no vote. Grant it. The question is whether the right remedy is handing one unelected official a stop button.
[C1.2] Start with the instrument itself. A veto can only block. It cannot build a grid, fund a levee, or plant a forest. But the deepest betrayals of the future are almost all omissions: the transmission line never built, the maintenance deferred, the reactor never approved.
[C1.3] A commissioner armed with a veto is powerless against every one of those and powerful only against action. That is not a pro-future institution; it is a status-quo machine aimed at the future's expense.
[C1.4] Now picture it working. It is 1975. A future-generations commissioner is told to block approvals of "wastes hazardous for millennia" — my opponent's own trigger [P1.3]. He vetoes the nuclear program.
[C1.5] The utilities build coal instead. Fifty years on, we are living inside that veto, and there is no one to remove, no election to reverse it, because his term was long and non-renewable by design. The mechanism does not make good judgments; it makes one person's contested forecast irreversible. Democracies err constantly too — but they err correctably.
[C1.6] That exposes the dilemma at the heart of the proposal. My opponent says legislatures "can amend the commissioner's mandate through ordinary constitutional process" [P1.5]. Then either the veto is genuinely binding — in which case an unelected official overrides the only people who can be held accountable for the consequences — or it is overridable by the same majority that wanted the policy, in which case it is an advisory council with extra steps and a constitutional fight attached.
[C1.7] And there is a price for the attempt. Nothing radicalises a populist movement faster than an unelected guardian blocking a mandate. The likeliest legacy is not restraint; it is the office abolished and long-horizon governance discredited.
[C1.8] Bind the future the democratic way: statutory carbon budgets, fiscal rules, trust funds, courts enforcing laws that voters actually passed. Commitments that bind because we chose them — not because someone vetoed us.
