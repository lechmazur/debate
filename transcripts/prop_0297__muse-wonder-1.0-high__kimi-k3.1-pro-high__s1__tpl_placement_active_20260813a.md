[P1.1] A statue on a courthouse lawn or central square is not just history — it is honor. It tells everyone who passes who the community still celebrates. Moving a highly controversial monument to a museum ends that endorsement without pretending the past never happened. That distinction is the whole case: relocation removes the privilege, destruction removes the evidence.
[P1.2] In a museum, the same object can be made to tell the truth it was built to hide. A Confederate general can be placed at eye level beside the Jim Crow-era speeches at its unveiling, the names of enslaved people, and photographs of the segregated world it defended. A fallen Lenin or Saddam Hussein figure can be shown toppled, scarred, next to testimony from those who lived under the regime. Context turns propaganda into a primary source.
[P1.3] Destroy it, and you lose the scale, craftsmanship, and intimidation that made it powerful — the very things future students need to see to understand how public art manufactures loyalty.
[P1.4] Destruction is also irreversible in a way relocation is not. Today's majority may be certain, but it cannot bind every future historian, descendant, or victim's family who might want to confront, study, or reinterpret that object. Preservation keeps options open; a smashed statue forecloses them forever. And erasure rarely produces the closure it promises.
[P1.5] It lets defenders claim a sanitized, martyred memory — "they had to destroy it because they couldn't answer it" — while leaving the ideas behind it untouched and harder to refute without the artifact itself.
[P1.6] Relocation is not compromise for its own sake. It is the stronger public judgment: this figure no longer stands over us, but we are confident enough to preserve and explain why it once did, and why it no longer does. That serves victims, students, and skeptics better than an empty absence.

[C1.1] Preservation is not the opposite of erasure. A monument can be destroyed while its history is thoroughly preserved through photographs, casts, fragments, archives, testimony, and an account of why the community removed it. What disappears is the intact object’s monumental force, not the past. PRO’s central distinction—relocation removes honor while destruction removes evidence—is therefore false.
[C1.2] We can retain the evidence without retaining the monument.
[C1.3] Nor is a museum a context-producing machine. Acquisition itself confers importance; display can restore aura, aestheticize propaganda, or create a site of pilgrimage. Labels change, galleries close, objects enter storage, and political control shifts. PRO celebrates preserving “the scale, craftsmanship, and intimidation that made it powerful” [P1.3], but those are precisely the qualities that can continue doing harm.
[C1.4] A contextual label cannot guarantee that every visitor reads domination as condemnation rather than fascination. Meanwhile, the public may inherit indefinite costs of transport, conservation, security, and contested ownership. Why should the people targeted by a monument remain responsible for safeguarding it?
[C1.5] PRO calls destruction uniquely irreversible, but preservation also forecloses choices: it commits future communities to custody of an object they may experience as an enduring threat. Future historians’ possible curiosity should not veto present victims’ democratic judgment. A documented, deliberate destruction can itself be historically instructive—a public repudiation after scrutiny, not a spasmodic attempt to forget. The empty site need not remain an “empty absence”; it can carry testimony, interpretation, or new commemorative art.
[C1.6] Destruction need not manufacture martyrdom either. Intact relics can be far more useful to revival movements than rubble: they can be venerated, reproduced, and demanded back. Museums cannot guarantee permanent control over an object’s political meaning.
[C1.7] Sometimes relocation is sensible, but the motion claims preservation is the better public response. That presumption fails when intact custody prolongs domination, burdens the harmed, or keeps propaganda available for renewed devotion. The public owes future generations an honest record, not perpetual custody of every instrument of intimidation. Memory must be preserved; monuments need not be.

[P2.1] A photograph is not a monument, and a fragment cannot show how the whole dominated a square.

A cast can record a face; it cannot reproduce material, scale, or the craftsmanship that made a regime look permanent. Once the intact object is gone, every claim about its power becomes an assertion others must take on faith — exactly what defenders of the old order want.
[P2.3] The museum objection proves too much. Yes, labels change and display can be done badly. But that is an argument for doing context well, not for surrendering the object. On a plinth outdoors, no label can compete with elevation and honor.
[P2.4] In a museum, the statue can be laid down, placed at eye level, surrounded by victims' testimony, and denied a heroic sightline. Pilgrims can also gather at an empty pedestal or destruction site; removing custody does not remove devotion, it removes our control over how the object is framed.
[P2.5] The reversibility point is decisive, and preservation does not mirror destruction. A preserved monument can later be stored, reframed, or — if a future community still chooses — destroyed. A destroyed monument can never be preserved later. Calling custody a foreclosure confuses an ongoing choice with a final one.
[P2.6] Relocation keeps democracy open across generations; destruction lets one moment permanently veto all later inquiry, including by descendants of victims who may want the artifact as proof, not rumor.
[P2.7] Costs and burden deserve respect, but destruction, documentation, security, and memorializing the empty site also cost money, and they buy less: a record about intimidation without the intimidating object to test it against. The public does not owe a monument honor. It owes itself the strongest evidence of why honor was withdrawn. Museums provide that; rubble asks us to remember on trust.

[C2.5] The reversibility argument confuses theoretical options with public benefit. [C2.6] Meanwhile, descendants of victims are invoked hypothetically, while present victims’ judgment is discounted.
[C2.7] Finally, documentation and site interpretation are needed under either policy, not unique costs of destruction. Relocation adds transport and continuing conservation, security, and governance.
