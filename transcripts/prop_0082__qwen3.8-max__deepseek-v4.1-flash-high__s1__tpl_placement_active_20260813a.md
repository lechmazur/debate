[P1.1] The central question is whether dating apps should be allowed to optimize for attention and revenue when those optimizations corrode the very thing users seek: credible paths to good matches. The answer is no. When swipe mechanics and paid boosts undermine match quality and trust, they should be limited.
[P1.2] Swipe design often turns partner selection into a high-speed, low-effort game. Endless stacks, rapid accept/reject, and variable rewards encourage snap judgments and compulsive checking rather than considered interest. The app gets longer sessions; users get more noise, more ghosting, and more matches that were made under conditions of fatigue or impulse. That is not a neutral marketplace.
[P1.3] It is an incentive structure that rewards volume over compatibility.
[P1.4] Paid visibility boosts make the problem worse. If exposure can be bought, matching becomes less about mutual interest and more about who can afford to leapfrog the queue. A paying user can be pushed past more compatible unpaid profiles, and even the suspicion of that distortion matters. Users begin to wonder whether they are seeing good matches or sponsored ones.
[P1.5] Once that suspicion takes hold, trust collapses: people assume bad faith, disengage, or abandon the platform.
[P1.6] The strongest objection is choice. But choice is thin when the interface is engineered to keep people swiping. A rule that limits engagement-maximizing mechanics is not paternalistic overreach; it is basic consumer protection against designs that profit from frustration. Platforms that genuinely serve users can compete on better matching, clearer consent, and success stories rather than on addictive loops and pay-to-win visibility.
[P1.7] Because dating markets depend on trust, the harms are not private mistakes users can simply avoid. They are systemic: low-quality mechanics drive out serious participants and degrade the network for everyone. The rule is conditional, not paternalistic: when a feature demonstrably worsens matches or trust, it must yield to the product’s core purpose.

[C1.2] Swipe mechanics are not the enemy of good matching; they are a fast, low-cost way to sort a huge pool. Throttling them doesn't create thoughtfulness; it creates friction, smaller active pools, and more dependence on opaque algorithms. A user who swipes quickly can still meet someone compatible. A user forced through endless prompts may simply quit, leaving serious daters with fewer candidates.
[C1.3] Bad behavior — ghosting, harassment, incompatible intentions — is a user and market-composition problem, not proof that the interface caused it.
[C1.4] Paid boosts are even more defensible. They don't force a match; they buy a chance to be seen. For people who are older, less photogenic, or outside the local beauty norm, organic algorithms can bury them. A boost is a rare lever against invisibility.
[C1.5] Ban it and visibility flows even more to those the algorithm already favors. That may increase inequality in dating, not trust.
[C1.6] Trust is better built through transparency, controls, and honest labeling than prohibition. Users can be told "this profile is boosted" and choose. Limits, by contrast, empower platforms to decide which discovery methods are legitimate, freeze out challengers, and call ordinary engagement "harmful" without reliable metrics.
[C1.7] The proposition mistakes correlation for causation. If match quality is poor, the likelier culprits are skewed gender ratios, harassment, and local market conditions. Suppressing swipes and boosts won't fix those; it will make apps less useful and less competitive.
