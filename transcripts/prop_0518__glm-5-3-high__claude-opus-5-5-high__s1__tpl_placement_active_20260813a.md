[P1.1] Noncompete clauses are a tax on mobility, and nearly all workers should be exempt from that tax. The exceptions employers demand — for "key personnel," "highly compensated" roles, or trade secrets — swallow the rule, because employers have every incentive to label ordinary workers as key, ordinary knowledge as secret, and ordinary salaries as highly compensated. The motion is right precisely because it rejects the carve-out game rather than merely narrowing it.
[P1.2] Start with power. A noncompete is a contract term imposed unilaterally at hire, when the worker has no leverage to negotiate it. It converts general skills — the worker's own human capital — into something the employer controls. The worker pays the price twice: lower wages while employed, because outside offers lose their bite, and reduced options after departure.
[P1.3] Economic research on Oregon's and Hawaii's noncompete bans consistently points the same direction: wages rise when noncompetes go, especially for the very workers the carve-outs would exclude from protection. If you exempt "high earners," you exempt exactly the people with the most at stake in salary suppression.
[P1.4] Second, trade secrets do not need noncompetes. We already have trade secret law, nondisclosure agreements, and fiduciary duties for genuine executives. If a company can identify real, protectable information, it can protect it with a targeted NDA. Noncompetes are the blunter, cheaper instrument precisely because they restrain the person rather than the misuse — and employers prefer them because restraining the person also suppresses competition itself.
[P1.5] Third, look at who gets swept in. Sandwich makers, camp counselors, and warehouse workers have all reportedly been bound by noncompetes. That is not a glitch; it is what happens whenever the default permits the clause. Carve-outs don't prevent this — they legitimize the practice and hand employers a roadmap for relabeling jobs into exempt categories.
[P1.6] The burden here matters. Restricting noncompetes restores the common-law norm that people can earn a living with their own skills. Any exception is a state-granted restraint on that liberty, and it should be rare and narrow, defended by evidence — [clipped]

[C1.1] The strongest evidence in PRO's opening proves our case. Oregon's reform, as generally described, restricted noncompetes for hourly and lower-wage workers. Hawaii's targeted the technology sector. Neither was a near-universal ban.
[C1.2] Both were carve-out regimes that drew lines. If those reforms raised wages, PRO has shown that targeted restriction works. It has not shown that we must abolish the tool for everyone.
[C1.3] That is the real clash. Nobody here defends noncompetes for sandwich makers. The question is whether the fix is a blanket ban or a precise one. Precision wins for three reasons.
[C1.4] First, the abuse PRO describes is solvable with bright lines, not labels. PRO says employers will "relabel ordinary workers as key." You cannot relabel a salary. Several U.S. states now void noncompetes below an earnings threshold.
[C1.5] That single rule protects the warehouse worker, the camp counselor, and the retail clerk automatically, with no judgment calls to game. Add mandatory notice, short maximum durations, and a requirement to pay the worker during the restricted period. Then the clause stops being a free tax on mobility. It becomes a priced bargain.
[C1.6] Second, NDAs are not a substitute at the top. Trade secret law punishes misuse after it happens, and misuse is nearly invisible. When a chief engineer walks into a rival with the product roadmap in her head, the old employer cannot see which decisions she shaped. It can sue only after the damage is done, and it must prove what was copied.
[C1.7] A small startup cannot afford that litigation. A narrow noncompete prevents the harm instead of litigating it for years.
[C1.8] Third, banning the tool changes behavior inside firms. Companies that cannot protect investment will share less, train less, and silo knowledge among founders. Without enforceable noncompetes, sellers of businesses also lose value, because buyers cannot trust the seller not to reopen across the street.
[C1.9] So PRO's framing gives us a false choice between the carve-out game and abolition. The better answer is tight, objective exceptions: protection for the many, enforceable bargains for the few.
