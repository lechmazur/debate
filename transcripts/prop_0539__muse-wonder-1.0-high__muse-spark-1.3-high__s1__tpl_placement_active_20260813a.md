[P1.1] Cross-border commercial surrogacy should stay legal only under enforceable standards because without them, the transaction reliably shifts risk onto the two people who cannot bargain it away: the surrogate and the child.
[P1.2] First, exploitation is the default incentive. An agency paid by wealthy foreign clients profits by recruiting a poorer woman quickly, keeping her fee low, and controlling her housing, information, and medical contacts. Consent signed in that setting proves little. Enforceable anti-exploitation rules change the bargaining structure: independent legal counsel in her own language, transparent pay deposited in escrow before pregnancy, no debt or recruitment fees charged to her, freedom to live at home and change doctors, and no contractual penalty for refusing a procedure or for a miscarriage.
[P1.3] Those are checkable conditions for a license, not aspirations.
[P1.4] Second, a child cannot be left legally parentless because adults crossed a border. In unregulated arrangements, the birth country may treat the surrogate as the mother while the home country refuses to recognize the foreign contract, leaving the baby without citizenship, a passport, or a clear caregiver. Intended parents have also walked away after divorce, disability, or a border closure. Standards must therefore require pre-birth determination of parentage, a confirmed citizenship and travel route, and funded contingency care if the commissioning parents default.
[P1.5] A clinic that cannot answer who the parents are and where the child can live should not be allowed to create that child.
[P1.6] Third, health protection must override commercial speed. The pressure to deliver a success story encourages transferring too many embryos, scheduling caesareans for travel convenience, and ending care at delivery. Enforceable standards require evidence-based embryo limits, surrogate-controlled medical consent, comprehensive insurance, and paid postpartum and complication coverage.
[P1.7] A total ban would push demand underground; unconditional legality invites a race to the cheapest jurisdiction. Conditional legality does the work: comply, be audited, and operate — fail, and lose the right to operate at all.

[C1.1] Licensed exploitation is still exploitation, and a passport does not make it ethical.
[C1.2] The promise of enforceable standards for cross-border commercial surrogacy collapses on the word enforceable. The clinic sits in one country, the agency in a second, the money in a third, and the baby is carried home to a fourth. When the surrogate is underpaid, coerced into a caesarean, or abandoned with complications after delivery, which state sends inspectors? Which court seizes the escrow?
[C1.3] We have seen this chase before: tighten rules in Thailand and the business reappears in Nepal, then Cambodia, then elsewhere. Conditional legality does not end the race to the cheapest jurisdiction; it sponsors it, giving clients a list of supposedly clean destinations while brokers hunt the next gap.
[C1.4] Worse, paperwork cannot cure the underlying transaction. A poor woman signing an English contract after a ten-minute meeting with an agency-paid lawyer is not bargaining. Escrow, housing rules, and consent forms dress up a market in which one side rents wombs because poverty leaves few alternatives and the other side shops globally for the lowest price and the weakest protections. Paying fairly for the use of a person does not stop treating her as a use.
[C1.5] And no clinic can guarantee what it promises on parentage. A birth certificate and a pre-birth order mean nothing if the parents' home state does not recognize commercial surrogacy. Then the child waits in legal limbo while states argue over citizenship. No audit prevents intended parents from divorcing, dying, or rejecting a sick child.
[C1.6] If the risk falls on women who cannot enforce their rights and children who cannot consent, the answer is not better forms. It is to stop creating children for cross-border sale.

[P2.1] That enforcement objection destroys the ban it is meant to support. If clinics, agencies, money, and parents sit in different countries, a prohibition has even less grip than a license: there is no escrow to freeze, no accredited clinic to audit, no medical record to demand, and no operator who fears losing access to the legal market. Demand for children does not disappear because a state declares it immoral; it moves to brokers who ask for cash and offer no postpartum care at all.
[P2.2] Conditional legality is enforceable precisely because enforcement happens before pregnancy, at gates states do control. No verified escrow, independent counsel, insurance, and citizenship route — no lawful transfer, no agency payment, and no automatic recognition of parentage when the family returns. A home state need not raid a foreign clinic; it can make noncompliant arrangements commercially useless by denying recognition, blocking local agencies from selling them, and refusing to let funds be marketed or transferred as a legal service.
[P2.3] Their paperwork attack also targets arrangements the motion already outlaws. An agency-paid lawyer after a ten-minute meeting is not independent counsel in the surrogate’s language. A contract that penalizes refusal of a caesarean is not surrogate-controlled consent. Pay quietly diverted by a broker is not money locked in escrow before transfer.
[P2.4] Calling every safeguard a form assumes bad compliance in order to prove compliance cannot work.
[P2.5] Same with parentage. If the home state will not recognize the arrangement, then the confirmed citizenship and travel route required before conception does not exist, so the arrangement is not legal and the embryo is never transferred. Divorce or death cannot be legislated away in any form of parenthood, but a pre-funded contingency bond can stop that tragedy becoming abandonment. A ban prevents neither; it merely ensures no bond was ever required.
[P2.6] The Thailand-to-Nepal shuffle was movement between loopholes; recognition only for compliant clinics removes the prize for finding the next one.
