[P1.1] A supreme court’s authority rests entirely on public trust; it commands neither purse nor sword. Life tenure erodes that trust by turning constitutional governance into an actuarial lottery. Replacing life tenure with long, non-renewable fixed terms—the norm across modern constitutional courts like Germany’s—delivers a transformative gain in democratic legitimacy at virtually zero cost to judicial independence.
[P1.2] Start with democratic legitimacy. Under life tenure, the power to shape the judiciary is distributed by biological accidents and partisan gaming. One elected government may appoint three justices in four years, while another appoints none. Factions respond by selecting ever-younger ideologues to lock in control for forty years, while sitting justices time strategic retirements to friendly administrations.
[P1.3] When a court wields veto power over national laws, allowing its composition to drift decades away from the governed public is fatal to democratic consent. Fixed, staggered terms ensure that every election carries predictable, equal weight in shaping the bench, anchoring the court to sustained democratic consensus rather than random timing.
[P1.4] Now weigh judicial independence. Independence does not require ruling until death; it requires insulation from political retaliation and reappointment pressure. A single, non-renewable term of twelve to eighteen years secures that insulation completely. A justice who cannot be removed and cannot be reappointed has zero incentive to please presidents or parliaments.
[P1.5] Even the residual worry that a retiring justice might angle for a post-court job is easily neutralized through full lifetime pensions and post-service restrictions—and is minimal for jurists finishing a capstone career.
[P1.6] In fact, fixed terms actively protect independence by defusing institutional warfare. When every vacancy is a forty-year prize, confirmations become existential partisan battles, provoking retaliatory threats of court-packing and jurisdiction-stripping. Regularizing appointments lowers the political temperature. Fixed terms trade the illusion of permanent unaccountability for the reality of a legitimate, durable, and fiercely independent court.

[C1.1] Supreme courts are legitimate not because they mirror the electorate but because they can defy it. Their democratic warrant is to enforce constitutional limits against temporary majorities, protect unpopular minorities, and decide according to law even when the public howls. Tying their composition tightly to recent elections does not legitimize that function; it destroys it.
[C1.2] Fixed, staggered terms would turn every vacancy from an exceptional event into a scheduled political prize. Instead of lowering the temperature, predictability raises it. Parties would campaign explicitly on the next two seats, vet candidates for youth and loyalty rather than judgment, and treat justices as their administration's delegation with an expiration date. What the other side calls equal influence is really guaranteed politicization — the court becomes a second legislature on a time delay.
[C1.3] Regular turnover does not anchor courts to lasting consensus; it chains them to short-term swings.
[C1.4] And the cost to independence is severe, not trivial. A justice with five years left knows exactly which president will name a successor, which media will define a legacy, which university, law firm, or political movement offers a second career. Lifetime pensions do not cure ambition. Lame-duck justices get discounted while they still sit, and near the end face overwhelming temptation to time rulings, dissents, and retirements to please future patrons.
[C1.5] Life tenure's blunt virtue is that it breaks that calculation: no next master to impress.
[C1.6] Worst of all, fixed terms punish precisely the courage we need most. Landmark protections for speech, criminal defendants, or racial minorities were unpopular when decided and required judges who did not need to face voters again. Shortening their horizon trades durable courage for rotating responsiveness.
[C1.7] That is a terrible bargain: an illusion of democratic control purchased with the reality of a more partisan, more timid, less independent court.

[C2.1] My opponent claims my warnings about youth-vetting and timed retirements actually indict life tenure. On the narrow mechanics, he is half-right — fixed terms do remove the incentive to appoint a 40-year-old to hold a seat for forty years.
[C2.2] But removing one pathology by scheduling the prize does not depoliticize it; it nationalizes it. When a vacancy arrives like clockwork every two years, every presidential campaign becomes an explicit campaign for those two seats, every Senate fight becomes a referendum on the future majority. Parties will no longer vet for youth, they will vet even harder for predictable loyalty, because they know exactly what window that loyalist will control. That is not less politicization, it is guaranteed politicization.
[C2.3] Nor do staggered terms demand “sustained consensus.” Do the arithmetic: with nine justices on eighteen-year terms, a two-term president appoints four justices outright — nearly half the court — and two successive same-party presidents remake a supermajority in a decade. One lucky electoral streak still captures the bench, only now each capture is legitimated as democratic rotation. Short-term swings do not disappear; they are installed on schedule.
[C2.4] The European example proves the opposite of what is claimed. Those courts can be bold because vacancies are split among rival institutions and typically require supermajorities, forcing compromise. Fixed terms alone, grafted onto winner-take-all presidential appointment, do not import that consensus; they just regularize spoils.
[C2.5] Finally, the “capstone with full pension” is fantasy. You cannot realistically bar a former justice from books, media, universities, arbitration, or political leadership, and pensions do not cure ambition for influence. Knowing the exact end date tells every sitting justice who will judge a legacy and who might hire it next.
[C2.6] The trade is therefore not real crisis for speculative worry. It is random politicization for certain, scheduled politicization — and that damages independence more than it helps legitimacy.
