[P1.1] The central question is whether we should bet population health on individual choices or on changing the products themselves. Reformulation mandates win because they work automatically, universally, and immediately—while labeling and education campaigns fail the very people they most need to reach.
[P1.2] Consider how people actually make food decisions. Most purchases happen in seconds, under time pressure, often with children in tow or after exhausting workdays. Even well-designed labels get ignored. Education campaigns assume rational actors with bandwidth to process information, motivation to change, and resources to act on what they learn.
[P1.3] That assumption systematically fails low-income populations—who face the highest burden of diet-related disease and the least capacity to act on nutritional guidance.
[P1.4] Reformulation bypasses all of that. When salt caps force manufacturers to reduce sodium across their product lines, every single consumer benefits regardless of whether they've seen a health campaign or can interpret a nutrition label. This is why iodized salt virtually eliminated goiter, why folic acid fortification reduced neural tube defects, and why trans fat bans reduced cardiovascular disease—structural interventions that required zero behavior change from individuals.
[P1.5] Labeling and education have had decades to prove themselves. Front-of-pack warning labels exist in country after country, yet ultra-processed food consumption keeps rising. Why? Because the food industry spends billions marketing these products, engineering hyperpalatability, and exploiting cognitive vulnerabilities that no label can overcome.
[P1.6] Education fights an uphill battle against that machine.
[P1.7] The health impact comparison isn't close. Reformulation delivers guaranteed reductions in harmful ingredients across entire populations. Labeling delivers marginal improvements among the already-health-conscious—people who were going to make better choices anyway. If we care about population health, especially for those least able to navigate complex nutritional landscapes, mandates don't just outperform voluntary approaches—they're the only serious answer.
