[P1.1] Food-delivery apps should not be designed like a casino cashier: technically offering cheaper choices while arranging the screen so the costliest option feels like the normal one. Defaults matter. Most users are ordering while hungry, busy, or distracted; they do not run a full cost-benefit analysis through service fees, delivery fees, menu markups, priority charges, and tips. When the default path is delivery at the highest fee tier, the app is not merely displaying a service.
[P1.2] It is steering people into avoidable spending.
[P1.3] The proposition is modest: default users to pickup or lower-fee options more often. It does not ban delivery, remove convenience, or stop someone from paying for speed when they truly value it. It simply says the first, easiest path should more often be the financially efficient one, especially when the restaurant is nearby, pickup is available, or slower/lower-fee delivery would arrive within a reasonable time. A user who wants premium delivery can still choose it.
[P1.4] The difference is that consent becomes more deliberate.
[P1.5] This also protects restaurants and consumers at the same time. Delivery apps already sit between a restaurant and its customer, often adding commissions, markups, and fees that make the meal more expensive without making the food better. Steering toward pickup can return traffic to the restaurant, reduce pressure on margins, and avoid training customers to see inflated app prices as normal. Lower-fee defaults similarly keep the platform useful without turning every ordinary meal into a luxury transaction.
[P1.6] The likely CON answer is “consumer choice.” But choice is exactly why default design matters. A manipulated choice architecture is not neutral just because an alternative exists three taps away. If the app knows how to highlight “fastest delivery,” it can also highlight “save money by picking up” or “lower fee, longer wait.” Transparency is not anti-consumer; it is pro-consumer.
[P1.7] The fair rule is simple: platforms should make the cheaper reasonable option easier to see and easier to select. Expensive fulfillment should be an available choice, not the quietly privileged path.
