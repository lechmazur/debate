[P1.1] Loneliness on campus is not a software problem. It is a presence problem. And the order in which a university spends its scarce dollars determines which problem it is actually solving.
[P1.2] Start with the basic mechanism. Belonging is built by repeated, unplanned, embodied contact with the same people—the chess club that meets every Thursday, the lounge where you keep bumping into the same three sophomores, the a cappella group that argues about arrangements until midnight. Decades of social research on friendship formation point to the same ingredients: proximity, repetition, and shared activity. In-person clubs, common spaces, and recurring events are precisely the delivery system for those ingredients.
[P1.3] A wellness app is not.
[P1.4] Second, consider opportunity cost honestly. University budgets are zero-sum in any given year. A six-figure license for a digital engagement platform is a student lounge not renovated, a dozen club budgets not funded, a programming coordinator not hired. The in-person option produces compounding social infrastructure—furniture, traditions, student leaders trained to run things—that persists after the check is cashed.
[P1.5] The platform produces a dashboard and a renewal fee.
[P1.6] Third, look at what students actually do with digital wellness tools. Engagement is typically shallow and front-loaded: high download numbers, steep drop-off, modest behavior change. Meanwhile, the students who most need connection—first-generation, international, commuters, the quietly struggling—are exactly the ones a push notification cannot reach into a dorm room and pull out. A weekly free-dinner club can.
[P1.7] A common kitchen open until 1 a.m. can.
[P1.8] Fourth, this is a sequencing claim, not a ban. Fund the physical and social substrate first; layer digital tools on top once that base exists. Reversing the order is like buying a fitness tracker before you own shoes.
[P1.9] The proposition asks where the first dollar should go. It should go where humans actually become less lonely: into rooms, into rituals, into each other's company.
