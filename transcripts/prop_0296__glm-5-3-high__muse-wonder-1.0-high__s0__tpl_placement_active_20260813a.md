[P1.1] Public honors are not history lessons; they are value statements. When a government puts a name on a school, a street, or a paid holiday, it is not neutrally recording the past—it is telling every citizen, including children who walk through those doors daily, "we celebrate this person." That distinction decides this debate, because the question is not whether we remember colonizers, slaveholders, and dictators, but whether the state should keep honoring them.
[P1.2] The proposition contains its own safeguards, and they matter. It applies only to names that *primarily* honor figures whose central legacy is conquest, bondage, or tyranny. A founder who owned slaves but built constitutional democracy has a mixed legacy; Leopold II, who turned the Congo into a mutilation economy, does not. This is not erasure of ambiguity—it is triage of the indefensible.
[P1.3] Three arguments carry the case.
[P1.4] First, names do work. A child attending a school named for a slaveholder absorbs a message about whose dignity counts. Germany understood this after 1945: it did not preserve Hitler Streets for "continuity," and few would argue it should have. If continuity cannot justify honoring the architects of the Holocaust, the principle is conceded—the only question is threshold, and the motion's "primarily honor" clause sets a reasonable one.
[P1.5] Second, continuity cuts both ways. The names themselves were often imposed, frequently without consent of the communities that must live under them—colonial street names in Algiers, Congo, or across the American South under Jim Crow. Renaming is not a rupture with history; in many places it is the restoration of it. The status quo is not neutral ground; it is a choice, continuously renewed, to keep spending public honor on figures who violated the public.
[P1.6] Third, history survives renaming better than it survives de facto celebration. We remember Caligula vividly without a national holiday. Plaques, museums, and honest curricula teach; honorifics do not. The claim that remembrance requires celebration confuses education with veneration—and it is veneration we are voting on.
[P1.7] The state's honor should go to the worthy. Rename.

[C1.1] Keeping a name is not endorsement. A street name becomes an address, a school name becomes generations of students and teachers, a holiday becomes rituals families built around it. Its present meaning is layered, not frozen at the moment of naming. Renaming therefore does more than withdraw praise; it disrupts a shared civic reference that innocent people now own.
[C1.2] The proposition turns on “primarily honor,” but offers no stable test for it. Primarily by original intent, by worst act, by modern perception, by textbook emphasis? Colonizer, slaveholder, and dictator describe much of pre-modern statecraft. That vagueness invites each new government to re-litigate founders, explorers, generals, and reformers with mixed legacies, renaming in cycles as power changes.
[C1.3] Continuity matters precisely because public names should not become the ruling party’s moral scoreboard.
[C1.4] Context also teaches better than substitution. A retained name with honest plaques, curricula, memorials to victims, and public debate forces a society to confront what it inherited. A clean new name lets it congratulate itself and move on, while the history becomes easier to ignore. Germany after a criminal regime collapsed within living memory is an exception of regime change, not a model for routinely purging centuries-old place names.
[C1.5] There is a democratic and practical cost as well. Renaming streets imposes new addresses, documents, signage, and business costs on residents who may not share officials’ judgment; renaming schools severs alumni and neighborhood identity; renaming holidays discards traditions that for many communities have grown into celebrations of their own survival, not of conquest. Those burdens fall on ordinary people, not on the dead men supposedly being punished.
[C1.6] A plural society should add truth, counter-memorials, and context, reserving renaming for local, exceptional, genuinely consensual cases. A blanket instruction to rename mistakes erasing a label for reckoning with history.
