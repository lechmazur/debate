[P1.1] Consent is a gatekeeper, not a license. A transaction can be freely agreed to and still be exploitative, deceptive, or harmful. I want to show why in three short steps.
[P1.2] First, consent answers only one moral question: did the agent agree? Ethics asks three. Were the parties comparably informed? Did they bargain under comparable power?
[P1.3] And does the deal itself impose unjustified harms on others? A "yes" to the first leaves the other two untouched.
[P1.4] Consider payday lending. A borrower signs, walks out with cash, and consents in any legal sense. Yet the structure — triple-digit interest, automatic rollover into new principal, repayment schedules engineered to last as long as possible — is built to extract maximum value from someone in distress. The signature is real.
[P1.5] The deal is predatory. The lender's business model depends on consent being meaningless as a moral safeguard.
[P1.6] Second, consent is shaped by background conditions that a handshake cannot cure. A farmer facing foreclosure will "agree" to terms a wealthy investor would never accept. Markets that recruit from desperate populations — surrogacy, organ sales, off-the-books domestic work — generate consent under severe duress. We rightly hesitate to call these ethical even when no one is coerced at the point of signature.
[P1.7] The choice is genuine and the choice is hollow; those are not the same thing.
[P1.8] Third, transactions have externalities the two parties cannot absorb. A buyer and seller can both consent to an informed, power-balanced trade in weapons for an abusive regime, or in confidential medical data sold to insurers who will then deny coverage. The deal is consensual; the harm falls on people not at the table. Consent among the parties has nothing to say about that.
[P1.9] Three failures, then: consent ignores the content of the deal, the conditions of choice, and the effects on outsiders. An ethical transaction requires fair terms, fair choice, and fair consequences. Consent is the floor, not the ceiling.

[C1.1] My opponent’s case only works if consent means a signature. It does not. Moral consent is competent, informed, voluntary, and bounded by what the parties may legitimately do. On that standard the proposition collapses: the three tests my opponent adds are either inside genuine consent or separate duties that do not show consent is insufficient.
[C1.2] Deception, fraud, incapacity, and coercion do not produce valid consent; they produce its appearance. A payday borrower misled about rollover terms has not given the consent that matters. But the borrower who reads the terms, understands them, and takes the loan because the alternatives are worse has consented. Her poverty is an injustice, not a defect in her choice.
[C1.3] Demanding “comparable power” does not protect the poor; it erases their only option and hands the decision to outsiders. A hard bargain between unequal parties is not automatically unethical; it becomes unethical when one side is tricked or forced, and those are failures of consent, not proof that consent is too weak.
[C1.4] Externalities make the same point. If a deal harms people not at the table, then not everyone affected has consented; their rights are violated. That is not a second criterion bolted onto consent—it is the same requirement applied to all affected persons. A seller who knowingly supplies an abusive regime is culpable for participating in murder, not because consent was merely insufficient.
[C1.5] The wrong is the rights-violation; no fair terms between buyer and seller would cure it.
[C1.6] So strip away defective consent and independent wrongs, and my opponent is left with competent adults making a voluntary, informed choice. For the ethical quality of a transaction, that is enough. To say otherwise is not to add fairness; it is to give third parties a veto over other people’s lives. That is the line between respect and paternalism.

[C2.1] The disagreement is narrower than my opponent pretends. We both agree fraud, incapacity, coercion, and rights-violations invalidate or constrain a deal. The live question is this: after a competent adult gives informed, voluntary consent, and no one’s rights are violated, is there still a further moral test that the transaction itself must pass? I say no. My opponent keeps answering a different question.
[C2.2] Take the payday borrower who reads the disclosed rate and rollover terms and signs because the alternatives are worse. Her consent is real. Her poverty is an injustice, but it is an injustice in the background conditions, not a defect inside the agreement. If the loan is genuinely her best available option, refusing it on the ground that her choice is “hollow” does not protect her; it removes the option and leaves her worse off.
[C2.3] The moral response is to attack poverty, expand alternatives, and regulate deceptive or usurious lending. It is not to say that her consent failed. When the lender deceives or traps her, consent is invalid. When the market is exploitative, that is a reason for law and redistribution.
[C2.4] Neither shows that informed consent between the parties was insufficient.
[C2.5] The same move appears in the externalities point. Buyer and seller cannot consent to violate the rights of people not at the table; but that is because rights set the boundary of permissible action, not because consent needs an extra ingredient. A seller who knowingly supplies an abusive regime commits an independent wrong. Remove the wrong, and the bilateral transaction stands or falls on consent.
[C2.6] So the opponent’s three failures collapse into two: defective consent and independent wrongdoing. Once those are removed, competent adults making a voluntary, informed choice have done what transactional ethics requires. The proposition is too strong: consent alone is enough, not because it cures injustice, but because injustice and third-party harm are separate moral facts.
