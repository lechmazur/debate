[C1.1] The proposition asks us to freeze automotive interface design around one technology — the button — as though safety were a property of hardware rather than a property of design. It isn't. A dashboard covered in identical rocker switches is just as dangerous as a bad touchscreen menu, because tactile memory only works when there are few, well-spaced, distinct controls. Modern cars have dozens of functions competing for space.
[C1.2] Force all of them into physical switches and you get exactly what older cars had: rows of look-alike buttons that drivers still have to glance at and hunt through. The "muscle memory" story only works in a demo video with three big knobs, not in a real cabin with forty functions.
[C1.3] The actual safety lever is total interface quality — response time, predictability, and how much attention a task consumes — not whether the physical substrate is plastic or glass. And the best modern implementations beat pure mechanical buttons on that metric. Steering-wheel-mounted controls let a driver toggle climate or hazards with a thumb, without moving hands or eyes at all — a lower distraction cost than reaching down to a center-console knob. Voice commands let a driver say "defrost" without touching anything.
[C1.4] These are not hypothetical: they already exist in production vehicles precisely because engineers found they reduce eyes-off-road time more than legacy switches do.
[C1.5] Mandating "keep physical buttons for core functions" locks manufacturers out of these superior paths. It says: no matter how good your voice system or steering-wheel control becomes, you must also maintain a redundant, dedicated mechanical switch, adding cost, complexity, and failure points — physical buttons can jam, wear out, or fail silently, with no way to patch them. A defective touchscreen menu can be fixed with a software update overnight; a defective physical switch needs a recall.
[C1.6] The real question isn't button versus screen. It's whether we lock in one 1990s solution or let engineers keep improving how drivers reach safety functions at all. That flexibility is what CON is defending.
