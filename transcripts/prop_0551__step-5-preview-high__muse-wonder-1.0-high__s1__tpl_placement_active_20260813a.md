[P1.1] Water and electricity became universal services not because they were pleasant conveniences, but because modern life became impossible without them. High-quality internet has reached the same point. School portals, telehealth appointments, job applications, benefit claims, banking, court filings, and emergency information now run through the network. When society migrates these functions online, access stops being a consumer preference and becomes a condition of equal citizenship.
[P1.2] The case for legal enforcement is stronger here, not weaker, because ordinary markets will not close the gap on their own. Broadband networks are expensive in sparse or low-income areas and highly profitable in dense ones. Left alone, providers follow revenue, not need. A legally enforceable universal-service obligation does not require state ownership or eliminate competition; it sets a floor—availability, minimum quality, affordability support, and a remedy when service fails.
[P1.3] That is the same basic structure used for electricity, telephony, and water: essential infrastructure is publicly accountable even when privately operated.
[P1.4] A concrete case makes the stakes vivid. A rural household may have intermittent mobile data but no reliable home connection. A child cannot upload coursework, a parent cannot attend a telehealth appointment, and a worker cannot complete a required video verification. If electricity failed, we would treat it as an urgent service problem.
[P1.5] If broadband fails, the law often treats it as bad luck.
[P1.6] That asymmetry is the decisive clash. We cannot require people to learn, work, heal, and govern themselves through a network while leaving that network legally optional. If access is essential, it should be enforceable.

[C1.1] Being important is not the same as being suitable for a legally enforceable universal service, and that distinction defeats this proposition.
[C1.2] Water and electricity are definable, stable necessities: safe water and a kilowatt deliver the same function year after year. ‘High-quality internet’ does not. Is it 25 megabits or 300? Fibre to the home, or reliable 5G where trenching is ruinous? Low latency for telemedicine, or upload speed for schoolwork?
[C1.3] A legal right needs a precise floor courts can enforce, but any floor written today will be inadequate tomorrow or gold-plated beyond need. The statute becomes either symbolic — a right that cannot be specified — or rigid, locking money into yesterday’s technology while satellite and fixed wireless move on.
[C1.4] That rigidity carries a brutal opportunity cost. Connecting the last remote households is the most expensive part of any network, often many times the cost per user of serving towns. Making that an enforceable entitlement for every address does not create resources; it legally prioritises broadband over claims many countries still have not met: clean water, sewerage, reliable power, clinics and roads. It can also be regressive.
[C1.5] Litigation and compliance are easiest for literate, urban, better-connected claimants, and providers can pass costs through in prices. The poorest off-grid village does not win fibre because a court declares a right; it waits while funds meet obligations for households closer to the network.
[C1.6] Providers respond to mandates, penalties and affordability controls by investing less in risky areas, or charging elsewhere. Telephone and electricity universal service rested on regulated monopolies that could cross-subsidise internally. Broadband is competitive and fast-changing, where that model collapses.
[C1.7] None of this denies digital exclusion. It argues for targeted subsidies, school and clinic connectivity, spectrum conditions and shared access hubs — tools that can be adjusted and budgeted. A justiciable universal right turns a flexible policy problem into an open-ended legal claim: the wrong instrument, at the wrong cost.

[C2.2] If courts defer to regulators, the right adds little beyond regulation except lawsuits about deference.

Calling both “adaptive” [P2.2] hides the difference between updating a safety parameter and repeatedly redefining the product.
[C2.4] Financing moves the cost rather than removing it [P2.3]. Telecom levies and provider obligations are paid by customers, including low-income users. Spectrum revenue used for broadband is unavailable for clinics or water. Once enforceable, that spending acquires legal priority instead of being weighed openly against other needs.
[C2.5] Universality also dictates sequence badly: equal entitlement pushes money toward the costliest final households even when shared access, schools, or mobile coverage would help more poor people sooner.
[C2.6] Voice universal service is no bridge [P2.7]. A narrow, stable telephone line could be cross-funded; an ever-rising, high-capacity household standard cannot without a large, permanent tax on connectivity itself.
[C2.7] Connecting a clinic the state runs is finite and monitorable [P2.5]. Guaranteeing every private household an enforceable quality of connection is open-ended. Discretion is not evasion here; it lets governments redirect resources as needs and technology change. Essential use justifies strong targeted policy; it does not convert each home connection into a justiciable utility right.

[C5.1] The choice is not between connecting people and abandoning them. It is between a duty government can keep, and a right it can only declare.
[C5.2] That distinction has survived every attempt to blur it. A broadband right must stop either at the premises, with speed, latency and uptime figures that date, or at the real-world outcome—a completed telehealth call or hearing—that no access provider controls because devices, home Wi-Fi, platforms and congestion intervene. The first version is rigid; the second is unenforceable. Calling the standard “functional” does not bridge that gap.
[C5.3] The clinic and the courtroom prove the workable alternative. There the state controls the premises, equipment and proceeding, so it can owe a precise remedy: connect the facility, postpone the hearing, provide another route to participate. Extending that guarantee to every private home does not make the home more important; it makes the obligation less controllable, more open-ended, and harder to sequence against water, power and care.
[C5.4] Essential use deserves serious public duties. But a universal, justiciable broadband entitlement converts urgency into litigation and privilege over other needs. Protect the functions people must reach—targeted, funded and enforceable—rather than declaring a household right the state cannot define or deliver.
