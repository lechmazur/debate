[P1.1] The entire legal architecture of safe-third-country rules rests on a substitution. A state that would otherwise have to hear an asylum claim on its own soil instead says: someone else will hear it, and that someone else is safe enough that this substitution does no harm. That claim is only true if the substitution is real — not a label, not a diplomatic fiction, but an actual functioning system where the person can survive, correct errors, and stay with their family while their claim is decided.
[P1.2] Strip out enforceable work rights, appeal rights, and family unity, and what you have left is not a "safe" country. You have a warehouse.
[P1.3] Take work rights first. An asylum seeker barred from lawful employment for months or years does not disappear into safety; they disappear into informal labor, exploitation, and dependence on smugglers or traffickers to survive. Removing someone into that condition is not risk-free — it manufactures a new harm, and the removing state is morally and causally responsible for it.
[P1.4] Take appeal rights. First-instance asylum decisions are wrong with some regularity everywhere — case backlogs, inconsistent adjudicators, language barriers. Without enforceable appeal, one bad decision becomes a final deportation to danger, with zero correction mechanism. That's not a technicality; it's the difference between an asylum system and a lottery with a one-way door.
[P1.5] Take family unity. Separating spouses, parents, and children as a byproduct of transfer isn't a neutral administrative cost — it's coercive, and it's often used precisely as a deterrent, pressuring people to abandon legitimate claims. A "safe" country that inflicts that pressure isn't safe in any meaningful sense.
[P1.6] Notice the shape of my claim: I don't need to show every third-country deal is bad. I only need the conditional — enforceability of these three rights is the threshold that makes the fiction of "safety" honest. Without it, "safe third country" is just a euphemism for outsourced refoulement.
