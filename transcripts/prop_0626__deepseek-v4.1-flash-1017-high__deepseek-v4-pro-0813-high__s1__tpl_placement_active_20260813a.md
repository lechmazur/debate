[P1.1] The line that matters is not whether a machine can be useful, precise, or even defensive. It is whether a human being decides that a specific person or object may be killed. Once a system selects and engages targets without meaningful human control, the state has crossed from automation in service of judgment to automation in place of judgment—and that is what should be banned.
[P1.2] First, accountability collapses. A machine cannot be blamed, punished, or instructed to exercise mercy. If an autonomous system kills civilians because its sensors misclassify a wedding convoy or because its learned behavior fails in an unfamiliar environment, the chain of responsibility becomes a fog. The commander who deployed it may not know how it chose; the programmer may never have anticipated the scenario; the operator may have had no chance to intervene.
[P1.3] Law of armed conflict depends on someone being able to say “no” before lethal force is used. A ban preserves that human veto.
[P1.4] Second, these systems are inherently unpredictable in the environments where they would matter most: cities, contested airspace, degraded communications, crowds. Precision in a test range is not meaningful control in war. And when several states deploy them, machine-speed reactions create escalation risks that no human tempo can safely manage. A system that fires before anyone can clarify a radar ghost or a misidentified target turns a mistake into a crisis.
[P1.5] Third, a ban is the only rule that matches the stakes. We do not need to prohibit autonomy for navigation, logistics, or target identification that remains under human supervision. We prohibit the final step: autonomous selection and engagement. That line is clear enough to stigmatize, verify in doctrine, and enforce in procurement.
[P1.6] Waiting for a catastrophe to write the rules is not precaution; it is abdication. The burden should be on any state claiming it can delegate killing to a machine and still preserve responsibility, discrimination, and restraint. That burden cannot be met.

[C1.1] PRO’s case rests on a false dilemma: either a human pulls the trigger, or no human judgment exists. In practice, human judgment can be moved to mission design, target categories, no-strike lists, geographic limits, and commander authorization. That is still meaningful control—often more meaningful than a tired operator rubber-stamping a screen prompt during a saturation attack. A blanket ban on the final selection step would prohibit the very systems best able to enforce those constraints consistently.
[C1.2] The human veto PRO celebrates is not the safeguard they assume. Humans misclassify targets, panic, retaliate, or freeze. A system that selects within lawful preset rules, using multiple sensors and strict criteria, can be more restrained than a frightened soldier under fire. The choice is not human versus machine; it is which forms of control produce fewer unlawful deaths.
[C1.3] PRO has not shown that the trigger-pull, in particular, is what keeps us safe.
[C1.4] Accountability does not disappear. The commander who deploys a system, defines its operating area, sets its engagement rules, and accepts the risk remains responsible. Engineers and testers remain accountable for certification. A ban is not necessary for accountability; it may weaken accountability by driving autonomous use outside ordinary military discipline and legal review.
[C1.5] The proposed line is also less clear than [P1.5] claims. “Meaningful human control” is politically elastic. A human can be given only seconds to approve a machine’s decision, making the control meaningless; a fast defensive system can be called fully autonomous even when it operates under strict human-set rules. That ambiguity invites weaponized definitions, not compliance.
[C1.6] Finally, a ban is strategically perverse. It binds transparent, law-governed states while doing little to constrain states or groups determined to use autonomy covertly. Autonomy is mostly software and hard to verify remotely. Declaratory bans widen the gap between those who disarm and those who do not—the very escalation danger [P1.4] warns about.
[C1.7] The better response is enforceable use restrictions, testing, and command responsibility, not a deployment ban.
