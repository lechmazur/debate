[P1.1] Public pension systems were designed as insurance against outliving one's ability to work, not to fund a three-decade retirement at the expense of a shrinking workforce. When retirement ages stay frozen while life expectancy climbs, the math breaks. Fixing that gap mainly through higher payroll taxes is economically destructive and generationally unjust; indexing retirement ages to longevity is the only sustainable remedy.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanics of demographic aging. If states rely mainly on payroll taxes, every extra year of average longevity demands a steeper tax hike on a smaller pool of active workers. Because payroll taxes are a direct levy on employment, ratcheting them up depresses take-home pay, raises hiring costs, and slows overall growth—eroding the very wage base pensions depend upon. In contrast, linking the retirement age to life expectancy delivers a double fiscal dividend: people contribute to the economy and tax base for longer while keeping the expected duration of payouts stable.
[P1.3] It balances both sides of the ledger without punishing work.
[P1.4] More importantly, payroll tax hikes inflict severe intergenerational unfairness. Payroll taxes fall squarely on younger and middle-aged wage earners—leaving passive wealth untouched—right when those households are struggling to buy homes and raise children. Confiscating an ever-larger share of young workers' paychecks so healthier older cohorts can spend a growing fraction of adulthood outside the workforce breaks the generational compact. Indexing the retirement age does not shrink retirement; it simply preserves a consistent ratio of working years to retirement years.
[P1.5] Of course, physical aging varies by occupation, and a roofer faces harder limits than a lawyer. Yet the answer to arduous labor is targeted disability insurance and early-retirement pathways for physically demanding trades—not freezing the baseline retirement age for millions of healthy professionals and handing the crushing tax bill to their children.

Compare the two mechanisms honestly: one takes from the shortest-lived to spare the best-paid; the other does the reverse.
[C1.7] That is the clash, and arithmetic does not settle it.
