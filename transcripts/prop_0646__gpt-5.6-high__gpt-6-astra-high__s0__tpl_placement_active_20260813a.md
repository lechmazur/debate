[P1.1] Elder care is not an ordinary market. Residents may be cognitively impaired, physically dependent, or unable to move without serious disruption. Families often choose under crisis conditions and cannot directly observe whether a facility has enough nurses, properly manages medication, or prevents neglect. By the time poor quality becomes visible, the harm may be irreversible.
[P1.2] Government therefore has a duty to regulate not only what facilities do, but ownership structures that predictably magnify these risks.
[P1.3] Private equity creates three especially dangerous incentives. First, acquisitions are often financed with substantial debt, leaving facilities responsible for interest and rent before resident care. Second, owners may extract value through management fees, dividends, or transactions with affiliated property companies. A sale-and-leaseback can produce immediate cash for investors while saddling the care provider with permanent rent obligations.
[P1.4] Third, funds commonly seek returns within a limited investment horizon. Yet elder care requires patient spending on staffing, training, maintenance, and resilience—investments whose benefits emerge over years. When budgets tighten, the easiest cost to cut is labor, even though attentive human care is the core product.
[P1.5] Ordinary consumer choice cannot reliably correct this. A frail resident cannot simply “shop elsewhere” after neglect begins; beds may be scarce, records opaque, and relocation itself harmful. Much of the sector is also supported by public money, so governments are entitled to prevent public funds from being diverted through opaque financial engineering.
[P1.6] Restriction need not mean prohibition. Governments can require ownership disclosure, scrutinize leveraged takeovers, limit excessive dividends and related-party payments, mandate continuity reserves, and block owners with unsafe track records. [P1.7] The decisive principle is simple: where residents cannot protect themselves and failure means neglect rather than a bad purchase, society should not wait for the market to punish reckless owners afterward. It should constrain the ownership incentives that make such failure more likely.
