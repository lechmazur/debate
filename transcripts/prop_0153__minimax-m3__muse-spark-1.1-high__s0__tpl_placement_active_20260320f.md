[P1.1] The question before us is not whether surrogacy should exist in a perfect world where no woman ever feels economic pressure. That world does not exist. The real choice is how we govern a practice that already happens, in which children are born, money changes hands, and people are vulnerable right now, today, in every jurisdiction that has tried to wish it away.
[P1.2] Prohibition has been the grand global experiment of recent decades. Its results are now legible. Where compensated surrogacy is criminalized, the industry does not disappear. It moves.
[P1.3] It goes underground. It crosses borders to less regulated destinations. It operates without contracts, without enforceable medical screening, without recourse for surrogates who suffer complications, and without stable parentage for the children. Cross-border reproductive care flows from restrictive to permissive jurisdictions, and the harm travels with it.
[P1.4] A surrogate post-delivery abroad has no remedy in her home courts. Intended parents have, in documented cases, returned with unregistered children. That is the actual record of the ban model.
[P1.5] Legalization with strong regulation offers a different and better path. It brings the practice into the light, where labor-style protections can apply: independent legal counsel for the surrogate, medical standards, cooling-off periods, caps on compensation that prevent pure commodification while permitting fair payment, and parentage orders established before conception. Frameworks already exist. The United Kingdom's regulated altruistic model, and detailed compensated regimes in jurisdictions like California, show the system is administratively feasible.
[P1.6] Their flaws are the flaws of incomplete regulation, not of regulation itself, and the proposition explicitly demands strong, not nominal, protections.
[P1.7] We do not promise a utopia. We promise the realistic, achievable standard of legality with the strongest workable defenses against coercion and exploitation. The opposing position promises the status quo, in which coercion flourishes unseen and exploitation has no remedy at all.
