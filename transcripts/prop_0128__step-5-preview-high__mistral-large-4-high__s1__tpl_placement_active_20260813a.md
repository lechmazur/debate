[P1.1] A diploma is a proxy, not a skill. When employers demand a bachelor's degree for a role that never required one—a sales supervisor, an IT support specialist, an executive assistant—they are not measuring ability; they are measuring pedigree. The proposition is straightforward and narrowly tailored: where degree completion is not genuinely necessary for performance, it should not function as a gate.
[P1.2] Degree inflation distorts the labor market. Countless mid-skill jobs that once trained capable workers on the job now screen out anyone without a credential. The result is not a better workforce but a thinner, more expensive pool that takes longer to fill. A candidate with three years of relevant experience and demonstrable output is rejected because a transcript is missing, while a recent graduate with theoretical knowledge and no track record is preferred.
[P1.3] When the credential replaces competence as the hiring standard, both employers and workers lose.
[P1.4] The cost is also structural. Credential requirements disproportionately screen out lower-income applicants, first-generation students, and workers who cannot afford four years of tuition or take time away from earning. Employers discard talent that is already in the labor market. If the core tasks can be learned in six months of structured on-the-job training, the degree is not a signal of competence; it is a filter for privilege and social background.
[P1.5] Better alternatives already exist. Skills-based assessments, portfolio reviews, apprenticeships, and probationary performance evaluate what the job actually demands. Several major companies have dropped degree requirements for numerous roles and found that hiring decisions improved when grounded in demonstrated capability rather than parchment. A probationary period reveals how someone codes, manages, or communicates far more reliably than a transcript.
[P1.6] Employers claim they want performance. Degree requirements do not guarantee performance; they guarantee attendance and debt. If the work can be done without the degree, the requirement is arbitrary, and it should go.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that if a task can be learned on the job, a degree requirement is irrational. But hiring is not task certification; it is a prediction about performance over months and years—under pressure, with customers, in writing, and in edge cases that never appear in a job description. A degree is not proof of one skill, but it is a cheap, consistent signal of literacy, sustained follow-through, and capacity to absorb structured training. For a sales supervisor it is not needed to make a cold call; it is a proxy for reading contracts, coaching a team, and de-escalating an angry client.
[C1.2] For IT support it predicts who will document an incident clearly enough for the next technician to fix it. Those are performance qualities even when no course is cited.
[C1.3] The phrase “not genuinely necessary” is too vague to operate as a rule. Necessary for day-one tasks, or for expected performance, client trust, compliance, retention, and promotion into the next role? Almost any individual task can be taught, so on this logic almost every requirement could be challenged, leaving employers to justify standards after hiring disputes arise.
[C1.4] Nor does removing degrees automatically produce fairer screening. It swaps one transparent filter for less transparent ones—referrals, polished portfolios, unpaid internships, subjective interviews—which can track family background just as strongly. Validated skills tests help in some settings, but they cost money to build, validate, and defend, and are impractical for many high-volume or client-facing roles.
[C1.5] Finally, where a degree truly adds nothing, competition already disciplines employers: an unnecessary filter shrinks the pool and raises hiring costs, and rivals that widen the funnel gain an advantage. The sound rule is not a blanket “stop” but case-by-case validation—keep the degree where it predicts performance, risk, client expectations, or legal duties, and drop it only where evidence shows it does not. The proposition’s sweeping mandate would override exactly those judgments.

[P2.4] The live question is who bears the burden, and a gate should justify itself, not the candidate it excludes.
