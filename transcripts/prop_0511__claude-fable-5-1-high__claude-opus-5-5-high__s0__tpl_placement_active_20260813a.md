[P1.1] Every tax is also an instruction. The question is what each of these three tools tells a landowner to do.
[P1.2] A land-value tax says: your bill is set by what the location is worth, not by what you build on it. Add four storeys, convert a parking lot to flats, and your tax does not rise. Sit on a vacant downtown lot waiting for prices to climb, and you pay the same as your neighbour who built housing. The instruction is: use land or sell it to someone who will.
[P1.3] Because land cannot flee, hide, or shrink in response to being taxed, economists across the spectrum have long recognised it as the closest thing we have to a tax with no deadweight loss. Pennsylvania cities that shifted their property tax toward land and away from buildings saw vacant parcels fill in and construction pick up relative to comparable neighbours.
[P1.4] A transaction tax says the opposite: don't move. Stamp duties punish the retiree who wants to downsize, the growing family that wants to trade up, the worker who wants to follow a job. Existing homes get locked in by owners who cannot justify a five-figure toll for changing address, so the effective supply available to buyers shrinks. And for local finance it is the worst possible base—revenue booms when sales boom and collapses precisely when a downturn arrives and councils most need money.
[P1.5] A rent cap says: don't build, don't maintain, and if you can, convert to a use I don't cover. St. Paul's voters passed a strict cap and new permit applications fell sharply within months. Stockholm's caps produced decade-long waiting lists. Caps raise zero revenue for the city while quietly shrinking the taxable base they leave behind.
[P1.6] So on both halves of the motion—supply and local finance—the comparison is not close. Land-value taxation is the only one of the three that rewards building, cannot be dodged, funds services on a stable base, and returns to the public the land values that public investment creates.

[C1.1] Every tax is an instruction, yes. But the motion isn't about which tax sounds elegant in a seminar. It asks which tool actually delivers more housing and sounder local finance. On that test, land-value taxation fails where it matters: in the real world.
[C1.2] First, supply. The binding constraint on housing in expensive cities isn't landowners' tax incentives. It's zoning, permitting and infrastructure. A land tax can tell an owner to "build four storeys," but if the parcel is zoned for one house, the instruction is meaningless.
[C1.3] In high-demand cities, vacant land is a sliver of the urban map. The shortage lives on lots already built at low density that the law forbids redeveloping. Pro's Pennsylvania example proves how modest this is. Those were small, revenue-neutral shifts in the property tax mix.
[C1.4] Their construction effects are hard to separate from local booms. And Pittsburgh, the flagship, abandoned its split-rate system after a reassessment backlash. A policy that dies when voters see their bills cannot sustain supply.
[C1.5] Second, local finance. Land is not the stable part of property value. It is the volatile part. Buildings depreciate predictably.
[C1.6] Land values swing with interest rates and speculation. So a land-heavy tax asks cash-poor households to pay bills tied to paper gains they cannot spend. California showed where that leads. Soaring assessments fed a homeowner revolt, and Proposition 13 then crippled local budgets for decades.
[C1.7] Land tax also depends on assessors valuing land separately from buildings, often with almost no vacant-land sales to go on. That invites appeals, litigation and political manipulation.
[C1.8] Now compare. A transaction tax collects when the owner has cash in hand: at sale. It needs no contested valuation, because the price is the valuation. It can be targeted at high-end sales or rapid flipping rather than downsizers.
[C1.9] Well-designed rent stabilization, which exempts new construction, protects existing tenants from displacement without taxing the building pipeline. It keeps effective supply where people already live.
[C1.10] Land-value taxation is the best tool on paper and the most fragile in practice. The motion asks about practice.
