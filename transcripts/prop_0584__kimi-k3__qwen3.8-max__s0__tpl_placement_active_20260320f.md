[P1.1] Price stability is the foundation on which everything else a central bank touches rests. Three reasons this motion gets the priority ordering right.
[P1.2] First, the burden of inflation is regressive and universal, while the burden of modestly higher unemployment is concentrated and reversible. Inflation is a tax on everyone holding wages, savings, or benefits — and it falls hardest on those with no ability to hedge: the worker without an indexed contract, the pensioner, the family whose grocery bill rises faster than their paycheck. A modest uptick in unemployment is painful for those affected, but monetary policy can reverse it quickly by easing.
[P1.3] The price-level ratchet from inflation never reverses; the lost purchasing power is gone for good.
[P1.4] Second, credibility is cheaper to keep than to rebuild. The 1970s showed what happens when central banks flinch: each time they prioritized employment over inflation, expectations drifted upward, and the eventual cure — the Volcker disinflation — cost far more unemployment than early firmness ever would have. The lesson generalizes: a central bank that visibly tolerates above-target inflation teaches firms and workers to build inflation into every wage demand and price list, and then the tradeoff itself worsens. Paying a modest unemployment cost now is insurance against a catastrophic one later.
[P1.5] Third, the tradeoff the other side will invoke is empirically smaller than feared. In the 2022–2024 disinflation, inflation fell from its peak back toward target while unemployment stayed historically low — the catastrophic "sacrifice ratio" never materialized. So this motion asks us to accept a modest, partly hypothetical cost to prevent a demonstrated, compounding harm.
[P1.6] And note the asymmetry that cannot be escaped: after tightening, a central bank holds rate room to cut and support jobs. A central bank that has lost its inflation anchor has no painless instrument left — only deeper pain, later. Prioritizing inflation is not indifference to workers; it is the only durable way to protect them.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a useful mandate for a blunt rule. Over the next three years, the central danger is not that central banks care too little about inflation, but that they overcorrect against a surge already fading, imposing lasting labor-market damage for marginal price-stability gains.
[C1.2] Our core is the asymmetry of harm. Inflation above target imposes costs, but when expectations remain anchored and supply pressures are easing, the benefit of forcing inflation down the last few tenths is small. The cost of a modest unemployment rise is not modest or easily reversible. Job loss concentrates on vulnerable workers: youth, recent entrants, lower-income households, and those with little bargaining power.
[C1.3] Even short spells erase savings, delay skill formation, and can become long-term unemployment. Rate cuts may restore vacancies later, but they do not restore lost wages, disrupted careers, or employers’ willingness to hire workers with gaps. Hysteresis is why modest contractions can leave permanent output scars.
[C1.4] The proposition also misreads the policy environment. Much recent inflation came from commodity shocks, supply-chain bottlenecks, and sectoral rebalancing—forces interest rates cannot directly fix. Raising rates enough to speed convergence mainly suppresses housing, durables, and investment. If the next three years bring weak growth, fiscal drag, or financial stress, an inflation-first rule would push banks to tighten or hold too long, turning a manageable slowdown into recession.
[C1.5] That is how credibility is actually endangered: not by gradual, transparent normalization, but by visible policy errors that force abrupt reversals.
[C1.6] Rejecting this motion does not abandon the target. It means central banks should weigh both sides of their mandates with real-world lags, not treat employment as an acceptable sacrifice whenever inflation is slightly high. With inflation already near target in many economies, the prudent course is flexible convergence: protect the anchor, but do not buy insurance with other people’s jobs.
