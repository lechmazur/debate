[P1.1] A blasphemy law is the one criminal statute where the alleged victim cannot testify, cannot be injured, and cannot be identified. God needs no bodyguards; believers do. That distinction is the whole case, and it decides the motion: protect people from violence, threat, and targeted harassment — always. Protect doctrines from criticism — never, because doing so requires the state to become a theologian.
[P1.2] Start with the fatal design flaw. To enforce a religious-insult law, someone in a courtroom must rule on what counts as sacred and what counts as an insult to it. There is no neutral way to do that. So in practice these laws do not float above the religious landscape; they sink into it, and they land on whoever is weakest.
[P1.3] In Pakistan, blasphemy accusations have overwhelmingly fallen on Christians, Ahmadis, and Shias — and on Muslims settling land disputes and personal grudges with an allegation that is almost impossible to disprove. Asia Bibi spent years on death row over a quarrel about a cup of water. In Indonesia, a Christian governor was jailed for blasphemy after political opponents clipped a campaign speech. The pattern is not accidental abuse of a good law; it is the law working as designed, because "you offended what I hold sacred" is an accusation with no evidentiary floor.
[P1.4] Second, the public-order argument inverts itself. A blasphemy law tells every mob that outrage is a legitimate legal currency — that if enough people are angry enough, the state will punish the speaker. That is a heckler's veto with a prosecutor attached. It rewards the most easily inflamed faction and teaches the rest that grievance escalation works.
[P1.5] Order built on that incentive is not stability; it is a standing invitation.
[P1.6] Third, the majority's belief that these laws are necessary is precisely why abolition must not wait for permission. Rights exist to constrain what 60 percent may do to the other 40. And nothing is lost: incitement, harassment, and true-threat laws already shield worshippers. Abolition removes the sword while keeping the shield.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that removing legal protections for religious sentiment automatically produces a more tolerant society, but this ignores the essential role these laws play as a pressure-release valve in multi-religious states. Abolishing them does not eliminate deep-seated sectarian tensions; it merely removes the only formal mechanism preventing those tensions from spilling into the streets.
[C1.2] When a state recognizes the gravity of religious insults through measured legislation, it provides a legal pathway for grievance. Without this, aggrieved communities often feel the state is indifferent to their identity, leading to vigilante justice and communal violence that the government is then powerless to stop.
[C1.3] Furthermore, the PRO argument that "rights exist to constrain the majority" is a dangerously narrow view of democracy. In many nations, these laws reflect a collective social contract—a mutual agreement to prioritize communal harmony over individual provocation. To label this "tyranny" is to misunderstand how fragile pluralism actually is. Abolition is a luxury of stable, secularized societies; for many others, it is a recipe for civil strife.
[C1.4] Finally, the claim that existing laws like "incitement to violence" are sufficient is a Western-centric fallacy. Those laws react to physical threats, whereas blasphemy laws protect the psychological and social dignity of a community. By forcing countries to abolish these laws, we are essentially demanding they adopt a secular fundamentalism that provokes the very disorder the motion claims to prevent.
