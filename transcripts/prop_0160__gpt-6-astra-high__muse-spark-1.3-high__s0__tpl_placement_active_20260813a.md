[P1.1] A device that directly stimulates the brain is not simply delivering information for someone to consider. It intervenes in neural activity itself. [P1.2] The first problem is that safety depends on the person, not just the product. Effects can vary with stimulation settings, neurological conditions, medication, and repeated use. Depending on the technology, adverse effects can include changes in mood, cognition, or seizure risk. Passing a factory test cannot establish that a device is appropriate for a particular user.
[P1.3] Medical supervision adds screening, controlled dosing, follow-up, and someone responsible for deciding when to stop. Those are substantive protections, not paperwork.
[P1.4] The second problem is the consumer market’s incentive structure. Imagine a concentration headset whose subscription business rewards daily use and increasingly intensive settings. The seller profits when customers interpret fatigue as a reason for another session. Even without sinister mind control, commercial optimization can conflict with neurological welfare.
[P1.5] A warning label does not resolve that conflict, especially when users cannot readily distinguish genuine improvement from altered self-perception or an emerging adverse effect.
[P1.6] The answer is not to prohibit beneficial technology. Medical access preserves treatment and rehabilitation where identifiable benefits can justify risks. It also makes manufacturers demonstrate meaningful outcomes rather than market vague promises of sharper thinking or better moods. [P1.7] CON will invoke autonomy and innovation. But innovation is not a reason to lower the standard precisely where mistakes may affect the capacities through which autonomy is exercised. [C1.6] Autonomy means letting competent adults decide what their own minds experience, within sane safety limits. Keep stimulation devices on store shelves under strong consumer standards, reserve implanted or high-power systems for closer control, and judge innovation by actual harm, not horror at the word brain.
