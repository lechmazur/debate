[P1.1] Severe inherited mitochondrial diseases starve the brain, heart, and muscles of cellular energy from birth. Conditions like Leigh syndrome cause progressive organ failure, neurological collapse, and early childhood death. Medicine offers no cure; hospitals can only manage a child’s decline. Mitochondrial replacement therapy (MRT) prevents transmission by placing the parents’ nuclear DNA into a donor egg with healthy mitochondria.
[P1.2] Public health systems should fund MRT on medical, fiscal, and ethical grounds.
[P1.3] First, for mothers with high mutation loads, MRT is the only clinical tool that prevents severe disease without erasing biological parenthood. Preimplantation genetic testing merely selects among existing embryos; when nearly all of a woman’s mitochondria carry mutations, every egg she produces crosses the disease threshold. Telling these families to simply adopt or use full donor eggs ignores both precedent and reality. Public systems routinely fund embryo screening for disorders like cystic fibrosis rather than ordering carriers to forgo genetic children.
[P1.4] Moreover, when clinical prevention is unaffordable, some couples risk natural conception, leading to gravely ill newborns. Nor is MRT genetic enhancement: mitochondrial DNA powers cellular energy, leaving nuclear traits untouched.
[P1.5] Second, public funding is fiscally sound. Because the subset of carriers who cannot use standard embryo screening is tiny, total budget impact is minimal. Conversely, caring for an affected child imposes immense downstream public costs: pediatric intensive care, ventilators, feeding tubes, seizure management, and years of complex palliative care. One targeted preventive procedure spares families devastating grief while saving far greater hospital resources.
[P1.6] Finally, withholding coverage creates inequity and magnifies clinical risk. Leaving MRT to private wealth rations healthy children by income while pushing desperate families toward loosely regulated fertility clinics abroad. Public funding pairs equitable access with strict gatekeeping: restricting MRT to severe, otherwise untreatable cases under rigorous domestic licensing and long-term pediatric monitoring. Public health should prevent fatal genetic disease before birth, not merely fund its tragedy afterward.
