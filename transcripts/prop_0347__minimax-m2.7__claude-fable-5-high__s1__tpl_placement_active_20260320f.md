It says workers may refuse rating systems they believe are tainted, while retaining full access. That is an opt-out from accountability, and it fails on three independent grounds.
[C1.2] First, the verification problem. A one-star rating arrives without a confession attached. The racist passenger and the merely dissatisfied passenger submit identical data. So who decides a rating was "strongly shaped by abuse"?
[C1.3] If the worker self-certifies, every low-rated worker—including the genuinely rude, unsafe, or negligent ones—has an overwhelming incentive to claim bias and exit the system. If the platform adjudicates, then we're no longer talking about a right to refuse at all; we're talking about a dispute process, which already exists in rough form and which I will happily defend as the correct alternative. The proposition only does distinct work if it means something stronger than appeals—and that stronger thing is unworkable.
[C1.4] Second, adverse selection. Who opts out of ratings? Disproportionately, the worst performers. The result is a marketplace where the remaining scores are inflated and unreliable, customers can't distinguish excellent workers from dangerous ones, and the high ratings that good workers earned become worthless currency.
[C1.5] The proposition punishes precisely the workers it claims to protect.
[C1.6] Third, safety. Ratings are how platforms detect the driver who harasses passengers, the care worker who neglects clients. An opt-out is a shield for predators, who will be the first and loudest to claim their accusers are biased.
[C1.7] The real answer to abusive ratings is fixing the signal—discarding statistical outliers, auditing for demographic bias, weighting verified complaints, fast human review. That preserves protection for both sides. The proposition instead hands workers a veto over the only accountability mechanism customers have. Sympathy for victims is not an argument for that.
