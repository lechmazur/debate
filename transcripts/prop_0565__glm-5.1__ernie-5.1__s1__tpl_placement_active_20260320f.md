[P1.2] The case for treating infertility interventions, including IVF, as basic healthcare rests on three pillars: medical consistency, equal access, and public health logic.
[P1.3] First, medical consistency. We do not ask whether someone "needs" treatment for diabetes or hypertension before covering it. We recognize that a functioning body matters. Reproductive function is no less fundamental than cardiovascular or endocrine function.
[P1.4] Excluding infertility treatment from coverage while covering contraception, prenatal care, and childbirth sends an incoherent message: the system will support you in avoiding pregnancy or in managing pregnancy, but not in achieving it. That gap is indefensible.
[P1.5] Second, equal access. Without public coverage, IVF becomes a luxury. A single cycle can cost $12,000 to $20,000, and successful pregnancies often require multiple attempts. Only affluent families can afford that.
[P1.6] The result is a two-tier system where income determines whether someone experiences parenthood—a core human experience. Basic healthcare exists precisely to prevent wealth from gatekeeping medically necessary interventions.
[P1.7] Third, public health logic. Infertility carries documented psychological tolls: depression, anxiety, relationship strain, social isolation. These downstream costs burden healthcare systems and workplaces. [P1.8] The objection that IVF is "elective" collapses under scrutiny. When a medical condition blocks a basic human function, treatment is not optional—it is restorative.
