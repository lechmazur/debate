[P1.1] A city cannot reasonably order someone to stop living outside while offering nowhere they can actually go. The central test is whether compliance is possible. Anti-encampment laws are justified only when they provide a genuine route off the street—not merely a penalty for remaining visible.
[P1.2] “Genuinely available” means more than a vacancy somewhere on a spreadsheet. Shelter must be accessible to the person being displaced, including relevant disability and family needs. Storage must let people preserve belongings they cannot take inside. Services must offer a usable connection to support, rather than a phone number or a waiting list.
[P1.3] This does not require perfect accommodation or guaranteed permanent housing. It requires a real alternative at the time the city demands departure.
[P1.4] That condition matters practically as well as morally. Without it, clearing an encampment generally changes where homelessness appears, not whether it exists. Someone who loses identification, medication, or work equipment may find it harder to obtain housing, maintain treatment, or keep employment. Fines add obligations to someone already unable to pay.
[P1.5] Repeated displacement can interrupt outreach relationships. An enforcement policy that undermines its own route to lasting compliance is not a serious solution.
[P1.6] The strongest objection is that other residents deserve safe sidewalks, usable parks, and protection from fires or violence. They do. But that establishes the need to address those harms—not permission to punish unavoidable outdoor survival. Cities can extinguish fires, stop violence, and restore emergency access without treating homelessness itself as misconduct.
[P1.7] Public safety should determine the intervention; visibility alone should not.
[P1.8] Providing alternatives also makes enforcement more defensible when someone rejects a genuinely usable placement: the city can then distinguish inability to comply from refusal. Without alternatives, that distinction disappears. Budget constraints do not make punishment produce beds. Cities should earn the authority to prohibit encampments by making departure realistically possible.
[P1.9] Otherwise, they are demanding an outcome while withholding the means to achieve it.
