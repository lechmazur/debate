[P1.1] States do not rent their air-defense radar by the hour from foreign corporations, nor do they let private boards ration their emergency power grids. Yet relying heavily on private cloud oligopolies and foreign infrastructure for critical AI invites that exact vulnerability. Building sovereign capacity does not mean crowding out commercial tech or running consumer chatbots. It means securing critical public-interest uses where national security, operational continuity, and democratic accountability cannot be outsourced.
[P1.2] First, heavy external dependence creates intolerable geopolitical and operational chokepoints. Compute is now foundational state infrastructure. When a nation relies on foreign servers or a few multinational hyperscalers, its core functions become hostage to another country's export controls, sanctions, and extraterritorial data-access laws. Even absent state coercion, private executives can alter service terms, reprioritize capacity, or suffer outages without public accountability.
[P1.3] During a cyberattack, military crisis, or disaster, a government cannot risk having its intelligence or emergency-response models throttled from abroad.
[P1.4] Second, critical public AI demands genuine data sovereignty and fiscal independence. Training models on national health registries, administering public benefits, or analyzing classified threats requires control over the physical infrastructure. Even "localized" commercial clouds remain tied to a foreign parent company's legal jurisdiction, opaque software stacks, and brutal vendor lock-in. Once essential agencies embed themselves in proprietary cloud ecosystems, switching costs explode, granting private monopolies permanent pricing leverage over taxpayers.
[P1.5] Third, commercial markets systematically starve public goods. Private providers rationally steer scarce chips toward high-margin buyers in ad-targeting, entertainment, and finance. Vital non-commercial missions, including pandemic modeling, extreme-weather forecasting, open science, and independent AI safety auditing, get priced out whenever capacity tightens. Just as states built public supercomputers for meteorology and national laboratories, sovereign AI compute guarantees dedicated capacity for missions the market undervalues.
[P1.6] Sovereignty means never asking a private monopolist or a foreign capital for permission to govern. For critical public functions, states must build the capacity they cannot afford to lose.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like prudent statecraft, but it mistakes a symptoms-level fix for structural strategy—and the cure is worse than the disease. Building sovereign AI compute is a breathtakingly expensive gamble that distracts from the real work of securing public-interest AI.
[C1.2] First, the proposition ignores the brutal pace of compute evolution. Governments that build hardware today inherit obsolescence tomorrow. Public supercomputers from a decade ago are museum pieces; a sovereign AI cluster funded through multi-year appropriations will be outpaced by private hyperscalers within budget cycles. We are not talking about bridges that last fifty years.
[C1.3] We are talking about infrastructure whose useful life may be three to five years before the next chip architecture renders it a liability.
[C1.4] Second, sovereign compute does not eliminate dependence—it relocates it. Building a national AI datacenter still requires imported semiconductor equipment, rare-earth supply chains, and specialized talent pools that no single nation fully controls. The United States, for example, would still depend on TSMC-fabricated chips or Dutch lithography tools. You trade one set of vulnerabilities for another, at far greater cost and with far less agility.
[C1.5] Third, and most critically, the proposition sets up a false binary. Securing public-interest AI does not require owning datacenters. It requires enforceable contracts, regulatory teeth, diversified vendor strategies, and data-governance frameworks that bind private providers to public obligations. The same logic that keeps air-defense radar sovereign does not demand the government fabricate its own semiconductors—it demands contracts, oversight, and redundancy.
[C1.6] Heavy dependence is a policy failure, not an inevitability.
[C1.7] Building sovereign capacity entrenches government in a technology race it cannot win, diverts resources from the regulatory and contractual tools that actually secure accountability, and creates a single, slow-moving target where a diversified private ecosystem offers resilience through redundancy. The state should be a shrewd buyer and fierce regulator, not a slow, underfunded chip manufacturer.
