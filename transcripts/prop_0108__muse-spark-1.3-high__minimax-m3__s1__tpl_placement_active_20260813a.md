[P1.1] American legislatures don't reflect American voters. They reflect a voting rule that manufactures extremism.
[P1.2] Under single-member winner-take-all, an election with 34% for an extremist, 33% for a moderate, and 33% for another moderate hands total power to the extremist. Everyone else gets zero representation. That is the Duverger trap: voters quickly learn that supporting their true favorite risks helping their worst enemy, so they settle for the lesser evil, and parties learn they win not by persuading the middle but by frightening and mobilizing their base.
[P1.3] Primaries make it worse. In a gerrymandered safe seat, the general election is decided in advance. The only real contest is a low-turnout primary where a small, intense faction can punish compromise. The rational strategy for an incumbent is never to work across the aisle, because bipartisanship invites a primary challenge while obstruction brings donations and media attention.
[P1.4] Polarization is not a cultural accident; it is the payoff structure of winner-take-all.
[P1.5] Ranked-choice and proportional systems change the payoff.
[P1.6] Ranked-choice lets a Republican in a blue city or a Democrat in a red county rank honestly without wasting a vote or playing spoiler. Candidates need true majorities built from second choices, so attacking every opponent alienates the very voters whose backup support you need. Places that have tried it, like Maine and Alaska, have seen more civil campaigns and more room for independents and moderates who would be crushed under plurality.
[P1.7] Proportional or multi-member districts go further: if a district elects three or five members roughly in proportion to votes, a 60-40 state stops producing 100-0 delegations. Gerrymandering loses its bite because packing and cracking voters cannot erase proportional shares. Millions of voters trapped in safe districts finally gain a voice.
[P1.8] The question is simple: keep rules that reward division, or adopt rules that reward breadth.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to perform radical institutional surgery on American democracy to cure a disease this surgery is poorly designed to treat, while quietly introducing serious new harms. I offer three reasons to reject it.
[C1.2] First, the diagnosis is wrong. Polarization in America is driven by geographic sorting, media ecosystems, social media, primary turnout, and the cultural realignment of the parties — not primarily by the ballot format. Canada, India, and the United Kingdom all use single-member winner-take-all districts and still field multi-party competition; voting method alone does not determine party count. If winner-take-all "manufactures extremism," why do Ottawa, London, and Delhi tell a different story?
[C1.3] The proposition treats a structural feature of mature democracies as if it were the disease. The ballot is a symptom-blamer, not a cause-fixer.
[C1.4] Second, the cure carries well-documented costs that have been ignored. Proportional systems routinely deliver chronic coalition fragility. Italy has cycled through dozens of governments since the war; Belgium once went 541 days without an executive; Israel has held five elections in under four years. Multi-party parliaments empower the most extreme tail, because every coalition needs a kingmaker.
[C1.5] In Germany's Bundestag, the AfD's parliamentary presence was delivered by proportionality, not despite it. Ranked-choice voting has its own pathologies: the "center squeeze" can elect the candidate everyone ranks second but few rank first, complexity confuses voters, and ranked ballots require exhaustive recounts that raise legitimate questions about auditability and public trust.
[C1.6] Third, the constitutional and federal structure is the silent casualty. Single-member House districts currently link a representative to a specific place and a specific people — the connective tissue of federalism and geographic representation. Multi-member proportional districts dissolve that link, diluting the voice of rural counties and minority communities that already feel distant from Washington.
[C1.7] The proposition would trade a familiar set of problems for an unfamiliar set, plausibly worse. That is not reform — it is a gamble with the country's operating system. Reject it.
