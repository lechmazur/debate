[P1.1] Health systems have finite money, staff, and time. Every expansion is a decision not to fund something else. On that unavoidable trade-off, prevention should come first — not because lives at the end matter less, but because prevention saves more of them, earlier, better, and for more people.
[P1.2] Prevention works upstream, before a risk becomes irreversible damage. Vaccination stops infection and its complications. Controlling blood pressure and sugar prevents strokes, kidney failure, and amputations. Smoking cessation, clean air, and early cancer screening reduce disease years before an intensive care bed is needed.
[P1.3] The result is not just fewer deaths, but more years lived without disability — time at work, with family, and out of hospital.
[P1.4] Marginal expansions of end-of-life care offer the opposite profile. An additional line of chemotherapy or prolonged intensive care for very advanced illness may extend life by days or weeks, often with severe side effects, repeated hospitalisation, and little chance of returning home. Those weeks matter to that person, but as a funding priority they deliver little health for huge cost, and can make dying more medicalised rather than comfortable.
[P1.5] That is also an equity difference. Prevention can reach whole communities, including people who rarely see a doctor until seriously ill and who bear the heaviest burden of chronic disease. Expensive end-of-life expansions concentrate vast resources on a few patients already inside tertiary hospitals. Prioritising prevention spreads benefit widely and helps people early, not only with heroic measures when it is too late.
[P1.6] This is not abandoning the dying. Palliative care, hospice, pain control, and treatments with meaningful benefit should be funded. The question is what gets the next increment of scarce funding. Prevention first prevents the most suffering, preserves the most healthy life, and keeps the system sustainable.
[P1.7] Spending ever more for marginal gains at the end, while underfunding what stops people getting sick, is not compassion — it is failing earlier, when help could do most.

[C1.1] The PRO’s case rests on a flawed, utilitarian framing that reduces health care to a numbers game, erasing the core ethical duty of medicine to care for individual patients regardless of prognosis or perceived “prevention compliance.” The PRO dismisses marginal end-of-life care as low-value for high cost, but this label ignores the profound, non-quantifiable good those extensions hold for patients and their families. A 6-month extension of functional life for a parent to attend their child’s wedding, or to say goodbye to loved ones, is not a trivial “marginal gain” even if it does not shift population-level mortality statistics.
[C1.2] The PRO’s framework treats these lives as expendable for aggregate efficiency, a moral failure no health system should accept. The PRO’s equity argument is also backwards. Preventive measures consistently fail to reach the most marginalized: people experiencing housing instability, food insecurity, or low health literacy face structural barriers that make vaccination, screening, and chronic disease management inaccessible even when publicly funded. These are the same people most likely to end up needing high-cost end-of-life care after falling through the cracks of preventive systems.
[C1.3] Prioritizing prevention over their end-of-life care punishes them twice—first for structural barriers the system failed to address, then for getting sick anyway. The PRO draws a false line between “worthy” prevention and “wasteful” end-of-life care. Many high-cost services lumped into their target category are foundational palliative and hospice supports that reduce suffering, keep patients out of emergency departments, and aid family caregivers. Cutting these to fund prevention would increase overall system strain, not reduce it.
[C1.4] A health system that abandons its most vulnerable patients in their final days to chase aggregate prevention metrics has lost its moral compass.

[P2.1] The wedding example proves the distinction the opposition tries to erase. Six months of functional life at home, well enough to be present for family, is not a marginal gain — it is meaningful benefit, and it should be funded. What the proposition deprioritises is the extra line of treatment or prolonged intensive care that offers days or weeks, often spent in hospital with severe side effects and little chance of returning home. [P2.2] That is why the charge of utilitarianism fails. Prioritisation is not judging whose life is worth more; it is recognising that the person who never reaches intensive care because a stroke, infection, or cancer was prevented is also an individual, with a family and a future. Refusing to weigh opportunity cost does not avoid rationing. It rations invisibly, in favour of the patient already in the tertiary bed and against the many who will become patients because prevention was underfunded.
[P2.3] The equity argument cuts the other way. Yes, marginalised people face barriers to vaccination, screening, and chronic disease control. That is a reason to fund prevention that reaches them — community outreach, primary care, help with blood pressure and diabetes before crisis — not to reserve the money for the most hospital-dependent care of all. High-cost end-of-life expansions require referral and access to specialist centres, advantages least available to those same marginalised patients.
[P2.4] Funding rescue after the system has failed them does not correct that failure; it leaves the failure intact and repeats it for the next person.
[P2.5] Finally, palliative care, hospice, pain control, and support for caregivers are not the target. They are essential care and often prevent unwanted emergency admissions. Conflating them with marginal expansions protects the lowest-value spending by hiding it behind the highest-value comfort care. Keep the comfort.
[P2.6] Prioritise prevention for the next pound. That is not abandoning the dying; it is refusing to abandon the living who could avoid dying early.
