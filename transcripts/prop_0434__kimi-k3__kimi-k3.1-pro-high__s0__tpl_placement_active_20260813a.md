[P1.1] Picture a nurse coming off a night shift, lying awake at 2 a.m. while bass thumps through the wall for the fourth night running. She calls the landlord and is told to document it. She calls the police; by the time an officer arrives, the music has stopped and the report reads "no violation observed." The harm is real, repeated, and nearly impossible to prove.
[P1.2] That asymmetry — noise is trivially easy to inflict and brutally hard to evidence — is exactly what this motion fixes.
[P1.3] Certified monitoring converts anecdote into record. Calibrated, tamper-evident devices produce time-stamped decibel logs: how loud, how long, how often. That's the difference between a tenant insisting "it's always loud" and showing ninety nights of readings above the legal limit after midnight. We already trust instrumental evidence everywhere — speed cameras, dashcam footage, smart-meter data, carbon-monoxide readings in habitability disputes.
[P1.4] Nobody calls a radar gun surveillance; it measures the violation, not the person. A certified meter does the same: it registers volume and duration, not conversations. Decibels, not dialogue.
[P1.5] The gains run in every direction. Shift workers, infants, the elderly — people whose health genuinely suffers under chronic sleep disruption — get a remedy that doesn't depend on an officer catching the moment. Landlords and housing courts get objective records that resolve disputes faster and cheaper than endless complaint loops. And deterrence improves: a tenant who knows violations leave a verifiable trail turns the music down; one who knows complaints evaporate has no reason to.
[P1.6] Certification is the safeguard, not a loophole. Calibration standards, chain of custody, and the other party's right to challenge or corroborate the data are how courts already treat every instrument reading. The evidence cuts both ways, too: a clean log clears the falsely accused as surely as a bad one proves the complaint.
[P1.7] The motion asks only that reliable measurement count. No one should have to lose sleep twice — once at home, and again being disbelieved.

[C1.1] PRO’s nurse deserves a remedy. But hardship does not justify expanding evidence generated and positioned by one disputant against another. Certification may show a meter was calibrated; it cannot show what caused the sound, how it traveled, or whether the setup was representative.
[C1.2] That gap is decisive. A microphone beside an open window or shared ventilation shaft can produce a flawless log of a misleading event. Bass transmitted through a building may implicate construction, several units, or street noise. Decibels alone do not identify the source; nuisance also depends on duration, context, local limits, and reasonable sensitivity.
[C1.3] Tamper-evidence does not make an interested neighbor’s setup neutral.
[C1.4] The speed-camera comparison collapses on exactly this point. Public enforcement uses fixed siting, defined targets, maintenance records, and institutional accountability. Here, PRO wants an interested party to choose the home, room, timing, and monitoring campaign, then invoke the aura of an instrument. “Decibels, not dialogue” is a slogan, not a privacy safeguard: sustained monitoring can reveal household routines, and source-identification pressures create demands for recordings or access to another person’s home.
[C1.5] Expanded use also changes behavior before any hearing. It invites surveillance escalation, retaliatory monitoring, and eviction files built from selectively chosen periods. The accused must then find money and expertise to contest acoustics, placement, and data. A clean log does not necessarily clear them either: absence of readings during one monitoring window proves little about other nights.
[C1.6] The fair response to under-enforcement is neutral, promptly available measurement: housing officers, qualified technicians, agreed protocols, and adjudicators able to order inspections. That makes reliable measurement count without turning neighbors into private enforcement agencies. PRO treats admissibility as a remedy for disbelief; expanding contestable evidence creates a second injustice. The nurse should not have to prove the impossible, but neither should a tenant face eviction because an opponent’s certified number is mistaken for a certified explanation.
