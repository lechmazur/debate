[P1.1] A cheeseburger costs $11 at the counter. On the app it costs $14, plus a service fee, plus a "small order" fee, plus a suggested tip on the inflated subtotal. The customer never sees the $11. The restaurant, quietly, wants them to.
[P1.2] Only one party in that transaction benefits from the darkness, and it is the platform.
[P1.3] That is the whole case. Delivery apps are not neutral marketplaces; they are intermediaries reselling another business's product with a markup that restaurants are pushed into embedding in menu prices to survive commissions that commonly run a quarter or more of the ticket. The customer believes they are comparing the restaurant's price against the restaurant's price. They are not.
[P1.4] Requiring the app to show the nearest lower-fee pickup or direct-order option when its own total is materially higher simply restores the comparison the customer thinks they are already making.
[P1.5] Notice how modest the rule is. It only fires on a material gap, so an app that prices honestly shows nothing. It does not cap fees, ban commissions, or force apps to process competitors' orders. It is one line of text: "Pick up directly for about $4 less."
[P1.6] If the app's convenience is worth four dollars to me, I tap "deliver" anyway and the platform keeps the sale. The rule only costs an app the orders it was winning through concealment rather than value. A business model that collapses when the customer learns the price was never a business model; it was an information tax.
[P1.7] We already accept this logic elsewhere. Airlines must show the all-in fare. Lenders must state the APR next to the monthly payment. Flight-search tools show direct-booking options beside their own.
[P1.8] Disclosure at the moment of decision is the least intrusive tool regulators have, and it works precisely because it leaves the choice with the buyer.
[P1.9] So the clash is not convenience versus regulation. Convenience survives either way. The clash is whether the premium for convenience is chosen by the customer or extracted from them. Pick chosen.

[C1.1] The rule sounds modest until you ask what it actually forces a business to do: advertise its competitor's storefront, in real time, inside its own app, at the exact moment a sale is about to close. That is not price disclosure. APR-on-a-loan and all-in airfare rules make a seller show the true cost of its own product. Nothing in consumer law makes a bakery post a sign saying "the shop across the street sells this cheaper."
[C1.2] Delivery apps would be the first industry ever mandated to actively route customers away, mid-transaction, to a rival's till. That is compelled cross-promotion, not transparency, and there's no principled stopping point once you accept it — why not force grocery stores to flag the farmers' market, or force Amazon to flag the manufacturer's own site?
[C1.3] It is also not workable. To trigger reliably, the app needs live, accurate knowledge of a specific restaurant's walk-in price, a specific competitor's fee structure, and real-time distance to "the nearest" alternative — data the app usually doesn't hold and the restaurant has no obligation to share. Get it wrong (stale menu price, wrong location, an option that's actually sold out) and you've built a machine for false-advertising liability and consumer confusion, aimed at a company for guessing about someone else's storefront.
[C1.4] And consider who pays when the diverted order doesn't happen: the same small restaurants PRO wants to protect. Delivery apps fund the marketing, search placement, and driver logistics that bring first-time customers to a restaurant that can't afford its own ad budget. Force the app to hand that discovered demand back to pickup, and the app recoups lost volume by raising the very commissions and fees PRO is trying to shrink. The rule attacks the symptom and starves the thing generating the restaurant's new business.
[C1.5] The final total is shown before checkout. The customer already knows the price they're paying. What's missing isn't disclosure — it's PRO's preferred outcome, mandated.
