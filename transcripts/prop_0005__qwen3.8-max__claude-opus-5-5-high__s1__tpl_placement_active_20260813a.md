[P1.1] Prescription drugs are not cereals. They are powerful interventions whose benefits depend on diagnosis, dosage, contraindications, and monitoring. The proper gatekeeper is a clinician, not an ad campaign. Direct-to-consumer mass-media advertising severs that gatekeeping role.
[P1.2] It asks people with no medical training to decide, based on thirty seconds of emotion and slick editing, whether they should request a drug that may carry serious risks.
[P1.3] The harm is not hypothetical. Advertising is designed to create demand, not careful understanding. It frames normal life—sadness, shyness, aging, occasional pain—as diseases requiring branded pills. Patients then arrive at appointments asking for a specific drug by name.
[P1.4] Physicians are pressured to prescribe something the patient may not need, or may not safely tolerate, instead of discussing watchful waiting, generics, therapy, or lifestyle change. Even when doctors resist, the encounter is distorted: scarce time is spent rebutting marketing rather than treating illness.
[P1.5] This also worsens costs and inequity. Mass-media campaigns favor profitable, heavily marketed blockbuster drugs, often newer medicines with aggressive pricing and less post-market experience. They steer patients away from older, cheaper, equally effective options. Patients pay the difference through premiums and copays, and public systems absorb inflated spending.
[P1.6] CON will likely say ads inform patients. But prescription-drug ads inform the way casino ads explain odds: they emphasize the promised payoff and soften the risk. Fine print cannot cure a business model built on persuasion. If the goal is education, regulators can require neutral, non-branded public-health information; if the goal is safety, we should not let drug sellers mass-market their own products to vulnerable audiences.
[P1.7] Because these drugs require individualized medical judgment, mass-media promotion is incompatible with safe prescribing. The safest line is prohibition: ban company-run consumer advertising, keep medical decisions in the exam room, and let evidence—not advertising budgets—drive prescriptions.
