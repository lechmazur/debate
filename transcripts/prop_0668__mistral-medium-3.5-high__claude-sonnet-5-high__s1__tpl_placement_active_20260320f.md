[C1.1] The proposition asks for a blanket ban—not targeted sanctions, not case-by-case licensing, but an across-the-board prohibition triggered by a country's general human-rights record. That is the wrong instrument, and here is why.
[C1.2] First, a ban does not remove the technology from the world; it just removes the exporting country's ability to attach any conditions. If European and American suppliers withdraw, China, Russia, and increasingly Israel's less scrupulous competitors step in immediately, with no end-use monitoring, no press scrutiny, no NSO-style ethics committee to even resign in protest. My opponent's own examples—Egypt, UAE, Mexico—show regimes shopping globally. A unilateral or even multilateral democratic ban just hands the market to suppliers with zero accountability, making outcomes for activists worse, not better.
[C1.3] Second, "poor human-rights record" is not a clean trigger—it is a politically contested label applied unevenly. Would this ban cover Israel, Saudi Arabia, India, or the Philippines, all US or European security partners with documented abuses? If yes, it wrecks core security relationships and invites retaliation; if no, exemptions swallow the rule and expose the policy as selective moralizing rather than principle. Either way, the "ban" becomes a tool of geopolitical convenience, not human rights.
[C1.4] Third, the same regimes facing internal unrest also face real terrorism, kidnapping, trafficking, and organized crime that ordinary citizens want police to solve. A facial-recognition system that finds a missing child or a wiretap platform that cracks a cartel cell doesn't stop being useful just because the same government also has political prisoners. Blanket bans punish the police function even where it is legitimate, and the civilians who rely on that policing are collateral damage in a policy aimed at the regime's leadership.
[C1.5] Fourth, conditional export with real end-use auditing, individual sanctions on abusive agencies, and continued engagement preserve leverage and information that outright bans destroy. Once you cut a country off, you lose visibility, influence, and any chance to shape how the tool is used at all.
