[P1.1] The decisive question is not which vault has better humidity control; it is who has the right to keep an object taken through colonial violence. A museum's competence is a fact about conservation, not a title deed. A stolen object does not become legitimate because the thief built a better case around it. If a seized heirloom is stored in a climate-controlled archive, the care may be real, but the wrong remains.
[P1.2] Restitution answers that wrong; improved storage does not.
[P1.3] The preservation test is self-serving. Colonial powers built the wealth, expertise, and acquisition networks that now let their museums claim superior care. To say "we preserve it better, so we may keep it" converts imperial advantage into a continuing veto. It rewards the taking with a custodial bonus: the longer the object is held, the stronger the defense becomes.
[P1.4] That standard invites delay and bad faith.
[P1.5] None of this requires indifference to fragility. Expertise can travel. Conservators can train local staff; museums can co-fund labs, share equipment, and arrange loans after restitution. Returns can be staged, insured, documented, and paired with conservation partnerships.
[P1.6] Those are terms of transfer, not excuses for refusal. Standards can be met in the country of origin; a defective chain of possession cannot be repaired in a foreign storeroom.
[P1.7] These objects are not merely portable matter. Benin bronze plaques, royal regalia, ancestral figures, manuscripts, and sacred vessels carry political memory, religious duties, artistic genealogies, and communal authority. In a European gallery, a Benin plaque can be admired for its casting; in Benin City, it can stand within a living history of kingship, craft, loss, and repair. Preserving material while severing context preserves only a shell.
[P1.8] "Universal" museums are still located somewhere and governed by someone. Universal access is not ownership. Return is not anti-preservation; it is preservation plus justice, with expertise shared and control returned, not superior care used to keep the spoils.

[C1.2] But preservation is not an alibi; it is a duty to the object and to future learners. Unique artifacts are non-renewable. If return knowingly moves a fragile Benin bronze or sacred vessel into conflict, looting, or neglect, it may be lost to all. Restitution then repairs a historical wrong by committing a present one against the artifact.
[C1.3] [P1.2] says restitution answers the wrong. Often it cannot. Empires are gone, objects have decades in holding museums, and claimants are modern states with elites and agendas. A stolen heirloom is not returned to a village when a national ministry takes title.
[C1.4] Joint stewardship, loans, co-curation, conservation funding, and guaranteed access can answer the taking without gambling survival. The motion forbids that by demanding return 'even when' preservation is superior.
[C1.5] That demand is dangerous because preservation inequality is real, not merely self-serving [P1.3]. Climate control, disaster planning, and labs need stable electricity, money, and security—often absent. A museum protecting an object for a century earns a defeasible claim to keep protecting it when transfer is riskier. This is not rewarding theft; it is refusing to let symbolic transfer destroy a non-renewable resource.
[C1.6] [P1.7] says material without context is only a shell. True, but material is the precondition for context. A warped or ruined plaque has no living history. Colonial-era objects have entangled histories; their context is not owned by one modern border.
[C1.7] Universal access is not ownership [P1.8], but state custody is not community access. Shared custody can restore dignity through acknowledgment and partnership—not only title. Because the motion makes superior preservation irrelevant exactly when it matters most, it should be rejected.
