[P1.1] The central question is whether young consumers deserve a bright line before a beauty product is sold to them as a health intervention, a cosmetic enhancement, or a lifestyle benefit. Clear category labels meaningfully reduce deception and harm where current norms fail.
[P1.2] Influencer and celebrity brands are not ordinary shelf products. They trade on intimacy: tutorials, before-and-after stories, personal routines, and “this helped me” framing. Young audiences are especially susceptible because parasocial trust makes the pitch feel like advice from a friend rather than advertising. When a creator says a serum “heals acne,” “balances hormones,” or “fixes gut-driven breakouts,” the claim can sound medical even if the product is only a moisturizer, supplement, or tinted cream.
[P1.3] That blur matters.
[P1.4] The proposition requires only a basic taxonomy, not a ban. Medical claims should be identified as claims to diagnose, treat, prevent disease, or materially change bodily function. Cosmetic claims should be identified as claims about appearance: covering, smoothing, coloring, improving look. Wellness claims should be identified as general lifestyle support, such as relaxation, confidence, self-care, or feeling better, without implying treatment.
[P1.5] These are familiar distinctions in consumer protection, and they can be enforced with short disclosures at point of sale and in captions.
[P1.6] This protects three groups at once. First, young people who may skip real treatment because an influencer’s product is marketed as therapeutic. Second, teens with body-image and health anxieties who are told ordinary cosmetics can fix medical or emotional problems. Third, honest brands that avoid being undercut by rivals who exaggerate.
[P1.7] CON may say the categories are fuzzy or burdensome, but the alternative is letting sellers choose the ambiguity that sells. When a product is presented to young audiences as beauty, medicine, and self-care all at once, the public interest is not paternalism; it is legibility. Require the distinction, keep the speech, and stop the blur from becoming the sales pitch.
