[P1.1] Countries should treat cyberattacks on civilian critical infrastructure as equivalent to armed attacks because international law defines an armed attack by its consequences, not by the delivery mechanism. A missile that destroys a dam or blacks out a city is an armed attack. Malicious code that opens the floodgates or shuts down a hospital’s power produces identical harm. If the trigger must be kinetic, we create a cyber loophole: a state can inflict the destruction of war as long as it routes violence through a keyboard.
[P1.2] Real operations already cross that line. The 2015 attack on Ukraine’s power grid cut electricity to hundreds of thousands in winter. Malware targeting industrial safety controllers at a petrochemical plant was built to disable emergency shutdowns and risk a toxic explosion. A hacker who seized control of a water-treatment system raised chemical levels to dangerous concentrations before an operator intervened.
[P1.3] These attacks did not merely steal data; they aimed at physical destruction and civilian suffering.
[P1.4] A critic might respond that cyber is different because attribution is hard and response should be law enforcement. But difficulty does not change the legal character, and law enforcement cannot stop a hostile state from collapsing a grid or poisoning a water supply. Equivalence does not mean automatic war. It means that when a state-sponsored cyber operation causes death, injury, or major destruction to civilian infrastructure, the victim state has the same right of self-defense it would have against a bombing, constrained by necessity and proportionality.
[P1.5] The response must fit the harm, not exceed it.
[P1.6] The central clash is whether the law looks at the weapon or the wound. PRO chooses the wound. Treating civilian-infrastructure cyberattacks as armed attacks closes the impunity gap, restores deterrence, and keeps the UN Charter relevant where modern aggression is cheapest and most deniable. If delivery mechanism decides the legal response, we guarantee that future aggression will be digital, destructive, and unanswered.
