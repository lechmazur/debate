[P1.1] Cities attach conditions to private development all the time; they do not hand out public assets as blank checks. Yet professional sports stadiums—monuments for billionaire owners and monopolistic leagues—are handed hundreds of millions in public cash, land, and tax exemptions on the mere promise of vague “economic development.” That giveaway should end. The motion is a basic rule of public accountability: no subsidy unless the team accepts enforceable revenue sharing or neighborhood-benefit requirements.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Stadium subsidies are a transfer upward. The typical deal makes taxpayers fund most construction and infrastructure while owners keep the revenue that matters: tickets, concessions, parking, naming rights, luxury suites—and crucially the increase in franchise value that public money helps create. When a team’s value soars after a publicly built stadium, that gain is privatized.
[P1.3] The promised boom rarely appears. Sports spending largely substitutes for other entertainment; jobs are often seasonal and low-wage; new tax revenue is frequently eaten by the subsidy itself. The public is left with debt, maintenance obligations, and a stadium that enriches a private cartel.
[P1.4] This imbalance is leverage, not accident. Teams play cities against one another and threaten relocation. The moment a stadium is finished, the city’s leverage is gone. That is why the requirement must be ex ante and enforceable: a handshake “community benefits” letter is worthless if a future owner can ignore it.
[P1.5] Revenue sharing lets taxpayers recoup upside—sharing luxury-box, parking, or event revenue—while neighborhood-benefit requirements ensure nearby residents are not merely absorbing traffic, displacement, and policing costs. Affordable housing, local hiring, and transit commitments are the price of using public money in a public neighborhood.
[P1.6] CON must defend unconditional subsidies. They must show either that stadium aid pays for itself without strings, or that forcing teams to share revenue or protect neighborhoods backfires. They cannot. Without enforceable conditions, the subsidy is a one-way ratchet: private profit, public risk.

[C1.1] The motion sells itself as accountability, but it is a straitjacket. It does not say cities must get a good deal; it says they may not subsidize a stadium unless the team accepts one of two particular conditions. That forbids the broad middle where most real negotiations live, and it hands teams a veto: refuse revenue sharing or a neighborhood package, and the city is legally barred from making any counteroffer.
[C1.2] CON need not defend blank checks. A city can require net-benefit tests, voter approval, fixed payments, infrastructure cost-sharing, relocation penalties, or clawbacks. The proposition makes those alternatives irrelevant. Imagine a mid-sized city that judges a stadium worth public support because it will anchor a district and generate new property taxes.
[C1.3] Its best tool might be a land swap, a fixed lease payment, or a tax-increment district—not a share of league revenue it cannot audit. The motion still says no unless the team accepts one of its two conditions. That is not accountability; it is a predetermined veto.
[C1.4] The two mandated conditions are also unreliable filters. Revenue sharing can be impossible where leagues centralize media and licensing money. Neighborhood-benefit requirements can be captured by well-connected groups, add cost without adding net public value, and are not the only way to mitigate traffic, policing, or displacement. A deal can have both conditions and still be a terrible subsidy; a deal can lack them and still be a net win for taxpayers.
[C1.5] If a subsidy is bad, reject it; if it is good, do not ban it just because the team refused a particular condition.
[C1.6] The real question is not “conditions or nothing.” It is whether the public’s return exceeds the public’s cost. Cities should have flexibility to negotiate that return in the form that fits the deal. The motion would ban beneficial deals, weaken city leverage, and substitute a slogan for judgment. Vote CON.
