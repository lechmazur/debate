[C1.2] First, conservation resources are not interchangeable lab equipment; they are scarce political capital. Preventing extinction is cheap relative to reversing it. A patrol team removing snares, a fence keeping out invasive predators, a wetland easement, or a breeding program for a species with a known range can produce measurable survival now. Mammoth revival asks governments to fund decades of elephant surrogacy, embryo failures, behavioral guesswork, and eventually vast artificial landscapes for a proxy animal that no living ecosystem expects.
[C1.3] The thylacine case is similar: Tasmania’s forests, prey base, and human land use have changed since the last thylacine died. Revival does not restore the lost world; it creates a high-maintenance exhibit.
[C1.4] Second, attention is not harmless spectacle. The motion accepts diversion, and that is the problem. Endangered species survive because someone keeps insisting on unglamorous work: habitat acquisition, enforcement against poaching, invasive-species control, pollution regulation. A mammoth headline may raise curiosity, but it also teaches the dangerous lesson that extinction is a technical error we can later correct.
[C1.5] That weakens the urgency needed to stop deforestation, overfishing, and climate change today. When governments chase resurrection, the species still alive but politically invisible lose both funding and narrative.
[C1.6] Third, the supposed spillover is not a defense of this motion. Cloning and genetic tools can help living endangered species without resurrecting lost ones. The black-footed ferret example proves conservation biotechnology deserves support—but as a complement to habitat protection, not as a substitute funded by sacrificing it.
[C1.7] Finally, permitting de-extinction under these conditions invites welfare harms, disease risks, and ecosystem disruption. Governments should invest in preventing extinction, not financing a costly promise to undo it.
