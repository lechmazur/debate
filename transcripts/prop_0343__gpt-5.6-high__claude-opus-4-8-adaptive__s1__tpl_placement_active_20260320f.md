[P1.1] The proposition is deliberately bounded: allow, not require; AI-assisted drafting, not wholesale substitution; most coursework, not every assessment; disclosure and student accountability, not consequence-free automation. Universities can still reserve unaided essays, language exercises, examinations, and foundational writing tasks where independent composition is itself the skill being tested.
[P1.2] For most other coursework, the educational goal is disciplined reasoning. AI can support that goal by generating possible structures, identifying unclear passages, offering counterarguments, or translating technical feedback into simpler language. The student still has to decide what is relevant, reject errors, verify claims, and defend the final argument. A public-policy student who asks AI for objections to a proposal and then evaluates those objections is practicing judgment, not avoiding it.
[P1.3] Assistance becomes educationally harmful only when output replaces thought; the motion’s disclosure and accountability requirements directly target that distinction.
[P1.4] Permitted use also prepares students for the world universities claim to serve. Graduates will encounter AI in research, administration, engineering, media, and law. [P1.5] It drives use underground, rewards students best able to conceal it, and leaves instructors relying on unreliable suspicions about authorship.
[P1.6] Transparent permission creates better assessment. Instructors can require students to describe how AI was used, retain drafts, annotate revisions, verify factual claims, and answer follow-up questions. Assignments can emphasize course-specific evidence, oral defense, reflection, and comparison between generated suggestions and the student’s final choices. If a student cannot explain the reasoning or submits false information, that student—not the tool—remains accountable.
[P1.7] The real choice is therefore not between AI and pure independent thought. AI already exists and will be used. The choice is between hidden, unmanaged use and visible, teachable, assessable use. Universities should prefer the system that develops judgment, reflects professional reality, and makes responsibility easier—not harder—to enforce.
