[P1.1] Morality begins with a simple truth: human suffering does not matter less because it occurs on the other side of a border fence. Citizenship is an accident of birth. Universal human welfare rests on the intrinsic worth of persons—our shared capacity to suffer and flourish. National loyalty, by contrast, attaches to an institutional arrangement shaped by historical treaties, conquests, and accidents of geography.
[P1.2] At its very best, the nation-state is an administrative division of labor: a practical way to organize care for a segment of humanity. Because the moral value of a nation derives entirely from the value of human beings, the local instrument can never outrank the universal foundation that justifies its existence.
[P1.3] One might compare a nation to a family, arguing that shared civic life and reciprocity create special obligations to fellow citizens. Yet associative duties are always morally subordinate to universal welfare. Loving your family lets you favor your own child with your free time, but it does not let you steal a stranger’s life-saving medicine to fund your child’s education. Special loyalties only govern how we distribute resources within the boundaries set by basic universal obligations.
[P1.4] A state cannot morally dump toxic waste into a river that poisons a neighboring country just to boost domestic factory wages, nor starve foreign populations to lower food prices at home.
[P1.5] The hierarchy between these claims becomes undeniable whenever they directly collide. When a soldier refuses an order to slaughter foreign civilians, or when we condemn a wealthy state for destroying surplus vaccines during a pandemic while poorer nations die, our moral judgment is clear. "I was being loyal to my country" has never absolved an assault on basic human welfare. Patriotism is only ever a virtue to the extent that universal morality permits it.
[P1.6] A claim that remains valid only until it severely harms humanity is, by definition, the weaker moral claim.
