[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a symptom for the disease. Severe housing shortages in university towns are not caused by international students; they are caused by decades of underbuilding, restrictive zoning, and municipal failure to approve enough homes. When a city cannot house its own nurses and teachers, the problem is not that a university recruited students from abroad—it is that the city approved too few apartments for anyone. Universities cannot zone land or compel construction; the motion asks them to solve a crisis they lack the power to fix.
[C1.2] Capping international enrolment is the coward's solution. It allows politicians to avoid reforming planning laws and building housing, while scapegoating foreigners for a crisis they did not create. Worse, it punishes the very local students the PRO side claims to protect. Often, international tuition fees cross-subsidize domestic places, fund bursaries for low-income locals, and keep courses open that would otherwise close.
[C1.3] Cut that revenue, and the university raises fees for locals or cuts teaching—the opposite of expanding access for the community it serves. The cap is a regressive tax on local opportunity.
[C1.4] The "severe shortage" trigger is arbitrary and politically malleable. In any desirable city with constrained supply, shortage is permanent by definition; the cap becomes a permanent brake on growth triggered merely by the university's success in attracting global talent. The premise also ignores that international students frequently expand housing stock—universities build dedicated accommodation for them, and their spending supports local construction, retail, and services for the whole town.
[C1.5] The honest response to a housing crisis is to build homes, not to restrict education. If a university is genuinely worried about student welfare, it should acquire housing stock, partner with cities on zoning reform, or require guaranteed accommodation deposits—not surrender its global mission to a temporary market failure it did not cause and cannot solve by exclusion. Capping enrolment treats the symptom while leaving the disease untouched.

[C2.1] The hospital analogy collapses on inspection. Hospitals face hard physical limits—finite beds, finite wards—and cannot print new capacity. [C2.2] The timing argument proves too much. If fees arrive this September while housing takes years to build, that logic applies equally to domestic enrolment. Singling out international students reveals not housing prudence but selective exclusion. Worse, the proposed "sequencing" is economically backwards: capping international recruitment slashes the funds universities use to build dormitories, partner with developers, and acquire housing stock.
[C2.3] The cap does not pause growth while construction catches up; it starves construction of the capital needed to catch up.
[C2.4] On cross-subsidy, the opponent asserts that revenue raising rents is "not free money," yet ignores where international students actually live. They disproportionately occupy purpose-built student housing, not family rentals competing with nurses. Cutting their numbers frees up zero flats for locals; it merely removes the tuition dollars that could have funded new units—and cuts the bursaries and courses that serve the community.
[C2.5] Finally, the "severe" trigger remains a permanent brake dressed as a temporary pause. In any desirable city, shortage is structural. The cap becomes indefinite austerity while the actual disease—restrictive zoning and glacial planning—goes untreated. Build homes, fund the builders, stop taxing the solution.
