[C1.1] The proposition sounds like it defends disabled users. It doesn't. It defends a punishment mechanism that would redirect resources toward legal defensibility rather than genuine accessibility—and the distinction matters enormously.
[C1.2] Start with what fines actually do. When a regulator imposes financial penalties, organizations respond by minimizing legal exposure, not maximizing inclusion. Legal teams expand. Compliance officers document "reasonable efforts."
[C1.3] Third-party auditors are hired to produce reports, not solutions. The result is checkbox accessibility: enough to survive a tribunal, not enough to serve a screen reader user navigating a complex interface. Physical accessibility analogies cut the other way—ramps and tactile paving are static, durable, verifiable. Digital accessibility is a moving target.
[C1.4] Every software update, every third-party widget, every A/B-tested redesign can introduce new barriers. Fining in that environment doesn't produce better products; it produces litigation and settlements that drain the budgets that should fund remediation.
[C1.5] The definitional structure of the proposition is itself a crisis. "Meaningful fines," "repeatedly," "basic standards"—none of these are settled. WCAG itself has tiered criteria, contested interpretations, and known gaps in testing methodology. Automated tools reliably catch perhaps thirty percent of real-world accessibility failures.
[C1.6] Manual auditing is expensive, inconsistent, and contested. Build a fine regime on that foundation and you create an enforcement lottery where liability depends more on which auditor you drew than how accessible your service actually is.
[C1.7] The PRO concedes smaller operators deserve consideration, then papers it over with "proportional, phased fines." But public-facing digital services include community platforms, small nonprofits, independent healthcare providers—entities with thin margins and no accessibility engineering team. A fine regime pitched at Google will land on them too.
[C1.8] Better tools exist: mandatory remediation orders with binding timelines, procurement requirements that exclude non-compliant vendors from government contracts, funded technical assistance, and public accessibility scoring. These produce accessibility. Fines produce lawyers.
[C1.9] The question isn't whether disabled users deserve better. They obviously do. The question is whether fines get them there. They won't.
