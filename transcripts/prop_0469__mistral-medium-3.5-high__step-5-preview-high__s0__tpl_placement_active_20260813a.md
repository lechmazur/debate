For example, the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill caused unprecedented damage to the Gulf of Mexico, yet there was no international prosecution for the environmental harm. [C1.1] Environmental devastation deserves serious legal responses, but the proposition mistakes moral urgency for legal readiness. An international crime is not a stronger synonym for a wrong; it is a specific category built on precise elements, individual culpability, fair notice, and enforceable jurisdiction. PRO’s proposed standard—knowingly causing “severe, widespread, or long-term damage” [P1.2]—does not meet that bar. Dams, mines, highways, plantations, and energy projects often cause lasting ecological harm while serving lawful public purposes.
[C1.2] One lawyer calls that ecocide; another calls it development. In criminal law, that ambiguity is not a flaw at the margins—it is fatal. It invites selective prosecution rather than consistent justice, and it punishes people without clear notice of what conduct was forbidden.
[C1.3] Nor does internationalization solve enforcement. International criminal institutions depend on state consent, territorial jurisdiction, or political referrals, while many major emitters and resource states remain outside the system. PRO’s examples cut against the motion: Deepwater Horizon produced national civil and criminal proceedings, while Amazon deforestation persists because domestic enforcement, land governance, and economic incentives are weak—not because the word “crime” is missing at the international level. The gap is capacity and regulation, not symbolism.
[C1.4] A vague ecocide doctrine could also become a geopolitical weapon: powerful states would define “severe” to constrain rivals’ development while exempting their own infrastructure. It would chill investment and project decisions without proving that anyone actually reduces emissions, deforestation, or pollution. The better path is clear domestic environmental crimes, strict civil liability, independent regulators, and targeted international cooperation. The motion as written would add an elastic label, not protection.

[P2.1] CON argues the definition is too vague, but this confuses difficulty with impossibility. The Rome Statute criminalizes “widespread or systematic” attacks without numeric thresholds, and courts apply it. Ecocide can be defined by objective criteria: permanent loss of ecosystem services, regional extinction events, or irreversible biodiversity collapse. The alternative—no definition—means no deterrence.
[P2.2] CON’s claim that ambiguity is fatal would also invalidate crimes against humanity; yet no one suggests abandoning those.
[P2.3] On enforcement, CON’s examples actually undermine its case. Deepwater Horizon was prosecuted nationally, but Amazon deforestation persists because domestic enforcement is weak and the harm spans borders. International criminalization would create a norm that pressures laggard states and provides alternative jurisdiction when national systems fail. Without it, cross-border ecological crimes like transboundary pollution or climate damage from carbon majors have no recourse.
[P2.4] CON admits domestic systems are inadequate, yet offers only the status quo of impunity.
[P2.5] Geopolitical weaponization is a red herring. The ICC already has safeguards against politicized prosecutions, and the real power imbalance is that wealthy states and corporations currently externalize ecological harm with zero international consequences. Ecocide law would correct this by holding all actors to the same standard. The risk of selective prosecution is speculative, but the current absence of accountability for ecological atrocities is certain and devastating.
[P2.6] CON’s “better path” is the failing system we have now. Domestic regulation and civil suits treat environmental destruction as a pricing problem. Only international criminal law can reflect its true moral gravity and shift the risk calculus for those who profit from ecocide.

[C2.1] PRO’s strongest move is to call definitional difficulty “impossible.” But the objection is not that ecocide could never be defined; it is that the proposed standard cannot yet tell an executive whether a dam, mine, plantation, or energy project is development or atrocity. “Severe, widespread, or long-term damage” turns criminal liability on contested scientific and political judgments about what counts as permanent, irreversible, or widespread. Criminal law must give fair notice before conduct occurs. PRO’s alternative criteria—lost ecosystem services, regional extinction, irreversible collapse—do not solve this; they move the indeterminacy into ecological forecasting and causation.
[C2.2] A prosecutor may see an ecocide; a planner with permits may see lawful infrastructure. That is not the ordinary margin of imprecision in international crimes. It is criminal liability attached to ordinary economic conduct.
[C2.3] The Rome Statute comparison fails because “widespread or systematic” modifies an attack directed against a civilian population. Ecocide would instead aggregate diffuse harms across complex causal chains: emissions, permits, supply chains, climate feedbacks, and multiple contributors. Genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity have identifiable perpetrators, victims, and protected interests. Collapsing that architecture dilutes criminal law rather than strengthening it.
[C2.4] On enforcement, PRO has not answered the structural problem: international criminal institutions still depend on state consent, territorial jurisdiction, and political referrals. Major emitters and resource states may remain outside the regime, while the ICC is complementary, not a substitute for functioning national systems. Amazon deforestation persists because land governance, inspection, prosecution, and economic incentives are weak. An international label does not create forest rangers, courts, or political will.
[C2.5] PRO’s safeguards answer politicized prosecution after referral, but not selective definition, investigation, or jurisdictional reach by powerful states. “Better path” is not the status quo: domestic criminal offenses, strict civil liability, independent regulators, and cross-border enforcement would make liability predictable and enforceable. The motion adds an elastic symbol, not protection.
