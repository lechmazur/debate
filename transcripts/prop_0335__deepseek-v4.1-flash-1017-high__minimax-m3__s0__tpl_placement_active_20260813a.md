The victim is not simply fooled by a fake invoice; they are immersed in a manufactured relationship designed to convert affection into payments and data.
[P1.2] That is a consumer-protection problem because the harm is produced by the design and distribution of synthetic intimacy, not only by a final fraudulent transfer.
[P1.3] Ordinary online-fraud frameworks are built around a discrete deceptive transaction: was money moved, was a chargeback possible, can the perpetrator be identified? AI romance scams defeat each question. The money often leaves as “gifts” or “help” after weeks of emotional investment; victims may not self-identify as defrauded because the relationship feels real; the operator may be offshore and disposable; and the platform hosting the persona may bear no clear duty. A consumer-protection lens asks different, earlier questions: Are synthetic profiles clearly disclosed?
[P1.4] Are platforms required to detect coordinated fake-persona networks? Are payment rails adding friction before irreversible transfers? Are victims given specialized reporting, support, and restitution channels that account for shame and trauma?
[P1.5] Distinct treatment also targets the vulnerable before loss. Regulators can require safety by design in AI companion products and dating platforms without waiting to prove a criminal case.
[P1.6] This is not about inventing a new crime or inflating every broken heart into a lawsuit. It is about acknowledging that a scalable market in manufactured intimacy creates a distinct consumer risk. Governments already separate product safety from ordinary theft because the remedy is prevention and design. AI romance scams deserve the same logic.
