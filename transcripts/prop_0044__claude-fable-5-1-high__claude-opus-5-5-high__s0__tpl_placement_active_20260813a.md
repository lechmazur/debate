[P1.1] When you pick up a smart thermostat in a store, the box tells you the color, the dimensions, the Wi-Fi standard, the price. It does not tell you the one fact that decides whether that thermostat is worth anything in four years: how long the company will keep the servers running and the security patches flowing. That silence is not an accident. It is the single most valuable piece of information about the product, and it is the one piece the seller is currently allowed to withhold.
[P1.2] Smart devices are different from ordinary goods. A toaster with no manufacturer is still a toaster. A smart speaker with no manufacturer is a paperweight that may also be an open door onto your home network. Owners of the Revolv home hub learned this when Google shut it down after buying the company; Spotify's Car Thing owners learned it when the device was remotely retired.
[P1.3] In each case the hardware worked perfectly. The product died by decision, and buyers had no way to price that risk at checkout.
[P1.4] The motion asks for something modest and precise: a guaranteed support window, printed where the buyer can see it before paying. It does not dictate how long that window must be. A company that only wants to promise two years may say two years. What it may not do is imply permanence and deliver abandonment.
[P1.5] Three things follow. First, the market starts working: longevity becomes a visible feature you can compare on the shelf, so firms that invest in long support finally get rewarded for it, the way energy labels made efficiency something manufacturers compete on. Second, waste falls, because devices stop being discarded for software reasons while the hardware is fine.
[P1.6] Third, the word "guaranteed" gives the promise teeth, turning a vague blog post about end-of-life into an enforceable consumer commitment.
[P1.7] Manufacturers already know their planned support horizon. Every one of them decides it internally. The only question is whether the person paying for the device deserves to know it too.
