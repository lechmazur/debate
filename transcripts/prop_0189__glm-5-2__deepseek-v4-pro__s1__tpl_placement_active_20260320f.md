Stormwater floods the system, and untreated sewage overflows directly into rivers, lakes, and harbors. That is not a malfunction—it is how the system was designed to operate.
[P1.2] Continued "overflow management" means managing a public health failure that recurs by design, not by accident.
[P1.3] The motion's key phrase is "even when the upfront cost is much higher." That framing hides the real comparison. Overflow management is not a one-time expense amortized over decades. It is a permanent operating cost—storage tunnels, real-time controls, treatment additives, monitoring, beach closures, fish kills, contamination advisories—that compounds every year and worsens as climate change intensifies rainfall events.
[P1.4] A city that "manages" overflows is paying indefinitely for the privilege of periodically poisoning its own waterways. Separation is expensive once. Continued overflow management is expensive forever.
[P1.5] More importantly, separation changes what the system *is*. A combined system cannot, by physics, avoid discharging raw sewage during wet weather. Separation removes the structural coupling that makes that failure inevitable. Green infrastructure—permeable pavement, bioswales, detention basins—becomes possible once stormwater is not funneled into the sanitary network.
[P1.6] The city gains capacity it can direct toward growth rather than dumping into rivers.
[P1.7] The opponent will emphasize cost. But cost measured against what? Against the price of chronic contamination? Against EPA consent decrees that force fixes anyway, just after years of penalty payments and litigation?
[P1.8] Against the equity reality that overflow outfalls cluster in lower-income neighborhoods?
[P1.9] Cities facing this choice are not choosing between a cheap option and an expensive one.
