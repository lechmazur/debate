[P1.1] Domestic subsidies and regulations alone guarantee failure on global emissions because they trigger carbon leakage. When Washington imposes costly rules on American steel, cement, or chemicals while leaving imports untouched, production simply shifts to coal-heavy factories in India, Vietnam, or China. The smokestacks move across borders; the carbon stays in the atmosphere. The United States may report flat or falling territorial emissions, but global totals barely budge—or actually rise as dirty production expands to fill the gap left by cleaner American industry.
[P1.2] A carbon border adjustment cuts this leak at the source. By pricing the carbon embedded in imported goods, it removes the competitive penalty for American firms that comply with domestic standards while imposing a direct cost on foreign producers who pollute freely. Faced with a choice between paying the tariff or cleaning up their production, exporters serving the massive U.S. market will decarbonize. The incentive is immediate and structural: lose access to American consumers, or modernize your entire supply chain.
[P1.3] Consider steel. American mills have invested in cleaner electric-arc furnaces, yet they continue losing market share to Chinese competitors running coal-fired blast furnaces. Without a border adjustment, U.S. climate policy merely deindustrializes the Midwest while Chinese emissions climb to meet American demand. With a border adjustment, Chinese steelmakers face a direct price on their carbon intensity.
[P1.4] To remain competitive in the United States, they must adopt cleaner technology—or cede the market to greener rivals. Either outcome reduces global emissions worldwide.
[P1.5] Subsidies, by contrast, are a fiscal treadmill with zero leverage over foreign behavior. They spend taxpayer money to make domestic production slightly cleaner while doing nothing to stop the global flow of carbon-intensive goods into American ports. Over the next five years, a border adjustment delivers immediate, enforceable pressure on supply chains across Asia and Latin America, whereas subsidies deliver only marginal domestic gains while the rest of the world continues business as usual.
