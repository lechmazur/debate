[P1.1] The real question is not how fast a car arrives; it is what kind of help arrives. For someone in psychotic crisis, a suicidal teenager, or an autistic adult overwhelmed by noise, a police cruiser is often the wrong vehicle. Officers are trained to enforce law, control scenes, and identify threats. That training can turn confusion into confrontation: a person who cannot follow commands may be tased, tackled, or arrested.
[P1.2] Even without serious injury, the outcome is a jail cell, an emergency room, and deeper distrust—none of which treats the crisis.
[P1.3] Non-police crisis teams change the intervention. Clinicians, peer specialists, and medics can slow down, assess, de-escalate, and connect people to treatment, housing, or follow-up care. They are not there to arrest; they are there to solve the underlying problem. That is why families often ask for them by name, and why they tend to produce fewer arrests and less use of force.
[P1.4] A slightly longer wait for a counselor who can help beats a rapid armed response that escalates.
[P1.5] The motion accepts that response times may rise in some neighborhoods. That is a cost, but it is not the decisive metric. Speed matters most when someone is bleeding or a shooter is active. For mental health calls, speed without fit can be dangerous.
[P1.6] A five-minute police arrival that ends in force is not faster than a twenty-minute clinician who gets the person into care. Police time freed from mental health calls can also be redirected to violent crime.
[P1.7] Finally, equity cuts toward expansion. Poor and minority neighborhoods are often both over-policed and under-served by mental health care. A longer wait for a non-police team is not the same harm as a faster route to jail or a funeral. Cities should expand coverage, triage by risk, and send armed officers only when weapons or violence make them necessary.
[P1.8] The choice is not fast versus slow; it is right versus wrong.

[C1.1] A mental health crisis is an emergency, not an appointment that can wait for the ideal responder. A teenager who has swallowed pills, a man cutting himself, a woman in psychosis wandering into traffic—in those minutes, the window to intervene closes. Delay is not a neutral cost; it is the difference between reaching someone alive and arriving after harm becomes irreversible.
[C1.2] The proposition asks cities to accept slower arrival in "some neighborhoods." Those will not be wealthy blocks beside a hospital. They will be areas farthest from staffing bases, hardest to recruit to, and already short on ambulances—often the same poor communities invoked to justify the change. A slower tier of emergency help there is not equity; it tells vulnerable residents that who responds matters more than whether anyone arrives in time.
[C1.3] Dispatch labels do not guarantee safety. A call screened as a nonviolent person off medication can become a person with a kitchen knife, a child locked in a bathroom, or an adult assaulting a parent. Unarmed clinicians cannot force entry, restrain someone, or protect bystanders. They must retreat and call police anyway—now with two delays instead of one, and a scene given time to deteriorate.
[C1.4] Police alone are not ideal, but safety and care should arrive together: co-response, crisis-trained officers, and clinicians embedded with emergency services. Removing the officer sacrifices the capability that makes de-escalation possible when words fail.
[C1.5] Expansion also collides with scarcity. There are not enough crisis clinicians to staff clinics, hospitals, and hotlines around the clock. Spreading them citywide produces thin night coverage, burnout, and longer queues, while pulling staff from follow-up treatment. Success on carefully screened, low-risk daytime calls does not prove a replacement system works at 2 a.m.
[C1.6] Cities should make crisis response faster, safer, and more clinical—not slower, thinner, and more fragile where residents can least afford it.
