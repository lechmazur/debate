[P1.1] The central question is not whether diversity in trials is nice to have. It is whether the cost of slowing approvals is justified by the harm caused by approving drugs without adequate evidence for the full population. It is. And here is why.
[P1.2] Drugs do not behave uniformly across bodies. Metabolism varies by sex, age, and genetic ancestry in ways that determine both efficacy and toxicity. Warfarin dosing, for instance, is affected by genetic variants that differ markedly across ethnic populations. Many cardiac drugs were tested predominantly on middle-aged men, then prescribed to women who metabolize them differently and suffer higher rates of adverse effects.
[P1.3] Older patients, who are the primary consumers of most approved drugs, are routinely excluded from the trials that generate the approval data. The result is not merely an academic gap—it is a pattern of licensed drugs doing predictable harm to groups whose biology was never properly studied.
[P1.4] The opposition will say speed saves lives. That framing only holds if we count the lives of trial-eligible populations and discount everyone else. When a drug is approved without adequate data on elderly patients and then prescribed to millions of them, the adverse events that follow are real deaths, real hospitalizations—just conveniently invisible at the approval date. Speed that produces a narrow evidence base is not inherently safe; it is cost-shifting.
[P1.5] The harm is deferred, diffused, and therefore easier to ignore.
[P1.6] Proportionate representation is not an obstacle dropped onto an otherwise well-functioning system. It is the correction for a decades-long structural failure. The motion asks us to accept some additional time in exchange for evidence that actually covers the people who will take the drug. That is not a concession; it is the definition of good science.
[P1.7] The question for this debate is whether demographic gaps in trial evidence constitute acceptable uncertainty. They do not—and no speed argument changes the fact that approval built on incomplete data is incomplete protection.

[C1.2] A therapy delayed by enrollment quotas is a therapy denied to every patient who expires during the artificial wait.
[C1.3] Strict proportionate representation sounds equitable until you try to run the trial. In oncology, requiring ethnic and age quotas that mirror national demographics would stall studies for diseases that disproportionately affect specific populations. A drug for sickle-cell disease, prostate cancer, or pediatric leukemia does not draw from a uniform demographic pool, and forcing artificial balance means recruiting patients who are clinically irrelevant while excluding those most likely to benefit. Science requires sufficient power in relevant subgroups, not demographic mirroring of the general census.
[C1.4] The proposition also mistakes pre-approval trials for the only source of safety data. Post-market surveillance, registries, and real-world evidence already capture how drugs perform across diverse populations once prescribed. The harms of deferral, by contrast, are immediate and concentrated among the sickest patients who need access now.
[C1.5] Most critically, the motion rigs the trade-off backwards. It accepts that regulators should withhold therapies from everyone until data satisfy a demographic checklist. The proposition offers the illusion of perfect information purchased with real blood.
[C1.6] We should reject it.
