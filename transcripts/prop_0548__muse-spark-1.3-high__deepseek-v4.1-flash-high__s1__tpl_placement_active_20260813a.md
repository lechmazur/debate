[P1.1] Violent crime lives in tiny places. In most cities a small share of blocks and intersections produces an outsized share of shootings and robberies. That concentration is the decisive fact. Hot spot policing goes where the violence is, while longer sentences wait until after the next victim is already bleeding.
[P1.2] Criminals respond far more to the risk of getting caught right now than to a threat of extra years far in the future. A visible, predictable police presence in a dangerous block changes the immediate calculation: carry a gun here tonight and you will likely be stopped, searched lawfully, or seen. A sentencing enhancement debated in a legislature changes almost nothing in that moment, especially for young men acting impulsively, in groups, under alcohol or provocation.
[P1.3] Increasing time for repeat offenders also fails on timing and targeting. It punishes only the few who are arrested, convicted, and sentenced after long delay, while leaving the place that generated the crime untouched for the next offender to use. Prisons already hold the highest-rate offenders; adding years mainly warehouses aging men past the age when violent offending peaks, at enormous cost, without preventing the next shooting on the same corner.
[P1.4] Tight accountability is what makes expansion work. Body cameras, clear limits on stops, tracking of complaints and outcomes, and deployment tied to data rather than bias prevent drift into harassment. That legitimacy matters operationally: residents in violent areas cooperate, call, and testify when they trust officers are there to protect the block, not occupy it.
[P1.5] One approach prevents violence before it happens, night after night. The other promises severity years later after harm is done. If the goal is fewer victims, not longer files, concentrated, accountable presence wins. That is both faster and cheaper than building cells to hold yesterday's shooter while today's takes his place.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes geography for cause. A violent block is not a cursed intersection; it is a place where a small number of repeat offenders congregate, feud, and recruit. The more reliable lever is separating those offenders from the street for longer.
[C1.2] Hot spot patrol is a presence effect: it works only while officers are visibly there. Crime responds by moving a block over, shifting hours, or going indoors. Once the unit is redeployed, the corner reverts. Accountability rules—vague, complaint-driven—make that presence slower, less assertive, and more risk-averse.
[C1.3] If officers fear discipline for a lawful but contested stop, the deterrent signal weakens. Expansion spreads scarce patrol across more corners, buying a temporary, displaceable effect.
[C1.4] Longer sentences for repeat offenders do what patrol cannot: they incapacitate the specific people who drive gun violence. A man with multiple prior violent convictions is not a random resident; he is a high-probability future shooter or robber. Keeping him in prison through his peak offending years prevents crimes no corner saturation would stop when he moves to the next block or victim.
[C1.5] It also changes the calculation before the crime. Hot spot policing threatens a stop; a repeat offender may gamble on not being the one stopped. A known, actual sentence—five, ten, more years—attaches to every future offense. That is a larger expected cost for the person already cycling through the system.
[C1.6] Accountability is valuable, but it is a constraint, not a force multiplier. The comparison is not perfect hot spots versus imperfect prisons. It is marginal effect: another year for a repeat violent offender removes a dangerous person for 365 days. Another patrol shift buys hours of deterrence that displacement can erase.
[C1.7] Decide on durability and targeting. Trust does not incapacitate anyone; a prison cell does. On the motion's own comparison, longer sentences for repeat offenders reduce violent crime more.
