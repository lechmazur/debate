[P1.1] Advertising-disclosure rules exist to stop deception, not to privilege a distribution technology. When a trusted intermediary is paid to promote a product and hides that fact, the audience is misled. Large-audience influencers occupy exactly that role. They are not private individuals chatting; they are commercial broadcasters with newsletters, streams, and feeds—the direct modern equivalent of traditional media personalities.
[P1.2] A creator with millions of followers can drive purchases more powerfully than a newspaper columnist or local TV host. The duty to disclose should match the power to persuade.
[P1.3] Consider a traditional TV segment and a sponsored post by an influencer of similar reach. If the broadcaster is paid to feature a product, the segment must be identified as advertising. If the influencer does the same, why should a buried hashtag or a disclosure hidden behind a “more” fold be acceptable? Social media feels personal, which makes covert advertising more manipulative, not less.
[P1.4] Followers believe they are hearing a friend’s honest preference rather than seeing an ad. A regime that forces a magazine to label an advertorial clearly while letting a mega-influencer soften, delay, or obscure the same disclosure is not a sensible adaptation; it is a loophole.
[P1.5] The motion targets influencers with large audiences, so it does not burden ordinary users. It covers creators who operate like media companies, with managers, agencies, and brand contracts. Equal strictness also prevents regulatory arbitrage. If traditional media must disclose conspicuously but influencers face only weak self-regulation, advertisers will migrate to the least transparent channel, dragging down standards everywhere.
[P1.6] Treating influencers as too informal to regulate strictly mistakes style for substance. The proposition demands rules as strict, not mechanically identical. Strictness means the same legal obligation to disclose clearly and the same accountability for deceptive concealment. The format can differ—pinned labels, on-screen text, or clear spoken disclosure—but the duty cannot.
[P1.7] When payment drives the message and the audience is large, the audience must know.

[C1.1] Disclosure exists to prevent deception—on that we agree. But the motion doesn't ask "should influencers disclose paid promotion." It asks whether they should face rules "as strict as" traditional media. That specific demand for formal equivalence is where the case collapses, because it ports rules built for one structure onto a completely different one, and the mismatch makes things worse, not better.
[C1.2] Traditional broadcast disclosure regimes exist inside a peculiar institutional architecture: a handful of licensed networks, public airwaves, in-house legal departments, and a strict editorial/advertising firewall enforced by a single accountable corporate entity. That's why "as strict as" is even coherent there—you're regulating maybe a few thousand gatekeepers. Influencer marketing is millions of individual speakers, across dozens of platforms and jurisdictions, often as sole proprietors with no compliance department. Importing identical strictness doesn't scale up transparency; it scales up arbitrary enforcement.
[C1.3] Regulators will catch a handful of visible mega-influencers as examples while the actual volume of promotional content flows through mid-tier and micro accounts untouched. That's theater, not accountability.
[C1.4] Worse, format-matching backfires functionally. A televised on-screen "sponsored content" banner works because the format is static and passive. Force the same rigid, segregated labeling onto a 15-second video or a scrolling feed, and creators either can't fit it, cut it for time, or bury it in ways more evasive than today's platform-native "Paid Partnership" tags—tags that are algorithmically attached, machine-readable, and appear at the top of the post before a single word is read. That's arguably stronger disclosure than a newspaper's small-print "advertorial," achieved precisely because platforms built tools fitted to their medium rather than copying broadcast rules wholesale.
[C1.5] There's also a chilling-price problem PRO never prices in. "Large audience" is a vague threshold. Rules calibrated for corporate broadcasters, applied identically to individuals near that line, will scare risk-averse creators into refusing brand work, or into disclosing everything so aggressively—including unpaid gifts, affiliate maybes, personal opinions—that audiences tune out disclosure altogether, the classic warning-fatigue problem. Proportionate, platform-appropriate rules protect audiences better than borrowed strictness that nobody can actually enforce evenly.
