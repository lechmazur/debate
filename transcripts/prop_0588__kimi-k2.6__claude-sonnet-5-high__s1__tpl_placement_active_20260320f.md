[P1.1] The next two decades will determine whether we cross critical climate thresholds. In that window, methane is the molecule that matters most. Over a twenty-year horizon, it traps roughly eighty times more heat than carbon dioxide, and atmospheric levels are rising faster than at any point in the instrumental record. Since oil and gas infrastructure is the largest industrial source of anthropogenic methane, tightening leak detection and repair rules is the single fastest lever available to slow near-term warming within the decade we actually have.
[P1.2] This debate turns on a straightforward resource question: where does an equivalent dollar do more atmospheric good now? Leak detection and repair is fundamentally a fix-it operation on hardware that already exists. Crews with infrared cameras, aerial monitoring, and handheld sensors locate fugitive emissions at valves, compressors, and pipelines, then seal them. The climate benefit is immediate because the molecule never reaches the atmosphere, and much of the gas can be sold, offsetting costs.
[P1.3] Carbon capture and storage, by contrast, demands new chemical plants, compression trains, pipelines, and injection wells. It consumes substantial energy to strip CO2 from diluted flue gas, energy that frequently comes from the same fossil system it aims to clean. The result is a slower construction timeline, a postponed climate return, and a price tag that buys far less atmospheric benefit per dollar in the next ten years.
[P1.4] Moreover, CCS leaves the upstream methane problem untouched. Even if capture equipment successfully buries carbon dioxide from power plant or refinery stacks, the wellhead, storage tank, and gathering lines keep venting raw methane into the sky. Because methane is so potent in the near term, preventing even modest volumes of fugitive leaks outweighs capturing larger volumes of CO2 on any meaningful decadal timeframe.
[P1.5] Speed, atmospheric potency, and the logic of equivalent spending all point in one direction. When we can either seal existing vents today or build carbon cemeteries that pay off decades from now, the choice that delivers larger near-term climate benefits is obvious.
