[C1.1] A unified humanitarian visa promises safety before travel, but its central mechanism is rationing, not rescue. Far more people may seek protection than European governments will admit. Once applications exceed places, officials must prioritize, delay, or reject. Those refused, stuck in backlogs, or unable to prove their case remotely do not simply stop fleeing; many still use dangerous routes.
[C1.2] Legalization only drains a black market when lawful supply is genuinely available. A capped, selective visa leaves smugglers with plenty of customers.
[C1.3] Nor does applying “outside EU territory” mean applying safely. A persecuted person may be unable to approach an embassy monitored by their own government. Someone in a region without a participating consulate must first cross borders, find documents, pay for travel, and survive waiting elsewhere. The proposal can therefore replace the deadly sea crossing with a deadly journey to the application queue—while favouring applicants with money, mobility, and paperwork over those in hiding or detention.
[C1.4] PRO’s promise of order also assumes away the hardest question: who is responsible after approval? A common visa requires common eligibility rules, decisions, security procedures, and reception obligations. If member states retain control over admissions and distribution, the scheme is not meaningfully unified. If the EU controls them, every dispute over quotas and frontline responsibility returns in a more explosive form.
[C1.5] Screening before travel does not settle where people live afterward or who supports them.
[C1.6] The humanitarian risk is deeper still. Governments seeking fewer spontaneous arrivals can present overseas processing as the legitimate route while making territorial asylum harsher. A limited visa programme then becomes an excuse to turn away everyone outside it, even though remote procedures cannot reliably assess trauma, credibility, or urgently changing risks.
[C1.7] Neither non-refoulement nor Article 78 dictates this particular instrument. Targeted evacuations, resettlement, and accessible national humanitarian schemes can protect defined groups without creating one fragile EU-wide gatekeeper. The proposition claims to replace luck with procedure; in practice, it replaces one dangerous lottery with another, then gives governments a procedural reason to look away.
