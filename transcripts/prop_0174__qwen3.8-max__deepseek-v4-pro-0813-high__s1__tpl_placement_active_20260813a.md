[P1.1] The central question is not whether new infrastructure is desirable, but whether governments are letting existing concrete fail while buying headlines with megaprojects. In wealthy countries, the dangerous asset is already built: bridges, parking structures, school roofs, water tanks, tunnels, and sea defenses from the postwar construction boom. Concrete looks permanent, but it decays. Carbonation and chloride intrusion corrode reinforcing steel; once rebar rusts, it expands, cracks cover, and capacity falls.
[P1.2] Much of this damage is hidden until spalling, deformation, or collapse.
[P1.3] That is why the safety crisis is under-acknowledged. Official assurances often lag behind engineers’ private warnings. Politicians prefer new projects because opening day is visible, while inspection, repair, and load rating are technical and unglamorous. The result is a predictable cycle: minimal visual inspections, deferred repairs, optimistic life extensions, then emergency closures after a scare.
[P1.4] The Morandi bridge collapse in Italy and the discovery of dangerous RAAC panels in UK schools and hospitals show how wealthy states can be surprised by concrete that was assumed to be safe. Even when no tragedy occurs, lane closures, weight limits, and emergency propping quietly degrade public safety margins.
[P1.5] Tripling maintenance budgets is not arbitrary. It is the minimum corrective needed to move from reactive patching to systematic condition surveys, structural monitoring, cathodic protection, concrete repair, and replacement of elements that cannot be saved. It also creates a hard gate: no new megaproject until the existing stock is funded at a level capable of preventing avoidable failures. New construction can wait; a failed bridge, school roof, or dam cannot be recalled after the fact.
[P1.6] The decisive comparison is between visible prestige and invisible risk. If governments are serious about safety, they must first prove they can keep aging concrete standing. Until maintenance is funded at crisis scale, new megaprojects are a luxury they cannot responsibly afford.
