[P1.1] When you buy a car, you are not leasing access to a set of features — you are purchasing a physical object. The debate over software subscriptions for already-installed hardware is, at its core, a property rights question, and the answer is straightforward: you cannot sell someone something and then charge them again to use it.
[P1.2] The practice works like this. A manufacturer installs hardware — heated seats, adaptive headlights, a more powerful engine mode — into every vehicle on the assembly line, regardless of trim level. The cost of that hardware is already baked into the purchase price. Then, through a software lock, the company withholds access and sells it back to you on a monthly basis.
[P1.3] You paid for the component. It sits in your car. You just cannot use it without a subscription. BMW tested exactly this model with heated rear seats.
[P1.4] Mercedes offered engine power as a paid upgrade on hardware already under the hood. This is not selling a service — it is collecting rent on property the customer already owns.
[P1.5] The principle that makes this wrong is simple: ownership means control. When you buy a physical asset, you acquire a reasonable expectation that its capabilities are yours permanently. Software subscriptions applied to pre-installed hardware invert this relationship. The manufacturer retains perpetual leverage over something sitting in your driveway, something you financed, insured, and maintain.
[P1.6] That leverage does not expire. It grows — because every future software update is another opportunity to restructure what you thought you owned.
[P1.7] The downstream harms compound quickly. Resale value becomes unpredictable when a used car's feature set depends on the new owner continuing a subscription. Features with safety implications — lane assist, enhanced braking sensitivity — become entangled with billing relationships that can lapse. Consumers in lower-income brackets who stretch to afford a vehicle lose access to hardware they literally possess.
[P1.8] The core principle is not complicated: hardware in your car belongs to you. The rest of this debate is about whether manufacturers can obscure that fact. They cannot.
