[P1.1] Cheap meat is a fiction. Its sticker price excludes the fertilizer runoff in waterways, the land and feed footprint, the greenhouse gases, the antibiotic use that erodes modern medicine, and the diet-related disease costs that show up later in hospitals and public budgets. When a product’s price omits those harms, production and consumption expand beyond what is rational, and everyone else pays the bill. Taxing meat production by its full environmental and health externalities is the straightforward correction: charge the industry for what it dumps on the rest of us, and let prices tell the truth.
[P1.2] The affordability objection is real, but it compares a visible price rise with an invisible status quo that is already regressive. Low-income communities are more likely to live near industrial livestock operations and suffer the air and water pollution; low-income households bear a larger share of diet-related illness and climate disruption. The current system does not protect them—it offers cheap meat while externalizing the costs onto their health, their environment, and their public services. A price that hides those costs is not affordable; it is deferred.
[P1.3] And the proposition does not forbid using the revenue to soften the blow. Rebates, expanded school meals, subsidies for vegetables and legumes, and targeted support can protect lower-income households while keeping the incentive to reduce harm. The tax should be phased in and calibrated, but the principle stands: producers who create the damage should pay for it, not taxpayers and not the families living downstream.
[P1.4] The central choice is not “cheap food versus higher prices.” It is whether we keep a pricing system that rewards the most damaging forms of production, or one that makes cleaner production competitive and funds support for the people who need it. Even if some prices rise, a meat tax based on full externalities is fairer, healthier, and more honest than the hidden bill we pay now.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept a certain harm—higher food prices for the poorest households—in exchange for three promises. Each promise fails.
[C1.2] First promise: the tax changes behavior. Food is not a luxury; demand for staple proteins among low-income households is inelastic. When prices rise, families do not eat less overall—they pay more, cut elsewhere, or downgrade to cheaper processed substitutes that are often worse for health. The affluent, who consume the most expensive cuts, barely notice.
[C1.3] So the policy collects pain and revenue but little reduction: a tax on necessity punishes rather than nudges.
[C1.4] Second promise: rebates will protect the poor [P1.3]. The checkout price is paid every day; rebates arrive annually, erode quietly, and get raided when budgets tighten. France's fuel tax triggered the gilets jaunes because working households felt the cost daily and the compensation never arrived. Trusting the rebate means trusting the discipline of every future government: the tax is permanent, the goodwill is not.
[C1.5] Third promise: that "full externalities" can be calculated and charged [P1.1]. They cannot. Health costs depend on entire diets, not individual products; the environmental gap between intensive beef and pastured poultry is enormous. [C1.6] A wrong price does not tell the truth—it launders a guess into law.
[C1.7] A production tax in an open economy does something worse: it squeezes domestic farmers while untaxed imports fill the shelves, exporting the emissions along with the jobs—unless the plan is border tariffs, which raise prices further still.
[C1.8] If the real targets are antibiotics, runoff, methane, and the pollution imposed on nearby communities [P1.2], regulate those directly and subsidize alternatives. That reduces harm without taxing dinner. The motion concedes the regressive cost and asks us to fund speculative benefits with it. The trade fails.
