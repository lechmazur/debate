[P1.1] Agricultural land is unlike any other asset class: it is the irreplaceable substrate of human survival, yet institutional investors treat it as a commodity for portfolio diversification. When pension funds and hedge funds acquire farmland, they impose quarterly return imperatives onto biological cycles measured in decades. This fundamental temporal mismatch erodes the very resilience the motion seeks to protect.
[P1.2] Financialized ownership severs land management from local knowledge. Institutional landlords typically prioritize short-term rental yields over soil health, pressuring tenant farmers to maximize immediate extraction through intensive monoculture rather than rotation and regeneration. When entities like BlackRock or sovereign wealth funds purchase vast tracts in the American Midwest or Ukrainian black earth, decisions about drainage, cover cropping, or barn maintenance get made by analysts in distant offices who will never taste the harvest. The result is accelerating topsoil loss and reduced genetic diversity—fragilities that amplify climate shocks rather than buffer against them.
[P1.3] Beyond ecological brittleness, unchecked accumulation hollows out rural communities. As institutional buyers drive land prices upward—paying premiums driven by portfolio theory rather than agricultural returns—family farmers cannot compete in purchase markets and face rising rents that consume marginal profits. Young farmers exit, schools close, and the social infrastructure of agricultural regions collapses into speculative land banks. The investors capture appreciation; the communities disappear.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim that institutional capital brings efficiency and technology. This conflates scale with productivity: resident owners steward soil as inheritance rather than depreciating asset, maintaining the drainage ditches and windbreaks that absentee landlords neglect to cut costs. The efficiency argument also cannot justify the destruction of irreplaceable human capital—the generational knowledge of microclimates and local ecosystems—that vanishes when farmers become short-term tenants of distant shareholders.
[P1.5] Capping institutional ownership is not hostility to markets; it is a prudent firewall preventing the financial sector from liquidating the productive base of our food system for quarterly returns. We limit foreign ownership of sensitive infrastructure for similar reasons. Agricultural land deserves no less protection.
