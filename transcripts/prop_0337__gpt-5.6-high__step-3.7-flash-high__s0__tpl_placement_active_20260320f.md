[P1.1] A feed optimized for engagement does not merely show teenagers what they like; it experiments on them, discovers which emotional triggers keep them scrolling, and repeats those triggers without a natural stopping point. Minors are still developing impulse control and are unusually sensitive to social reward, novelty, and exclusion. That combination creates a foreseeable risk: sleep displaced by late-night scrolling, attention fragmented by constant checking, and self-worth tethered to an automated popularity contest.
[P1.2] “Less addictive” need not mean a blank screen or state-approved content. It means changing the objective and interface: recommend from topics a user deliberately follows, limit hyper-personalized rabbit holes, add stopping cues, and avoid autoplay or endless refresh for minors. Relevant discovery remains; deliberate use remains; creators can still reach young audiences. What falls is compulsive consumption engineered for its own sake.
[P1.3] Default design is the right intervention because the burden should sit with the party controlling the machinery, not with a fourteen-year-old expected to outsmart it. Safety settings buried behind menus are weak precisely because addictive design exploits inertia. Platforms already rank every item and choose every nudge; requiring a safer ranking default changes an existing editorial choice rather than censoring speech. The platform captures the advertising upside while families absorb the developmental downside.
[P1.4] Reasonable age assurance can be proportionate and privacy-preserving; imperfect enforcement is not a reason for zero protection.
[P1.5] Lower engagement is not a regrettable side effect that defeats the policy. When the product is optimized to maximize time on platform, some lost engagement is the measure of success: more sleep, homework, sport, conversation, or simply chosen boredom. The counterfactual is not frictionless entertainment versus paternalism. It is corporate optimization versus a modest childhood safeguard.
[P1.6] Adults routinely demand safer defaults for products children cannot adequately evaluate; digital feeds deserve the same principle. Protect developmental autonomy now so young people can exercise meaningful autonomy later.
