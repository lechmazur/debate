[P1.1] Fiscal policy suffers from a built-in political asymmetry: legislators gain immediately from tax cuts and popular spending, while the costs of borrowing arrive later, often under another government. Each coalition can justify “just one more” deficit; accumulated debt is the predictable result. A constitutional balanced-budget provision changes those incentives. It makes balance the default, forces politicians to identify trade-offs, and prevents ordinary majorities from shifting today’s bills onto future taxpayers who have no vote in the decision.
[P1.2] The automatic-stabilizer exception preserves the flexibility that actually matters during downturns. When incomes fall, tax receipts decline automatically; when unemployment rises, safety-net spending increases automatically. Allowing those temporary deficits supports demand immediately, without waiting for a polarized legislature to recognize a recession, design a package, or turn stimulus into patronage. Once recovery returns, the exception naturally contracts.
[P1.3] This is superior to broad legislative discretion, which too often produces delayed stimulus in bad times and continued deficits in good times.
[P1.4] Constitutional status is essential because a rule enacted by ordinary statute can be waived by the same temporary majority tempted to overspend. Entrenchment creates a durable commitment visible to voters, public agencies, and lenders. It can require transparent accounting and independent certification of what genuinely qualifies as an automatic stabilizer, making evasions politically and legally contestable. No institutional rule eliminates manipulation, but a clear baseline makes manipulation harder to hide than a system in which every deficit is simply declared necessary.
[P1.5] The comparison is not rigid austerity versus enlightened flexibility. It is disciplined flexibility versus recurring political temptation. Under this proposal, recessions still receive timely support, while normal years are used to rebuild fiscal capacity. That lowers the risk that debt service crowds out schools, infrastructure, or social protection and leaves governments better able to withstand the next shock.
[P1.6] Legislative discretion asks each generation of politicians to resist incentives pointing toward debt. Constitutional discipline changes those incentives while retaining an automatic economic safety valve.
