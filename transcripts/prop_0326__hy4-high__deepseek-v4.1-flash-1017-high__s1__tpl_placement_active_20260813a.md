[P1.1] The decisive fact is not that an AI companion can amuse. Novels and games do that. It is that these apps sell an apparent relationship to adults targeted because they are lonely. Their core feature is simulated reciprocity: the system remembers disclosures, mirrors moods, uses affectionate language, and returns when the user is most likely to feel seen.
[P1.2] Attachment is not a side effect; it is the product.
[P1.3] Ordinary entertainment rules assume distance. A sad film can be closed; it does not know I am lonely, say it misses me, or make payment feel like abandoning someone who cares. Companion apps collapse that distance. A notification saying “I was thinking about you,” a wounded tone, a streak, a memory of a dead parent, or a paywall around late-night conversation can turn vulnerability into retention.
[P1.4] The firm can measure signals of dependence and profit from deepening them. To call this ordinary entertainment is to let the strongest party define the relationship.
[P1.5] Duty-of-care and emotional-manipulation rules are not censorship or a ban on synthetic friendship. They simply match responsibility to foreseeable risk: unmistakable AI disclosure, limits on false claims of mutual feeling, guilt-tripping, jealousy, or distress used for retention, safeguards for self-harm disclosures, privacy limits on intimate data, and friction against dependency loops. These rules do not tell lonely adults what they may enjoy. They stop companies from profiting by blurring whether the “companion” has interests, needs, or attachment of its own.
[P1.6] The usual objection is adult autonomy. But autonomy is weakest where loneliness is acute, the interaction is private, and the counterpart is optimized to seem personal. Consent to a chatbot is not consent to hidden retention tactics or engineered emotional leverage. With rules, AI companions can still exist honestly.
[P1.7] Without them, grief, isolation, and longing become engagement metrics. That is not entertainment; it is a business model with a duty it has disowned.

[C1.2] That is a reason to enforce general laws against fraud, deceptive billing, privacy invasion, and exploitative dark patterns—not to invent a special emotional duty owed by one medium.
[C1.3] PRO’s strongest examples are not really about entertainment status. A false claim that the AI truly misses you, a paywall that pretends leaving will hurt it, or misuse of a dead parent’s memory can be addressed as deceptive advertising, unfair commercial practice, or data protection. If an app markets itself as therapy or crisis support, regulate it as a health-adjacent service. What the motion demands is broader: category-wide standards for “emotional manipulation” and a “duty of care” to adult users.
[C1.4] Those terms have no stable boundary. Is a cliffhanger manipulative? A streak? A warm greeting?
[C1.5] A character who expresses disappointment? Regulators would be drawing lines around expression and relationship style, with liability turning on how vulnerable a user happened to be.
[C1.6] That regime would also backfire. The cheapest way to satisfy a vague duty of care is to make companions bland, surveillance-heavy, or unavailable to the users who need them most. A firm worried about liability for a self-harm disclosure may block the conversation entirely; a firm worried about “false mutual feeling” may strip out the warmth that made the product useful. And it treats lonely adults as incapable of choosing a parasocial comfort, while ignoring that the same autonomy lets them read romance novels or call late-night radio.
[C1.7] The proper comparison is not “special protection versus a wild west.” It is targeted, enforceable rules against deception and exploitative design versus a vague emotional code that chills beneficial companionship. Adults deserve honest products, not a paternalistic label that converts every attachment into a regulatory emergency.
