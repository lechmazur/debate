[P1.1] Workplace AI is not just a new tool. It often becomes the rule-maker: allocating shifts, setting pace, ranking applicants, scoring calls, flagging “underperformance,” recommending discipline, and deciding which tasks a person sees next. Those are terms and conditions of work. Large employers should therefore be legally required to negotiate deployment with elected worker councils: the people whose jobs are being redesigned need an organized, informed voice before the redesign is locked in.
[P1.2] That duty is modest. It is not a worker veto, a ban on automation, or a command to surrender proprietary code. It requires usable disclosure, good-faith bargaining before rollout and major upgrades, and agreement-seeking over safeguards: training, workload limits, human review of adverse decisions, data use, monitoring boundaries, redeployment, and error correction. Elected councils matter because individual employees lack leverage and scattered complaints arrive after harm is done; representatives can build expertise, compare patterns across departments, and speak without being handpicked by management.
[P1.3] Employer-only governance is structurally unreliable. The firm buys the system, controls the data, frames the efficiency gains, and can treat lower headcount, intensified monitoring, or shifted safety risks as someone else’s cost. A warehouse algorithm that trims walking time can also compress breaks and raise injury risk; a call-center scoring model can make an eight-hour shift continuous surveillance. Workers often know these failures first, but voluntary consultation gives them nothing when management can simply proceed.
[P1.4] The objection from speed and managerial freedom gets the comparison backwards. Large firms already have procurement, legal, compliance, and integration processes; if they can afford an AI rollout, they can afford negotiation with the people who must make it work. Councils reduce costly misfires, resistance, and turnover by surfacing practical knowledge before deployment. Speed without legitimacy produces opaque systems, grievances, and bad implementation.
[P1.5] The choice is not innovation versus obstruction; it is unilateral control versus accountable adoption.

[C1.1] Mandatory negotiation is bad governance design because it attaches a slow, adversarial bargaining duty to a technology that changes continuously. “Deployment” cannot be limited to the first rollout; AI is retrained, tuned, and extended. A hospital improving a fall-risk warning, a warehouse adjusting routing, a bank recalibrating fraud filters could each be forced back to the table with an elected council at every major update. The predictable result is delay, legal uncertainty, and hold-up, not better workplace protection.
[C1.2] The mechanism is mismatched to expertise and accountability. Elected worker councils represent a political constituency, not necessarily the workers whose tasks an AI system affects, and they seldom have the skills to judge model error, audit results, or safety tradeoffs. Giving them mandatory bargaining power means a council can extract staffing floors, resist data use, or block a safety improvement—not because the AI is harmful, but because negotiation is leverage.
[C1.3] Meanwhile the people harmed by bad decisions include patients, customers, investors, and future workers; the council does not answer to them.
[C1.4] The claim that this duty is modest is not how bargaining works in practice. “Good faith” disputes generate years of litigation. An employer that already satisfies privacy, safety, anti-discrimination, and labor law can still be accused of failing to negotiate enough. This does not make AI safe; it creates process risk and compliance cost. Firms respond by delaying deployment, watering down useful tools, or moving the decision elsewhere.
[C1.5] The better path is to enforce clear, specific duties: notice, audits, human review, non-discrimination, safety standards. Worker voice can be encouraged through voluntary councils, unions where chosen, and consultation. But making a one-size-fits-all elected council a legal tollgate on AI deployment would slow beneficial adoption and shift control to the least accountable and least expert part of the system. The motion does not merely add accountability; it substitutes negotiation for sound regulation.
