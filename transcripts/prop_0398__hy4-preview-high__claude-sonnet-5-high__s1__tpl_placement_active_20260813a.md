[P1.1] Pay is set in markets, not in a vacuum. A headquarters salary is not merely a reward for output; it is a market-clearing price that embeds a location premium—higher pay for expensive housing, commuting, taxes, and local wage competition. Labor has no single global price; the same role commands different pay in different cities because markets differ. When a remote worker moves to a much cheaper region, that premium stops buying anything.
[P1.2] The worker still wins the main benefit of the move: lower costs. Keeping the full headquarters salary would create an unfair double windfall—high nominal pay plus low expenses—while charging the employer for a cost of living the employee no longer faces.
[P1.3] Take a Seattle designer earning around $130,000 who relocates where rents are roughly 40% lower. Rent might fall from about $3,000 to roughly $1,400; groceries and services follow. Even if her pay adjusts to roughly $100,000, her real standard of living can rise, because the cost side falls faster than the cut. She should not expect to keep the whole $130,000 as a permanent bonus for having once lived in an expensive city.
[P1.4] Location adjustment also protects remote work itself. If movers keep headquarters bands, firms will either restrict remote hiring to expensive hubs or create a two-tier workforce: colleagues doing identical work paid very differently based on where they used to live. That invites resentment and pushes firms toward contractors or offshore hires instead. Adjusting to local markets keeps remote roles open and ties pay to the real cost of replacing the worker.
[P1.5] Exceptions exist—contracts, scarce skills, short transitions. But the general default should be location adjustment. The move is voluntary; the worker trades pay for lower costs and a chosen lifestyle. Fair pay follows the market and the cost of living, not a perpetual headquarters entitlement.

[C1.2] Location adjustment also ignores who actually competes for remote talent. A remote engineer in a cheaper city isn't competing against local baristas for local wages—she's competing against every other remote-capable engineer nationwide, many of whom kept headquarters-level pay. If her employer cuts her to "local market rate," a rival firm paying national rate simply hires her away. [C1.3] The market PRO invokes to justify cuts is the same market that punishes those cuts.

Somehow that savings never gets redistributed downward.
[C1.5] Finally, consider the mechanism required to enforce this: employers verifying addresses, tracking moves, adjusting pay by zip code—a surveillance regime built on residence rather than performance. Two employees doing identical work, sitting one town apart, could be paid meaningfully differently based on a metro-area boundary that has nothing to do with skill or output. That's not fair market pricing—it's arbitrary tax on where someone sleeps.
