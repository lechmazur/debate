[P1.1] Public universities exist at public expense for public benefit. That dual fact should anchor this entire debate. When taxpayer dollars fund higher education, the public has a legitimate stake in whether those dollars produce graduates who can participate productively in the economy.
[P1.2] Right now, that stake is being squandered. We have well-documented skills shortages in nursing, engineering, computer science, and various technical health fields—shortages that delay infrastructure projects, strain healthcare systems, and constrain economic growth. Simultaneously, public universities continue to expand enrollment in programs with consistently poor employment outcomes, graduating students into underemployment and debt. This is not a fringe concern or a hypothetical mismatch.
[P1.3] It is a measurable, recurring pattern that wastes human potential and public money.
[P1.4] The proposition offers a measured response. Capping enrollment in low-employment programs does not mean eliminating them. It means recognizing that when a field consistently produces more graduates than the labor market can absorb, unlimited expansion serves no one—not the students who face dim prospects, not the taxpayers who subsidize the instruction, and not the economy that needs skills elsewhere. Redirecting those resources toward shortage fields creates a better alignment between what universities produce and what the nation needs.
[P1.5] Consider what happens without this policy. A student drawn to a low-employment program enrolls, accumulates debt, and graduates into a market with few openings. Meanwhile, a qualified applicant is turned away from a nursing or engineering program because capacity is full. One student's education becomes another's missed opportunity.
[P1.6] That is the real cost of refusing to act.
[P1.7] The opposition will invoke academic freedom and the intrinsic value of all disciplines. Those are important principles—but they do not override the obligation of publicly funded institutions to be responsible stewards of limited resources and student futures. A cap is a floor on accountability, not a ceiling on aspiration.

Public universities serve the public interest best not by micromanaging degree supply, but by maintaining broad educational capacity that endures macroeconomic shifts.
[C1.2] First, chasing shortages creates market whiplash. Labor markets are volatile and cyclical; academic pipelines are slow to retool. Just years ago, computer science was heralded as an inexhaustible shortage demanding massive expansion. Today, entry-level tech graduates face severe hiring freezes and automation-driven contractions.
[C1.3] Forcing universities to mandate enrollment caps based on trailing labor indicators guarantees tomorrow's structural gluts, leaving taxpayers with stranded capital costs and students with degrees in freshly saturated fields.
[C1.4] Second, the proposition commits a fatal accounting error. Furthermore, bottlenecks in shortage fields like nursing are driven by shortages of clinical hospital placements and certified faculty, not excess literature students.
[C1.5] Capping affordable majors will not solve those external constraints; it will simply destabilize university budgets.
[C1.6] Finally, this policy misunderstands long-term economic resilience. In an era reshaped by artificial intelligence, narrow technical skills depreciate faster than ever. The disciplines PRO targets build the durable capabilities essential for a forty-year career: complex communication, cognitive adaptability, and critical reasoning. Capping access at public institutions turns comprehensive education into an exclusive luxury reserved for wealthy students at private universities, while trapping public graduates in rigid vocational tracks.
