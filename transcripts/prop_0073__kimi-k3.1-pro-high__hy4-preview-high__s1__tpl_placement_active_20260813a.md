[P1.1] When a public institution still draws income, prestige, or usable property from land seized from Indigenous communities, the injustice is not confined to the past. It is reproduced in every budget the land supports. A university built with proceeds from expropriated territory, or a government agency leasing that territory today, cannot honestly describe itself as merely inheriting history: it continues to enjoy the asset while the dispossessed community continues to bear its absence.
[P1.2] That continuing benefit creates a duty of restitution, not a demand for ancestral collective guilt. The responsible party is the institution holding the advantage, and the duty arises under the motion’s specific conditions: historical seizure, an identifiable Indigenous community, and a demonstrable ongoing public benefit. This is not an invitation to reopen every boundary or punish people for where they were born.
[P1.3] The motion’s remedies are deliberately practical. Where land is surplus, culturally significant, and transferable, return can restore both an economic base and self-determination. Where return would disrupt essential public services or legitimate third-party interests, long-term revenue sharing ties repayment to the benefit actually received. Equivalent restitution—such as land purchases, endowments, or community-controlled development funds—covers other cases.
[P1.4] Negotiation should determine the form; it should not become a veto over whether anything is owed.
[P1.5] Long-term arrangements matter because a one-time payment can be consumed while the institutional advantage persists. A community whose land financed generations of public education needs durable capacity: housing, language revitalization, health services, and investment chosen by its own governing institutions. Restitution therefore repairs a relationship as well as a balance sheet.
[P1.6] Opposition will likely invoke cost, delay, and administrative difficulty. But difficulty measures the work of repair, not the absence of obligation. Keeping an asset because returning or sharing its proceeds would be inconvenient simply makes the original seizure fiscally rewarding. Nor does restitution require impoverishing schools or parks: phased agreements, capped revenue shares, and equivalent assets can preserve essential services.
[P1.7] The decisive principle is simple: public institutions may keep benefiting from seized land only if they stop treating that benefit as cost-free. [clipped]

It turns every current budget of a university, hospital, or park into a disputed accounting exercise, because the trigger is not a discrete wrong but ‘historically seized land’ wherever an institution still draws some benefit [P1.2].
[C1.2] Consider a state university on nineteenth-century ceded territory. Its research forest, farm leases, and tuition-supported programs are funded by revenue commingled with state taxes and present-day labor. No method isolates the portion of current income ‘still’ attributable to the original seizure rather than to later public investment. ‘Demonstrable ongoing public benefit’ [P1.2] therefore becomes arbitrary: any dollar can be traced to the land, or none can, depending on the accountant. A duty that cannot be measured cannot be owed fairly.
[C1.3] Even where benefit is clear, the remedies harm the public they claim to help. Land return collides with third parties and essential services: a school, hospital, or utility cannot be vacated without displacing current patients, students, homeowners, and businesses, many with no link to the original wrong. Revenue sharing is a perpetual tax on operating budgets; it raises tuition and fees or cuts clinics and transit that Indigenous families also use. Equivalent restitution is often unaffordable: buying replacement land or endowments at today’s prices can dwarf the historical asset’s value and crowd out core public spending.
[C1.4] This is not mere inconvenience [P1.6]; it is fiscal displacement of present needs.
[C1.5] The better metric is present need, not historical tracing. Injustice should be answered with tax-funded investment in housing, health, education, and cultural preservation for disadvantaged communities today. That aid is revisable and fiscally honest; the motion’s property-based liability is not. It forces institutions to manage a sprawling ledger of ancient wrongs instead of delivering universal services, and shifts cost to future taxpayers who seized nothing.
[C1.6] Restitution that cannot be measured or administered cannot be just.

If no one can isolate the portion of current value “still” traceable to the seizure rather than to later public investment, then “equivalent restitution” cannot be equivalent; it is a bargaining outcome.
[C2.2] Worse, the proxy grows with the public system: schools, roads, clinics, and labor make the land valuable. Charging that enhanced value to the claim converts public investment into a private annuity.
[C2.3] PRO also reassures us that “Nobody needs to evict patients or homeowners” [P2.4] and that revenue sharing attaches only to the attributable benefit, not clinics or transit [P2.5]. Present-need investment is not letting the “beneficiary decide when the ledger closes” [P2.7]; it is measurable, democratically revisable, and aimed at deprivation in housing, health, and education.
[C2.5] A perpetual revenue share floats with booms unrelated to the seizure and can fall when the community most needs stable funding.
[C2.6] So the clash is stark. PRO offers an unmeasurable, open-ended ledger and calls it repair. CON offers accountable public investment. Restitution that cannot be measured or administered cannot be just.

[P4.4] The crucial distinction is who controls revision: general appropriations can be withdrawn unilaterally, whereas restitution gives the affected community standing in determining how repair occurs.
[P4.5] This answers CON’s central dilemma. They demand exact historical measurement before recognizing any duty, yet exact retention of the entire benefit is their preferred baseline. Approximation in repair is not a greater injustice than precision in nonpayment. [P4.6] CON’s remaining comparison also fails. Present-need programs may be valuable, but they are funded from the same public budgets, exposed to the same fiscal tradeoffs, and revocable regardless of whether an institution still enjoys the disputed asset. They can supplement restitution; they cannot erase it.
[P4.7] PRO therefore wins on both administrability and justice. Land return where feasible, protected revenue arrangements where useful, and equivalent restitution elsewhere prevent evictions and uncapped burdens.
