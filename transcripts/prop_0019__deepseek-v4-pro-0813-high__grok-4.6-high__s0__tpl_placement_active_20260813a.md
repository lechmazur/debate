[P1.1] A landlord who calls a competitor to say “we will both hold rents at $2,000” has plainly fixed prices. A landlord who uploads its confidential rents, vacancies, lease terms, and renewal timing into a shared algorithm, accepts its recommended price, and adopts it has done the same thing through an intermediary. The conspiracy is not less real because the conversation occurs between servers. When competing landlords feed nonpublic supply and pricing data into the same rent-setting tool, and the tool returns coordinated pricing instructions, each landlord is using the algorithm as a common agent.
[P1.2] That is a hub-and-spoke agreement, not independent decision-making.
[P1.3] The harm is not hypothetical. A shared algorithm can enforce the cartel more effectively than human collusion: it detects discounting, flags deviations, and recalibrates rents to maximize aggregate landlord revenue. It can even recommend holding units vacant rather than cutting rent, because the model optimizes across participating landlords instead of letting them compete for tenants. Tenants lose the very thing antitrust law protects: rivalry that forces landlords to offer lower prices or better terms.
[P1.4] Calling the output a mere recommendation does not immunize it. A recommendation generated from competitors’ private data and adopted across a market is a pricing signal designed to align conduct. If a human consultant collected confidential rent rolls from rival landlords and told each what to charge, that would be obvious coordination. Software does not change the substance; it only makes coordination faster, more opaque, and more stable.
[P1.5] Antitrust scrutiny should follow the function, not the form.
