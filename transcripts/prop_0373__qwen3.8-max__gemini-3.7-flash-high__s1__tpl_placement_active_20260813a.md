[P1.1] The proposition is not that every title belongs in every child’s hands. It is that our current wave of challenges and removals has passed the point where legitimate concern ends and censorship begins. Public schools and libraries serve many families, not one household. When a single complaint can trigger pulling books from shelves or reading lists, the result is not careful curation; it is privatized veto power over other people’s children.
[P1.2] The core problem is overbreadth. Some objections are genuinely age-based: a graphic passage may be unsuitable for an elementary classroom, and a parent can reasonably ask for an alternative assignment. But recent challenges routinely go far beyond that. They target whole libraries, seek to restrict books for advanced teens because a younger student might glimpse them, and often focus on works by or about Black, Indigenous, LGBTQ+, immigrant, or religious-minority people.
[P1.3] Even when a title is not removed, the chilling effect is real: librarians and teachers stop acquiring or recommending anything controversial, so students lose access to materials no formal decision ever condemned.
[P1.4] That is why the test cannot be “is this book appropriate for the youngest possible reader?” If it were, high-school literature would be selected by kindergarten standards. The test should be whether a public institution has a fair, expert-led process and whether the remedy is proportional. Age-based placement, opt-outs, content notes, or restricted shelves can address real concerns. Wholesale removal cannot.
[P1.5] It treats discomfort as danger, substitutes one family’s preferences for a plural community’s judgment, and most harms students who need books as mirrors: a queer teenager, a student from a minority faith, a young person confronting grief or abuse. Those readers matter too. The motion wins because the excess is not incidental; it is the point: challenges now aim at erasing viewpoints, not merely adjusting access.

[C1.5] Challenging material is civic participation at work. Ensuring that school libraries contain age-appropriate, educationally sound resources is not going too far; it is fulfilling the basic contract between public schools and the families they serve.
