[P1.1] A house is not a stock. When a family buys a single-family home, they are buying shelter, stability, and a foothold in a community. When a large institutional investor buys that same home, they are buying a cash-flow instrument. The motion asks whether cities should keep the second buyer from drowning out the first in markets that are already too tight to absorb both.
[P1.2] They should, and here is the core of why.
[P1.3] First, the harm is concentrated and direct. Institutional buyers don't spread their purchases evenly. They cluster in specific, often moderate-priced neighborhoods, the exact starter homes first-time local buyers depend on. They come with all-cash offers, waived inspections, and the ability to close in days.
[P1.4] An ordinary family with a mortgage contingency simply cannot compete with that on a Tuesday afternoon. The result isn't a fair auction; it's a structural mismatch where the local buyer loses before bidding begins.
[P1.5] Second, the supply argument cuts our way, not CON's. These investors are not building new housing. They are converting existing owner-occupied stock into permanent rentals. Every such purchase doesn't add a home; it removes one from the ownership ladder forever, and converts a potential owner into a permanent tenant of a distant landlord.
[P1.6] That permanently shifts wealth-building away from local households toward shareholders.
[P1.7] Third, the remedy is narrow and proportionate, which matters. The motion is not a blanket ban on rentals or investment. It targets large institutional buyers, in single-family homes, in tight markets where crowding-out is already demonstrable. That is a scalpel, not a sledgehammer.
[P1.8] Cities already zone, license, and tax housing differently by use; preferencing owner-occupants in a defined emergency is squarely within that tradition.
[P1.9] The burden CON must meet is steep: they must show that letting concentrated capital outbid families for scarce starter homes somehow serves those families better than protecting their chance to buy. I don't think that case can be made. When the market structurally favors capital over residents, a city's job is to rebalance the scale.
