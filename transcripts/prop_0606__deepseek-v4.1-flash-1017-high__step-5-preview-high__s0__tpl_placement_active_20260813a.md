[P1.1] Essential medicines are not ordinary products. When a patent-protected medicine is priced beyond what a country’s health system or patients can bear, the market has stopped allocating by need and started allocating by ability to pay. The proposition supplies the missing safety valve: a defined affordability threshold converts that failure into legal grounds for compulsory licensing. Not automatic confiscation—grounds, subject to existing law, notice, and royalty—but a real lever so governments are not left bargaining with a monopoly while people ration doses.
[P1.2] This is the right trigger because it is concrete, transparent, and narrowly tied to access. A country can define affordability relative to public health budgets, household income, and the cost of alternatives. If the price crosses that line for an essential medicine, access becomes the decisive public interest. That is exactly when compulsory licensing is designed to be used.
[P1.3] The patent remains; the patent holder is compensated; another manufacturer can supply. The policy targets the barrier—price—not the entire patent system.
[P1.4] The alternative is worse. Without a threshold-based ground, governments must either ration treatment, impose catastrophic costs on households, or negotiate under threat of denial. During the HIV/AIDS crisis, generic competition and licensing pressure transformed access to antiretroviral therapy from a luxury into something health systems could scale. The same logic applies today to insulin, cancer drugs, and curative hepatitis treatments: when the price is the wall, licensing is the door.
[P1.5] Opponents will warn about innovation. But compulsory licensing for essential medicines is a narrow, compensated exception, not abolition of patents. It applies after a price breach and to medicines whose absence harms health, not to every profitable product. A system that protects monopoly pricing even when patients cannot afford the medicine does not protect innovation; it protects avoidable suffering.
[P1.6] The threshold should be designed carefully, but its existence is the point: access to essential medicine must be a right with a remedy, not a hope.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a plausible slogan for a workable legal rule. A price threshold sounds precise, but affordability is not a market fact; it is a political number. Should it measure GDP, health budgets, household income, or cost of alternatives? A threshold crossed by a public tender may not be crossed in private pharmacies, or may become irrelevant after currency movement.
[C1.2] PRO says the threshold should be designed carefully, but the motion treats it as sufficient: whenever price exceeds it, compulsory licensing is justified.
[C1.3] That ignores cause. High prices can reflect import taxes, weak procurement, cold-chain costs, distribution failures, or low volumes rather than patent monopoly. A compulsory licence aimed at a patent may leave the actual access barrier untouched.
[C1.4] Worse, the proposition confuses legal authority with medicine. A licence permits production or import; it does not create active ingredients, bioequivalent manufacturing capacity, regulatory approval, procurement funds, or delivery to clinics. A country may win a legal right and still leave patients empty-handed.
[C1.5] An automatic price trigger also creates strategic behavior. Firms may launch at the threshold, withdraw products, delay registration, or litigate the threshold itself. That could harden opposition to licensing and reduce voluntary discounts, differential pricing, and patent pools that often work faster. For medicines with few alternatives, it can discourage entry altogether.
[C1.6] Existing compulsory licensing is justified when patents are not worked, when supply fails, when conduct is anti-competitive, or in emergencies. Those are access-linked inquiries. A mechanical price line is not. Access to essential medicines deserves remedies tied to actual supply failure and public need, not a number that can be crossed without a single additional pill reaching a patient.

[P2.7] The decisive comparison is timing. CON’s “actual supply failure” standard is too late: by the time supply fails, people are untreated. The proposition lets countries act when price makes access fail—before the pharmacy shelf is empty.
