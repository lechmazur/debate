[P1.1] The motion is narrower than its critics will want it to be. It does not ask anyone to atone for their ancestors or to hand back a family home. It asks what a state owes when its own institutions are still collecting on land that was seized.
[P1.2] Start with a principle every legal system already accepts: you do not get to keep the proceeds of a wrongful taking. If a university's endowment was seeded by land taken through broken treaties or coerced cessions, and that endowment still pays out every year, the injury is not ancient history. It is a line in this year's budget.
[P1.3] This is not hypothetical. Many American land-grant universities were capitalized with land expropriated from tribal nations. Several western states still hold trust lands whose grazing, timber, and mineral income funds public schools today. The taking was in the past, but the income keeps coming.
[P1.4] Three features make this the strongest possible case for restitution.
[P1.5] First, the beneficiary is identifiable and public. This is the state itself, the same continuous legal entity that signed the treaties and took the land. No private citizen is being blamed, and no one's guilt is inherited.
[P1.6] Second, the claimant is identifiable. Indigenous nations are continuing political communities, often with documented treaties, mapped territories, and recognized governments. This is a creditor with paperwork, not a guess about descendants.
[P1.7] Third, the remedy is flexible. The motion offers land return, long-term revenue sharing, or an equivalent. Where returning the land would disrupt a hospital or a campus, the state can share the revenue instead. So the objection that "it's impractical" is an argument about which remedy to choose, not about whether one is owed.
[P1.8] And it works. New Zealand has negotiated treaty settlements for decades, combining land, cash, and co-management, without economic collapse or social unraveling. Canada and several U.S. states have done versions of the same.

[C1.1] The motion asks states to pay restitution because they still profit from historically seized land. This assumes history is a ledger that can be balanced by seizing from today’s citizens to pay yesterday’s debts. It cannot.
[C1.2] First, the legal chain is a fiction. Modern states are not the same entities that signed old treaties. Borders were redrawn, empires collapsed, and populations migrated. The state that benefited is as unrecognizable as the claimant is identifiable.
[C1.3] To pretend otherwise is to invent a corporate soul for governments, one that never died and never changed hands. If identity can survive every revolution and annexation, then so can liability, and no state anywhere would ever be free of the past.
[C1.4] Second, the “beneficiary” today is not a villain in a ledger. It is a school funded by timber revenue, a hospital on a former prairie, a bridge built by taxpayers who arrived after the seizure. To seize revenue or land now is to commit a second taking—this time from people who did nothing wrong. Justice does not work by distributing inherited guilt across generations.
[C1.5] Third, the remedy is incoherent. “Equivalent restitution” has no metric. Do we calculate the rental value of Manhattan since 1626? And who receives it? Many nations have contested claims, and modern tribal citizens are themselves mixed descendants of both victims and settlers.
[C1.6] The motion pretends we can litigate centuries of human movement with a spreadsheet. Worse, it offers no limiting principle: if “still benefit” is the test, every public road and water system becomes a permanent mortgage.
[C1.7] Finally, returning land often harms the very communities meant to benefit. Close the hospital, and Indigenous patients lose care. Seize the campus, and tribal students lose seats. The motion mistakes symbolic transfer for material justice, and it ignores that the most dispossessed today need functioning services, not title to a disputed tract.
[C1.8] Current justice requires modern tools: sovereignty, self-government, and negotiated partnerships. Not retroactive guilt.
