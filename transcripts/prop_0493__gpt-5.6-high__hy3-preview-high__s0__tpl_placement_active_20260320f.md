[P1.1] Water is not an ordinary commodity. A community can survive without a local bottling plant; it cannot survive without a reliable aquifer. [P1.2] Aquifers make private extraction uniquely dangerous. They cross property lines, recharge slowly and uncertainly, and often reveal damage only after wells fall or springs weaken. A corporation captures each bottle’s sale price while households, farmers, and municipalities inherit deeper wells, treatment costs, subsidence, and drought risk. Market exchange cannot price those harms properly because affected residents never consent to the sale and cannot simply purchase a replacement watershed.
[P1.3] Permits and extraction fees are inadequate. Safe-yield estimates depend on changing rainfall and incomplete underground data; enforcement follows reported pumping; and once depletion or contamination occurs, money cannot quickly restore the aquifer. A ban prevents that lock-in rather than litigating after damage.
[P1.4] Commercial bottling is also a particularly weak claim on scarce water. It packages water—often where safe public supplies already exist—then adds plastic, transport, and retail markup. [P1.5] This policy does not prohibit emergency distribution or public provision, nor does it deny payment to contractors for treatment and delivery. Consumers retain alternatives such as municipal taps, refill systems, and publicly accountable supply. Essential infrastructure may use private services; the underlying water must remain under accountable public stewardship.
