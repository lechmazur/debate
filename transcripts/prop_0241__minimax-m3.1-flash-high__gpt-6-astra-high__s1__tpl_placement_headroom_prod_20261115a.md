[P1.1] Retribution asks what a wrongdoer deserves; rehabilitation asks what punishment should make possible. That distinction is decisive. Criminal law operates in a world where offenders return to families, workplaces, and communities, so a sentence that merely inflicts deserved suffering can leave every future harm untouched. If an offender leaves prison more hostile, less employable, and more disconnected, punishment has satisfied its logic while failing the people it should protect.
[P1.2] Retribution is not worthless, but it is backward-looking: it treats suffering as payment. Rehabilitation treats punishment as a forward-looking instrument for reducing violence and restoring productive membership. This matters because the central measure of criminal justice should be whether it makes communities safer over time, not whether offenders received precisely calibrated amounts of pain. Incapacitation, deterrence, and compensation reinforce this goal when they help reform conduct; vengeance does not necessarily do so.
[P1.3] Rehabilitation also delivers the accountability retributivists demand. It can require accepting responsibility, making restitution, complying with treatment, and accepting proportionate restrictions. Unlike vengeance, however, it does not confuse deserved suffering with moral repair. A person with untreated addiction imprisoned without treatment may pay heavily while learning nothing.
[P1.4] A drug-court sentence, supervised employment program, or restorative requirement may preserve consequences while changing the conduct that caused the harm. These are accountable responses aimed at preventing the next offense.
[P1.5] The strongest retributive objection is that rehabilitation can excuse offenders or sacrifice victims. It need not either. Responsibility must remain real, restrictions proportionate, and victims entitled to acknowledgment and protection. Yet those commitments do not require pointless suffering.
[P1.6] A system that both punishes and rehabilitates respects victims more than one that celebrates the finality of suffering while ignoring recurrence. Rehabilitation is not an alternative to punishment; it is the more important aim that should guide how punishment is designed.
