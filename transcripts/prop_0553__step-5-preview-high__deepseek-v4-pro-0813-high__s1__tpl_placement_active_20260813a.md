[P1.1] Owning a device should mean owning it. Today, a laptop or smartphone sold in India is increasingly treated as a sealed appliance: if the screen cracks or the battery fades, the owner faces either an exorbitant authorized repair bill or a black-market fix of uncertain quality. A national right-to-repair law corrects this inversion of property rights. It guarantees that when a consumer pays the full price for a device, they also receive the practical means to keep it working: access to genuine parts, diagnostic tools, and service documentation.
[P1.2] The sale transfers ownership; it should not transfer a permanent lease back to the manufacturer.
[P1.3] The Indian stakes are immediate. Households here routinely stretch the life of electronics because replacement is expensive. Yet manufacturers often restrict repair to captive service networks, which drives prices up and pushes consumers toward premature upgrades or unauthorized tinkering. India already has a dense, skilled informal repair sector.
[P1.4] Legalizing access to parts and documentation would formalize that ecosystem, lower repair costs, create legitimate employment, and extend the usable life of devices across income groups.
[P1.5] The environmental case compounds the economic one. India sits among the world’s largest generators of electronic waste, much of it from devices discarded while still repairable. Every additional year a phone or laptop stays in use is a year of avoided mining for rare materials and avoided toxic disposal. Right to repair is not merely consumer convenience; it is a public-interest measure for resource conservation.
[P1.6] Opponents will warn about safety and security. Those concerns are addressable through calibrated legislation governing consumer electronics, not a rationale for permanent monopoly. If a device is so fragile that qualified repair requires manufacturer captivity, the defect lies in the design, not in the right to fix.
