[P1.1] A menu that hides mandatory charges is not a price list; it is a lure. When a customer sees a $24 pasta, they have made a rational decision about value. Only after eating do they discover the "service" fee that turns it into $29. That is bait-and-switch in fine print.
[P1.2] The case for all-in menu pricing rests on a simple principle: markets function only when buyers can compare true costs before committing. Hidden mandatory fees destroy that comparison. A diner choosing between two restaurants cannot weigh a $20 advertised steak against a $22 advertised steak if the first adds 18% at the end and the second adds 10%. The menu has become a deceptive instrument that prevents the very choice it purports to facilitate, trapping consumers in a sunk-cost spiral once they have invested time and committed to the table.
[P1.3] This systematic distortion enriches the seller at the buyer's expense.
[P1.4] Opponents claim all-in pricing hurts competitiveness or clutters menus, but this confuses voluntary discounts with mandatory charges. No one objects to a restaurant advertising a discount from an all-in price. What we object to is advertising a price that was never real. The European Union requires VAT-inclusive pricing because the displayed price must be the payable price.
[P1.5] Airlines and hotels moved to all-in pricing because "drip pricing"—revealing fees one by one—exploits cognitive biases and locks consumers into decisions they would not have made with full information.
[P1.6] The stakes are not abstract. Surveys show that consumers feel deceived by surprise fees, and that trust in a restaurant evaporates when the final bill exceeds the menu. A family budgeting $80 for dinner finds themselves paying $95. Transparency is not a burden on business; it is the foundation of voluntary exchange.
[P1.7] When the price on the menu is the price on the bill, customers order with confidence and return because they were not tricked.
[P1.8] Mandatory means mandatory. If it cannot be waived, it is the price. Put it on the menu.

[C1.1] The proposition's fatal flaw is equating "all-in price" with "transparency." They are different. A mandatory service charge is not just extra food cost; it is a separate charge with a separate purpose, often an auto-gratuity or staff service pool. Folding it into the menu price erases that information.
[C1.2] If a diner sees "$29" all-in, they cannot tell whether $24 is food and $5 is service, or $29 is food and no service charge. That matters: if the charge goes to staff, customers may double-tip; if it does not, they may assume it does and under-tip. The separate line is more transparent, not less.
[C1.3] The real abuse is late revelation. But the remedy is early, prominent separate disclosure—on the menu header, table card, website, and stated before ordering—not mandatory bundling. That solves the surprise without disguising the components of the bill.
[C1.4] PRO's VAT analogy fails. VAT is a uniform tax on the whole bill; a service charge can vary by party size, event type, or day, and its distribution rules are not uniform. A single all-in menu price cannot accurately represent an 18% auto-gratuity that applies only to parties of six or more. The restaurant would either advertise a misleading average or print a schedule of conditions.
[C1.5] Separate disclosure handles variable charges honestly.
[C1.6] All-in pricing also creates new deception: customers may believe the menu price already covers a fair tip, when the service charge may be distributed differently or not at all. Better to say plainly: "A mandatory 18% service charge applies to parties of six or more and is distributed to staff." That is transparent. Burying it in the price is not.
[C1.7] Mandatory means it must be paid, not that it loses its identity. Disclose it clearly before the order. Do not force every restaurant to fold a distinct labor and service charge into the price of the food.

[P2.1] The opponent mistakes line-item breakdown for transparency, but transparency is knowing what you will actually pay before you commit. A diner comparing restaurants does not need to parse whether the difference between $24 and $29 is food or service; they need to know they will pay $29, not $24 plus hidden extras. When the menu shows $24 but the bill says $29, the customer cannot compare that restaurant against a competitor advertising a true $29. That is the deception we are fixing, and separate disclosure does not solve it because the damage happens at the comparison stage, before any bill is seen.
[P2.2] The claim that all-in pricing erases information about staff compensation is wrong. The proposition requires the displayed price to include mandatory charges; it does not forbid restaurants from noting "price includes 18% service charge distributed to staff." The customer gets both facts: the total cost and the breakdown. What we remove is only the bait-and-switch, not the information.
[P2.3] Indeed, a separate line at the end often obscures whether the charge reached workers, whereas an all-in price forces the restaurant to justify the total upfront.
[P2.4] The variable-charge objection collapses under scrutiny. A restaurant can display "Parties of six or more: $35 per person, including mandatory service charge." That is an all-in displayed price that honestly reflects the conditional cost. The alternative—advertising $29 and springing the extra 18% at the end—is exactly the drip pricing that exploits sunk costs once the customer has sat down and ordered.
[P2.5] Finally, the tipping argument cuts against the opponent. If the menu price includes the service charge, the menu can say so, preventing double-tipping. If it does not, customers must guess whether the mysterious $5 on the bill reached the server or vanished into overhead. All-in pricing with a clear label resolves the confusion; a separate line at the end merely postpones it and leaves the customer unable to budget accurately from the start.
