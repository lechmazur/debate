[P1.1] The motion's key qualifier—*"when long-term integration outcomes are comparable"*—strips away the strongest argument economic-selection advocates usually make. We are not trading integration quality for sentimentality. We are asking what matters beyond integration once that floor is met. And on that question, family reunification wins decisively.
[P1.2] Family reunification builds something that points-based economic selection structurally cannot: rootedness. A migrant who arrives through family ties comes embedded in a household, a neighborhood, a school community, a local economy. That person is not a fungible labor unit who can be substituted by the next higher-scoring applicant. They have stakes—children enrolled in local schools, a spouse building a career, aging parents who need care and who, in turn, provide childcare.
[P1.3] These dense reciprocal obligations produce exactly the kind of long-term commitment that policymakers say they want but struggle to engineer through economic filters.
[P1.4] Economic selection, by contrast, optimizes for a snapshot. A points system rewards credentials, salary offers, and age at the moment of application. But labor markets shift. The engineer admitted today may face displacement tomorrow; the credential that earned points may lose relevance within a decade.
[P1.5] What persists across economic cycles is social infrastructure—family bonds, community ties, intergenerational investment. When integration outcomes are comparable, the family-based migrant brings an asset the economically selected migrant does not: a built-in reason to stay, to invest, to put down roots rather than treat the destination country as a provisional arrangement.
[P1.6] There is also a legitimacy dimension worth naming directly. Migration systems that visibly prioritize keeping families together command more public trust than those that appear to auction residency to the highest-scoring candidate. Family reunification is intelligible to citizens in a way that points calculators are not. People understand why a spouse or a child joins a settled resident.
[P1.7] They do not intuitively grasp why a marginally higher salary offer should determine who gets to build a life in their country.
[P1.8] When integration is comparable, choose the pathway that builds permanence, not just employability. Family reunification does that. Economic selection, by design, does not.

[C1.5] When integration is comparable, the state must prioritize the migrant who offers the highest return on public investment and the greatest flexibility for the future. Economic selection achieves this; family reunification merely signs a blank check.
