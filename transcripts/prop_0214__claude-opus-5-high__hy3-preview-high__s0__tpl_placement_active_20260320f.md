Forced sterilization is not an event that recedes into history; it is a permanent surgical condition, carried in a body, and often into the next generation as childlessness, dissolved marriages, lost inheritance, and families that were never allowed to exist. You cannot declare a chapter closed while the injury is still being lived.
[P1.2] Notice who gets to say "closed." It is always the institution that holds the scalpel and the file. States that ran eugenic programs — Sweden, Japan under its postwar eugenics law, Virginia and North Carolina, Peru in the 1990s, Canada in cases involving Indigenous women reported well into this century — did not stop because survivors were satisfied. They stopped because the programs became embarrassing.
[P1.3] Then the same bureaucracies sealed the paperwork and let the clock run. Japan's survivors spent decades in court simply to be recognized. That is not closure; that is a defendant grading its own case.
[P1.4] Which is why archives are not a sentimental add-on. They are the mechanism. Compensation schemes in this area routinely fail not because the money is refused but because the proof is locked away: the operation is recorded in institutional language, under a pseudonym, in a file a survivor is not permitted to read. Descendants often learn only that a grandmother "was in the colony."
[P1.5] Open records are what convert a suspicion into a claim, and a claim into a remedy. Without access, "compensation available" is a promise designed to expire.
[P1.6] And the forward-looking stake is real. Coerced sterilization has recurred — in prisons, in immigration detention, against disabled and Indigenous women — precisely where records were closed and no one was ever held to account. Impunity is instructional. States that pay and disclose teach their institutions that consent is enforceable.
[P1.7] States that wait for survivors to die teach the opposite lesson, and it is learned.

[C1.1] Implementing sweeping compensation and opening sensitive archives sounds like justice, but it creates practical and ethical problems that can actually harm the very people it intends to help. The core issue is that "stronger" compensation and "full" archival access are not precise tools; they are blunt instruments that risk privacy violations, administrative paralysis, and the commodification of trauma.
[C1.2] First, consider the archive. Forced sterilization programs often targeted the most vulnerable: the poor, the disabled, and minority groups. The files from these programs contain not just the survivor’s story, but the private medical and social histories of entire families and communities. Releasing these records under the banner of "access" can strip these communities of their remaining privacy, exposing them to stigma that has persisted for decades.
[C1.3] A survivor’s right to know should not automatically override the privacy of a neighbor or a cousin who was also in the system but does not want their history made public.
[C1.4] Second, "stronger compensation" often turns into a bureaucratic nightmare that outlasts the survivors. When compensation is made too easy to claim without rigorous verification, it encourages fraud, which drains funds away from legitimate survivors. Conversely, if the bar is set high to prevent fraud, the state effectively forces elderly survivors to relitigate their trauma in court just to get a payout. This is not closure; it is a second victimization by the legal system.
[C1.5] Finally, treating these abuses as "closed chapters" is not the same as forgetting them. It is about choosing where to put the finite resources of a state. Every dollar spent on a bottomless compensation fund for events from half a century ago is a dollar not spent on preventing current abuses in the healthcare system or supporting marginalized communities today. We should not treat history as a debt that can never be paid off, because doing so traps both the state and the survivors in a cycle of resentment rather than allowing for a difficult but necessary move toward a shared future.
