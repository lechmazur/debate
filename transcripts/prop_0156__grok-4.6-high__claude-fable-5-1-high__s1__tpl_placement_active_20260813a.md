[P1.1] The eurozone's post-2010 crisis response recast the union as a creditor-debtor hierarchy, not a community of equals. That recasting damaged political trust more lastingly than residual institutional continuity could ever repair.
[P1.2] From 2010, the programs forced on Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Cyprus were sold as discipline, not mutual aid. Northern publics were told they were bailing out the feckless. Southern publics were told their votes no longer counted once the Troika arrived. Both stories were poison.
[P1.3] Solidarity is a claim of shared fate. Conditionality plus humiliation is its opposite.
[P1.4] The political consequences arrived fast. Governments that signed the memoranda lost office or were replaced. Technocratic cabinets were installed in Athens and Rome. Protest parties—Syriza, Podemos, the Five Star Movement, and Eurosceptic forces in France, Italy and Germany—drew strength from the same wound: Europe had become a machine for imposing rules rather than sharing risk.
[P1.5] Trust in EU institutions fell hardest where the adjustment was harshest. In creditor countries, resentment hardened into a conviction that southern democracy itself was a moral hazard.
[P1.6] Yes, the euro did not break. Banking union and the European Stability Mechanism followed. Those are facts about a currency's survival, not about political solidarity. Real solidarity would have meant common debt, a shared fiscal capacity, or at least an honest admission that the monetary union was flawed by design.
[P1.7] The chosen path doubled down on national liability, external surveillance, and lectures about competitiveness. It kept the notes circulating while teaching citizens that European politics could override elected governments without answering to them.
[P1.8] Institutional continuity is not solidarity. The distrust between North and South, between voters and Brussels, and between peoples and their own elites cut deeper and lasted longer. The crisis response saved a currency area by spending down the political capital that made a European union worth having.

[C1.1] Judge this motion by what Europeans actually did, not by what angry people said while doing it. Between 2010 and 2015, creditor democracies lent hundreds of billions of euros to countries they had no treaty obligation to help, then repeatedly softened the terms—cutting interest rates, stretching Greek maturities out for decades, deferring payments. The ECB, over loud objections from Frankfurt's own constituency, pledged to do whatever it takes and made the pledge credible. No member was expelled.
[C1.2] No creditor walked away. That is solidarity as revealed behaviour, and it is the largest cross-border risk-sharing exercise any group of sovereign states has undertaken voluntarily.
[C1.3] PRO's case rests on a double standard. Every episode of resentment counts as distrust; every transfer of money and sovereignty is dismissed as "mere currency survival." But a shared currency held together by voluntary lending and voluntary rule-taking is exactly what shared fate looks like in practice. Conditionality did not negate solidarity—it made solidarity possible in democracies where lenders' voters also had to consent.
[C1.4] Now weigh the distrust. Yes, protest parties rose. Look at what they did with power. Syriza signed a third programme within months.
[C1.5] Podemos never asked to leave. Five Star dropped its euro referendum; Le Pen abandoned Frexit. Greeks, at the peak of humiliation in 2015, told pollsters by large majorities they wanted to keep the euro. Support for the single currency across the eurozone stayed high through the whole period.
[C1.6] Voters were angry at outcomes; they did not stop trusting the project enough to want out. Distrust that never converts into exit is bruising, not deep.
[C1.7] Finally, the trajectory. The crisis response built the ESM, the banking union, and a habit of muddling through together. That machinery is why, in 2020, the same creditors accepted NextGenerationEU—common debt on a scale PRO says would have counted as "real solidarity." Political capital wasn't spent down; it compounded.
[C1.8] A response that ends with Germany co-signing European bonds did not poison the well. It dug it deeper.
