[P1.1] The core of this debate is not whether human rights matter; it is whether voluntary promises can replace verifiable information. Global supply chains are deliberately complex: a phone, a garment, or a battery can pass through mines, workshops, ports, and subcontractors across dozens of jurisdictions. When harm occurs, parent companies often say they did not know, and local victims lack the evidence to prove otherwise. That asymmetry is the problem.
[P1.2] Mandatory standardized, audited due-diligence reports change the incentive structure. Today, firms can publish glossy responsibility pages that highlight good policies while omitting high-risk suppliers. Standardization forces disclosure of the same categories—supplier tiers, risk assessments, mitigation actions, grievance outcomes, and audit findings. Independent audit turns assertions into testable claims.
[P1.3] Investors, unions, NGOs, insurers, and competitors can then compare firms instead of guessing. A company that cleans up its sourcing can prove it; a company that hides behind subcontractors cannot. Audited reports also give public authorities a workable baseline for procurement, trade rules, and enforcement without inspectors in every factory.
[P1.4] This is especially necessary because voluntary schemes punish leaders and reward laggards. If only conscientious firms investigate, they expose themselves to reputational and legal risk while others stay silent. A uniform legal requirement levels the playing field and prevents a race to the bottom.
[P1.5] The proposition also respects practical limits. It targets large multinationals, not small businesses. These firms already manage complex financial reporting, tax disclosures, and internal controls; requiring human-rights due diligence is a comparable use of their existing compliance capacity. It does not demand that governments micromanage every supplier relationship.
[P1.6] It demands truthful, comparable information about serious risks.
[P1.7] CON will likely warn about costs and box-ticking. But the alternative—unverified, self-selected statements—has already been tried. If countries want supply chains to respect basic rights, they must first make those supply chains visible. Mandatory, standardized, audited reporting is the minimum infrastructure for accountability.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on one assumption: that standardized, audited reporting makes harm visible. That assumption has already been tested, and it failed. Britain's Modern Slavery Act has required corporate supply-chain statements for years; the broad verdict, even among the organizations meant to use them, is boilerplate disclosure and little measurable change. Reporting regimes produce documents, not diligence.
[C1.2] The reason is structural. Audits are not neutral inspections; they are services purchased by the audited. Factories know the playbook: announced visits, coached workers, double books, spruced-up dormitories. Rana Plaza had been covered by social audits before it collapsed, killing over a thousand garment workers; Ali Enterprises in Pakistan burned with workers locked inside weeks after a compliance certification.
[C1.3] Standardization sharpens the gaming: a fixed template tells suppliers exactly what to fake. When a metric becomes the target, firms manage the metric, not the harm. Worse, the stamp of "audited" pacifies consumers, investors, and regulators, draining pressure for measures that might actually bite.
[C1.4] Second, the predictable response to mandated disclosure of serious risks is exit, not reform. Facing legal and reputational exposure for what they find, rational firms drop high-risk suppliers and shift sourcing out of the poorest regions—exactly where jobs are scarcest and abuse deepest. Production slides into informal subcontracting beyond audit scope. Brands have repeatedly answered sourcing scandals by cutting suppliers rather than fixing them.
[C1.5] The workers this motion claims to protect lose their livelihoods, and the chain grows less visible, not more.
[C1.6] Third, the "level playing field" [P1.4] cuts against PRO. Uniform compliance is cheap for a company with a legal department and crushing for its small suppliers, who absorb the cost through lower wages and corner-cutting—or get dropped.
[C1.7] PRO calls this the "minimum infrastructure for accountability" [P1.7]. An instrument that reliably yields paperwork, rewards concealment, and pushes the vulnerable out of formal supply chains is not infrastructure. It is theater with a compliance budget.

[P4.1] A1: Publication is not “only” paperwork. It turns private assertions into a public record that governments, public buyers, investors, unions, and courts can use. A required report can be enforced as a filing duty, and because it is audited, false or missing entries point to a named auditor as well as a firm. [P4.2] A2: The audit conflict is weakened by publication, not ignored. When assurance remains private, the brand controls what is seen. When the audit is public, the auditor’s name, scope, and findings can be checked by rivals, NGOs, unions, and regulators. The brand still pays, but the audience changes: a sanitized audit now invites comparison and challenge instead of merely reassuring insiders.
[P4.3] This is why CON’s attack keeps missing the comparative point. They criticize reports as imperfect, but their world has no substitute for finding out what is happening across borders. Without standardized disclosure, “we did not know” remains unfalsifiable. With it, boilerplate is visible as boilerplate, missing tiers are visible as missing tiers, and supplier exit becomes a change to explain rather than a silent adjustment.
[P4.4] CON also assumes firms will simply rebrand exit. But a standardized report forces them to account for risk assessments, mitigation, and changes over time. If a company abandons a high-risk region, that pattern can appear in successive reports and invite scrutiny. Under voluntary practice, the same exit leaves no trace.
[P4.5] The decisive clash is therefore not whether this policy solves every abuse alone. It is whether countries should build the minimum record that makes consequences possible. CON offers no detection mechanism; PRO does. Even imperfect audited disclosure shifts power from secrecy to comparison, and that is why PRO wins.
