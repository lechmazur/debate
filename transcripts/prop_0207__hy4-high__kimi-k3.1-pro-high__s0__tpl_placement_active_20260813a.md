[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are sold as protection for the poor, but their rule is: pay the most to whoever burns the most fuel. That is not a poverty program; it is an upside-down transfer. Cars, generators, large homes, and fuel-intensive firms capture the largest gains, while a landless family, a bus-riding renter, or a rural household with little cash gets a thin indirect share. Governments should compensate people, not litres.
[P1.2] Broad price controls hide costs; they do not abolish them. Below-cost pump prices make the treasury, suppliers, or citizens pay through deficits, arrears, shortages, queues, and smuggling. Diffuse costs let governments quietly crowd out clinics, schools, water, and transit to preserve a benefit flowing disproportionately upward. Cheap fuel also rewards congestion, diesel generators, and waste; cash helps a poor household without paying it to consume more petrol.
[P1.3] Fuel prices do feed into food and transport, but that is why targeting matters. A cash transfer can arrive before prices rise, adjust with prices, and target pensioners, low-income families, disability recipients, remote communities, or transit users. It can cover rent, school costs, medicine, or food—whatever the household needs. A universal subsidy cannot calculate need: it over-compensates the affluent commuter while leaving the poorest family’s other bills untouched.
[P1.4] “Targeting is hard” reverses the burden. A state that can run nationwide price controls and reimburse refiners can build a payment list, use benefit or tax records, verify locally, and hear appeals. Targeting may err; universal subsidy is misallocation by design. Errors can be audited and corrected; broad controls keep subsidising every non-poor litre indefinitely.
[P1.5] Phase gradually: put cash in place first, raise prices predictably, protect essential services, and review transfers as prices change. The decisive comparison is moral and practical: broad controls buy short-term calm with hidden, regressive fiscal costs; targeted transfers buy actual protection with a visible budget line, while prices no longer reward waste.

Wealthier households may receive more subsidy in absolute litres, but poorer households often devote a larger share of income to fuel, fares, and food whose prices depend on transport. Universal support reaches them automatically through ordinary purchases. Its generosity to the affluent is a reason to tax affluent consumption more effectively, not a reason to remove protection from everyone and hope a registry reconstructs it.

Measuring richer households’ extra litres and clawing back the excess is harder than enrolling poor ones. Assuming that machinery while calling a poor registry impossible is selective pessimism.
[P2.2] The “share of income” point proves less than claimed. A poor household may feel a fuel shock intensely; a price control still gives the same discount to every litre and almost none to the family buying little fuel but facing higher fares, food, or rent. Automatic reach is automatic leakage, not automatic adequacy. CON’s excluded households still must buy the commodity, then depend on pass-through through traders, operators, queues, or shortages.
[P2.3] Timing is met by sequence, not hope: cash first, staged price increases afterward, using existing rolls, local verification, emergency top-ups, and a published schedule before prices move. “An appeal process does not buy this week’s food” attacks cash arriving after the shock; that is bad implementation, not a cash-first replacement. Price controls fail continuously too: deficits become arrears, suppliers ration, queues grow, black markets appear, and the cheap nominal price is unavailable when people need it.
[P2.4] [C1.5] says subsidies adjust automatically with purchases. Exactly: they compensate litres, not vulnerability. Cash can vary by household size, region, disability, or fare exposure; a pump discount cannot tell a school journey from a weekend drive. [C1.6] and [C1.7] make political risk a reason to hide the bill.
[P2.5] A visible transfer can be audited, topped up, and defended; an untargeted subsidy lasts until fiscal crisis, then quietly cuts clinics, transit, or wages. Keeping universal prices while trimming industry subsidies preserves the defect: every non-poor litre is still paid for. Continuous protection cannot be built on a tool that misses need by design.

[P5.1] CON ends by saying a successful payment list is not proof nobody poor is missing. True. That cuts for PRO: first payments, bridge claims, and local top-ups can be gates before each price step. A detected gap blocks the reform.
[P5.2] Under universal price control, no gate exists: the same posted discount is called protection whether a poor family faces a fare rise, a queue, a black-market price, or a clinic cut to finance arrears. No missed-beneficiary list exists: the policy names a litre, not a person.
[P5.3] That is the asymmetry. Cash risks are visible and front-loaded: identify households, pay them, watch prices, adjust, and stop if coverage fails. Broad controls hide failure in supply and budgets, then treat continuity of the printed price as success while effective protection disappears. A frozen transfer leaves a named claim to challenge; an underfunded control leaves no claim at all.
[P5.4] So CON is not defending a floor. It is defending a number at the pump. PRO wins because replacement makes protection a testable obligation owed to people before prices move, not an assumption attached to fuel. If the test fails, prices should not move; if the hidden subsidy fails, the poor discover it last.
