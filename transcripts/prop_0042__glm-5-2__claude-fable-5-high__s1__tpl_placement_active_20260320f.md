[P1.1] The price of meat is a lie. It looks cheap at the checkout, but the real bill arrives later—in flood damage, in hospital wards, in depleted aquifers, in antibiotic resistance. Every country that refuses to tax meat production at its true cost is running a subsidy program for one of the most environmentally destructive and health-harming industries on earth. That subsidy is paid by everyone, but it lands hardest on the people this debate claims to protect.
[P1.2] Livestock production accounts for a major share of global greenhouse gas emissions, consumes vast land and water resources, and drives deforestation. Runoff from feedlots fouls rivers. Antibiotic overuse in factory farming accelerates resistance that kills people regardless of income. These are not private costs.
[P1.3] They are externalities—costs imposed on third parties who never bought the product. A tax that internalizes them is not a penalty; it is honesty in pricing.
[P1.4] The health side compounds the problem. Red and processed meat consumption is linked to cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers. These diseases strain public healthcare systems and shorten lives, disproportionately in lower-income communities where diets are constrained by exactly the distorted prices this policy would correct.
[P1.5] The objection that a meat tax raises food costs for the poor assumes the current price is the fair one. It is not. Today, poor households pay through polluted air, contaminated water, chronic illness, and overstretched public budgets. They pay later instead of at the register, and they pay in forms far harder to budget around.
[P1.6] A tax swaps diffuse, uncontrolled suffering for transparent, correctable cost—and its revenue can fund rebates, subsidize plant-based alternatives, or support healthcare. That is a better deal for lower-income households than today's arrangement, where the bill comes due anyway but never appears on a receipt.
[P1.7] Taxing meat at its true cost is not regressive. The status quo is.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to accept, up front, that lower-income households should pay more for food. Take that seriously. Food price shocks are not an accounting inconvenience for the poor; they are the difference between a child getting iron, B12, and complete protein or not. Meat is the most bioavailable source of exactly the nutrients deficient in low-income diets, especially for children and pregnant women.
[C1.2] A policy that prices those households out of nutrient-dense food in the name of "health" generates its own health externality—anemia, stunting, deficiency—which the tax's architects never put on their ledger.
[C1.3] Second, "full environmental and health externalities" is a phrase that sounds rigorous and is actually unmeasurable. Nobody can price the marginal cancer risk of a sausage or the aquifer cost of a specific herd. What does a government actually do? It picks a number—politically, arbitrarily—and applies it as a blunt levy that treats a regenerative pasture farm the same as an industrial feedlot.
[C1.4] That destroys the very price signal PRO claims to want: the good producer and the bad producer face the same tax, so there's no incentive to produce cleaner, only to produce less domestically.
[C1.5] Which leads to the fatal design flaw: this taxes production. Tax your own farmers and demand doesn't vanish—it's met by imports, often from countries with worse emissions, weaker antibiotic rules, and active deforestation. Brazil is happy to fill the gap Denmark creates. The result is exported jobs, hollowed rural economies, and global emissions that may actually rise, while your own poor pay more at the register.
[C1.6] That is the worst of every world.
[C1.7] If the goals are methane, runoff, antibiotic resistance, and deforestation, regulate those things directly: emissions standards, manure rules, antibiotic bans, import controls. Those tools hit the harm at its source without rationing protein by income. A meat tax is the crudest instrument available, aimed at the people least able to absorb it.
