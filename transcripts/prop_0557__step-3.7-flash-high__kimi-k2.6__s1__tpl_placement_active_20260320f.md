[P1.1] The widespread adoption of test-optional undergraduate admissions policies, rolled out widely during the COVID-19 pandemic, has failed to deliver on their stated promise of expanding equitable access to higher education. Instead, these policies have created a more opaque, subjective admissions landscape that disadvantages the very low-income, underrepresented minority, and first-generation students they were designed to help. Public universities must reinstate standardized test requirements to restore a uniform, verifiable metric of academic readiness that cuts through grade inflation and reduces reliance on privileged, unstandardized application markers.
[P1.2] First, high school GPAs have risen sharply over the past two decades, with top grades no longer reliably distinguishing highly prepared applicants from those with weaker academic foundations. Without consistent test score data, overworked admissions officers lean far more heavily on subjective factors: legacy status, costly extracurriculars, and polished personal essays that can be coached or even ghostwritten for affluent applicants. A low-income student with a strong academic record but no test score is easily overlooked for a legacy applicant with identical grades but a “well-rounded” application built on private tutors and unpaid internships.
[P1.3] Second, test-optional policies have not boosted representation for underrepresented groups. Data from the University of California system, which moved to fully test-blind admissions in 2021, shows that admission rates for high-achieving Black, Latino, and low-income students to its most selective campuses declined after the policy shift, precisely because admissions officers reverted to subjective criteria that correlate strongly with family wealth and parental education.
[P1.4] Finally, standardized tests are not an insurmountable barrier for low-income applicants. Many public universities already offer free test prep, fee waivers, and in-school testing for low-income students, so the test score gap is a solvable resource gap, not a flaw in the assessment. Reinstating requirements does not mean ignoring context; it adds a consistent, comparable data point to an admissions process that has grown too skewed toward privilege.
