[P1.1] More border enforcement treats irregular migration as a border-control problem. Yet many seasonal and sectoral migrants do not prefer irregularity; they use it because legal entry and work are unavailable while employers still demand labor. Enforcement closes one route without removing the underlying pull. Legal pathways attack that pull directly.
[P1.2] A well-designed seasonal worker program can give employers predictable labor and workers a lawful route tied to verified vacancies, enforceable standards, and time-limited work permission. Sectoral schemes can do the same in agriculture, hospitality, construction, or home care. This changes incentives: it removes the need to pay smugglers, reduces employers’ ability to exploit unauthorized workers, and makes lawful compliance safer. It converts migration from an underground transaction into a regulated relationship that can be monitored.
[P1.3] That also improves enforcement. If worker status and employer obligations are recorded, authorities can focus on recruitment fraud, wage theft, trafficking, and dangerous conditions rather than trying to identify unauthorized workers. Inspections become possible at workplaces. More enforcement alone is reactive: it must detect movement whose causes remain intact.
[P1.4] Legal channels are preventive, reorganizing the labor market around declared jobs and accountable firms. Thus the policy does not replace enforcement; it makes enforcement more targeted and productive.
[P1.5] Consider a farm hiring workers for a bounded harvest. A verified seasonal channel connects workers and employers, records conditions, and imposes departure obligations. Legal capacity must track labor demand, while recruitment-fee and job protections guard against exploitation. Even with safeguards, legal access is the only approach addressing both why migration occurs and who benefits from it.
[P1.6] More patrols may shift crossings elsewhere and raise smuggling costs. Lawful, sector-specific routes reduce the volume authorities must enforce against while improving rights and oversight. The choice is not enforcement versus legal migration, but enforcement blind to labor demand versus a policy confronting that demand through enforceable regulation.

History shows that without strong borders, even generous legal programs are overwhelmed by uncontrolled inflows. Thus, expanding legal pathways does not outperform border enforcement in reducing irregular migration.
