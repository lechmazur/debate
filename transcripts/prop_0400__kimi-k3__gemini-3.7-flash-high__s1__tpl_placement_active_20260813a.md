[P1.1] When the letter arrives saying your benefits are cut, your visa is denied, or you have been flagged for fraud, the first question is: who do I talk to? Increasingly, the honest answer is no one. Australia's automated welfare-debt scheme issued hundreds of thousands of unlawful debt notices before collapsing in scandal. In the Netherlands, an algorithm-driven fraud dragnet falsely branded thousands of families as cheats and wrecked lives.

[P1.3] The principle here is old. Decisions about benefits, immigration status, and criminal exposure are exercises of coercive power over the most consequential parts of a life. Due process has always meant that before the government takes something that vital, a person must hear you — someone with authority to grasp context, weigh hardship, and reverse course. Automation should not quietly repeal that guarantee by relocating judgment into a system no one can question.

[P1.7] The efficiency objection answers itself: faster wrong decisions are not better decisions, and the cost of human review is trivial next to a wrongful eviction, deportation, or prosecution. A state that can take your livelihood owes you, at the very least, a person.

[C1.1] A blanket legal right to a human decision-maker mistakes the biology of the decider for the fairness of the outcome. Due process is not a guarantee of human presence; it is a guarantee of accuracy, consistency, and protection against arbitrariness. Mandating a human for every consequential government determination actively undermines those principles.
[C1.2] First, human decision-makers are notoriously inconsistent and prone to bias. Decades of administrative reality reveal staggering disparities: two individuals with identical criminal records, asylum claims, or disability applications routinely receive radically different verdicts depending entirely on which official they draw, the time of day, or unexamined prejudice. Objective, standardized algorithms apply the exact same rules to every applicant. Unlike human bias, which is covert and notoriously hard to root out, code can be audited, tested for disparate impact, and systematically corrected across the board.
[C1.3] Second, an absolute right to human determination creates catastrophic bottlenecks that directly harm the vulnerable. When welfare agencies, immigration departments, and public housing authorities are legally barred from using automated determinations to quickly approve aid or resolve claims, backlogs explode. For a family facing eviction or an immigrant seeking work authorization, waiting eighteen months for a human caseworker to stamp a file is not due process—it is administrative denial. Automated triage and direct programmatic approvals allow millions of rightful claimants to receive life-saving support instantly.
[C1.4] Third, the proposed right creates a dangerous illusion of justice. Faced with impossible caseloads, agencies will inevitably reduce human involvement to perfunctory rubber-stamping. A tired caseworker spending forty seconds scanning a system output satisfies the formal right to a human, but offers zero genuine review while insulating the underlying process from real scrutiny.
[C1.5] True justice does not require a human at every algorithmic front door. It requires transparent criteria, clear avenues for contestation, and rigorous oversight.
