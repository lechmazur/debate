That was not the absence of solidarity; it was solidarity under stress: creditors exposed their taxpayers, debtors accepted adjustment, and every country stayed in the single currency.
[C1.2] PRO’s case treats distrust as the decisive metric, but it ignores the counterfactual. An uncontrolled Greek exit or Italian default would likely have triggered bank runs, currency chaos, and recession across the continent. The blame game would not have vanished; it would have become existential. States would have spent the next decade litigating who destroyed the euro.
[C1.3] The response instead converted a potentially fatal collapse into a series of ugly but survivable negotiations. That is what preservation of political community looks like in a union of sovereign states, not its opposite.
[C1.4] Much of the anger PRO cites is real, but much of it is misattributed. Populist parties grew from migration, globalization, and domestic dissatisfaction as much as from bailout politics. If the post-2010 response had only deepened distrust, Europe would not have been able to move toward shared borrowing when the next shock came.
[C1.5] Finally, conditionality was not merely punishment. Lenders were accountable to their own voters, and loans had to be repaid. The choice was not between sentimental solidarity and austerity; it was between a flawed common framework and fragmentation. Because the framework held, Europeans retained the capacity to argue, reform, and mutualize more later.
