[P1.1] A constitutional right to a minimally clean and healthy environment is a floor, not a ceiling. It does not ask courts to perfect nature or choose energy policy. It says government may not knowingly leave people in conditions that destroy basic health—air that scars lungs, water that poisons children, soil that makes a neighborhood sick. Once that floor is recognized, legislatures remain free to choose the means: permits, cleanup, subsidies, zoning, enforcement.
[P1.2] Courts decide only whether the floor has been breached.
[P1.3] This is the central clash: rights are needed precisely where politics fails. Pollution’s costs are diffuse, delayed, and borne by people with little voice—children, the poor, future generations—while benefits concentrate among powerful actors. The ballot box is a blunt remedy when harm is cumulative and victims are unborn or unable to move. Constitutional rights exist to correct that kind of democratic blind spot, not to replace democracy.
[P1.4] Existing rights are too porous. Courts often demand proof of deliberate cruelty or find environmental harm too systemic for life and bodily-integrity clauses. An explicit environmental floor closes that gap. It also fits enforceability: the duty runs against governments, because governments license, monitor, and permit the conduct that poisons.
[P1.5] A court need not run an agency; it can declare a minimum violated and require a health-protective plan, with agencies retaining technical discretion.
[P1.6] Opponents will call this vague and undemocratic. But “minimally clean and healthy” is no vaguer than “cruel and unusual” or “due process,” and less contestable than “reasonable.” Judges already review health standards. And constitutional rights are supposed to be countermajoritarian when basic health is at stake. A society that can constitutionally ignore poisoned water has not balanced policy; it has abandoned the premise that all persons count.

[C1.1] Every environmental problem PRO describes is real. The question is not whether pollution harms people — it obviously does — but whether unelected judges applying a phrase like "minimally clean and healthy" are the right instrument to fix it. They are not, and forcing the fit will make outcomes worse, not better.
[C1.2] Start with indeterminacy. "Cruel and unusual" and "due process" carry centuries of common-law content that judges inherited. "Minimally clean and healthy" has none. Is fifty parts per billion of a contaminant a floor violation?
[C1.3] What about a rural water system that can't afford full treatment, or a factory town where shutting the plant means mass unemployment? These aren't legal questions with right answers a judge can find — they're polycentric tradeoffs among health, cost, energy access, and time, the exact kind of question courts are structurally bad at resolving because a lawsuit only has two sides and a docket has no budget line.
[C1.4] That indeterminacy collides with an enforceability problem PRO can't escape. This right runs against governments, but the remedy isn't "stop doing X" — it's "spend money, build infrastructure, monitor forever." That's a positive right wearing negative-rights clothing. PRO says courts need not run an agency, just declare the floor breached and order a "health-protective plan."
[C1.5] But that is running an agency, one lawsuit at a time — indefinite judicial oversight of permitting, budgets, and technical standards, the same open-ended supervision that turned school-desegregation and prison-conditions litigation into decades-long institutional entanglements with mixed results at best.
[C1.6] And the countermajoritarian argument cuts both ways. Locking today's environmental science and priorities into constitutional text doesn't protect the powerless — it freezes policy against the very legislative flexibility needed as science, technology, and tradeoffs evolve. A coal town facing a court-ordered shutdown before replacement power exists doesn't experience "countermajoritarian protection" — it experiences unelected judges picking winners.
[C1.7] Statutes, agencies, and tort law can already target specific pollutants with revisable, technical standards. That's the right tool. A constitutional right is the wrong one dressed up as compassion.

Governments already publish health-based standards for lead, arsenic, particulates, sewage. A court can ask a narrow question: has government knowingly allowed conditions that its own science deems dangerous?
[P2.3] That is no vaguer than "cruel" or "deliberately indifferent." Hard boundary cases don't erase clear ones: children with lead poisoning, communities breathing toxic air. Rights often have margins; that's not a reason to have none.
[P2.4] Second, [C1.4] calls this a positive right in disguise. But many constitutional duties cost money. The right doesn't command a particular budget or technology. It bars a government from maintaining a known health catastrophe without a health-protective plan.
[P2.5] Cost and unemployment are real, but a floor allows phase-in; it forbids permanent poison. Courts can require a plan, set deadlines, and defer technical choices to agencies. That is not "running an agency"; it is making the political branches justify and remedy the violation. The school/prison analogy proves too much: those involved ongoing supervision of entire institutions.
[P2.6] Here the trigger is a threshold breach; once cured, judicial involvement ends.
[P2.7] Third, the countermajoritarian worry [C1.6] is backwards. Majorities already decide where pollution goes, and they usually send it to the powerless. A floor doesn't freeze science: it incorporates evolving health knowledge and prevents regression below minimal safety. Statutes, agencies, and tort law fail precisely when agencies are captured, budgets cut, and tort remedies preempted or impossible to prove.
[P2.8] A backstop isn't redundant; it's the only remedy when ordinary channels are the thing failing.
[P2.9] The decisive comparison: CON offers a system where poisoned communities must wait for the same political process that poisoned them. PRO offers a floor below which no government may go. That is modest, enforceable, and rights-shaped.
