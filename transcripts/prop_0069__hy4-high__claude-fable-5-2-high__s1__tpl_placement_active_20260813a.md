[P1.1] People should not have to live as a permanent API call. Algorithmic reputation systems turn scattered traces—gig ratings, school proctoring flags, payment histories, platform complaints, location patterns—into a portable judgment that can follow someone into jobs, rentals, admissions, and earning platforms. The wrong is not one decision based on relevant evidence; it is a shadow dossier that crosses contexts without meaningful notice, consent, or a chance to answer. A legal right to disconnect says that when a person enters a new institution, the machine-made verdict built elsewhere does not automatically enter with them.
[P1.2] That right is not a right to erase consequences. A landlord may check rent history for the unit being leased; an employer may verify credentials; a school may grade work submitted to it. What a person may refuse is the importation of cross-domain scores, risk labels, and behavioral predictions whose relevance is unproven and whose ingredients they cannot inspect. Decisions must rest on local, relevant, human-checkable reasons.
[P1.3] The danger is context collapse. A low delivery rating during illness, an absence during a family crisis, an old housing dispute that was settled, or a complaint from a hostile customer becomes a general marker of “unreliability.” Once converted into a score, the excuse vanishes and the burden flips: the person must disprove an invisible accusation. Because that score is recycled, one error can become a job barrier, a housing barrier, and a school barrier. Stale facts become fresh punishment; poverty, disability, neighborhood, or language patterns can be repackaged as neutral risk.
[P1.4] Voluntary fairness cannot solve this: the score-buyer wants cheap sorting, the broker wants more data, and the subject has no leverage. Efficiency and safety justify targeted checks, not a lifelong portable dossier. Law must change the default: no retaliation for opting out, contestable records, and reasons a person can answer. That is the difference between screening and social exile.
