[P1.1] Liquid sugar is not a staple food; it is an engineered health hazard. Opponents inevitably claim that taxing sugar-sweetened beverages is regressive because lower-income households spend a larger share of their income on consumer goods. But the true, devastating regressivity is the epidemic of chronic disease we permit every day without one.
[P1.2] Type 2 diabetes, hypertension, liver disease, and dental decay do not strike society equally. Lower-income communities suffer the highest rates of diet-related morbidity, targeted by relentless advertising and trapped in environments saturated with cheap liquid sugar. When a low-wage worker loses their livelihood to diabetes complications or drowns in medical debt, that outcome is infinitely more regressive than a higher price tag on a bottle of soda.
[P1.3] Moreover, the objection over financial burden misunderstands price elasticity. A tax designed to meaningfully shift consumption works precisely because people change their habits. Because lower-income consumers are the most price-sensitive, they reduce their intake the fastest and deepest, substituting toward water and untaxed alternatives. As a result, they do not simply bear a higher tax burden—they avoid the tax, spend less on harmful beverages, and capture the lion’s share of the health benefits, reduced medical expenses, and preserved working years.
[P1.4] A policy is not compassionate because it keeps diabetes cheap. Leaving sugary drinks untaxed allows multinational corporations to privatize enormous profits while socializing catastrophic health costs onto the most vulnerable. If we measure equity not by the shelf price of soda, but by the protection of human health, longevity, and economic security, an aggressive beverage tax is one of the most progressive public health interventions a government can deliver.
