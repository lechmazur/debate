[P1.1] The central question is who pays for the basic costs of work. Platform companies direct labor, set terms, and profit from it, but they shift health coverage, paid leave, and retirement security onto workers, families, and taxpayers. The proposition does not ban gig work or force every worker into traditional employment. It requires something modest: benefits that accrue in proportion to hours worked.
[P1.2] This matters because gig work is often unstable by design. A driver or courier may work thirty hours across several apps, yet have no sick leave when illness strikes and no retirement account beyond what they can improvise. When an injury forces time off, the loss is private, even though the platform keeps the upside of that labor. Prorated benefits turn that around.
[P1.3] Each hour worked would generate contributions toward health coverage, leave, and retirement, ideally in a portable account that follows the worker across platforms. That preserves the flexibility people value while ensuring work provides more than immediate cash.
[P1.4] The government’s role is essential here. Voluntary benefits are unlikely to spread because platforms compete on labor costs. A firm that offers coverage is undercut by rivals that externalize it. A uniform, hours-based rule creates a level playing field and stops a race to the bottom.
[P1.5] It also avoids the brittle employee-versus-contractor fight. Instead of asking whether every gig worker fits an old legal category, it ties protection to the measurable fact of work performed.
[P1.6] Critics will warn about costs. But the real cost now is hidden: workers absorb risk, and public programs fill gaps. If platforms pass costs through, the answer is transparency and regulation, not leaving workers bare. The proposition makes platform work sustainable by making it accountable.
