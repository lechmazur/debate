[P1.1] Organized crime in Latin America is not primarily a cocaine problem or a cartel problem in the abstract — it is a revenue problem. Cartels and gangs diversify across extortion, kidnapping, fuel theft, and human trafficking, but cannabis has historically been one of their most reliable, low-risk cash crops: easy to grow across the region, easy to move in bulk, and sold to a market that is enormous and largely unpoliced by moral stigma, since most consumers see it as a soft drug.
[P1.2] Every dollar of that cannabis revenue currently flows untaxed into criminal structures that use it to buy weapons, bribe police, and fund the more violent parts of their business. That is the lever PRO is pulling: legalization does not need to solve organized crime entirely, it only needs to cut off one major funding stream and redirect it into state hands, and even a partial cut changes the balance of power.
[P1.3] We have a working model close to home. After several U.S. states legalized cannabis, price and demand data showed Mexican cartels' cannabis export revenue collapsing — growers in Sinaloa and Durango reported abandoning fields because they could no longer compete with legal, taxed, quality-controlled product grown openly in Colorado or California. That is not a hypothetical mechanism; it already happened once legal competition existed. Latin American legalization would do the same thing domestically: a regulated industry with licensed growers, quality control, and retail storefronts undercuts the illegal product's main advantages — price stability and lack of legal risk for the buyer.
[P1.4] Legalization also reallocates state capacity. Police and prosecutors currently spend enormous resources chasing low-level cannabis growers and couriers, people who are not driving cartel violence but who clog courts and prisons. Redirecting that enforcement toward actual violent trafficking, extortion rings, and money laundering is itself a strategic gain, independent of the revenue effect. Add tax revenue that can fund rural development and alternative livelihoods in cannabis-growing regions, and you get a double blow: less criminal income, more state capacity to fight what's left.
