[P1.1] A synthesized performance by a dead legend is not a remaster of old recordings; it is a new product built from an identity. The question is who controls that identity after death. The answer should be the artist: through consent given while alive, or the estate or heirs chosen to speak for them. Explicit permission is the minimum rule that turns AI “resurrection” into stewardship rather than extraction.
[P1.2] If a label can generate a new Whitney Houston single, it no longer needs Whitney in the studio. It can prompt a model on her phrasing and style until the track sounds like her. Fans hear Whitney, streams credit Whitney, and the label captures her value. She cannot refuse, negotiate, or approve.
[P1.3] Her life’s labor becomes an uncompensated input. Permission forces the people she trusted—and the market that still pays—to agree before that substitution is sold.
[P1.4] We already know permission works. A recent Beatles release used AI tools to recover John Lennon’s voice from an old demo, but only with his bandmates’ and estate’s approval. It completed an old recording with consent; it did not license anyone to manufacture new Lennon performances on demand. That is the line worth protecting.
[P1.5] The objection that the dead have no rights, or that estates will hoard, misses the point. The issue is authority over a persona, not immortality. We already require permission for samples, covers, name-and-image advertising, and holograms. “Ask first” does not ban AI tributes; it forces consent, credit, and payment. If an estate is unreasonable, fix that narrowly—do not let labels treat dead artists as public-domain puppets.
[P1.6] Without permission, catalogs are strip-mined into clones, fake comebacks crowd out living musicians, and fans buy a simulation nobody authorized. AI should extend an artist’s reach only when the people entrusted with that legacy say yes. Explicit permission is not censorship; it is the line between honoring a legend and exploiting a corpse.

[C1.1] The proposition is too broad. It treats every AI performance influenced by a dead artist as identity theft. Most are not. Musicians have always built careers by absorbing dead masters: a young singer can echo Billie Holiday’s phrasing tomorrow without asking her estate.
[C1.2] Death should not make a style more ownable than it was in life. Explicit permission converts cultural influence into private property and gives estates, companies, and heirs a veto over expression they had no right to control before.
[C1.3] The real harm is narrower: using a dead artist’s name, voice, or likeness to make a product seem authorized. That is deception and false endorsement, already answerable through right-of-publicity, trademark, unfair-competition, consumer-protection, and contract law. If a label prompts a model until it says “Whitney Houston” and markets the result as Whitney Houston, it can be stopped. If a filmmaker makes a clearly fictional synthetic performance in the manner of Kurt Cobain, the Cobain estate’s blessing should not be required.
[C1.4] The permission rule would also freeze ordinary creativity. Styles are learned from many influences; a model trained on broad musical history may sound “like” several dead artists at once. Requiring permission from each estate would create an impossible thicket, and heirs with no relationship to the artist could suppress work they dislike. An estate can negotiate around a known voice or image; it cannot own the grammar of a genre.
[C1.5] The Beatles example proves the limit: restoring John Lennon’s voice from his own recording involved an actual recording and existing rights. It does not follow that every AI act inspired by a dead artist needs estate sign-off. Police fraud, impersonation, and unauthorized use of a specific persona; do not give the dead a posthumous monopoly over the styles they helped create.
