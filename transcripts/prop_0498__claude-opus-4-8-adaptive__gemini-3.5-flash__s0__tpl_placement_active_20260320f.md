[P1.1] When the referee also fields a team, the game is rigged before kickoff. That is the precise problem with major app-store operators who both run the marketplace and sell their own apps inside it. They write the rules, see every rival's data, control search rankings, set the fees their competitors must pay, and approve or reject the very apps that threaten their own. Asking that operator to police its own self-preferening is asking the referee to call honest fouls against his own teammates.
[P1.2] Structural separation removes the conflict at its root.
[P1.3] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, the incentive is unavoidable, not occasional. A firm that controls the platform and competes on it will always face a standing temptation to tilt the field: bury a rival in search results, deny it a key API, time an approval delay to coincide with its own product launch. We have seen credible patterns of exactly this—platform owners promoting house apps over more popular third-party ones. You cannot regulate away an incentive this structural with promises of good behavior.
[P1.5] Second, behavioral remedies have repeatedly failed. Conduct rules, fairness pledges, and monitoring require regulators to detect subtle, technical, constantly-shifting forms of preference inside a black box only the operator can see. The operator always knows more, moves faster, and can comply in letter while evading in spirit. Separation is the only remedy that doesn't depend on perpetually outsmarting the party being policed.
[P1.6] Third, the harm lands on the people who can least defend themselves: independent developers who depend on a single gatekeeper for access to billions of users, and consumers who lose the better app that never got a fair shot. Innovation gets taxed and throttled at the chokepoint.
[P1.7] The proposition is narrow and surgical: it targets only the dual role—running the store while competing in it. Keep the store. Keep the apps. Just don't let the same entity own both.
[P1.8] Remove the conflict, and you don't have to trust anyone to resist it.

[C1.3] Structural separation is a wrecking ball where a scalpel is needed. It fails on three fronts.

[C1.5] Second, it guts the economic model that benefits third-party developers.
