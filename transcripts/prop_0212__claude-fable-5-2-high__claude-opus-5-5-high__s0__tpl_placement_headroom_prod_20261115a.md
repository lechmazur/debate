[P1.1] A secret that has outlived its security purpose protects only one thing: the reputation of those who kept it. The motion already concedes the hard case — genuine, live security concerns stay protected. What remains is the question of who owns the record of what a state did to people once the danger is gone. The answer is the people it was done to.
[P1.2] Start with families. A daughter in Spain who does not know which roadside pit holds her father. A Kenyan detainee told for decades that no records of his torture existed. For them the archive is not history; it is the only place the truth physically exists.
[P1.3] Withholding it extends the original wrong by another generation.
[P1.4] Second, closure without security justification is how institutions launder their past. Britain held thousands of colonial files in a secret facility for decades, denying they existed, until elderly Kenyan claimants forced them into court — and those files helped prove abuses officials had long denied. Other files had been burned on the eve of independence. That is what discretionary secrecy produces: not careful stewardship, but curation by the accused.
[P1.5] Third, broad opening works. Germany opened Stasi files to the people named in them, with third-party names redacted and structured access for researchers. Millions applied. The predicted wave of revenge never came; what came was a society that could argue about its past with evidence instead of rumour.
[P1.6] So the privacy objection is an argument about how to open — redaction, tiered access, priority for victims — not whether. And the stability objection fails: buried records do not disappear, they leak, selectively, as weapons for whoever holds them.
[P1.7] The other side must defend something specific: continued secrecy after the security reason has expired. Ask them whom, exactly, that secrecy serves.
