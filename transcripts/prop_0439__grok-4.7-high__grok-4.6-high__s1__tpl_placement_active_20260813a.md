[P1.1] Prohibition does not stop kidney sales; it drives them underground. Black markets recruit the poorest sellers, skip screening and aftercare, and leave donors and recipients exposed to infection, complications, and exploitation that no one can monitor because the trade is illegal. Demand from patients dying on dialysis remains relentless, while altruism alone never closes the gap. Enforcement catches only scraps.
[P1.2] The ban fails twice: it does not end sales, and it selects for the worst brokers and the most vulnerable people.
[P1.3] A regulated legal market reverses both failures at once. Transparent compensation, not trafficker prices, would draw additional willing donors who would never give a kidney for free but would do so under informed consent, medical supervision, and guaranteed follow-up. Those organs would reach patients now waiting years or dying before a match, converting lost time into restored life-years. Screening, matching, and long-term care become conditions of lawful payment rather than costs black-market clinics simply omit.
[P1.4] A domestic legal channel also undercuts international trafficking by offering a safer, policed alternative that desperate buyers and sellers currently lack.
[P1.5] The comparison is direct. Under prohibition, shortage and shadow trade reinforce each other: fewer legal kidneys mean more pressure to buy illegally, and illegal sales stay dangerous precisely because they cannot be regulated. Under a compensated system, supply rises inside the law, waitlists shrink, and the incentive to risk an unmonitored clinic falls. Objections that payment commodifies the body do not erase the body count.
[P1.6] Patients lose years of life while a safer supply is forbidden, and the poor who sell anyway do so without the protections a legal market would require. Regulation reduces black-market harms and increases life-years gained. Prohibition does neither.

[C1.1] Prohibition is not the engine of kidney trafficking. Shortage, poverty, and brokers willing to skip medical risk are. A paid legal market would not reverse those forces. It would license a domestic slice of them and leave the rest intact.
[C1.2] Legal compensation undercuts the black market only if excluded sellers and impatient buyers prefer the official queue. They will not. Real regulation must screen, match, wait, and refuse unhealthy or coerced donors. Those rules are why an illegal clinic still has a product: speed, an unapproved match, and a seller too sick, too young, too foreign, or too pressured to pass.
[C1.3] Traffickers already sell that remainder. A lawful price at home does not police a surgery abroad.
[C1.4] Payment does not convert need into consent. The extra “willing” donors a market attracts are people for whom money is the reason, not a bonus. Forms cannot cancel rent, debt, or a recruiter. Aftercare on a license evaporates when the seller cannot take unpaid time off.
[C1.5] The harm is not secrecy. The harm is extraction from people who cannot refuse.
[C1.6] Life-years are counted too cheaply. Each paid nephrectomy puts surgical risk and lost renal reserve on donors already likelier to face hypertension and poor follow-up. If payment crowds out unpaid family donors, you have not added transplants; you have repriced them. If paid organs come from poorer, sicker bodies, graft survival falls.
[C1.7] Recipients’ extra years cannot be booked without subtracting donor losses.
[C1.8] A market drawn from economic distress does not shrink black-market harms. It nationalizes a share, treats the body as inventory, and still leaves an illegal remainder for everyone regulators must exclude. That is a larger trade, not more life.
