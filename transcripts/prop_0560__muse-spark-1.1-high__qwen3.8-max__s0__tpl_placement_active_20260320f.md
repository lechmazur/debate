[P1.1] Every health system rations. The only question is whether we ration rationally. Funding a marginal expansion of high-cost end-of-life care means paying more to extend the very end of life by a little, at very low quality, for very few. Prevention means paying less to give much more life, at full quality, for many more people.
[P1.2] The priority is obvious.
[P1.3] Preventive public health buys health where health is cheapest and most valuable. Hypertension screening, tobacco quit lines, school nutrition, lead abatement, childhood immunization - these stop heart attacks, strokes, cancers and disabling lung disease before they happen. They preserve decades of healthy life, keep people working, parenting, and out of hospitals. High-cost end-of-life expansions do the opposite: new oncology infusions, prolonged ICU ventilation, or a fourth line of therapy that on average adds weeks, often in pain and confusion, for hundreds of thousands of dollars.
[P1.4] A system that can't afford community nurses but can afford that trade has its values backwards.
[P1.5] That is not just inefficient, it is unjust. Marginal end-of-life gains cluster among those who have already had a full lifespan. Prevention clusters among the young, the poor, and those with the most life left to lose. A child who gets asthma from second-hand smoke or diabetes from a food desert never gets a fair innings if we spent his prevention budget on someone else's last month.
[P1.6] Prioritizing by greatest lifetime benefit is fairness, not ageism.
[P1.7] Prevention also reduces future demand for precisely the expensive terminal care we are debating. Fewer smokers means fewer late-stage lung cancers to treat. Less obesity and hypertension means fewer heart failures needing ICU beds at 80. Prioritize prevention and even end-of-life care becomes less scarce.
[P1.8] That is the only allocation that both maximizes health and shares it fairly across lives.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a budget spreadsheet for a health system. Prevention matters, but a system’s deepest obligation is to people who are sick now, especially when they face severe suffering and death. To prioritize prevention over marginal expansions of end-of-life care is to say that statistical future lives routinely outweigh present, identifiable patients. That is not justice; it is accounting dressed as ethics.
[C1.2] PRO caricatures end-of-life care as futile ICU days and expensive last-ditch chemotherapy. But marginal funding often buys better palliative teams, home hospice, pain control, social work, or careful second opinions. These are not mere life-extension; they are dignity, relief, and support for families. If a cancer patient has months left, telling her the money belongs to a future hypertension program treats her remaining life as less worthy because it is short.
[C1.3] That principle would also devalue disabled and chronically ill patients whose expected gains are smaller.
[C1.4] Prevention is not the automatic bargain PRO assumes. Many interventions are costly, slow, uncertain, or benefit relatively advantaged groups who can act on advice. Screening can overdiagnose; behavior-change programs often fail to reach the poorest; environmental remediation can take decades. A dollar diverted from end-of-life care may produce health someday, but it abandons a person dying today.
[C1.5] PRO’s fiscal story is equally fragile. If prevention succeeds, people live longer and eventually require other costly treatments; savings are delayed, uncertain, and often smaller than promised. More importantly, a health system is not a business where current obligations can be liquidated for future efficiencies. The patient in respiratory distress or uncontrolled pain cannot wait for a downstream curve to bend.
[C1.6] The fair standard is not maximum aggregate benefit. It is urgent need and equal concern. Health systems should fund prevention generously, but not by demoting terminal care. The question is not prevention versus greed; it is whether we may leave the suffering at the bedside to improve population averages.
[C1.7] We should answer no.
