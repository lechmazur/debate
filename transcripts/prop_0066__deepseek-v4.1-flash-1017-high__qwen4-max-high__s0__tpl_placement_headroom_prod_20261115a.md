[P1.1] Public space is not a place where privacy ends; it is where privacy is shared under a fragile bargain: strangers can see us, but they cannot instantly identify us, catalogue us, or follow us home. AR glasses with live face recognition smash that bargain. The wearer looks like an ordinary person, but everyone in the frame becomes a search result. A woman leaving a shelter, a protester in a crowd, a nurse at a clinic, a person at a bar—each can be named, cross-referenced, recorded, and tracked without knowing it happened.
[P1.2] That is not a minor convenience. It is a structural change in the balance of power between strangers.
[P1.3] The proposition is deliberately narrow. It does not ban AR glasses. It does not ban cameras. It bans one function—live identification of real people in public—except where a narrowly defined professional duty justifies it, such as a warranted law-enforcement operation or a controlled security procedure with accountability.
[P1.4] Those exceptions can be audited, trained, and limited. A free-for-all cannot.
[P1.5] The core clash is consent and asymmetry. Ordinary public observation is reciprocal and forgettable; face recognition is silent, scalable, and permanent. The person being scanned cannot opt out, cannot see the database, cannot correct an error, and often cannot even tell the scan occurred. That enables stalking and domestic abuse, chills protest and everyday association, and turns mistakes into real-world accusations because a match feels authoritative.
[P1.6] Even if the technology improves, the power imbalance remains.
[P1.7] A society that lets every pair of glasses run live identification in public has effectively abolished practical anonymity. The burden is on CON to show why that enormous shift should be the default. Narrow professional exceptions preserve legitimate uses; mass public scanning does not. Ban the default.
