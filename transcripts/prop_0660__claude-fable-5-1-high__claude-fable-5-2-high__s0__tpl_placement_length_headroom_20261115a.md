[P1.1] Start with what these laws actually do, because the productivity scare depends on misdescribing them. France, Portugal, Ontario, and Australia did not outlaw evening emails. They gave employees a protected right to decline unreasonable after-hours contact and required employers to set clear expectations. Emergencies survive.
[P1.2] Genuine on-call arrangements survive. Senior staff who choose to work late still can. What dies is the unspoken norm that a message at 10pm demands a reply by 10:15.
[P1.3] That norm is the clash. Knowledge work runs on attention and recovery, not on hours of availability. Picture an analyst who gets a "quick question" from her manager at 9:40pm. She answers, then lies awake replaying it.
[P1.4] The next morning her deep work is foggy, she rereads the same paragraph three times, and the real cost of that message lands on tomorrow's output, invisibly. Multiply that across a team for a year and you have burnout, sick leave, and resignations. Replacing a senior engineer costs months of lost output. Attrition is a productivity loss; it simply isn't counted that way by the people arguing against these laws.
[P1.5] The welfare side is not speculative. Sleep, family time, and the ability to mentally detach are the mechanisms by which people recover cognitive capacity. Protecting them is not a gift to workers at the firm's expense; it is maintenance on the firm's core asset.
[P1.6] Notice who already behaves this way voluntarily. Volkswagen stopped routing emails to employee phones after hours years before any law existed, and Daimler let vacationing staff auto-delete incoming mail. Serious companies did not do that out of charity. They did it because fragmented, always-on attention produces worse work.
[P1.7] So the burden on CON is specific: show a productivity loss larger than the burnout, turnover, and degraded focus these laws remove. Boundaries that experienced firms adopt on their own cannot plausibly be the thing wrecking knowledge-sector output.
