[P1.1] When a filmmaker's paycheck or a performer's residual depends on how many people watch their work, the party holding the data holds all the power. Streaming platforms currently operate a system where compensation is tied to audience reach, yet the metrics determining that compensation remain a black box. That arrangement is fundamentally exploitative, and requiring standardized viewing data disclosure fixes it.
[P1.2] The core principle is straightforward: if you write a contract that pays based on viewership, both sides must see the ledger. Every other medium where compensation tracks audience size—network television, syndication, box office—operates with verified, third-party ratings. Nielsen data, box office returns, and syndication reports give creators leverage to negotiate and audit their deals. Streaming alone insists that creators should trust the house's math while sitting at the house's table.
[P1.3] Requiring standardized data simply extends an existing fairness norm to a new technology.
[P1.4] Opacity enables underpayment. Without standardized reporting, platforms define "a view" however they like—thirty seconds, two minutes, a complete watch—and can shift definitions between contracts or bury methodology in fine print. A performer whose residual triggers at one million views has no way to know whether the platform counted their show's audience honestly or gamed the threshold. Standardization eliminates that vulnerability by fixing definitions and requiring auditable disclosure.
[P1.5] This is not about forcing platforms to reveal competitive strategy or proprietary algorithms. It is about the narrow, concrete scenario the proposition names: when compensation depends on audience reach, the data underlying that compensation must be shared in a standardized form. Anything less lets the paying party serve as scorekeeper, referee, and auditor simultaneously—a conflict of interest no contract regime should tolerate.
[P1.6] The path to victory here is simple: fair dealing requires symmetric information. Where money follows metrics, those metrics must be visible, verifiable, and uniform. Opposing transparency in this context is opposing the basic conditions of equitable contracting.
