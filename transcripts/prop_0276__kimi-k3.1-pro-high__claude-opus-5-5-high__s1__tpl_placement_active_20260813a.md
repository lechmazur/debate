[P1.1] A controllable delay is not merely bad luck; it is a service failure by the company that sold the itinerary, controlled the operation, and holds the evidence about what went wrong. Yet today’s complaint-heavy model often reverses responsibility: the passenger must preserve documents, decode eligibility rules, submit forms, and chase responses. Automatic compensation puts the burden back where it belongs.
[P1.2] The principle is simple: pay compensation when clear, preannounced thresholds are met for disruptions within the airline’s control, including missed connections caused by those disruptions. The airline should identify eligible passengers from its own booking and operational records, notify them, and issue cash or original-payment compensation without demanding a claim. Disputed edge cases can still be reviewed, and weather or other genuinely uncontrollable events need not trigger the same payment. “Automatic” means the reliable cases do not require persistence, not that every delay produces an arbitrary cheque.
[P1.3] This is fairer because the existing system quietly discriminates. A frequent flyer who knows the rules may recover money; a parent managing children after a missed connection, an elderly traveller, or someone working irregular hours may simply give up. Equal contractual rights are hollow when redemption depends on spare time, legal confidence, and tolerance for bureaucracy. Consider a passenger missing the last connection because an airline’s staffing failure delayed the first flight: the airline already knows the itinerary, the cause code, and the arrival time.
[P1.4] Making that passenger prove the obvious serves delay, not justice.
[P1.5] Automation also improves incentives. Compensation attached promptly to controllable failures makes poor scheduling, inadequate staffing, and unreliable connections visible business costs rather than expenses exported to travellers. Airlines remain best placed to prevent those failures or price the risk honestly.
[P1.6] The likely objection is cost, but uncompensated disruption is not cost-free; passengers already pay through lost work, missed events, and hours spent complaining. The choice is between hidden, unevenly imposed costs and a transparent system that rewards reliability. When the seller causes the failure and possesses the proof, compensation should be the default—not a prize for successful complaining.
