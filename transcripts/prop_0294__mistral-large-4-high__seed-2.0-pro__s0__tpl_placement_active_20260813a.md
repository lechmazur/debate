[P1.1] When a state board strips references to systemic racism or a governor's appointees rewrite how slavery is described, students don't get a conservative or liberal education—they get a political one. History textbooks covering war, colonialism, and national identity are precisely the battlegrounds where shifting political majorities reshape the past to fit the present. Leaving these narratives to whoever wins the next school board election means curriculum whiplash: today's students learn one version of the Civil War, tomorrow's learn another, depending on which party holds the gavel.
[P1.2] Independent scholarly review breaks this cycle. Historians operate under methodological standards—primary source verification, peer review, engagement with historiographical debate—that no politician can override with a vote. When textbooks are vetted by scholars rather than political appointees, the narrative of colonialism isn't sanitized to protect national myths, nor reduced to ideological catechism. It is presented with the complexity the evidence demands: competing motives, unintended consequences, and scholarly disagreement included.
[P1.3] The alternative isn't neutrality; it's volatility. Political control doesn't produce balanced textbooks—it produces whichever narrative serves the current majority. A student in 2024 shouldn't have their understanding of American expansionism depend on which party won in 2022. Independent review creates a stable, evidence-based foundation that actually prepares citizens to grapple with difficult history.
[P1.4] We trust scientists to teach science without legislative micromanagement of every lab result; history deserves the same insulation from partisan interference.
[P1.5] Scholarly oversight isn't elitism—it's the difference between education and indoctrination. When textbooks answer to methodology rather than election cycles, students learn to weigh evidence rather than parrot talking points. That is the foundation of democratic citizenship.
