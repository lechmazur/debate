[P1.1] Dynamic pricing asks fans to trust two invisible processes: how they reach the front of the queue, and why the price changes before they get there. Sellers control both. When major events have passionate, time-sensitive demand and few substitutes, that control justifies a corresponding duty: disclose the complete queueing and pricing history, not merely the final price and a reassuring slogan about “demand.”
[P1.2] First, disclosure makes consent meaningful. Imagine a fan who enters a queue, watches estimated waits fluctuate, and finally reaches tickets at several times the initially advertised price. Was that genuine demand, staged inventory release, a malfunction, or a pricing rule designed to exploit urgency? A final checkout screen cannot answer.
[P1.3] A timestamped history can show queue movements and resets, inventory releases, price changes, and the rules triggering them. Fans can then judge whether waiting was worthwhile, while artists, consumer authorities, and competing sellers can identify patterns that individual buyers never could.
[P1.4] Second, transparency deters conduct that opacity rewards. A seller operating under disclosure knows that unexplained queue jumps, selective access, misleading scarcity, or abrupt price escalations will leave a record. That changes incentives before harm occurs. It also gives legitimate dynamic pricing a defense: if prices genuinely responded to demand and queues operated fairly, the history is evidence of good practice rather than an embarrassment.
[P1.5] Third, this is a proportionate condition on using the practice, not a ban on it. Sellers already generate transaction and queue logs to operate these systems. Histories can be published after the sales window in standardized, machine-readable form, with a plain-language summary. Personal data can be anonymized and sensitive security details protected without deleting the substantive record.
[P1.6] Disclosure need not mean surrendering pricing source code or every trade secret; it means revealing what happened to customers and prices.
[P1.7] The likely objection is that records are complex or could be gamed. But complexity created by the seller cannot become the customer’s blindfold. And a system whose fairness collapses when its outcomes are visible is not a reason for secrecy; it is the strongest [clipped]

The very queue movements and resets that would expose unfairness are also the anti-bot interventions that must stay hidden. [C1.5] Even for ordinary fans, more data is not more consent. A checkout screen can and should disclose the pricing rule, possible range, and key queue terms before purchase. [C1.6] Finally, the remedy is disproportionate. Sellers already face consumer-protection and unfair-pricing law; regulators and auditors can examine records under confidentiality. Fairness is achieved by fair rules and accountable enforcement, not by stripping systems of every operational secret and calling the exposure 'consumer rights.'
