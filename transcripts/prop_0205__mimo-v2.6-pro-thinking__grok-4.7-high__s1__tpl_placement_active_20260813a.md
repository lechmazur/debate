[P1.1] When you buy a phone, you should choose your wallet, your browser, your assistant. The operating-system provider shouldn't have already chosen for you. That's what this motion targets: not the existence of Apple's wallet or Google's assistant, but the incumbent's ability to use control of the OS to pre-install those products into default position and bury or nag competitors into invisibility.
[P1.2] The mechanism is well understood. Defaults are not a mild nudge — they are the single most powerful lever in software distribution. Most users never change factory settings, not because they've compared options and concluded the default is best, but because switching requires hunting through settings menus, dismissing warning prompts, and re-authenticating payment credentials. That friction is a distribution advantage that no rival, however innovative, can buy its way around.
[P1.3] A better mobile browser cannot win on merit when the OS vendor's browser gets the home screen, the link-opening privilege, and the "are you sure you want to switch?" warning, while rivals get buried in a folder.
[P1.4] This is a structural conflict of interest. The OS provider sells the shelf and stocks it with its own products. In any other market we would call that self-dealing. A supermarket chain that owns a dairy brand can stock its milk — but if it controls the entire shelf layout, forbids rivals' placement, and labels competitor milk "unverified," regulators step in.
[P1.5] Mobile platforms do exactly this with default payments and default assistants, and the harm compounds because these are the fastest-growing markets in consumer software.
[P1.6] The proposition is narrow and proportionate. It bars preferential treatment in default settings — it does not ban pre-installation, does not require rival software, and does not touch the vendor's freedom to compete on quality. Users can still pick Apple Wallet or Gemini. What they cannot do is have the choice made for them before the box is opened.
[P1.7] The opponent will frame this as consumer convenience. But convenience engineered by a gatekeeper to protect its own adjacent products is not convenience — it's a moat. Break [clipped]

[C1.1] A legal bar on OS providers shipping their own wallet, browser, or assistant as the default mistakes integration for foreclosure. Every finished phone must open links, take a payment, and answer a question on day one. That is product design, not a crime against choice. The motion would make the device worse for the people it claims to protect.
[C1.2] [P1.6] calls the bar narrow. It is not. Preferencing in default settings covers opening a link in the system browser, tapping to pay with the wallet bound to the secure element, or invoking the assistant the OS trusts with the screen. Strip those defaults and you do not get a neutral marketplace.
[C1.3] You get a setup gauntlet, a blank or regulator-chosen default, or deep system access for whoever wins the menu. None of that is merit.
[C1.4] The supermarket analogy fails because a phone is not a rented shelf. The operating system is the product: security model, updates, biometrics, and all. Rivals can compete on that product. They are not owed the factory position on someone else's system, any more than a carmaker must boot a rival's maps before the driver can leave the lot.
[C1.5] Switching already exists, and some friction is a feature. Payment credentials and browser privileges are high-value targets. Warnings and re-authentication stop a malicious app from quietly becoming the thing that opens every link or authorizes every tap. Calling that friction an illegitimate moat asks us to trade security for easier customer acquisition.
[C1.6] Three different functions do not justify one ban. A browser default, a wallet tied to NFC and fraud controls, and an assistant with system-level context are not the same lever. A categorical bar cannot be proportionate to all three, and it crowds out case-by-case antitrust where real exclusion occurs. Users pick platforms partly because the stack works out of the box.
[C1.7] Require easy switching. Do not outlaw the default that makes the phone a phone.
