[P1.1] Adult friendship has become hard enough to sustain that treating it as purely private is already a policy choice, and a bad one. The difficulty is not lost kindness. It is that adult life now taxes the conditions friendship needs: repeated contact, leftover time, and physical proximity.
[P1.2] Work relocates people just as housing costs scatter the friends they already have. Dual careers and caregiving eat unstructured hours. Commutes, school pickup, and the disappearance of cheap third places turn a casual drink into a scheduling puzzle. Remote work can protect focus while deleting the weak ties that used to thicken into real ones.
[P1.3] Picture two colleagues who liked each other for years and never became friends because neither had a free weeknight within a thirty-minute radius. That is not a character defect. Friendship is overlapping lives, and we redesigned those lives against overlap.
[P1.4] Calling this private is false. Institutions already decide who we meet and how often: office hours, zoning, transit, school calendars, neighborhoods with benches or only drive-throughs. They do it by accident, often against friendship. The costs then become public: worse health, thinner informal care for aging parents, weaker neighborhood trust, more paid services where a friend once brought a meal or watched a child.
[P1.5] Markets will sell apps and coworking desks. They will not rebuild the unpaid web of neighbors and coworkers. Friendship behaves like a public good. Connected neighborhoods help everyone, but no single adult can restore missing hours and nearby people alone.
[P1.6] The answer is not assigned friends or compulsory intimacy. It is treating time, proximity, and shared space as design goals the way we already treat traffic and childcare. Mix uses so adults actually encounter one another. Stop treating workplace connection as a weekend hobby.
[P1.7] Fund libraries, parks, and civic programs as social infrastructure, not extras. Leaving friendship to private luck is how we got this scarcity. Institutions that already shape adult contact should start shaping it on purpose.

That is a value judgment, not a repair of accident.
[C1.3] Spillovers do not justify the leap. Loneliness can worsen health, and a neighbor sometimes replaces paid care. Other private habits spill over too, and we do not hand them to planners. Parks, libraries, and transit already serve their own purposes.

[C1.5] It cannot supply the choice that makes a bond.
[C1.6] The workplace is the worst instrument. Employers already control time, place, and advancement. Turning that power toward connection invites obligatory socializing and penalties for the person who goes home to a child or prefers a professional distance. Friendship under hierarchy is not a neighbor's help.
[C1.7] It is an extension of the firm.
[C1.8] Deliver safer streets, transit, more housing, and civic space. Do not adopt adult friendship as a public purpose. People decide whom to keep and what unstructured time a relationship is worth. That discretion is friendship's condition, not its obstacle.
[C1.9] Institutions that chase scarce overlap will only counterfeit the good they cannot make.
