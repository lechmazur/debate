[P1.1] Allowing access to puberty blockers and hormone therapy when parents and qualified clinicians agree is not a blank check for every questioning teenager. It requires three strict safeguards: a formal diagnosis of gender dysphoria, medical recommendation from qualified specialists, and parental consent. Denying access even when all three align means enforcing a blanket ban—insisting that in the clearest, most agonizing cases, a one-size-fits-all prohibition serves a child better than the doctors examining them and the parents raising them.
[P1.2] That ban fails because waiting until eighteen is not a neutral choice. Puberty does not wait for legal adulthood. For an adolescent with severe, persistent gender dysphoria, undergoing endogenous puberty means watching their body irreversibly change in ways that deepen their distress—a dropped voice, permanent skeletal shifts, or unwanted chest development. Forcing a diagnosed teenager through years of preventable physical changes inflicts acute psychological trauma, worsening depression, isolation, and self-harm risk while requiring invasive surgeries in adulthood.
[P1.3] Puberty blockers buy crucial time to evaluate a child without racing a biological clock; when clinicians and parents agree dysphoria is entrenched, hormone therapy lets adolescents develop safely alongside their peers.
[P1.4] Every serious pediatric treatment involves real trade-offs. Blockers and hormones carry genuine medical considerations, including bone density, fertility impacts, and the risk of regret. That is why they belong behind rigorous clinical assessment and informed consent, not banned outright. Across pediatrics—whether prescribing psychiatric medications, potent autoimmune therapies, or major surgeries—doctors and parents routinely weigh medical risks against the immediate danger of leaving a suffering child untreated.
[P1.5] Medicine never solves clinical complexity by outlawing care for everyone.
[P1.6] Parents witness the daily reality of their child’s distress and hold primary responsibility for their lifelong welfare. Qualified clinicians bring diagnostic rigor and ongoing medical monitoring. When both agree that the concrete harm of untreated dysphoria outweighs manageable clinical risks, locking the clinic door does not protect vulnerable youth—it abandons them.

[C1.3] PRO calls waiting non-neutral. Correct—but intervention is not neutral either. Puberty blockers are not merely a pause button pressed while everyone thinks. [C1.6] Nor does rejecting this motion mean locking the clinic door or abandoning suffering teenagers.
