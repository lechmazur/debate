[P1.1] Wearable devices collect our heart rates during panic attacks, our steps while recovering from injury, and our sleep patterns through grief. This is not just health data—it is a continuous diary of our most vulnerable moments. Without strict opt-in, insurers gain unrestricted access to this intimate feed and will use it to justify higher premiums or coverage denials based on temporary or contextual factors. Once shared, this data cannot be unshared, and its interpretation cannot be controlled.
[P1.2] The coercion is immediate: customers who decline to share data face financial consequences, while those who comply surrender permanent control over information that may be misread. This is already reality: programs like John Hancock’s Vitality tie insurance discounts to wearable data, effectively making participation a condition of competitive pricing. The implications are dire and systemic. Wearable metrics often capture socioeconomic disadvantages rather than true health risks—a factory worker’s irregular sleep from night shifts, a low-income parent’s limited activity due to childcare and multiple jobs, or a student’s stress during exams all get flagged as high-risk behaviors.
[P1.3] Insurers will inevitably treat these as justification for higher premiums, punishing those who can least afford it. Moreover, the data is inherently noisy: a single week of poor sleep or reduced activity from illness can trigger lasting financial penalties. The discrimination is compounded: those with less stable lives often have less stable data, creating a feedback loop where disadvantage begets higher costs. The slippery slope is unavoidable: once insurers access wearable data, they will demand more—location history to assess ‘risky’ neighborhoods, social media sentiment analysis, even genetic predictions.
[P1.4] Strict opt-in with tight limits on use is the only barrier. Without it, insurance transforms from shared risk to continuous surveillance, where the most vulnerable bear the highest costs. Privacy means control over one’s own information; fairness means insurance should protect, not punish. This prohibition is necessary to uphold both.
