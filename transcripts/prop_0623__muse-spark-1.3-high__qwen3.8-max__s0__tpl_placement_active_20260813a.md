[P1.1] Low-wage labor markets are not level playing fields. A single cleaner, care aide, or kitchen worker negotiating alone has almost no leverage, and a decent employer who wants to pay more gets undercut by the competitor next door who pays rock bottom. The result is predictable: wages stuck, turnover chronic, standards eroded — not because work matters less, but because no individual firm can afford to be the only one to raise them.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining breaks that trap by setting minimum pay and basic standards for an entire industry at once. Instead of hundreds of isolated fights, workers, employers, and government agree a floor that applies to everyone in hospitality, care, construction, retail, or trucking — with room to adjust for skill, region, and shift work. No firm gains by slashing wages, so competition shifts to quality, service, and efficiency.
[P1.3] That levels the field upward for business. Responsible employers are protected from being punished for decency. Industries plagued by churn gain stability, stronger incentives to train, and fewer shortages because jobs become worth staying in. Compliance also becomes simpler: one clear standard is easier to know and enforce than a maze of company policies.
[P1.4] The alternatives cannot do this. A single national minimum wage is necessary but too blunt and too low to reflect different working conditions. Firm-by-firm union drives take decades and leave most workers uncovered, especially women, migrants, and young people in fragmented workplaces.
[P1.5] Sectoral standards extend dignity at scale. They recognize that pay is not set by abstract supply and demand but by rules — and we should choose rules that prevent exploitation, reward good employers, and make essential work pay.

[C1.1] The strongest case against sectoral bargaining is not that low pay is acceptable; it is that industry-wide compulsion is the wrong tool for a fragmented, fast-changing labor market. It replaces firm-level adaptation with centralized wage floors that governments cannot calibrate well. When pay and conditions are fixed for an entire sector, they ignore the difference between a profitable urban hotel chain and a rural guesthouse, between a national retailer and a three-person contractor, between peak-season tourism and off-season construction. The result is predictable: hours are cut, hiring slows, automation accelerates, and marginal workers—often the very people the motion claims to help—face fewer entry points.
[C1.2] PRO says sectoral bargaining stops a race to the bottom. But it also suppresses the competition that allows new firms, worker-friendly startups, and niche employers to enter. If labor costs are standardized across an industry, incumbents with scale and legal teams absorb compliance more easily than small rivals. Sectoral rules can become a barrier to entry, protecting established firms and union structures while making markets more concentrated.
[C1.3] That is not leveling the field upward; it is hardening it.
[C1.4] Sectoral bargaining is also less simple than PRO suggests. To be legitimate, it must carve out skill levels, regions, shift patterns, training wages, part-time and seasonal work, and fast-moving occupations like platform work. That creates complex classification disputes, litigation, and enforcement burdens—especially in industries defined by subcontracting, franchising, and high turnover. Governments then must monitor not just minimum pay but the internal architecture of entire labor markets.
[C1.5] The better path is targeted, adjustable protection: a strong statutory minimum wage indexed to local conditions, robust enforcement, wage theft penalties, portable benefits, and supports such as tax credits that raise take-home pay without destroying employer demand. These tools help low-paid workers without imposing one rigid wage architecture on every firm in an industry. On balance, governments should raise floors, not freeze labor markets.

[C5.1] The decisive reason to reject sectoral bargaining is error control. PRO assumes a board can price whole industries—skill, region, season, franchises, platform work—accurately and quickly. But the more detailed the floor, the more ways it can be wrong, and the harder it is to fix. A rural guesthouse cannot wait for next year’s review; escalators can force raises when revenues fall.
[C5.2] If schedules are set too high, hours disappear, hiring slows, and small entrants fail. If set too low, PRO’s dignity promise evaporates.
[C5.3] A broad minimum wage with indexation and targeted credits accepts the same uncertainty without concentrating it. It raises incomes while letting the state fix one number, support workers directly, and punish wage theft. That is not misfiring blindly; it is limiting the blast radius of inevitable mistakes.
[C5.4] PRO promises to stop undercutting, but illegal undercutting is stopped by inspections and penalties, not industry-wide pay architecture. Legal low pay is addressed by credits and stronger statutory floors. Sectoral bargaining asks governments to price every job in every local market, then blames fragile firms for failing. Vote CON because workers need raises that survive contact with reality, not rigid standards that break the jobs they rely on.
