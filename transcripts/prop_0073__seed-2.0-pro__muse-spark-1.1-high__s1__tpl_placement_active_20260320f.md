[P1.1] When a private person is found still holding stolen property, every court in every state orders it returned, or fair compensation paid. No one argues that because the theft happened 50 or 150 years ago, the thief gets to keep collecting rent on it forever. This motion does nothing more than hold states to that same basic rule.
[P1.2] This is not open-ended reparations. It applies only when two very specific conditions are met: first, land was seized from Indigenous communities without fair agreement or just compensation; second, a public institution is still actively benefiting from that exact land today. We are not debating historical harm, or symbolic apology. We are debating ongoing, current unearned profit that the state collects every single year.
[P1.3] Consider the most common case: public universities built directly on seized reservation land. Every tuition dollar charged, every research grant won, every endowment return from campus real estate flows directly from that original seizure. That benefit does not expire with time.
[P1.4] Critics will claim this opens endless claims, or bankrupts governments. That is false. The motion gives states full flexibility: return the land, set up a small permanent revenue share, or negotiate equivalent restitution. There is no forced lump sum payout, no mass eviction.
[P1.5] Most importantly, the rule only applies while the state still holds and benefits from the asset. If the land was transferred to private owners long ago, this does not apply.
[P1.6] This is not radical. It is basic fairness. If you would not let a private landlord keep stolen property, you cannot let the state do it either.

[C1.1] The stolen bike analogy collapses on contact with history. Private theft has a victim present to sue, a thief present to punish, and a clear title record. Conquest and treaty cessions 150 years ago created the legal title every deed office still relies on. [C1.2] And what does PRO want to undo? Not a private landlord's rental. Public universities, state hospitals, capitol buildings. These institutions do not collect rent for shareholders; they educate, heal, govern.
[C1.3] A revenue share does not punish a thief, it taxes today's students to pay perpetual rent to a separate government based on ancestry. At a public university built on disputed land, every dollar skimmed for restitution is a dollar off financial aid, lab funding, or staffing - costs borne by 18-year-olds who were not alive when the land was taken, many of them Indigenous students themselves.
[C1.4] PRO promises flexibility and limits. There is no limit. Almost all state-owned land in the West was once Indigenous land. Which community gets it when three tribes claim the same valley?
[C1.5] What counts as "fair agreement" by 1870 standards? Who decides "equivalent restitution"? This turns budgets into open-ended tribal claims courts and creates a permanent ethnic property right inside a democracy that is supposed to hold public land for all citizens equally.
[C1.6] Justice cannot mean turning every state agency into a tenant on its own soil forever. If we care about Indigenous poverty, fund scholarships, health clinics, infrastructure for everyone in need, directly and transparently. Don't encumber the public square itself with a backward-looking rent that divides the public along bloodlines and bankrupts the very institutions that serve it.
