[P1.1] Recurring water shortages demand policies that cut actual waste, not just spread harm equally across users. Blunt universal restrictions force low-income households to forgo basic needs—skipping showers, letting home gardens die, closing small home-based businesses—while large high-volume users like golf courses, industrial facilities, and luxury estates make trivial cuts that still leave them consuming 10 to 100 times more than a family of four. Sharply rising marginal prices for heavy users fix this core flaw.
[P1.2] First, the policy targets waste directly: a 200% to 400% surcharge on every gallon used above a generous basic household threshold gives large users a far stronger incentive to eliminate non-essential use (decorative lawn watering, outdated inefficient industrial processes) than a universal cut that lets them claim exemptions for "essential" tourism or economic activity. Pilot programs in drought-prone regions have cut surplus high-end use far faster than equivalent universal restrictions, as heavy users game loopholes while vulnerable families bear the brunt of mandatory cuts.
[P1.3] Second, it is fairer than universal restrictions. Price hikes only apply to use beyond a threshold that covers all essential household needs: a low-income family using 5,000 gallons a month for drinking, cooking, and hygiene pays no extra, while a 1-acre estate using 50,000 gallons for lawns and pools pays steep surcharges on the 45,000 excess gallons. No more forcing families to choose between clean clothes and drinking water to cover cuts for users who waste water by the tankerful.
[P1.4] Third, it avoids the perverse incentives of universal restrictions: heavy users can simply opt out of public systems by buying private bulk water or drilling wells, draining shared reservoirs while avoiding restrictions. A use-based surcharge applies to total consumption, making that opt-out uneconomical and keeping shared supplies stable for all.

[C1.1] Recurring shortage is not an efficiency problem to be nudged—it is a physical constraint that must be met with certainty. Price mechanisms cannot guarantee that certainty; blunt universal restrictions can. That difference decides this debate.
[C1.2] First, heavy users are precisely the people least responsive to price. A luxury estate or a profitable industrial facility treats a 300% surcharge as a cost of doing business, not a behavior signal. If your reservoir has six months of water left, you cannot wait to see whether a wealthy household's price elasticity kicks in—you need a hard, enforceable cap on withdrawal now. Universal restrictions deliver exactly that: a mandatory reduction that does not depend on anyone's willingness to pay.
[C1.3] Pricing schemes let the shortage's actual physical outcome ride on the assumption that demand is elastic enough—and water demand studies consistently show it is stubbornly inelastic, especially at the top of the income distribution where the whole plan is aimed.
[C1.4] Second, tiered pricing takes time PRO doesn't have. Setting thresholds, adjudicating exemptions for medical, agricultural, and small-business use, running the public utility commission hearings usually required before rate structures change, and defending the inevitable legal challenges from major users—all of this unfolds over months or years. Recurring shortages demand an emergency-order lever that can be pulled in days: mandatory outdoor watering bans, occupancy-based caps, enforcement patrols. That is what restrictions offer and pricing structurally cannot.
[C1.5] Third, pricing creates a perverse institutional trap. If a rising-price scheme actually succeeds in cutting heavy use, utility revenue collapses precisely when conservation succeeds, forcing rate hikes on remaining ordinary users to cover fixed infrastructure costs—the classic utility "death spiral." Restrictions carry no such self-defeating incentive; the utility's financial health never depends on people continuing to overconsume.
[C1.6] None of this says price signals have zero value as a supplement. But when the question is which tool should be the primary, load-bearing response to recurring shortage, only guaranteed mandatory limits reliably produce the guaranteed outcome the crisis actually requires.
