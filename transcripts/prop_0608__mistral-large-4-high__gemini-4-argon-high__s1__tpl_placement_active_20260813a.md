[P1.1] Corporate "net-zero" claims currently rest on a dangerous fiction: firms can grow emissions while buying paper credits to cancel them out. This offset market is structurally broken. Additionality is routinely unproven—would that forest have been planted without the credit revenue? Permanence is fragile; carbon sinks burn, saturate, or leak.
[P1.2] Verification remains patchy. Yet companies use these questionable credits to claim climate leadership while their actual operations expand.
[P1.3] The proposition imposes necessary legal discipline. It caps offsets at a small share and requires the overwhelming majority of reductions to occur in-value-chain. This forces genuine transformation rather than financial engineering. When a manufacturer must decarbonize its own plants, logistics, and suppliers rather than purchasing distant credits, it drives permanent efficiency and structural change.
[P1.4] In-value-chain cuts are verifiable, durable, and aligned with long-term corporate survival. Offsets, by contrast, function as moral hazard—a fee paid to continue polluting while claiming virtue.
[P1.5] Picture an airline promising net-zero by 2050 while adding routes, buying forest credits of dubious permanence. Under this proposal, legal limits would force that carrier to electrify ground fleets, redesign operations, and pressure suppliers. The choice becomes stark: decarbonize the business or stop calling it net-zero.
[P1.6] Voluntary pledges have produced a credibility crisis. Without legal caps, "net-zero" remains marketing decoupled from atmospheric reality. The law must require most reductions where emissions originate—in the value chain—while confining offsets to a residual, tightly regulated role.
