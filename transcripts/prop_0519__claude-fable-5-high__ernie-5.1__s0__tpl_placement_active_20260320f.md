[P1.1] A statutory minimum wage is a single number asked to do the work of an entire labor-market policy. In fragmented service industries—fast food, home care, janitorial work, ride-hail—that number fails on three predictable fronts, and sectoral bargaining fixes each one.
[P1.2] First, the minimum wage is blunt. The same figure binds a rural diner and a downtown airport contractor, so legislators set it cautiously, calibrated to the most fragile employer in the most fragile region. Sectoral bargaining sets standards by industry, using the actual economics of that industry—what the work pays for, what margins exist, what workers in that sector need. California's fast-food council and New York's earlier wage boards show the model: tailored floors that a one-size statute could never reach.
[P1.3] Second, the minimum wage only sets a wage. The worst abuses in fragmented services are not just low pay—they are unpredictable scheduling, wage theft, misclassification, no sick leave, no training ladders. A sectoral agreement can set standards across all of these because workers and employers negotiate the whole employment relationship. A wage hike leaves every other dimension untouched, and employers routinely claw back the raise through cut hours and shaved shifts.
[P1.4] Third, consider durability and enforcement. Minimum-wage politics is episodic: a hard-fought hike, then years of inflation eroding it, then another bruising campaign. Federal stagnation since 2009 is the cautionary tale. Sectoral structures update continuously and build enforcement into the industry itself—worker representatives who know where violations happen, and compliant employers with a direct stake in policing competitors who undercut them.
[P1.5] That last point matters most in fragmented sectors: when labor standards are taken out of competition, the race to the bottom stops, and good employers stop being punished for decency.
[P1.6] The Nordic countries famously maintain high effective wage floors with no statutory minimum at all—proof that bargained sectoral standards are not a supplement to wage law but a superior substitute. That is the comparison this debate should turn on.

[C1.3] The Nordic comparison the proposition relies on is a category error. [C1.4] Worse, sectoral bargaining is an insider's game. Industry incumbents negotiate floors that also raise barriers to entry, protecting established firms from new competitors who might pay more. [C1.5] Finally, durability. The proposition claims sectoral structures "update continuously," but in practice they update when political conditions allow, which in fragmented industries means rarely. Minimum-wage hikes, once legislated, are simple to raise again.
