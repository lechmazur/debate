[P1.1] Governments should favor demand-side industrial policy for batteries and clean tech because the central problem is not a lack of factories; it is lack of bankable, durable markets. Producer subsidies pour money into capacity and hope buyers arrive. Demand-side policy does the opposite: it pulls investment into existence by making adoption profitable and predictable.
[P1.2] Clean technologies improve through deployment. Each installed battery pack, heat pump, or storage system creates learning-by-doing, service networks, and scale economies that cut future costs. Demand-side policy accelerates this loop directly.
[P1.3] The best demand-side tools are familiar but powerful: consumer rebates for EVs and heat pumps, storage auctions, grid interconnection reform, public procurement for electric buses, building codes, and performance standards. These instruments reward firms only when products are actually sold, installed, and used. That discipline matters in fast-moving technologies. Battery chemistry is still changing—lithium iron phosphate, sodium-ion, solid-state, recycling routes.
[P1.4] If states hand large subsidies to selected producers, they risk freezing the wrong design, inflating excess capacity, and funding firms that optimize grants rather than customers. Demand-side policy lets firms compete on cost, durability, and safety, while the state sets the destination: mass deployment, lower emissions, and secure supply.
[P1.5] Producer subsidies also carry predictable political-economy costs: overbuilding, lobbying, weak accountability, and trade retaliation. A subsidy race can leave taxpayers owning stranded plants while consumers still face high prices or missing infrastructure. By contrast, demand-side support builds legitimacy because citizens see benefits—cheaper bills, cleaner transit, visible charging networks.
[P1.6] This does not mean ignoring supply chains. Demand-side policy can be combined with permitting reform, workforce training, and targeted procurement that rewards domestic or allied production. But the engine should be market pull, not fiscal push. If countries want batteries and clean tech to scale without wasting scarce public funds, they should pay for outcomes, not capacity.

[C1.2] Demand-side policy assumes a mature, responsive supply base. But battery production requires massive upfront capital, years of construction, and complex upstream inputs—lithium processing, cathode manufacturing, precision engineering. A consumer tax credit does not de-risk a ten-billion-dollar plant. Only producer-side support, direct government procurement, or guaranteed offtake contracts give manufacturers the confidence to build.
[C1.3] Absent domestic capacity, demand-side subsidies simply transfer taxpayer money to foreign producers—primarily Chinese firms—while leaving the importing country strategically exposed.
[C1.4] The PRO side warns that producer subsidies freeze the wrong technology. In a pandemic, trade war, or conflict, a nation with subsidized demand but no domestic production has neither batteries nor leverage.
[C1.5] Producer subsidies build the industrial base, engineering talent, and supply resilience that rebates cannot.
[C1.6] Learning-by-doing requires manufacturing, not just installation. Every battery produced domestically trains workers, develops supplier networks, and drives cost reductions through scale. Demand-side policy captures these benefits only after the factory exists. For capital-intensive sectors where supply chains are fragile and geopolitical competition is intense, countries must build the supply side.
[C1.7] Favoring demand-side policy over producer subsidies is a choice to remain a permanent consumer of the technologies that will define the next century.

Manufacturing capacity is fungible and retooled; consumer subsidies for specific products are not. In a strategic competition, you cannot afford to wait for the market to decide when the market is dominated by a geopolitical rival.

Procurement and auctions for final products only disburse funds once goods are delivered; they provide no upfront capital for the capital-intensive refineries and material plants that constitute actual surge capacity. Producer subsidies build the upstream industrial base that demand-side instruments merely assume already exists, making them decisive for overcoming foreign control of critical inputs and ensuring that surge capacity is genuinely domestic.
