[P1.1] Recognition is not a footnote. It is one of the most valuable things the international community distributes. When we recognize a government, we hand it something no rebel army, no opposition coalition, no exiled parliament can mint for itself: legitimacy. That legitimacy is then cashed in for loans, for trade access, for diplomatic cover, for seats at the United Nations.
[P1.2] To say that all of this should flow automatically to whoever happens to exercise territorial control is to say that brute force is the only credential a state needs.
[P1.3] The alternative is not utopian. It is conditional recognition, anchored in minimum democratic and human-rights standards. This is the standard that, over decades, made space for the end of apartheid in South Africa, for the isolation that squeezed Pinochet's Chile, for the leverage that eventually pried open authoritarian regimes from Eastern Europe to Latin America. Without that pressure, those regimes held on longer and cost their people more.
[P1.4] The objection will be that this is "unrealistic," that we must engage whoever actually governs. But engagement on what terms? If recognition is automatic, our leverage evaporates the moment the general takes the palace. The recognition itself becomes the reward for the coup.
[P1.5] Pure territorial recognition has a built-in incentive problem: it pays dictators and punishes reformers, because the worse the human-rights record, the less likely internal reform is to remove the incumbent, and the more durable the territorial grip.
[P1.6] Conditionality is not about overthrowing governments. It is about refusing to launder their legitimacy for free. A foreign ministry that grants recognition purely because a regime sits on its capital is not being neutral. It is being an accomplice to the next seizure of power.
