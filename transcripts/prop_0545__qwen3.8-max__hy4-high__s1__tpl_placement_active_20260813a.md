[P1.1] The central question is whether a better default can turn existing public support into actual transplants. Opt-in systems do not merely protect choice; they lose donors through inertia. Many people would donate, but never register, carry a card, or update a database. When a potential donor dies suddenly, clinicians face a narrow window and an unclear record.
[P1.2] Under presumed consent, the default reflects that majority preference while keeping an exit: anyone who objects can register refusal, and families can still raise known objections. That shift is not a fictional consent; it is a policy inference from what most people would choose, coupled with safeguards for dissent.
[P1.3] This increases transplant rates because it expands the pool of eligible donors at the point where the shortage is most acute: unexpected deaths among otherwise suitable donors. Jurisdictions that have adopted opt-out defaults generally see higher donation rates than comparable opt-in systems, and the mechanism is straightforward. Hospitals can act on a clear default rather than waiting for a formal registration that may not exist. Each additional eligible donor can mean multiple organs, so even modest increases in consent translate into meaningful gains for patients waiting for kidneys, hearts, lungs, or livers.
[P1.4] The ethical and family-distress claims fail because presumed consent, properly designed, does not override autonomy; it changes the burden of action. People remain free to opt out, and families are still consulted, especially when they know of a loved one’s objection. In practice, families under opt-in often suffer more distress because they must guess an unrecorded wish or fight a system that treats silence as refusal. A transparent default, supported by public education, reduces that uncertainty.
[P1.5] It treats donation as the ordinary civic baseline while protecting sincere dissent. For most patients, that means more life-saving transplants without adding coercion or grief.
