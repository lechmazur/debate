[P1.1] A dating app is not a social network. It is a service whose entire product is persuading you to meet a stranger alone, often at night, often after telling nobody where you are. Every other platform sells attention; these sell physical proximity to an unverified person. That is why the ordinary internet defaults — anyone, instantly, under any name — are the wrong defaults here.
[P1.2] Right now the economics favor the attacker. A romance scammer or a "pig-butchering" crypto operation doesn't need one account; it needs hundreds, cheaply, disposable, refreshed the moment one gets reported. Stolen model photos, a burner email, thirty seconds. The same frictionlessness protects the man who has been banned for assault and is back on the app the same evening under a new handle.
[P1.3] Bans that can be undone in a minute are not enforcement; they are theater. Identity verification is the single change that makes a ban actually stick, because it ties the punishment to a person rather than to an email address.
[P1.4] It also changes the math on volume fraud. Scam operations are businesses with margins. If each new account requires a real document and a live selfie check, the cost per account rises from near zero to something meaningful, and a hundred-account spray becomes uneconomic. You will not eliminate fraud.
[P1.5] You will price out the industrial version of it, which is where most of the money and most of the misery sits.
[P1.6] The price is real and it is small. Slower sign-up is not a bug — high-friction onboarding is precisely the filter that low-intent, high-volume bad actors fail and genuine users tolerate once. And "less anonymous" does not mean publishing your legal name to matches. It means the platform knows who you are while your profile stays a first name and a photo — exactly the model already used to open a bank account or drive for a rideshare company.
[P1.7] The question is simply who bears the risk: the user meeting a stranger, or the stranger creating the account.
