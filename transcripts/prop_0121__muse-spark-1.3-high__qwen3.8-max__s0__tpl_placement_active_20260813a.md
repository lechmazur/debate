[P1.1] At nineteen you can vote, enlist, and sign a lease, but a notification rule would mean you cannot tell a therapist you feel hopeless without triggering a letter home. That contradiction decides this debate.
[P1.2] Mental health care only works when people actually show up and tell the truth. For young adults, fear of parental discovery is one of the most powerful reasons they do not. They stay silent about depression, anxiety, eating disorders, sexual assault, substance use, identity, and conflicts with their own parents precisely because those are the topics they least want automatically reported. A system that demands disclosure as the price of admission does not increase family support; it ensures many never seek help at all, turning treatable distress into dropout, self-harm, and suicide attempts.
[P1.3] Confidentiality is not a luxury here, it is the treatment. No one is honest with a therapist they believe is forwarding notes to their parents. Self-censorship makes diagnosis impossible and alliance impossible.
[P1.4] Paper privacy is meaningless if billing outs the patient. Most young adults remain on a parent's insurance into their mid-twenties, and a routine explanation of benefits can reveal provider, dates, and diagnosis. Allowing confidential communication, suppressed statements, or a discreet self-pay path closes the backdoor that currently deters care.
[P1.5] This does not shut parents out or ignore safety. Young adults can and often do invite parents in when that helps. Clinicians already must break confidentiality for imminent risk. What must end is automatic exposure for everyone else.
[P1.6] In unsupportive, shaming, or controlling homes, forced notification risks punishment, loss of tuition or housing, and worse symptoms.
[P1.7] The opponent's choice is oversight of an empty clinic. Private access means more young people get help in time.

When parents are the policyholders, billing statements let them know what they are paying for, catch errors or fraud, and coordinate care. [C2.1] PRO claims “consent solves what coercion cannot.” But consent presupposes a patient who has entered care, stays in care, and has enough insight to invite help. The motion is not about a mature college student choosing family therapy; it is about making parental notice impossible even when the patient is deteriorating. In conditions where denial is a symptom—eating disorders, mania, severe depression—waiting for permission can mean waiting until the crisis exception is the only option.
[C2.2] Their roommate example shows the flaw. A roommate may hear about an eating disorder on Tuesday, but a roommate usually cannot change housing, pay for treatment, contact a family physician, or notice the pattern over months. Parents often can. If the system bars routine notification and hides billing, the people with practical leverage are kept outside while the people with clinical access have only partial observation.
[C2.3] That is not prevention; it is privatized risk.
[C2.4] Insurance statements are not a demand for diary entries. They are the ordinary way a policyholder knows what services were billed, whether appointments occurred, and whether charges look like errors or fraud. PRO would preserve totals while suppressing provider and service details. But a total with no meaningful description cannot catch a misfiled claim, a duplicate charge, or a provider who never saw the patient.
[C2.5] It turns family coverage into a black box paid by parents but unavailable for basic stewardship.
[C2.6] Finally, universality is not precision. It shields young adults in unsafe homes, yes, but it also bars supportive parents from noticing missed care, offering transport, or intervening when therapy is abandoned. PRO frames this as partial information versus none. Notice and billing preserve a third option: families know care has begun and can support attendance without session notes.
[C2.7] The better rule is narrow: confidential content, robust safety exceptions, and opt-outs where disclosure is dangerous—not a blanket blackout that leaves families guessing while bills vanish.
