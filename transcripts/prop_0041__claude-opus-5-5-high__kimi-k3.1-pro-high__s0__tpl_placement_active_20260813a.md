[C1.2] A prohibition defended exclusively with examples of higher prices, while sweeping in lower ones, is already too broad.
[C1.3] The claim that every targeted discount must be funded by “quietly overcharging someone else” [P1.8] does not survive inspection. A retailer with unsold inventory can gain by selling an additional unit at a discount while leaving everyone else’s price unchanged. Nobody pays more; the retailer earns something rather than nothing; and a price-sensitive customer obtains a product otherwise out of reach. Algorithms optimize margin, yes—but sometimes margin is increased by extending sales, not raising prices.
[C1.4] Competition reinforces that possibility: rivals can win customers by offering them a better final checkout price, not merely by publishing an attractive price to the crowd.
[C1.5] Nor are PRO’s central harms unique to this practice. A rural monopolist can exploit captive customers with one uniform price. A retailer can use postcode-level discrimination without personalizing each offer. Surveillance can support manipulative advertising even if personalized pricing disappears.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, secret individual prices do not eliminate consumer comparison altogether: shoppers can compare the actual totals different retailers offer them, abandon baskets, and switch sellers. Opacity may justify safeguards; it does not establish that every individualized price is exploitative.
[C1.7] The sensible target is harmful conduct: deceptive differentials, discriminatory proxies, excessive prices, or data collected without proper authority. A blanket ban instead sacrifices mutually beneficial discounts while leaving much of the underlying surveillance and market power untouched.
[C1.8] PRO must justify the whole prohibition, not merely condemn its ugliest application. Regulate the squeeze; do not ban the price cut.
