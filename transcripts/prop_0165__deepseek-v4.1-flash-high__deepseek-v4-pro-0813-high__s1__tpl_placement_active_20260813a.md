[P1.1] Public EV charging is infrastructure, not a private club. A driver pulling into a publicly accessible station should be able to charge without checking which app, membership, connector, or payment silo the operator uses. That is why governments should require open interoperability standards for public charging networks.
[P1.2] The core problem is network effects. A charging network is more valuable when any EV can use it and any operator can connect. Proprietary dominance fragments that value: drivers juggle apps, subscriptions, and RFID cards; small towns get chargers only one brand can use; and businesses hesitate to host stations that lock out customers. Open standards at the essential layers — connector compatibility, payment, authentication, and roaming — turn many private stations into one usable public network.
[P1.3] Think of ATM networks or mobile roaming: providers compete fiercely, but the customer can cross boundaries without changing identity.
[P1.4] This is also a competition policy. Public charging depends on public subsidies, curb space, and grid connections. Letting proprietary systems dominate converts public support into private toll booths. Open standards lower entry barriers, so competition shifts to price, location, charging speed, and reliability — the things drivers care about.
[P1.5] A standard is not a mandate for one company's technology; it is a floor that lets many technologies compete above it.
[P1.6] The innovation objection fails: interoperability does not ban proprietary features or premium services. It bans lock-in at the public interface. Firms can still innovate in hardware, software, pricing, and customer experience; they just cannot make the network unusable for everyone else. Safety and reliability also improve when failures and performance are visible across a common interface.
[P1.7] If EV adoption is to be broad and fair, charging must feel like pulling into a gas station, not joining a different club every fifty miles. Governments build the rules for public infrastructure. They should require open plugs, open payments, and open roaming now, before proprietary dominance becomes too entrenched to undo.

[C1.1] Mandatory interoperability sounds like consumer protection, but it is premature industrial design. The motion asks governments to impose open standards on public EV charging just as charger speeds, connector types, payment authentication, and grid integration are still evolving. A mandated standard is not a neutral floor; it is a technical bet made by slow institutions. If the bet is wrong—say, for bidirectional charging or much higher power—operators must drag costly compliance systems through upgrades while competitors avoid the same burden.
[C1.2] That slows deployment and does nothing for the driver in a charging desert.
[C1.3] The deeper problem is investment. Charging networks are capital-intensive and often lose money for years. Proprietary differentiation can be the only way a builder recovers that cost: a reliable network, clear app, predictable maintenance, and seamless billing justify the upfront risk. A blanket interoperability mandate forces the first mover to share the user experience at the public interface, reducing the return on building in marginal areas.
[C1.4] The predictable result is fewer chargers, because waiting for someone else to build becomes rational.
[C1.5] The ATM and mobile-roaming analogies reverse the sequence. Those systems standardized after the technology matured and after commercial incentives made interconnection valuable. Charging is not there yet. Tesla built the most dependable fast-charging network while proprietary, then opened parts voluntarily as the market matured and as rivals improved.
[C1.6] The voluntary move created interoperability without regulators freezing an early design.
[C1.7] Public subsidies do not require this mandate. Governments can attach open-access, pricing, uptime, or payment conditions to the specific chargers they fund, and competition law can address genuine foreclosure. That targets abuse without making the state the architect of a young industry. The real choice is not open-versus-closed forever; it is whether to force standardization early or let proprietary and voluntary interoperable models compete.
[C1.8] We should referee safety and transparency, not design the charging stack. Reject the mandate.

[P2.1] C1.1 treats “open standard” as if government must pick today’s chemistry and freeze it. That is a strawman. We can require interoperability at the public interface—payment, authentication, roaming, and connector compatibility—while allowing performance-based updates, multiple compliant designs, and proprietary features behind that interface. A rule for how a driver pays or authenticates is not a bet on 350 kW versus 1 MW; it is a bet against every network inventing its own toll booth.
[P2.2] C1.3 says lock-in is the reward for capital risk. But interoperability expands the market: a station any driver can use has more potential customers, higher utilization, and easier host-site deals. Operators still compete on reliability, speed, location, pricing, and service. In marginal areas, the answer is subsidy plus a common interface, not fragmentation—a charger usable by only one brand’s customers has a smaller revenue base and is more likely to fail.
[P2.3] C1.5’s sequence argument cuts the other way. ATM and roaming standards arrived when interconnection became valuable, and incumbents still had to be pushed. Waiting now lets proprietary networks capture prime sites and then resist opening. Tesla’s voluntary opening actually concedes that interoperability is valuable; it does not prove voluntary opening will happen everywhere, soon enough, or on fair terms.
[P2.4] Voluntary openness can be a tactic: open enough to quiet regulators, closed enough to hobble rivals.
[P2.5] C1.7’s subsidy conditions cover only the subsidized charger. The driver still faces a patchwork, and competitors can build private moats around the rest. Competition law is ex post, slow, and hard to apply to network effects.
[P2.6] The real choice is not early perfection versus evolution. It is whether the default is one usable public network or many private clubs. Open standards set a floor; they do not cap the ceiling. Proprietary dominance turns public infrastructure into private tolls.

[C2.1] The weakest part of PRO’s rebuttal is the claim that a public-interface mandate is a thin, neutral floor. P2.1 says government can require payment, authentication, roaming, and connector compatibility while leaving innovation elsewhere. But those are not bookkeeping details; they are the competitive core. Payment can involve card readers, app wallets, RFID, plug-and-charge, fleet accounts, and emerging bidirectional export.
[C2.2] A binding standard must pick protocols, divide liability for failed sessions, set security duties, and then revise them through rulemaking. If the chosen interface misses commercial fleet billing or vehicle-to-grid authorization, the operator cannot simply innovate behind it—the failure sits at the customer connection. That is a technical bet, relabeled as a floor.
[C2.3] P2.2 assumes open access creates demand. It mainly raises use where utilization is already high. In a rural site with five daily visits, universal app acceptance does not make the station profitable; it may remove the anchor fleet contract or branded service guarantee that justified construction. The lower return can mean no station where the desert is worst.
[C2.4] P2.3 and P2.4 reverse the lesson. ATM and roaming standardized after networks were widely built and mature. Charging is still moving on power levels, connector cooling, and bidirectional integration. Mandating the interface now is less like enabling shared ATMs and more like fixing a check-clearing standard before checking was common.
[C2.5] Voluntary roaming and cross-network payment are already emerging; grants, permits, leases, and utility access can carry open conditions without freezing the whole design. If a firm uses shallow openness tactically, competition and consumer law are the targeted answer.
[C2.6] P2.5 understates that subsidy conditions can attach to more than the charger. And P2.6’s one-network-versus-private-clubs choice is false: technical openness still leaves different pricing, queuing, maintenance, and app quality. The mandate does not make the experience uniform; it constrains the layer where innovation is most unsettled. The mandate therefore risks fewer, not more, usable stations.
