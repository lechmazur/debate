[P1.1] A gallon of water used to cook dinner or bathe a child is a human necessity; the ten-thousandth gallon used to keep a sprawling lawn green during a drought is a luxury. Blunt universal restrictions, like odd-even watering days, flat percentage cutbacks, or blanket outdoor bans, fail because they rely on clumsy micromanagement rather than total volume. Under an odd-even rule, an estate with an acre of turf simply runs its sprinklers twice as long on permitted days, while a frugal family that already replaced its grass faces arbitrary mandates.
[P1.2] During recurring shortages, regions should instead rely mainly on steeply tiered pricing: keeping baseline water affordable while imposing sharply rising rates on heavy users.
[P1.3] First, pricing delivers reliable conservation because no one can cheat a water meter. Blunt restrictions require municipal patrollers hunting for midnight sprinkler violations, an unenforceable game of whack-a-mole. Worse, universal percentage cuts punish households that already conserve because they have no fat left to trim.
[P1.4] By contrast, a sharply escalating price schedule is automatic and targeted. Rather than bureaucrats dictating whether a resident must sacrifice a vegetable garden or a car wash, or micromanaging diverse businesses, a steep price signal compels heavy users to cut their lowest-value waste first.
[P1.5] Second, steep tiering is far more equitable. When cities rely on universal restrictions, falling water sales create utility revenue shortfalls, forcing utilities to hike fixed base charges on everyone, making low-income savers pay more for using less. Sharply rising rates on heavy users solve this. Cities can guarantee a cheap baseline tier adjusted for household size, protecting large working-class families.
[P1.6] Meanwhile, punitive top tiers hammer discretionary excess. Even if a wealthy estate still pays to keep its lawn lush, those steep surcharges generate vital revenue to subsidize low-income water bills and fix leaky pipes. Protect the baseline, price the excess, and retire blunt mandates.

[C1.1] A drought is not a budgeting problem. It is a physics problem. A reservoir at twenty percent does not care how much revenue the utility collected last quarter; it cares how many gallons left the pipes this week. That is the test any policy must pass: does it deliver a guaranteed, fast, predictable cut in total withdrawals when the city needs one?
[C1.2] Universal restrictions do. Prices don't.
[C1.3] Start with what pricing actually is: a bet. Raise the top tier and demand might fall a lot, a little, or barely at all, and you find out months later when the bills clear. Water bills arrive after the water is gone. A city facing a dry summer cannot run an elasticity experiment on its last reservoir.
[C1.4] A watering ban, by contrast, removes a known category of demand within days, which is why cities that have stared down real "Day Zero" scenarios leaned on staged, mandatory restrictions as the backbone and treated pricing as garnish.
[C1.5] Second, the proposition concedes its own failure. The wealthy estate "still pays to keep its lawn lush." That is the whole point: steep tiers let the people with the most discretionary waste simply purchase an exemption. Meanwhile the "heavy users" the tiers actually bite are not estates.
[C1.6] They are six-person households, families caring for a dialysis patient, home daycares, and tenants in master-metered apartment buildings who never see a price signal at all because the landlord pays and splits the rent. Size-adjusted baselines sound fine until you ask which utility holds verified household-size data for every meter. None do.
[C1.7] Third, shortages run on legitimacy. Conservation collapses when neighbors watch sprinklers run next door. Restrictions declare that nobody wastes; prices declare that wasting is fine if you can afford it. The first builds the social norm that actually produces deep, durable cuts.
[C1.8] The second corrodes it.
[C1.9] And note the revenue trap: a utility that funds itself on top-tier sales now needs heavy users to keep using heavily.
[C1.10] Protect the reservoir first. Price the margins later.
