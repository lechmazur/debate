[P1.1] Trade blocs were built for a world of containers. That world is no longer where the jobs, exports, or bottlenecks are.
[P1.2] In most established blocs, tariffs on goods are already low, often in low single digits. Shaving another point helps a narrow set of importers at the margin, but it does not open a market that is legally closed. For services, closure is the normal barrier: a qualified nurse cannot work across a border without retraining, an engineer cannot sign off on a project, a therapist cannot serve a client online, and a fintech or cloud provider cannot operate because its home licence is treated as foreign and therefore invalid.
[P1.3] That is not a tax. It is a ban.
[P1.4] Mutual recognition turns that ban into trade without forcing identical rules. Each member keeps its own standards, but agrees a credential or licence properly granted and supervised at home is good enough abroad, backed by registries, audits, and suspension rights. It is the difference between re-earning a degree and showing it.
[P1.5] Prioritising it is also where the return now sits. Services employ most workers in bloc economies, and digital delivery removes the need for a factory, warehouse, or local subsidiary. A small telehealth firm, tutoring platform, engineering consultancy, or software provider can export on day one if its qualifications and digital authorisations travel. Under tariff-first trade, that firm gets nothing, while large goods shippers with lawyers and supply chains capture most of the gain.
[P1.6] Finally, recognition solves shortages tariffs cannot. Ageing populations need carers and clinicians; energy transitions need certified electricians, grid engineers, and auditors; governments need cyber specialists. You cannot tariff-cut your way to a licensed professional allowed to practise. You can recognise one who already exists, end brain waste among migrants forced into unskilled work despite training, and let expertise move to where demand is highest.
[P1.7] If negotiating time is scarce, spend it unlocking people and services, not polishing tariffs already near the floor.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a difficult next step for the first priority. Mutual recognition can help, but trade blocs should not place it ahead of new tariff cuts because tariffs remain the most transparent, negotiable, and broadly shared barrier to integration. Average duties may be low in many mature blocs, but averages hide tariff peaks, sensitive agricultural and industrial lines, and tariff escalation that traps processors and manufacturers in costly input chains. Even a small duty on repeated cross-border shipments of components can determine whether a regional supply chain competes.
[C1.2] Cutting those tariffs lowers prices for consumers, reduces costs for small exporters, and strengthens the bloc’s industrial base in ways that are immediate and verifiable.
[C1.3] Mutual recognition, by contrast, is not a simple unlock. It requires trust in foreign regulators, disciplinary cooperation, complaint mechanisms, and equivalence assessments that can take years and collapse after one scandal. Calling a licensing rule a “ban” does not make it safe to waive. If a nurse, engineer, or financial adviser is recognised across borders, the importing country must be confident that supervision, ethics rules, and enforcement are genuinely comparable.
[C1.4] Rushing that process invites regulatory arbitrage, weakens consumer protection, and can turn public safety into a bargaining chip. Digital services make this harder, not easier: data protection, cybersecurity, fraud, and tax compliance cannot be solved by treating a home licence as automatically sufficient.
[C1.5] The PRO also assumes tariff cuts mainly benefit large goods shippers. That ignores how tariffs on food, fuel, building materials, machinery, and intermediate goods affect households and small firms every day. Recognition deals are more likely to benefit already-mobile professionals and platform firms while leaving less visible barriers in place. The sensible sequence is not to abandon tariff liberalisation, but to pursue recognition where feasible without displacing the gains tariff cuts reliably deliver.
[C1.6] Trade blocs should keep cutting the barriers they know how to cut first.

[P2.1] Tariff peaks do not rescue tariff-first priorities. They are concentrated in exactly the sensitive farm and industrial lines where blocs have resisted cuts for decades. Calling those lines both the main remaining prize and the easy, reliable win tries to have it both ways. For the great bulk of goods trade, duties are already low, so another cut trims a point from a shipment that can already move.
[P2.2] Mutual recognition changes something that cannot legally move at all into something that can. A marginal price reduction does not beat a binary opening.
[P2.3] Difficulty is an argument to start now, not to delay. If equivalence assessments, disciplinary sharing, and registries take time, postponing them while polishing tariffs only deepens shortages. Nor is recognition automatic trust with no safeguards. It can be scoped sector by sector, tied to a live home regulator, shared enforcement records, host-country discipline, and immediate suspension after failure.
[P2.4] That is safer than the present alternative: understaffed hospitals, projects delayed for lack of certified engineers, and skilled migrants working beneath their training.
[P2.5] The distributional claim also reverses. Food and building-material tariffs are often the very peaks governments protect, so household relief is promised precisely where delivery is least likely. Recognition helps households directly by increasing supply where scarcity bites: more recognised carers and clinicians shorten waits, more electricians and engineers lower project costs, and more competing online tutors, accountants, and therapists restrain prices. It also lets a small digital firm export without a foreign subsidiary, and a qualified worker earn at the level she trained for.
[P2.6] Finally, tariffs offer no answer to data, fraud, or cyber risk. Recognition can condition access on cooperation against exactly those harms. Refusing it does not stop cross-border digital demand; it leaves that demand informal and harder to police. Regulated entry beats exclusion pretending to be protection.

[C2.1] PRO’s central contrast—“a marginal tariff trim versus a binary opening”—is too neat. In mature blocs, the remaining tariff problem is not a single point shaved off a finished good; it is peaks and escalation that make entire chains uneconomic. A seemingly modest duty may look small in a schedule, but when it applies to a component crossing the border several times, it can decide whether a regional factory exists at all. Tariff cuts also work through price signals every consumer and firm can see, while mutual recognition works through discretion, equivalence reviews, and regulatory confidence that are far harder to scale.
[C2.2] PRO says difficulty is a reason to start now [P2.3], but that confuses starting with prioritizing. Negotiating capacity is finite. If blocs put recognition first, they must build disciplinary links, complaint systems, audit trails, and data-sharing before the public can trust the result. A single failure—an unsafe building sign-off, a fraudulent telehealth clinic, a data breach—can freeze recognition across the sector.
[C2.3] Tariff liberalization does not carry that same fragility: once the duty falls, the market can use it without trusting a foreign regulator’s day-to-day supervision.
[C2.4] The household argument also overreaches [P2.5]. Recognition may add supply in some licensed occupations, but it mostly helps those who already hold credentials and can navigate cross-border registration. Tariff cuts on food, materials, machinery, and intermediates reach households and small firms even where users are not mobile professionals. PRO’s answer—that those tariffs are protected—does not make recognition easier.
[C2.5] Health, safety, immigration, and platform liability are politically sensitive too, often more so than customs schedules.
[C2.6] Finally, “regulated entry beats exclusion” [P2.6] assumes recognition can police digital risk better than ordinary enforcement. It cannot, unless member states first align supervision. Prioritizing recognition before that alignment trades broad, enforceable liberalization for narrow access deals that are slow, brittle, and vulnerable to backlash.
