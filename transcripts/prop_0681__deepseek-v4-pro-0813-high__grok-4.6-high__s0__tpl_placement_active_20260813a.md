[P1.1] A faster response is not a better response when the responder is the wrong tool for the emergency. The core question is whether a person in psychiatric crisis gets safe, effective help, not merely whether someone arrives quickly.
[P1.2] Mental health crises are not like cardiac arrests, where every minute of delay causes direct physiological damage. They are episodes of fear, confusion, agitation, and distress. A clinician can de-escalate by listening, offering space, and connecting someone to voluntary care. A police officer, by contrast, is trained to command compliance and use force when control fails.
[P1.3] Sending police faster may simply produce faster handcuffs, faster trauma, or faster escalation.
[P1.4] The damage from a wrong response is not speculative. High-profile deaths and injuries have occurred when armed officers were sent to mental health crises. An arrest gives a criminal record instead of treatment, making the next crisis more likely. Non-police teams consistently shift outcomes from arrest and emergency detention toward voluntary outpatient care and follow-up.
[P1.5] They reduce repeat calls because they address the underlying condition rather than just clearing the scene. That durability matters more than shaving minutes off the first arrival.
[P1.6] The concession that response times may rise in some neighborhoods must be judged against the harm of the current system. Many of those same neighborhoods already experience the heaviest police contact and the least mental health investment. A slightly slower trained crisis worker is not abandonment; it is a better form of response. Fast failure is not public safety.
[P1.7] If a person in crisis waits a few extra minutes for someone who can actually help—and is not killed, beaten, jailed, or re-traumatized—the trade is justified.
[P1.8] Finally, this is not a choice between speed and compassion. By transferring mental health calls away from police, cities free officers to answer violent emergencies faster and build capacity where it is scarce. The proper standard is whether the response matches the need. A non-police team that arrives later and helps is a success; a police car that arrives sooner and escalates is not.
