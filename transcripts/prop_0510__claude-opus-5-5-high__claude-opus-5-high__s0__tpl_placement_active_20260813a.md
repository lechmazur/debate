[P1.1] The question is not whether high debt matters. It does. The question is when a country should pay it down, and the worst possible moment is when unemployment is rising, tax revenue is collapsing, and households are cutting spending at once. Automatic stabilizers such as unemployment insurance, progressive taxes, and means-tested support exist for exactly that moment.
[P1.2] My case rests on three points.
[P1.3] First, stabilizers are the most debt-responsible form of fiscal support. They switch on when the economy weakens and switch off when it recovers, without new legislation. A laid-off warehouse worker draws benefits for months, not forever. When she is rehired, the payments stop and her taxes resume.
[P1.4] This support is temporary, targeted, and self-reversing, which is precisely the kind of deficit bond markets tolerate. CON must explain why the fiscally dangerous choice is a program designed to end itself, rather than the permanent tax cuts or ad hoc bailouts that politicians reach for when rules are absent.
[P1.5] Second, suppressing stabilizers in a slump often raises debt ratios instead of lowering them. The debt ratio is a fraction, and GDP is the denominator. Cut support when private demand is already falling, and output falls further. The eurozone periphery after 2010 is the cautionary tale.
[P1.6] Front-loaded austerity during recession deepened the contraction, and debt-to-GDP kept climbing anyway. Consolidating into a downturn shrinks the denominator faster than it shrinks the numerator.

[C1.2] That is a permanent statutory widening enacted under crisis pressure — the opposite of the self-reversing mechanism PRO describes in [P1.3].
[C1.3] Which collapses the heart of PRO's case. A benefit that lapses when the warehouse worker is rehired is temporary. A statute raising replacement rates or extending eligibility is not. Emergency generosity has a well-known ratchet: it is politically cheap to enact in a downturn and politically impossible to remove in a recovery, so the cyclical boost is temporary while the structural cost is forever.
[C1.4] PRO's own sequence — support now, consolidate later — is precisely the promise that the 2010s and 2020s show governments do not keep.
[C1.5] Second, at elevated debt, fiscal space is not a straight line; it has a cliff. Markets tolerate small deficits and then abruptly do not. When the risk premium moves, interest costs consume the very transfers you legislated, banks holding sovereign debt wobble, and mortgage rates rise for the same households you meant to protect. Italy in 2011 did not get a gentle warning.
[C1.6] For emerging markets the channel is faster and crueler: currency depreciation and inflation, a tax on the poor that no benefit expansion offsets.
[C1.7] Third, PRO's denominator argument assumes a multiplier fixed by textbook. At high debt with a jittery spread, expansion can partly finance itself into higher rates and crowding out — the boost leaks into the interest bill.
[C1.8] The responsible answer is not to stop stabilizing. It is to build stabilizer capacity *before* the downturn and to tie expansion to a credible debt anchor. The motion's "even if" is the problem: it writes a blank cheque and calls it prudence.
