[P1.1] The core claim here is simple: when a public university's home city cannot house the people already living in it, the university cannot keep expanding its intake as if housing were infinite. Capacity is not just seats in a lecture hall. It includes a bed to sleep in. And when there aren't enough beds, unconstrained recruitment stops being growth and becomes displacement.
[P1.2] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, public institutions owe a duty to the public that funds them. A public university is subsidized by local taxpayers and exists partly to serve the surrounding community. When it recruits students faster than the housing market can absorb, it converts a public asset into a driver of rent inflation for the very residents who underwrite it. International recruitment is heavily revenue-motivated, and revenue is a weak justification for pricing nurses, teachers, and existing domestic students out of their own neighborhoods.
[P1.4] Second, caps are a targeted response to a targeted harm. The motion does not say "cut international students" as a blanket policy. It says cap recruitment *when shortages become severe*. That is a conditional, proportionate brake, triggered only when the evidence of harm is real.
[P1.5] Picture a mid-sized university town where a wave of new students, unable to find rooms, floods a rental market that was already tight—vacancy near zero, three students bidding on every flat. In that scenario, adding more demand you cannot house is not neutral. It actively hurts people. A cap is the responsible tool for that moment.
[P1.6] Third, unconstrained capacity fails even the students it recruits. Bringing people across the world into a city with nowhere to live produces homelessness, exploitative landlords, and hidden hardship. Recruiting students you cannot decently accommodate is not access; it is a broken promise.
[P1.7] CON must defend the harder position: that a public university should keep expanding intake even while local families and its own students are squeezed out of housing. My side simply asks institutions to match ambition to the beds available. That is not xenophobia. It is stewardship.
