[P1.1] Labor markets only work when both sides know the price. Today, employers know exactly what every role pays and how often people advance; workers negotiate blind. That asymmetry isn't a quirk—it's a subsidy, paid by employees to employers, and it falls heaviest on those least positioned to demand information: women, minorities, first-generation professionals, anyone who doesn't have a network whispering the real numbers. Mandatory disclosure of pay bands and promotion rates by role simply ends the subsidy.
[P1.2] Three reasons this requirement is justified even at the cost of managerial flexibility.
[P1.3] First, secrecy is where pay discrimination lives. Disparities rarely survive sunlight—not because managers are villains, but because opaque, ad hoc decisions accumulate bias invisibly. When pay bands are public, a woman earning fifteen percent below band for identical work can see it, ask about it, and fix it. Promotion-rate disclosure does the same for the subtler problem: companies that hire diversely but promote homogeneously.
[P1.4] You cannot manage—or contest—what no one can see.
[P1.5] Second, transparency makes the whole market more efficient. Workers sort to jobs that actually value them, employers stop wasting interview cycles on mismatched expectations, and compensation flows toward performance rather than negotiation bravado. Jurisdictions like Colorado and New York City already require posted pay ranges; the sky did not fall, and job seekers gained a baseline that pay-secrecy never offered.
[P1.6] Third, weigh what "managerial flexibility" actually means here. It is not the flexibility to reward stars—bands accommodate that, and exceptional cases can sit at the top of a band or justify a re-leveling. The flexibility lost is the flexibility to pay two people differently for the same work without ever explaining why. That discretion isn't a management tool; it's an accountability gap.
[P1.7] We confine this to large employers, who already maintain pay structures and HR data—disclosure costs them a spreadsheet, not a strategy. The proposition trades a small amount of arbitrary discretion for fairness, efficiency, and trust. That trade is worth making.
