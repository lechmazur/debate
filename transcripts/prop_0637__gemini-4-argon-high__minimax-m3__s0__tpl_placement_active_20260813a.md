[P1.1] Blocking new apartments near transit does not freeze a neighborhood in amber; it simply forces wealthier newcomers to outbid existing tenants for the scarce homes already there. Municipalities must allow densification by-right near transit because the localized friction of new construction is vastly outweighed by the permanent devastation of regional housing scarcity.
[P1.2] First, "by-right" approval is necessary to break the bottleneck of discretionary review. When every proposed apartment building requires case-by-case permits and public hearings, the process is routinely captured by incumbent homeowners with the time and resources to shrink or kill projects. Multi-year delays inflate financing costs and strangle supply. By-right zoning establishes predictable rules upfront: if a project near a station meets the building code, it gets built automatically.
[P1.3] Second, compare short-term local displacement risks against the counterfactual of inaction. Upzoning a transit corridor can indeed incentivize redeveloping older buildings, creating immediate disruption for tenants on those specific parcels. Yet refusing to build guarantees a worse, broader wave of displacement. Affluent workers drawn to transit-accessible neighborhoods do not vanish when construction is banned; they simply outbid existing residents for the older housing stock.
[P1.4] One new high-density building near a rail stop acts as a sponge, absorbing dozens of households who would otherwise gentrify the surrounding blocks. Furthermore, short-term localized displacement is a solvable problem: cities can use the property tax windfall from new density to fund tenant relocation stipends or right-to-return guarantees. Conversely, no municipal program can house people when physical units do not exist.
[P1.5] Finally, capping density around expensive public transit infrastructure is fiscal and environmental malpractice. It rations car-free mobility and job access to a privileged few while forcing working families into distant sprawl with punishing commute expenses. Trading manageable, short-term transition costs for permanent housing abundance and climate resilience is a trade municipalities must make.

[C1.2] Start with the mechanism. "By-right" is not merely faster approval. It is the deliberate removal of community leverage over what gets built on a specific block. When a municipality waives discretionary review, it also waives its ability to require affordability, family-sized units, ground-floor small-business space, or anti-displacement protections on the very parcels where redevelopment pressure is highest.
[C1.3] The proposition calls this streamlining. In practice, it is municipal self-mutilation. Cities that have stripped local conditions in exchange for raw speed have repeatedly produced luxury flats and investor units, not housing for the displaced.
[C1.4] The "sponge" theory is the load-bearing wall of PRO's case, and it does not hold. New market-rate units near transit are overwhelmingly occupied by higher-income newcomers, not the lower-income residents displaced from surrounding blocks. When a six-story luxury building replaces a walk-up, the displaced tenant does not move into the new tower. She leaves the neighborhood, or the city, or the housing market altogether.
[C1.5] The supply created is real; the displaced households it supposedly absorbs largely are not. PRO's own [P1.4] concedes the problem and waves a property-tax windfall at it, but revenue is not a unit, and a relocation stipend does not reconstruct a rent-stabilized household.
[C1.6] Finally, the timeline. "Short-term" displacement of renters is a euphemism for the destruction of multigenerational networks, immigrant-owned businesses, and community institutions that took decades to build and cannot be rebuilt by a one-time check. A displaced senior on a fixed income does not wait out the abstraction; she gets pushed out, permanently.
[C1.7] The proposition forces a false choice between speed and tools. A better path exists: targeted upzoning paired with mandatory affordability, community land trusts, right-to-return ordinances, and tenant protection overlays. By-right strips those tools away exactly where they are most needed.
