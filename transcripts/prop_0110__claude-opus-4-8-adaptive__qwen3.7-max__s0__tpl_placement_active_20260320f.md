[P1.1] The proposition is about value: health per dollar. So let's keep our eyes on that ratio, not on any single feature anyone admires. The claim is that systems organized around public coverage extract more population health from each dollar than systems built predominantly on private insurance. The evidence for that is not subtle—it's the central pattern of the developed world.
[P1.2] Start with the cleanest natural experiment we have. The United States, the developed world's flagship for predominantly private insurance, spends far more per person than peer nations—roughly twice the average—yet trails them on the outcomes that actually measure population health: life expectancy, infant mortality, preventable deaths. Countries with predominantly public systems—think the UK, Canada, France, Japan—spend dramatically less and live longer. That is the per-dollar comparison stated in the motion, and the private-heavy model loses it.
[P1.3] Why does this happen? Three mechanisms, each structural rather than accidental.
[P1.4] First, administrative overhead. A single public payer negotiates and processes once. A private insurance market multiplies billing departments, claims adjudication, marketing, underwriting, and profit margins. Those dollars buy paperwork, not health.
[P1.5] The US famously spends a far larger share of its health budget on administration than single-payer peers.
[P1.6] Second, pricing power. A public system negotiating for an entire population sets prices; fragmented private insurers cannot, so providers and drug companies charge more for the identical pill or procedure. Same care, higher price, worse value.
[P1.7] Third, prevention and universality. When everyone is covered from birth, primary care and chronic-disease management happen early and cheaply. Predominantly private systems leave gaps—uninsured and underinsured people skip prevention and arrive in emergency rooms, the most expensive door in medicine. Coverage gaps don't save money; they defer cost to a worse, pricier stage.
[P1.8] My opponent will point to waiting lists and innovation. Hold them to the motion: those are not population-health-per-dollar wins, and on the metric that counts, queues for elective procedures don't kill the way uninsurance does.
[P1.9] The ratio is what we're judging. On that ratio, public systems win.
