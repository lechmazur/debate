[P1.1] When a grandmother dies, her shelf of paperbacks goes to her grandchildren. When she dies with two thousand e-books, hundreds of albums, and a lifetime of games bought on a platform, those purchases simply evaporate, reabsorbed by the company that already took her money. The real question is whether the word "buy" means anything at death. It should, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, consumers paid full value for something presented as theirs. Storefronts say "Buy Now," not "Rent Until You Die." People spend thousands over decades believing they are building a library. Fine print nobody negotiates quietly converts that purchase into a personal lease that self-destructs when the buyer does.
[P1.3] Law routinely refuses to let standard-form contracts strip away basic protections. Inheritance is one of the oldest principles in property law, and it deserves that same floor.
[P1.4] Second, inheritance takes nothing that rights-holders were ever owed. The legitimate fear about digital resale is perfect copies flooding a market. Inheritance is different: one deceased owner, one library, one transfer to next of kin. The dead person stops reading; the heir starts.
[P1.5] Active copies do not increase by even one. The publisher already sold that copy at full price. Letting it vanish does not protect their market. It hands them a windfall from a funeral.
[P1.6] Third, this is technically easy. Platforms already run family sharing, account recovery, and legacy-contact tools. Moving a library on presentation of a death certificate and proof of executorship is ordinary administration. Banks manage far harder transfers every day.
[P1.7] And the stakes are not only financial. A father's annotated e-books, a mother's curated music collection, the games a family played together are today's bookshelf and record cabinet. A legal guarantee keeps them in the family instead of deleting them by default.
[P1.8] CON must defend a world where buying something means your children lose it when you die.

[C1.2] A heritable, perpetual right to a copy is a different good at a different price. E-books and games are cheap precisely because the license ends with the licensee.
[C1.3] Guarantee inheritance and you reprice every license already sold. Sellers respond rationally: higher prices, subscriptions instead of purchases, tighter authentication. The people harmed are the ones PRO wants to help — readers who preferred a cheap lifetime license to an expensive heritable one.
[C1.4] PRO says active copies never increase. That confuses a copy with a customer. The publisher sold to one reader whose demand is now gone; the heir is a new reader who would otherwise have bought. And a guaranteed transfer right on a death certificate creates the cleanest laundering channel the resale market has ever had: accounts "inherited" by strangers, with paperwork from jurisdictions nobody can check.
[C1.5] Family sharing is revocable and bounded; statutory inheritance is neither.
[C1.6] Nor is next of kin the deceased's choice. A guarantee hands an estranged son his mother's reading history, her marginalia, her correspondence with people who never agreed to be inherited. Mandatory transfer overrides the will and third parties' privacy at once.
[C1.7] The keepsakes PRO mourns — annotations, playlists, DRM-free files — are data, protectable through portability and estate access without turning every license into heritable property. That narrower path saves the bookshelf. PRO's guarantee saves it by destroying the bargain that made it affordable, and handing it to whoever the statute names, not whoever the dead loved.
