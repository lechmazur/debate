[P1.1] Student debt is the only major form of consumer credit in America that survives almost every financial catastrophe a person can suffer. Lose your house to a medical crisis, run your credit cards into the ground, watch your small business collapse — all of that debt can be discharged in bankruptcy under ordinary hardship standards. Student loans cannot. Current law requires proof of "undue hardship," a standard courts have interpreted so harshly that it typically demands showing near-total, permanent incapacity — not merely that you will never realistically earn enough to pay the debt off.
[P1.2] That gap between "genuinely can't pay" and "legally allowed to discharge" is the core injustice this debate is about, and it is where PRO wins.
[P1.3] Consider a borrower who took out loans for a degree that led to a low-wage job, or no job at all — a for-profit college certificate that turned out worthless, a graduate program in a saturated field, or someone who got sick or disabled mid-career. Decades later, through no fecklessness of their own, they are still carrying balances that have often grown due to interest and fees far beyond what they originally borrowed. Wage garnishment, tax refund seizure, and Social Security offsets can follow them into retirement.
[P1.4] This is not a hypothetical population — it describes millions of older Americans whose loans will never be repaid no matter how long they live, yet who are legally barred from the fresh start bankruptcy is supposed to guarantee everyone else.
[P1.5] My case rests on three pillars I'll develop through the round: first, that current law fails the basic moral purpose of bankruptcy — giving genuinely insolvent people a way out of unpayable debt; second, that "long-term inability to repay" is a workable, evidence-based standard courts already use in analogous contexts, so this isn't a leap into chaos; and third, that the harms CON will raise — moral hazard, lender losses, taxpayer exposure — are manageable and already priced into how the loan system works, while the harm of doing nothing is borne [clipped]
