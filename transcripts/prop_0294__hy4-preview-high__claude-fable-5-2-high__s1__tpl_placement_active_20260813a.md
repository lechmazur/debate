[P1.1] When a government changes and the history textbook changes with it, no new evidence has been found. That is the problem. Public schools teach memory of war, colonialism, and national identity—the subjects rulers most want to rewrite. The motion asks whether the narrative should be shaped mainly by independent scholars or mainly by shifting political control.
[P1.2] It should be the former, because only scholarly review ties claims about the past to evidence, not to the election calendar.
[P1.3] No one proposes to expunge democratic input. The target is 'mainly.' If politicians can mainly decide the account of a colonial land grab or a wartime atrocity, the account becomes a tool of legitimation. War and colonialism leave records and treaties.
[P1.4] Independent historians test narratives against those sources; political actors test them against approval. The result is visible when a chapter flips after an election: a campaign once called 'civilizing' is later called 'exploitation,' then quietly dropped—not because the evidence changed, but because the ruling coalition did. A textbook under scholarly review can interpret, but cannot deny documented events because they embarrass the current majority.
[P1.5] Identity is most vulnerable. Politicians can commission a curriculum that flatters the nation's self-image; scholars must show how that identity was formed, including the violence and exclusion it often rests on. The defender of political control will call this democracy. It is not; it confuses popularity with truth.
[P1.6] Elected officials are accountable to a temporary majority; historians are accountable to evidence and peer review. Such review can include diverse scholars and public comment, so it is not an unaccountable priesthood. It replaces the less accountable power of a transient majority to decide what children must believe about the past.
[P1.7] If the past is mainly owned by whoever holds office, there is no shared history—only a slogan that changes with the government. The textbook must be anchored in scholarship so democratic argument begins from facts, not from whoever won.
