[P1.1] Governments should guarantee universal primary care because it is the part of health systems where universal access is most morally urgent, most cost-effective, and least compatible with market rationing. Primary care is first contact: the clinician who catches hypertension before a stroke, treats asthma before the emergency room, vaccinates children, manages diabetes, refers to specialists, and prevents small problems from becoming catastrophic ones. If that gateway depends on ability to pay, the result is delayed care, avoidable hospital use, and worse outcomes for the poor and chronically ill.
[P1.2] Our model is not state-run everything. Elective and supplementary services—cosmetic procedures, private rooms, faster access to non-urgent amenities, alternative therapies, many specialist procedures people can choose to purchase—can remain private. The guarantee is a floor: every resident can see a primary-care clinician without financial barrier. That floor protects equal concern for persons while leaving room for choice above it.
[P1.3] It can be delivered through public clinics, regulated insurance, or subsidized private practitioners; the essential point is universality at the point of need.
[P1.4] Three arguments should decide this debate. First, health need is unpredictable and medical demand is highly unequal; leaving primary care to voluntary purchase leaves the sickest and lowest-income unable to afford it. Second, primary care generates public benefits beyond the individual: infectious disease control, maternal and child health, chronic disease management, and reduced emergency burden. Society cannot rely on private decisions to produce those benefits.
[P1.5] Third, universal primary care is fiscally rational. Prevention and coordination are cheap relative to emergency admissions and late-stage treatment. A system that pays for a clinic visit but not for an avoidable ICU stay is not spending less; it is spending badly.
[P1.6] The real clash is whether basic medical care is a commodity or a civic guarantee. Allowing private elective care concedes choice and innovation; guaranteeing primary care ensures no one is abandoned where the stakes are life, death, and preventable suffering.
