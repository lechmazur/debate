[P1.1] Continuous glucose monitors can do something conventional screening cannot: they reveal patterns in real life, not merely a snapshot of glucose on one clinic day. Among people without diabetes, this can expose previously hidden post-meal spikes, nocturnal lows, or sustained instability, especially in people with prediabetes, PCOS, intense exercise habits, or a family history of diabetes. That information enables earlier clinical evaluation, better nutrition and activity decisions, and, for some people, early intervention before complications accumulate.
[P1.2] The strongest case for adoption is not that every healthy adult needs constant surveillance; it is that voluntary, appropriately screened use creates a low-friction pathway from hidden dysfunction to actionable knowledge. A single glucose check can miss what a person’s daily pattern reveals. Someone who discovers a repeated post-meal spike can change the timing or composition of meals; someone who sees an unexpected overnight low can discuss it with a clinician rather than guess. These are plausible mechanisms, not speculative cures, and they operate upstream of overt disease.
[P1.3] Critics will emphasize anxiety, false alarms, incidental findings, and rising costs. Those are genuine risks, but many are avoidable design problems rather than an inevitable verdict on the technology. Clear thresholds, periodic review rather than constant self-diagnosis, and clinician guidance can prevent healthy readings from being treated as emergencies. The proper benchmark is whether the technology is offered responsibly, not whether every possible misuse will occur.
[P1.4] Public-health benefits will also be distributed more broadly than harms. One person may become anxious for several weeks; thousands can gain better metabolic control, exercise confidence, or earlier access to care. Consider a person who feels unwell after meals but has repeatedly normal laboratory tests. A monitor may identify a consistent pattern worth investigating; anxiety alone provides no comparable route to useful intervention.
[P1.5] Therefore, adoption should be voluntary, evidence-based, and paired with education and follow-up. If managed that way, the benefits exceed the anxiety and over-medicalization they may cause.

But the arithmetic runs the other way: millions of healthy users generating noise and worry, so that a fraction might learn something a targeted A1C test would have caught for a few dollars.
