[P1.1] Algorithmic reputation systems now decide whether you get hired, housed, admitted, or trusted—and they do not forget. A disputed customer rating, a stale background flag, or a scoring model trained on someone else’s bias can trail a person from platform to platform, job to job, apartment to school. People should have a legal right to disconnect from these systems when they cross contexts that shape basic life chances.
[P1.2] Without that right, exit is a fiction. You can delete an app, but the score may already have been sold, scraped, or mirrored into tenant-screening tools, recruiting software, and risk models. Error becomes permanence. Context collapse becomes destiny.
[P1.3] The worker who left a toxic workplace, the student mislabeled by an automated proctoring flag, the tenant dinged by a roommate’s history—all face the same trap: the algorithm keeps the memory even when institutions should not.
[P1.4] A legal right to disconnect is not a demand to abolish evaluation. It is a demand for boundaries. It means you can refuse persistent cross-domain profiling, demand deletion or siloing of reputation data after a defined period or role ends, and obtain a clean slate for decisions that should rest on present fitness, not past algorithmic residue. Society already recognizes analogous limits: bankruptcy gives financial fresh starts; juvenile records can be sealed; credit reporting has time bounds.
[P1.5] Reputation algorithms deserve the same human-scale guardrails.
[P1.6] The alternative is a world where private scoring becomes a portable caste marker—opaque, hard to contest, and inescapable. Markets can still rate and hire. Platforms can still moderate. They simply cannot chain a person’s past scores into every future door.
[P1.7] Dignity, mobility, and second chances require the legal power to cut the wire.

They are bounded exceptions, not a general right to exit evaluation.
[C1.5] A disconnect right also harms those it promises to help. It also deprives good tenants and workers of proof that they are reliable, pushing them back toward credentials, referrals, and personal connections.
[C1.6] Since people with damaging records opt out first, the remaining pool becomes less trustworthy.
[C1.7] The state should not grant algorithmic amnesia. It should require fair memory: bounded, contestable, accountable. That preserves second chances without licensing fraud, endangering schools and tenants, or replacing measurable performance with hidden prejudice.
