[P1.1] American copyright exists to reward original human expression, not to mint property rights over whatever a machine emits. The proposition preserves that principle while leaving room for genuine AI-assisted creation: if a human can document substantial creative control over the final expression, protection remains available. If not, the output stays unprotected.
[P1.2] The core problem is authorship. A person who types “paint a sunset over Mars” and prints the result has not authored the image in any meaningful copyright sense. The machine has made countless concrete choices about line, color, composition, and detail. Granting a monopoly to the prompt-writer would privatize expression without human creativity, swelling the copyright registry with thin claims and inviting trolls to assert rights over images, text, or code they never shaped.
[P1.3] A documentation requirement supplies the missing line. It asks for evidence of human judgment: iterative prompts, selection among outputs, rearrangement, painting over, editing, fixing errors, combining elements, and decisions that determine the final look or sound. That is not burdensome for real creators. Anyone using AI as a tool in a serious workflow already keeps drafts, project files, prompt histories, and edit logs.
[P1.4] The rule simply says: if you want an exclusive right, show what you actually controlled.
[P1.5] This also protects the public domain. Purely automated outputs should be free for everyone to use, remix, and build upon. If a company wants to protect investment in AI products, it can rely on contracts, trademarks, trade secrets, or licensing, but copyright should not become an automatic subsidy for compute.
[P1.6] The debate is not “pro-AI” versus “anti-AI.” It is whether copyright should require proof of human creative contribution. It should. Protecting documented human control encourages responsible tool use, prevents land grabs, and keeps copyright tied to its central legal and cultural purpose: human authorship.

[C1.2] The proposed test is also unworkable. What counts as substantial? Is one exquisitely crafted prompt less creative than fifty lazy revisions? Is curating a thousand outputs to find the perfect image not authorship?
[C1.3] Copyright has always used a famously low bar — a modicum of creativity — precisely to avoid judges playing art critic. This would replace that modest threshold with an invasive audit of process.
[C1.4] That audit will not burden trolls; it will burden ordinary creators. A freelance illustrator, indie game maker, or YouTuber iterating with AI will not keep litigation-ready edit logs. [C1.5] Worse, everyone will have incentive to lie about AI use rather than disclose it.
[C1.6] And leaving undocumented outputs unprotected does not free culture — it makes creators prey. Investment in difficult, expensive AI workflows — film previsualization, illustrated textbooks, indie games — collapses if competitors can free-ride instantly. Contracts and trade secrets are useless once a work is public.
[C1.7] Copyright should judge the originality in the final expression, not demand a diary of how the human got there.
