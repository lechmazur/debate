[P1.1] Africa’s service state fails most clearly where identity fails. A woman who cannot prove who she is cannot open a mobile-money account, collect a subsidy, enroll a child, or cross a border for work without paying a broker. That daily tax of non-recognition falls hardest on women, refugees, and people born outside formal systems. A continent-wide digital ID framework is the African Union’s best instrument for ending it at scale.
[P1.2] National systems already exist, and they already leak. What they do not do is recognize one another. A trader in a neighboring market, a patient seeking care across a border, a seasonal worker moving through a regional bloc — each hits incompatible registries, paper certificates, and repeated capture by whoever stands at the gate. The AU should pursue interoperability: shared standards, mutual recognition, and a trust framework so proof of identity travels with the person.
[P1.3] That is how pensions reach the right household, vaccinations are recorded once, disaster cash does not wait on a week of paper, and continental trade is not strangled by identity friction.
[P1.4] The motion does not ask us to pretend surveillance risk is imaginary. It asks whether that risk defeats the project. It does not. The alternative is not privacy.
[P1.5] It is fragmented surveillance: agencies, telcos, and vendors already hold sensitive data, often with weaker law, no audit, and no service in return. A continental framework can impose purpose limits, independent oversight, and federated checks instead of one giant database. Exclusion is itself coercive. People without ID are not free of the state; they are invisible to its benefits and exposed to informal gatekeepers.
[P1.6] And “pursue a framework” is not “switch on a panopticon.” It means minimization, redress, and revocation designed before rollout, not after scandal.
[P1.7] Judged by the people the patchwork cannot see, gains in health, cash transfers, banking, and lawful movement outweigh a risk that already exists in worse form and can be bounded by design. The AU should build the framework.

[C1.1] The motion is honest about its price: more surveillance. So judge it by whether the promised service gains are real enough to be worth a permanent, irreversible cost. They are not.
[C1.2] Start with the mechanism PRO never examines. Digital ID does not deliver services; it gates them. The moment identity becomes the key to the ration, the pension, the clinic, everyone whose fingerprints have worn thin from farming, whose iris scan fails, whose record was deduplicated as a "ghost," loses the thing they already had. India's Aadhaar is the cautionary case: a system sold as inclusion that produced authentication failures at ration shops and people cut off from food they were entitled to.
[C1.3] Uganda's national ID has been widely criticised for locking elderly people out of pensions and health services. The very woman in PRO's opening [P1.1] is the one most likely to fail a biometric match — and unlike a village clerk, a database cannot be argued with.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's safeguards are the part the AU cannot deliver. The AU can convene, standardise, and recommend; it cannot compel a member state to fund an independent regulator or resist its own security services. So what actually gets built continentally is the interoperable plumbing — the easy, vendor-supplied part — while "purpose limits, independent oversight, redress" [P1.6] remain aspirations in member states with no enforcement. You export the capability and localise the accountability.
[C1.5] That is the worst possible split.
[C1.6] Third, irreversibility. A leaked password can be changed; a leaked iris cannot. And identity registries outlive the governments that build them. Rwanda's 1994 identity cards, marked with ethnicity, are the continent's own memory of what a tidy population register becomes in the wrong decade.
[C1.7] Ethiopia's recent conflicts showed how fast ID can become a checkpoint tool.
[C1.8] PRO says the alternative is fragmented surveillance. Fragmentation is a feature. It means no single failure, no single capture. The alternative I defend is cheaper and safer: fund civil registration, birth certificates, and tiered verification — recognition without a continental spine of biometrics.
