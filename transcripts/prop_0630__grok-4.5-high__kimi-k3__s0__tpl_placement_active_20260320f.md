[P1.1] Gene-edited crops deserve streamlined rules distinct from traditional GMO regulation because their precision and typical absence of foreign DNA produce plants whose risk profile matches conventional breeding far more closely than transgenic insertion. Traditional GMOs often introduce DNA from distant species, raising legitimate questions about novel proteins, outcrossing, and ecological effects that justified heavy process-based scrutiny. Gene editing, by contrast, can rewrite a plant’s own sequences with single-base changes or clean deletions—edits that occur naturally or via mutagenesis and leave no foreign genetic material.
[P1.2] When the finished crop is indistinguishable from one developed through decades of conventional breeding, subjecting it to multi-year transgenic-style reviews is pure regulatory overkill.
[P1.3] That distinction accelerates real agricultural gains without sacrificing oversight. Climate pressure, pests, and soil limits demand faster trait delivery: drought tolerance, fungal resistance, reduced browning, or higher nutrient density. Streamlined, product-focused rules let developers clear limited, low-risk edits on the basis of the actual change—composition, allergenicity, agronomic performance—rather than the tool used. Oversight remains; large rearrangements or intentionally novel traits still face scrutiny.
[P1.4] What disappears is the automatic presumption of hazard that freezes innovation while competitors advance.
[P1.5] Treating every CRISPR edit like a transgenic event simply imports process fetishism into product safety. It delays climate-ready varieties farmers need now and keeps chemical inputs higher longer. Limited allowance under distinct rules is therefore the science-aligned path: open the gate for precise, non-transgenic improvements, keep risk assessment focused on the plant itself, and leave the heavier GMO machinery for true foreign-DNA constructs. Countries that make this separation will field better crops sooner; those that refuse will keep slow rules for no safety benefit.

[C1.5] Third, "limited" is not a stable legal category.
