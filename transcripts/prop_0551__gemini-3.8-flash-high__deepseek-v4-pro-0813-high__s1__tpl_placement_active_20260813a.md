[P1.1] In the twentieth century, society recognized that leaving electricity and clean water to unregulated commercial markets condemned rural and impoverished communities to second-class status. Governments stepped in with universal service mandates, legally requiring providers to connect every home at fair, regulated rates. Today, high-quality internet occupies that exact structural role. It is no longer an entertainment luxury; it is the foundational conduit of modern civilization.
[P1.2] Without reliable, high-speed broadband, an individual cannot apply for employment, attend school, access telehealth, complete tax filings, or participate in public discourse. Yet when broadband is treated as a discretionary consumer commodity, market mechanisms fail predictably. Private internet service providers systematically underinvest in sparsely populated rural regions and lower-income urban neighborhoods where the capital expenditure of laying fiber yields low short-term returns. This digital redlining entrenches inequality, leaving millions stranded on unaffordable, obsolete connections.
[P1.3] Treating broadband as a legally enforceable universal service corrects this market failure through three clear mechanisms. First, it establishes binding performance baselines, ensuring minimum upload, download, and latency standards so families receive functional connectivity rather than nominal access. Second, it creates an enforceable carrier-of-last-resort obligation, guaranteeing that geography or income cannot justify exclusion.
[P1.4] Third, it mobilizes universal service funds—analogous to rural electrification programs—to distribute high capital costs fairly across the entire network.
[P1.5] Crucially, this model does not demand state-run monopolies. Just as private utilities build and manage power grids under public oversight, commercial providers can compete and innovate while bounded by a mandatory public mission. The core principle is accountability: essential social infrastructure cannot be made contingent on corporate profit margins.
[P1.6] To deny a citizen high-quality internet today is to lock them out of modern economic and civic life. Classifying broadband alongside water and electricity ensures that technological progress empowers the whole public, not just the most profitable slice of it.

[C1.1] The water-electricity analogy is seductive but collapses on inspection. Clean water and grid power rest on settled life-critical standards: potability has a biological threshold, and voltage or frequency have been stable for decades. “High-quality internet” has no such stable legal meaning. A court-enforceable right forces judges and regulators to freeze yesterday’s speed and latency metrics. Technology moves faster than legislation; a 100 Mbps mandate becomes obsolete before litigation ends, yet providers remain legally exposed for failing an outdated benchmark.
[C1.2] That is not accountability, it is rigidity.
[C1.3] Second, water and power are largely natural monopolies served by one set of pipes or wires. Broadband is not. Fiber, cable, fixed wireless, satellite, and mobile networks already overlap many households. Converting broadband into a legal utility can entrench today’s incumbent as carrier of last resort, dull the competitive pressure that drives faster speeds and lower prices, and let incumbent firms use universal-service subsidies as a protective moat against new entrants.
[C1.4] Third, the funding mechanism is less equitable than it sounds. Universal-service charges often fall on all subscribers, so a low-income urban family can end up subsidizing expensive rural fiber. Yet the main barriers for many disconnected households are device cost, digital skills, and service affordability, not the absence of a legal right. A high-quality mandate channels scarce funds into last-mile construction while adoption may barely rise.
[C1.5] The argument that broadband is needed for jobs, school, and telehealth proves importance, not entitlement. Food, housing, transport, and medical care are also essential, but states often handle them through targeted support, competition policy, and public facilities rather than court-enforced utility mandates. Internet access matters greatly; a litigation-prone, one-size-fits-all universal service right is the wrong tool.
