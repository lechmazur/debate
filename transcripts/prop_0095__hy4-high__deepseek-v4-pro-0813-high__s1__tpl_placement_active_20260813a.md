[P1.1] The central fact is simple: owning footage is not owning a living actor’s face, voice, and interpretive choices. Those are the instruments of the actor’s labor. Ordinary editing polishes a performance; AI de-aging or synthetic voice can materially rewrite it—erase decades from a face, supply vocal tones never produced, or shift emphasis and emotion after the actor has gone home. That is not color correction.
[P1.2] It is a new, computer-mediated use of a human performance.
[P1.3] So the rule is modest: before that new use, the studio must secure explicit, informed consent and pay separate compensation for it. Explicit matters because broad future-technology boilerplate lets a performer “agree” before knowing their face will be rebuilt or their voice simulated. Compensation matters because the studio is monetizing the very thing audiences value: the recognizable person, their craft, and the belief that the performance is theirs. Avoiding reshoots or manufacturing a younger version of an actor may be cheaper, but cost savings are not a license to take an extra use of someone’s identity for free.
[P1.4] Consent also protects meaning. If a restrained, weary line is synthetically made brighter or angrier, or de-aging makes a character appear to do something at an age the actor never performed, the audience is asked to attribute choices to a living person who did not make them. Reputation, professional identity, and moral association are all implicated. The actor, not the VFX department, should decide whether that attribution is acceptable.
[P1.5] The opposition will stress efficiency: continuity, scheduling, youthful looks, damaged audio. Those may be good reasons to use the tools with permission; they are not reasons to skip permission. Asking first is cheap next to the right at stake. Paying first is fair next to the value captured.
[P1.6] Consent and compensation do not ban AI de-aging or synthetic voice. They keep the decision with the living performer whose face, voice, and work the studio proposes to alter.

[C1.1] The opening’s central line—“owning footage is not owning a living actor’s face, voice, and interpretive choices”—assumes the point at issue. A commissioned screen performance necessarily includes the right to use that recorded face, voice, and interpretation within the production. The performance is raw material for a collaborative final cut. Directors routinely replace lines with ADR, move reactions between takes, change rhythm in the edit, and reshape the performance without new consent.
[C1.2] De-aging and synthetic voice are more powerful tools, but they remain internal post-production choices, not a second public exploitation of identity. If the tool changes legal duties, every editing software upgrade becomes a legal event.
[C1.3] The proposal’s trigger is also unworkable. “Materially alter” has no stable meaning: removing a breath, smoothing skin, replacing an inaudible word, or de-aging a face all change the performance. Requiring explicit, separate consent for every material AI touch would give actors a veto over the director’s cut and turn post-production into a hold-up economy. An actor could greenlight the film’s script at signing, film the part, and then demand extra payment after shooting merely because the studio chooses synthetic voice or de-aging.
[C1.4] That is not modest protection; it is a radical new performance right.
[C1.5] The compensation demand double-counts. The actor is already paid for the performance, and unions and personal contracts already handle residuals and use. Using AI within the same film is not a new market; it is a cost-saving alternative to reshoots, prosthetics, or casting another performer for flashbacks. Mandating a second payment taxes continuity-driven projects, independent films with thin budgets, and minor roles, while mostly empowering stars with leverage.
[C1.6] If the real concern is deception or use outside the film, existing fraud, defamation, and publicity rules already apply. But the proposition is broader: it would make every internal alteration something a studio must beg to perform, slowing production without protecting audiences and replacing negotiated contracts with an unworkable veto.
