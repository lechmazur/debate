[P1.1] Restitution is not a courier service. An object crossing an airport does not undo a century of extraction. The proposition names the missing half of the remedy: former colonial powers should fund the conditions that make return real—conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity—rather than declare justice finished when the crate is opened.
[P1.2] Consider what a returned object actually needs: stable storage, trained conservators, security, documentation, and a public narrative that explains why it is home. Colonial museums often have those things because colonial wealth built them; many receiving institutions are expected to absorb the costs overnight. That is not equality; it is a reverse transfer of risk. If a returned mask cracks in a leaking store-room or a bronze disappears from an underfunded gallery, the former power has secured the moral credit while the receiving community bears the loss.
[P1.3] Funding prevents restitution from becoming abandonment.
[P1.4] Provenance research matters for the same reason. Colonial catalogues are often thin, euphemistic, or silent about coercion. Research in source communities can restore names, histories, and chains of custody—and identify other objects that should never have left. That is not a luxury add-on; it is how restitution becomes accurate and ongoing.
[P1.5] Museum capacity is also about sovereignty. A community that can conserve, display, lend, and interpret its own heritage is not dependent; it is empowered. The funding must be recipient-led, long-term, and without veto rights over how objects are shown. But “no strings” does not mean “no support.” Reparative justice includes repairing the institutional damage colonialism caused.
[P1.6] The opponent will say return is enough and funding is paternalism. But a check with no control is not paternalism; it is the difference between an apology and a repair. Treating shipment as the finish line lets former powers escape the harder, costlier part of decolonization. The motion asks them to finish the job.
