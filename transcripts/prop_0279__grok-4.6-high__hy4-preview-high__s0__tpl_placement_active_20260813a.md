[P1.1] Choosing not to have children is ordinary adult judgment, not a civic failure. Parenthood is not a lifestyle accessory. It is a decades-long, irreversible assignment of money, sleep, health risk, and daily labor to a person who cannot consent until they already exist. Adults who refuse that assignment are not skipping a quiz.
[P1.2] They are declining a job they do not want and cannot quit.
[P1.3] The charge of selfishness does not survive contact with the facts of the choice. Selfishness is using someone as a means. A child is not an entitlement, and other people’s future grandchildren are not a tax on a stranger’s body or bank account. Childfree adults still pay taxes, staff clinics and classrooms, care for aging relatives, mentor, and often absorb the overflow labor parents cannot.
[P1.4] Having a child to please relatives, to patch loneliness, or to “do one’s duty” is the heavier moral wager: it places an unchosen life at the center of someone else’s unfinished story.
[P1.5] Society treats childlessness as a problem because fertility numbers fall and panic fills the gap. Panic is not policy. If a country wants more children, it can make housing cheaper, childcare real, and medical care reachable for people who already want kids. Stigma, workplace interrogation, and the ritual of treating childfree women as unfinished do not manufacture better parents.
[P1.6] They manufacture resentment and neglected children. Demographic worry has other answers—immigration, later work, productivity—none of which require classifying a private life as a public defect.
[P1.7] Wanting a child is a valid desire. Not wanting one is equally valid. A decent society recruits parents by supporting those who choose the work, not by treating everyone else as a problem to be solved.

[C1.1] PRO's picture of parenthood as merely 'a job they do not want and cannot quit' [P1.2] is too small. Children are not just private projects; they are a public good. Every pension, hospital, fire crew, and classroom rests on a generation that someone chose to bear and raise. When adults who could parent decide not to, they still plan to spend old age drawing on that future generation.
[C1.2] That is not a morally neutral lifestyle preference; it is free-riding. [P1.3] It is not enough to say childfree adults 'pay taxes.' Taxes transfer money, but they do not create the workers and caregivers those taxes depend on. If everyone opted out, there would be no next generation to fund the system, and the childfree retiree would discover too late that the care home has no staff and the pension has no base.
[C1.3] This is why the charge of 'selfish' is not refuted. Selfishness is not only using a person as a means; it is also accepting the benefits of a cooperative system while avoiding its most costly duty. A person may have sympathetic reasons for not wanting children, but choosing the comforts of adult autonomy while relying on others' children is, at least in part, a selfish posture.
[C1.4] PRO calls demographic worry 'panic' and says immigration or productivity can fill the gap [P1.5]-[P1.6]. Those tools help, but they do not remove the problem: importing workers still requires someone, somewhere, to have and raise children, and productivity cannot invent caregivers. Society is therefore right to treat voluntary childlessness as a problem to solve—not by coercion or shaming the infertile, but by supporting family formation, valuing parental labor, and refusing to call withdrawal from the intergenerational project simply 'valid.' A society that stops asking who will sustain it has already begun to decline.
