[P1.1] The strongest reason to support this motion is that single-use takeaway packaging is a hidden public subsidy for private convenience. A foam box, plastic fork, or flimsy film lid may be cheap at the counter, but its true cost is paid elsewhere: in litter collection, clogged drains, landfill space, polluted rivers, and the long cleanup of materials that persist for decades. When those costs are not paid by the seller or the consumer, the market price is distorted. The result is overproduction of packaging that is used for minutes and managed, badly, for years.
[P1.2] This motion is carefully targeted. It does not ask cities to ban takeaway food. It asks them to phase out the most wasteful items: products designed for a few seconds of use, difficult or impossible to recycle, and likely to leak into the environment. Expanded polystyrene containers, plastic cutlery, straws, and thin mixed-material packaging fit that description.
[P1.3] They are the obvious first movers because the harms are high, the usefulness is narrow, and substitutes are widely available.
[P1.4] The objection about inconvenience and transition costs does not defeat the policy; it describes the normal cost of correcting a market failure. Reusable cups, returnable containers, and paper alternatives already work in many places. Predictable phase-outs let suppliers adapt, create economies of scale, and make alternatives cheaper. Governments can reduce burdens on small businesses through gradual timelines, procurement cooperatives, and limited hardship exemptions.
[P1.5] But they should not allow firms to keep externalizing cleanup costs onto the public simply because change is uncomfortable.
[P1.6] Voluntary action will not solve this. A responsible business that switches to better packaging may lose price competition to rivals still using the cheapest disposable. Regulation creates a level playing field. The decisive comparison is not convenience versus no change; it is short-term adjustment against permanent public harms.
[P1.7] Cities and countries should phase out the worst packaging because the public should not keep paying for private convenience.

[C1.1] The motion’s fatal weakness is the phrase “most wasteful.” That is not a technical category; it is a political label. Is a foam clamshell more wasteful than a heavy compostable bowl that needs industrial processing and often ends in landfill? Is a paper cup with a plastic lining better than a reusable cup that must be washed with hot water and transported back? Life-cycle comparisons routinely flip depending on whether we count carbon, water, litter, or landfill volume.
[C1.2] A phase-out forces regulators to pick winners by intuition, then locks in substitutes before we know they are better. That is not correction of a market failure; it is a new central-planning failure.
[C1.3] The costs are also not “some inconvenience.” Takeaway margins are thin, and small independent restaurants cannot negotiate packaging prices like chains. Switching suppliers, retraining staff, finding leak-proof alternatives, and absorbing higher unit costs can decide whether a marginal shop survives. Consumers feel it too: higher prices, fewer options, and worse performance when a “green” container leaks through a bag. For disabled or elderly customers who rely on delivery, packaging is accessibility infrastructure, not a disposable luxury.
[C1.4] And the environmental case is weaker than the motion assumes. The harm is waste leaking into public spaces and waterways, not the mere existence of single-use items. We can target that harm directly with litter fees, extended producer responsibility, deposit-return schemes, recycling standards, and enforcement against dumping. Those instruments make producers pay for cleanup and reward better design without banning the affordable, hygienic packaging that makes takeaway possible.
[C1.5] A phase-out is a blunt ban dressed as precision. It imposes certain, concentrated costs on small businesses and consumers while promising benefits that depend on substitutes actually being better, return systems actually working, and enforcement actually happening. Price the harm; don’t ban the product.

[P2.3] Where reusable systems impose washing or transport costs, those are still costs internalized by the business, not cleanup costs dumped on taxpayers.

If accessibility is the concern, the answer is targeted accommodations for disabled and elderly customers, not a blanket right to keep the most polluting packaging cheap.

Exemptions split the market so compliant small shops compete against exempt rivals; technical help does not pay higher packaging invoices. Consumers get higher prices and worse performance, and disabled customers get “targeted accommodations” only after the accessible default has been removed.
[C2.6] That is not “some inconvenience”; it is a regressive cost on thin-margin businesses. The motion asks us to accept certain transition costs for a benefit that depends on substitutes being better. Price leakage directly; don’t ban the product and hope.
