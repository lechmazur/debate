[C1.1] The proposition asks us to make statutes readable at the price of making them less lawlike. That is the wrong trade. The operative text of a statute is not a pamphlet; it is the instruction set for courts, agencies, police, businesses, and citizens in disputes where money, liberty, property, and safety hang in the balance. When precision is sacrificed, the cost is not merely stylistic.
[C1.2] It is broader delegations to prosecutors and judges, more loopholes, more inconsistent enforcement, and more litigation over what the legislature actually meant.
[C1.3] PRO says citizens cannot be bound by laws they cannot understand. But modern law regulates complex realities: securities fraud, pharmaceutical approval, environmental emissions, digital privacy, bankruptcy, criminal sentencing. A non-lawyer can grasp the gist—“do not defraud investors,” “do not dump pollutants”—while the operative statute still needs defined terms, exceptions, thresholds, and cross-references to avoid collapsing into slogan. If “plain language” means deleting technical definitions because they are hard for lay readers, the result is not democratic empowerment.
[C1.4] It is vague law, which hits least-resourced people hardest because they cannot predict how officials will apply it.
[C1.5] The stronger principle is accessibility without sacrificing operative precision. Legislatures can require official summaries, plain-language guides, indexed definitions, and better publication formats. Those tools give citizens entry points without turning the binding rule into a paraphrase. PRO’s motion goes further: it demands plainness even when precision suffers.
[C1.6] That is the decisive flaw. Once ambiguity enters the statute, courts must fill gaps, agencies gain discretion, and regulated parties can exploit drafting gaps. The public loses twice: first from unreadable laws, then from unmanageable ones.
[C1.7] Judges can resolve ambiguities, PRO argues, but resolving ambiguity is not the same as preventing it. Every avoidable gap transfers policy choices from elected lawmakers to unelected interpreters. In a democracy, the legislature should bear the burden of exact choices, not outsource them through fuzzy language.
