[P1.1] The forty-hour, five-day workweek is a relic of the industrial age, designed for factory floors and assembly lines, not the knowledge economy we inhabit today. Compressed schedules force ruthless prioritization, eliminate meetings that exist only to fill time, and reduce the cognitive fatigue that erodes decision quality. Pilot programs across Iceland, the UK, and parts of the US have indicated that four-day weeks maintain or increase output while slashing absenteeism and attrition.
[P1.3] In knowledge work—software engineering, design, law, management—the marginal product of hour thirty-five is often negative. A programmer debugging code at hour forty is introducing bugs, not shipping features, and a manager making hiring decisions while exhausted is selecting for bias.

This is not a gift; it is a correction to a market failure where individual firms overwork staff because they do not bear the full social cost of exhaustion, while competitors reap the benefits of rested rivals.
