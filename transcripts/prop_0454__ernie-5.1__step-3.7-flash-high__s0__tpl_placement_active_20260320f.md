[P1.1] When unemployment spikes, the economy loses not just jobs but the tax revenue that funds every public service. Automatic cash‑transfer stabilizers act the moment that loss appears, injecting money straight into the hands of people who will spend it. The multiplier of a dollar in a low‑income household is roughly one and a half to two, meaning each transferred dollar generates more than a dollar of economic activity.
[P1.2] By contrast, waiting for a politically negotiated stimulus can take months—months in which output collapses, tax receipts evaporate, and the debt‑to‑GDP ratio actually worsens.
[P1.3] The objection that we “can’t afford” higher debt during a downturn confuses nominal debt with fiscal sustainability. Debt sustainability is a function of debt relative to GDP, not the headline number. In a recession, GDP falls faster than debt rises if we borrow to keep demand alive. Moreover, interest rates are typically at historic lows when unemployment climbs, so the cost of borrowing is minimal.
[P1.4] The real cost of inaction is a deeper slump that leaves a larger debt burden and a smaller tax base for years to come.
[P1.5] Automatic triggers also solve the political problem. Discretionary stimulus is subject to partisan gridlock; automatic stabilizers fire without a vote, eliminating delays and the risk that a future government will reverse the support. The United States’ pandemic‑era Economic Impact Payments and the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act both showed that rapid cash transfers blunt the rise in unemployment and accelerate recovery, even though they added to the deficit in the short term.
[P1.6] Therefore, the decisive trade‑off is not “debt versus stability” but “short‑term debt versus long‑term fiscal health.” By preserving output, automatic cash‑transfer stabilizers protect the revenue stream that ultimately pays down that debt. Governments should adopt them precisely because the debt they incur during downturns is an investment in the economy’s capacity to repay it.
