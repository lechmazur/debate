[P1.1] Start with what this motion actually asks. It does not ban workplace apps, it does not forbid anyone from voluntarily using a personal phone for work, and it does not touch small businesses scraping by. It says one narrow thing: a large employer should not make installing its software on your own device a condition of keeping your job. That modesty is the case.
[P1.2] First, ownership. Your phone holds your children's photos, your bank logins, your medical searches, your location around the clock. Conditioning a paycheck on ceding space in that device is not a request; under the power imbalance of employment, it is conscription of private property. Consent given under threat of unemployment is not consent in any meaningful sense.
[P1.3] And the intrusion never clocks out. A tracking app does not know your shift ended. Location pings at your daughter's recital, at a protest, at a doctor's office — data the employer never needed, collected because the device never leaves your pocket.
[P1.4] Second, the commingling problem cuts both ways, and CON will struggle with it. Put corporate mail on a personal phone and one lawsuit can drag the whole device into discovery, family photos included. Lose the phone at a bar and the client list goes with it. Employers who genuinely care about security should want work data on managed hardware, not scattered across thousands of unpatched personal phones running whatever the user last downloaded.
[P1.5] Third, cost. A large employer can issue a phone or laptop for a few hundred dollars a head. Requiring workers to supply the hardware, the data plan, and the storage instead is a quiet wage cut — an operating cost shifted onto the people with the least power to refuse.
[P1.6] That is the decisive comparison: the alternative is cheap, standard, and easy. When a company can simply hand you a device, demanding yours instead saves pennies and buys surveillance. The line we defend is simple — the employer's convenience ends where the worker's property begins.
