[P1.1] Ask why we want battery and clean-tech factories. Not for concrete and ribbon-cuttings, but for cheap, rapidly deployed abatement. Large producer subsidies stimulate supply without guaranteeing the abatement is cheap, used, or timely. Demand-side industrial policy—procurement mandates, clean electricity and vehicle standards, public fleet conversion, and consumer incentives tied to real deployment—does the opposite: it creates a guaranteed market, then lets competition decide who serves it best.
[P1.2] That is the decisive path.
[P1.3] Start with efficiency. Producer subsidies are blunt technology-push. They force governments to pick favored firms or chemistries before anyone knows which will win. That invites lobbying, subsidy races, and stranded white elephants.
[P1.4] Demand-pull need not pick a winner. When a city commits to an all-electric bus fleet, or a country sets a zero-emission vehicle mandate, any firm can compete for that order. Government buys outcomes, not factories.
[P1.5] Experience makes the mechanism plain. Global solar costs fell dramatically not because taxpayers built champion panel makers, but because feed-in tariffs and renewable mandates created huge, reliable demand; scale and learning did the rest. Producer-subsidy programs too often left bankruptcies and idle plants because the protected firm still had no market. Batteries and clean tech are on the same curve: a gigafactory without assured offtake is a warehouse for stranded risk.
[P1.6] Demand-side policy fixes the real failure. The barrier is not only building a plant; it is underpriced carbon, high upfront costs, and missing complementary assets—chargers, grid links, installers. Subsidizing a factory does not charge a single electric truck. Mandates, procurement, and consumer incentives pull investment through the whole value chain and build the political coalition that protects climate policy: drivers, riders, and households with lower running costs, not firms hooked on the next handout.
[P1.7] Producer subsidies are brittle: they provoke trade disputes and become permanent corporate welfare. Demand rules can be technology-neutral. Factories should follow demand, not precede it on credit. Favor demand-pull; let the market be bankable first.
