[P1.1] An export ban does not make a grain shortage disappear; it decides which side of a border bears it. That is why the AfCFTA should discipline crisis export bans: integration is most valuable precisely when harvests fail and panic tempts governments to retreat behind borders.
[P1.2] When a major supplier abruptly bans maize, rice, or wheat exports, neighbouring governments cannot reliably plan purchases, prices can jump, and mills or traders dependent on cross-border supply are left stranded. The damage does not stop there. Farmers in the exporting country lose customers and may face weaker prices, reducing incentives for the next planting season. Traders turn to informal routes, neighbouring states consider retaliation, and everyone learns to hoard rather than trust regional markets.
[P1.3] A measure advertised as national protection therefore exports instability while weakening the production and trading networks needed for recovery.
[P1.4] The rules should limit bans, not prohibit every emergency restriction. A state facing a genuine, imminent domestic shortage could retain a tightly defined safeguard, but it should have to demonstrate necessity, notify partners, consult where feasible, use the least trade-restrictive effective measure, and accept a fixed expiry date with review. Humanitarian purchases deserve protection. Independent dispute settlement and meaningful consequences for unjustified restrictions are essential: voluntary assurances are least reliable when domestic political pressure is greatest.
[P1.5] Governments have better tools for protecting vulnerable consumers: releasing reserves, facilitating imports, and providing targeted income or food support. These address access to food without severing regional supply or punishing producers. An export ban often privileges today’s visible consumers over tomorrow’s harvest and invisible consumers across the border.
[P1.6] Sovereignty is no answer. States exercise sovereignty when they make reciprocal commitments, and every member gains assurance that its own food supplies will not be switched off unilaterally. Emergency flexibility remains; arbitrary economic self-help does not.
[P1.7] The choice is not between compassion and free trade. It is between shared rules that make neighbours part of food security, and a system in which each crisis triggers beggar-thy-neighbour policies. Enforceable limits turn regional interdependence into insurance rather than vulnerability.

[C1.2] PRO’s safeguard package [P1.4] is built for a normal trade dispute, not the first week of a shortage. Necessity proof, notice, consultation, least-restrictive design, fixed expiry, and adjudication consume time and expose emergency decisions to sanctions. Governments will not simply comply; they will relabel controls as licensing, quality checks, procurement, or port delays. A clear ban at least tells neighbours what is happening.
[C1.3] A legal ceiling on bans drives the same restriction underground.
[C1.4] PRO says [P1.5] reserves, imports, and cash support are better tools. They fail in the case that triggers export controls. Reserves run out. Imports depend on willing sellers, transport, credit, and foreign exchange.
[C1.5] Cash cannot buy grain already shipped abroad. When drought or panic thins a regional market, export demand can drain the last sacks from domestic shelves while ministers are blamed for hunger.
[C1.6] Continent-wide enforcement also mistakes geography. A maize failure in one basin, a port disruption in a wheat importer, and a currency crash in a rice market are not one legal problem. A uniform justiciable rule lets import-dependent states sue producers exactly when those producers face the prior claim of their own population. That is not reciprocity; it is a call on another country’s grain in its worst week.
[C1.7] Keep temporary published national controls, and coordinate around them through crop information, humanitarian corridors, reserve notices, and consultation. Those tools improve decisions without handcuffing the authority citizens will blame. PRO offers an insurance metaphor [P1.7]; insurance cannot require the hungry policyholder to leave the warehouse unlocked.

[P2.1] The opposition’s central mistake is to present a false choice: instant national action or weeks of continental litigation. Nothing in enforceable limits requires a minister to await judgment while shelves empty. A genuine, imminent shortage can justify an immediate, short safeguard, with prompt notice and subsequent review. Necessity, proportionality, and expiry discipline what follows; they do not create a veto over the first emergency response.
[P2.2] CON attacks a permission slip that was never proposed.
[P2.3] The claim that better tools “fail” also proves too little. Reserves can run down and imports can be difficult, but an export ban replenishes neither reserves nor foreign exchange, repairs no port, and recalls no grain already shipped. It merely changes who can buy the remaining supply. When grain is scarce regionally, destroying confidence that cross-border contracts will be honoured makes future sellers and financiers more cautious, not more generous.
[P2.4] A government may protect its people without acquiring an unreviewed, open-ended licence to shift costs onto neighbours.
[P2.5] Geography is no objection to a common legal test. Continent-wide rules need not prescribe identical treatment for drought, port disruption, and currency crisis; they require each government to justify its measure on the facts of that crisis. The domestic population’s “prior claim” is real, which is why the safeguard exists.
[P2.6] But neighbours are not demanding charity: they are asking governments not to tear up the reciprocal access on which their food planning relies. A prior claim cannot mean unlimited power.
[P2.7] Evasion is equally weak. Governments can disguise many regulations; enforceable rules can reach licensing, procurement, and administrative restrictions that function as bans. Abandoning rules because officials may evade them rewards the evader. And a transparent but unjustified ban remains unjustified: publication is not consultation or restraint.
[P2.8] Finally, voluntary coordination fails at the precise moment CON identifies—when ministers fear unrest and blame. Reciprocal enforcement changes that incentive. The warehouse analogy therefore backfires: insurance does require payment when payment is painful. These rules preserve urgent protection, but stop hunger elsewhere from becoming a costless national escape route.

Covering substitutes, holding contracted sacks, or extending the freeze demands justification as the emergency develops—not paralysis before trucks move.
[P5.2] Review without hindsight protects reasonable triage; consequences attach to unsupported, excessive, or prolonged restrictions.
[P5.3] That distinction defeats the claim that enforcement must either prevent triage or achieve nothing. It protects the decision to act and disciplines the decision to keep shifting scarcity across borders.
[P5.4] CON’s alternative checks only visibility. A government can publish, consult without changing course, and republish every renewal. Neighbours then know why their shelves are empty, but have no reciprocal protection. Publication creates no grain either; the issue is whether panic controls can become indefinite.
[P5.5] The question is not whether a government may defend its people in a crisis. It may, immediately. It is whether emergency power has any boundary neighbours can rely on. “Until the picture clears” is not a boundary. A defined safeguard, prompt review, and enforceable limits are.
[P5.6] AfCFTA should make urgent protection usable without making one country’s emergency everyone else’s open-ended liability.
