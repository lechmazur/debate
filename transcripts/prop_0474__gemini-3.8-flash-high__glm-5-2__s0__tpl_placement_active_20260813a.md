[P1.1] Modern cyber conflict rarely begins with an official military command; it begins in bulletproof data centers shielded by sovereign borders. When a nation knowingly harbors persistent, state-tolerated botnet infrastructure, it is not an innocent bystander with weak policing. It is operating a digital safe harbor—granting sovereign immunity to syndicates that paralyze hospitals, extort municipal grids, and disrupt critical infrastructure. Harboring that architecture is an act of deliberate complicity, and it demands sanctions comparable to those imposed on direct cyber-aggressors.
[P1.2] International norms have long recognized that states cannot escape accountability by outsourcing aggression. In maritime piracy, state-sponsored terrorism, and cross-border militancy, nations that knowingly shelter hostile actors face the same punitive measures as the perpetrators themselves. Botnets rely on the exact same dependency. They cannot survive without command-and-control servers, resilient upstream transit, and domestic legal protection.
[P1.3] When a host government ignores verified takedown notices, shields operators from extradition, and tolerates attacks against foreign adversaries, it becomes an indispensable co-belligerent. Without that sovereign shield, the botnet collapses.

If the cost of maintaining a digital safe haven is merely toothless diplomatic scolding, the asymmetric incentive to harbor botnets remains irresistible.
[P1.5] Comparable sanctions—such as financial asset freezes, telecommunications transit restrictions, and tech export controls—are the only tools capable of flipping that calculus. They force harboring governments to decide whether sheltering criminal infrastructure is worth economic isolation. Sovereign responsibility cannot end where server racks begin. If a state knowingly hosts the weapon, it must answer for the strike.

[C1.2] Consider the difference in kind. When a state launches a cyber operation against another country's infrastructure, it has made a deliberate decision to harm. When a state tolerates botnet operators within its borders, the range of possibilities is vast: complicity, yes, but also corruption, capacity gaps, jurisdictional fragmentation, or simply a government that cannot control every server farm in its territory. The proposition treats a Russian intelligence service ordering a NotPetya deployment and a small state whose under-resourced police ignore a colocation facility as morally equivalent.
[C1.3] They are not.
[C1.4] The "knowingly harbors" standard is dangerously elastic. Botnet operators route through proxies, lease infrastructure through shells, and shift command-and-control across borders in hours. If the standard is "state-tolerated infrastructure exists here," then virtually every connected nation is a candidate for sanctions, because botnets operate from compromised hosts worldwide.
[C1.5] The maritime piracy and terrorism analogies fail because those involve actors conducting physical violence with no plausible deniability about the host state's awareness. A training camp visible from satellite imagery is not the same as a command-and-control server whose location rotates daily.
[C1.6] Worse, this policy creates perverse incentives. Sanctions isolate states and reduce their capacity for cybersecurity cooperation. [C1.7] The proportional response to tolerated infrastructure is graduated pressure—capacity-building assistance, targeted law enforcement cooperation, and diplomatic consequences—not the equivalent of treating a negligent landlord as the triggerman.
