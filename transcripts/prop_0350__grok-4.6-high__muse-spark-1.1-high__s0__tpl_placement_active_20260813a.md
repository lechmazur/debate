[C1.1] Food is not tobacco and the environment is not a factory floor you can fence off. People must eat three times a day in wildly different contexts — home, work, mosque, night shift. Restricting one billboard or moving chips one shelf down does not teach anyone what to do at the next context. It assumes people are passive objects pushed by placement, not learners who carry habits with them.
[C1.2] That model badly overstates advertising and understates human agency. The child who does not see a cereal mascot on TV sees it on a classmate's shirt, on TikTok, on the family table where taste was formed. You cannot de-market a whole food culture without an ever-expanding list of bans that crushes small shops and takeaways while supermarkets adapt overnight.
[C1.3] What actually changes weight is proximal behavior: what is eaten, how much, whether movement is daily. That is not fixed by zoning. It is fixed by skill and routine. A campaign is not a poster saying "eat fruit."

[C1.5] Environment-first policy is both paternalistic and regressive. It tells low-income families they cannot be trusted to choose and then raises their food costs through reformulation, shrinkage and passed-through compliance costs. An empowerment-first policy respects them enough to invest in their capacity to choose. In a world of endless food variation, capacity beats control.
