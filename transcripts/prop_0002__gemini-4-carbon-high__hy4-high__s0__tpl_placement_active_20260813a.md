[P1.1] For the past twenty years, thriving American cities have played a cruel game of musical chairs. Beginning in the mid-2000s, growing industries and urban amenities drew millions of people toward metro cores from Seattle to Boston to Austin. Yet across vast swaths of residential land in these cities, apartments, fourplexes, and townhomes remained illegal. When surging demand hits a legally frozen supply, prices explode.
[P1.2] Had cities legalized denser housing two decades ago, they would unquestionably be more affordable today.
[P1.3] Start with the math of urban land. Where jobs cluster, dirt is expensive. Exclusive single-family zoning forces one household to buy an entire costly lot, turning basic shelter into a luxury good. Legalizing duplexes, walk-ups, and apartment buildings lets four, ten, or forty families split that exact same land cost, structurally lowering the price floor per home.
[P1.4] Next, consider how cities absorb newcomers. Critics often dismiss new infill as expensive, ignoring what happens when those buildings are outlawed. Affluent workers do not vanish when cities ban apartments; they simply outbid teachers, nurses, and service workers for existing bungalows and older rentals. Legalizing density in the mid-2000s would have built vertical sponges throughout the urban boom, absorbing wealthy demand instead of funneling it into neighborhood displacement.
[P1.5] Finally, the twenty-year window unlocks two decisive advantages: cheap financing and natural depreciation. Cities only reforming zoning today face steep borrowing costs. Had density been legal two decades earlier, builders could have capitalized on the entire post-2008 decade of near-zero interest rates, expanding multifamily supply when credit was uniquely cheap. Moreover, buildings age.
[P1.6] Apartments completed in 2007 or 2013 would no longer command premium rents today; they would have matured into a deep reservoir of middle-income housing.
[P1.7] Legalizing density need not cure poverty alone; it need only beat twenty years of engineered scarcity. Splitting land costs, absorbing demand, and compounding supply across two decades leaves urban housing far more affordable than banning it by law.
