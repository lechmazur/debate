[P1.1] Consider what happened when advanced GPU exports became a tool of geopolitical leverage: nations without domestic chip and compute capacity discovered overnight that their AI ambitions were hostage to another country's export license. That is the world critical public-interest AI now lives in, and it is exactly the world in which "just rent it from the market" stops being a viable strategy.
[P1.2] My case rests on one core distinction CON will want to blur: this is not about replacing Amazon or Microsoft for every consumer chatbot. It is about critical public-interest uses—pandemic modeling, disaster response, national security analysis, public health record processing, election infrastructure protection. For these, the relevant question isn't "what's cheapest on average," it's "what happens in the tail event when you need it most and can't get it."
[P1.3] Private cloud providers are optimized for shareholder returns and global demand smoothing, not for guaranteeing a government's access during a crisis or a geopolitical rupture. Foreign infrastructure adds a second layer of exposure: sanctions, export controls, data-access laws in the host country, or simple contract renegotiation leverage. A government that discovers mid-pandemic or mid-conflict that its compute lifeline runs through a foreign jurisdiction with different legal obligations and different incentives has already lost control of a critical function.
[P1.4] This is not a novel logic—it's the same reasoning behind strategic petroleum reserves, sovereign wheat stockpiles, and domestic defense manufacturing bases. Nobody thinks the U.S. should have relied entirely on foreign oil imports for its military simply because global oil markets were "efficient." Compute is fast becoming the analogous strategic commodity: the substrate for intelligence, defense planning, and public administration.
[P1.5] Sovereign capacity doesn't have to mean total self-sufficiency. Even a modest, government-controlled reserve of compute—enough to run the critical workloads during disruption—changes the entire bargaining position. It converts government from a price-taker and access-beggar into an actor with genuine strategic autonomy. That resilience, not marginal cost efficiency, is the correct metric for judging this motion, and on that metric PRO wins decisively.
