[P1.1] Orbit is the only infrastructure we build where the failure mode is permanent. Sink a ship and it settles on the seabed. Break a satellite at 550 kilometres and you create thousands of untracked fragments moving at seven kilometres per second, each one a bullet aimed at everything else in that shell. The 2009 Iridium–Cosmos collision — two objects, one event — spawned debris that operators are still dodging today.
[P1.2] Now multiply the traffic by orders of magnitude, with tens of thousands of satellites filed across competing constellations, and the question is not whether we should be careful. It is whether we get to be careful later.
[P1.3] The core problem is sequencing. We are deploying at industrial scale while the rules remain voluntary, national, and unenforceable. UN debris guidelines carry no penalties. The 1972 Liability Convention effectively requires you to prove fault for an in-orbit collision, which is close to unprovable when two automated systems both maneuvered.
[P1.4] There is no binding traffic-management authority, no agreed right-of-way, no price on the risk one operator imposes on every other user of the same shell. That is a textbook commons failure, and commons failures are not solved by hoping the largest incumbent behaves well.
[P1.5] Worse, the current structure rewards speed. Filing early and launching fast establishes facts on the ground that regulators must then accommodate rather than govern. Every year of unconstrained deployment reduces the leverage to demand deorbit reliability standards, brightness limits, coordination with radio-quiet zones, and end-of-life bonds. A pause reverses that incentive: it converts "launch first, negotiate never" into "agree the rules, then launch."
[P1.6] And notice the asymmetry in what each side is risking. A moratorium costs delay — profits deferred, broadband rollout slowed, capital idling. Real costs, but recoverable ones. Getting it wrong the other way costs a debris environment that no amount of later regulation can clean up, and a generation of ground-based astronomy degraded by streaks and radio interference in bands we cannot relocate.
[P1.7] Delay is reversible. Debris is not.
