[P1.1] The federal debt has crossed a threshold where every new dollar of spending is borrowed from future capacity. This is not a routine budget choice; it is a decision to bind the next generation’s hands. When annual interest payments rival the cost of major categories of discretionary spending—defense, education, infrastructure—deficit reduction stops being an austerity slogan and becomes a precondition for responsible governance.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. Persistent deficits increase outstanding debt. Higher debt raises sensitivity to interest rates. Even without a dramatic spike, rising rates inflate the cost of servicing existing debt.
[P1.3] That service comes first—before defense, before roads, before any new program. The Treasury does not default on interest; it defaults on priorities. This crowding-out effect is already visible in annual appropriations battles, where every increase in net interest tightens the squeeze on everything else. Every new commitment made now is not merely an expense; it is a claim on a shrinking fiscal margin that leaves less room to respond to recessions or emergencies.
[P1.4] Critics argue that America can borrow cheaply because the dollar is the world’s reserve currency. That confuses past credibility with future immunity. Confidence is an asset, not a law of physics. Reserve-currency status buys time; it does not repeal arithmetic.
[P1.5] When it erodes, adjustment arrives abruptly through higher rates, inflation, or forced austerity under crisis conditions—always more painful than deliberate consolidation undertaken while we still have room to maneuver.
[P1.6] The proposition does not demand abandoning all spending. It demands ordering. Serious deficit reduction should take priority over most new spending commitments because debt service is not optional, but new commitments are. Prioritization is not miserliness; it is prudence.
[P1.7] We can choose gradual, deliberate consolidation now, or we can surrender that choice to markets and circumstances later. The responsible path is to stop adding to the pile before the weight decides for us.
