Treating a cosmetic drop like a slot machine confuses resemblance with risk, and builds sweeping law on a psychology metaphor rather than actual gambling.
[C1.2] That overbreadth matters because free-to-play lives or dies on flexibility. A relatively small minority of willing payers, overwhelmingly adults, subsidize access for millions who never pay at all. Add strict youth caps plus friction-heavy defaults — parental opt-in, mandatory cooldowns, hard limits — and you do not merely trim excess. You make the entire model legally precarious, depress legitimate conversion, and push studios toward upfront prices, heavier advertising, or excluding younger audiences entirely.
[C1.3] The first players priced out are not wealthy teens overspending; they are low-income kids for whom free access was the only access.
[C1.4] Alternative funding is not a free swap. Ads typically pay far less than optional purchases and tax every player’s time; flat-rate passes and direct cosmetic sales already exist wherever they can sustain a game. If they could fund every free title at scale, developers would not need mandates to adopt them. Forcing that transition means fewer games, blander design, and more paywalls — a concrete loss of cultural participation imposed to prevent comparatively rare spending harms.
[C1.5] Parents, not universal defaults, are the right control point. Platforms already offer purchase approval, spending alerts, limits, and refunds that families can tailor to a thirteen-year-old with an allowance versus a working seventeen-year-old spending their own earnings. A strict state cap overrides that judgment, demands invasive age verification from everyone, and predictably drives teens to borrowed accounts, false birthdays, and offshore stores where no protection applies at all.
[C1.6] Target deception, hidden odds, and unauthorized charges directly. Do not impose strict youth caps on an ill-defined category and sacrifice the free ecosystem most young players depend on.
