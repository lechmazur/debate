[P1.1] Pay gaps persist not mainly because women and Black workers do different jobs, but because the same job is priced differently when price is hidden. Mandating salary ranges attacks that hidden pricing directly, at the moment when information asymmetry is worst: before an offer locks in a lifetime of compounding raises.
[P1.2] First, ranges anchor negotiation to the job, not the person. In opaque markets, employers start from a candidate's prior salary, negotiating confidence, and network knowledge — all proxies shaped by past discrimination. A Black analyst or a woman returning from caregiving enters discounted, and each low offer justifies the next. A posted range breaks that chain.
[P1.3] She does not have to guess high and risk being labeled demanding, or guess low and accept less for identical work. He can see the band is $85,000 to $105,000 and negotiate qualifications, not identity. Even candidates who never negotiate benefit, because the employer must place them inside a commitment it must explain to applicants and employees.
[P1.4] Second, ranges discipline employers before bias becomes a paycheck. To post a range, a firm must define the role's value internally, compare it across teams, and defend gaps between similar workers. That audit effect narrows manager discretion, where much within-job disparity lives. It also helps underrepresented workers sort toward firms that pay competitively, rewarding equitable employers instead of firms that profit from secrecy.
[P1.5] The efficiency cost is real but small. Some firms will post wide ranges, some matches may be deterred by a ceiling, and hiring may take longer as bands are set. Yet matching already wastes time on interviews that collapse over undisclosed pay. Transparency prevents those late failures, broadens applicant pools, and lets workers self-select on fit rather than gambling.
[P1.6] Modest posting friction cannot outweigh closing pay differences that compound for decades.

Excluded locations and longer searches fall hardest on workers with no savings cushion — the same workers PRO claims to help. A woman who takes a worse job after three extra months of searching has not been made whole by a number in an ad.

[P2.1] The claim that discretion 'survives untouched' [C1.1] mistakes less discretion for no discretion. Without a posting, an employer can offer one candidate $70,000 and another $110,000 and never reconcile the two. With a band, both offers must fit a preannounced range, be defensible to coworkers who saw the ad, and survive comparison when the lower-paid hire learns what newcomers were promised. Discretion remains, but its cheapest form — quietly lowballing the uninformed — is removed.
[P2.2] Wide bands do not rescue that argument. A $60,000 to $190,000 range is poor compliance, not proof that real bands fail; it invites regulator, applicant, and employee scrutiny because it is public. The Colorado opt-outs prove the opposite of futility. Firms excluded Colorado because disclosure had bite, and exclusion for remote roles is visible, reputationally costly, and closable by broader adoption.
[P2.3] A firm hiring locally cannot opt out of its own labor market, and most hiring is still local.
[P2.4] The sorting objection concedes the comparison. Even if occupation and hours explain much of the gap, the motion does not require eliminating most of it. It requires a reduction in gender and racial gaps that outweighs efficiency losses. Within-job disparities compound through percentage raises, bonuses, and retirement contributions tied to base pay, so narrowing entry pay is not a trivial slice.
[P2.5] Opacity also drives sorting itself: workers cannot choose higher-paying firms or occupations whose pay they cannot see, and transparent employers gain applicants they missed.
[P2.6] Finally, the efficiency horror story assumes posting creates mismatches that transparency otherwise prevents. Late interviews that collapse over pay, offers rejected after weeks, and candidates who cannot judge a commute against a wage are already lost matches. Setting a band may burden small firms once; exploiting asymmetry burdens underpaid workers every paycheck for a career.
