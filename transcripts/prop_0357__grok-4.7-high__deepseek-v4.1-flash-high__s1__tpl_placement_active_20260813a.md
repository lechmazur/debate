[P1.1] A car is not a streaming account. When a buyer pays for the vehicle, the computers, sensors, and control modules travel with the title. Core repair functionality—the ability to diagnose faults, calibrate safety systems, and restore the car to safe operation—is part of what was sold. It should not vanish because a connectivity subscription lapsed.
[P1.2] The requirement is narrow. It does not abolish paid navigation, concierge services, or entertainment. What it forbids is a lapsed data plan used as a software lock on the functions that keep a purchased car repairable. Luxury features can be extras.
[P1.3] Repair access is how ownership of a durable machine is exercised. Those diagnostics and repair tools can remain on the vehicle. Cybersecurity does not require that a missed payment hide a fault code from the owner or the shop.
[P1.4] Automakers already treat software as a second cash register. Seat heating, performance profiles, and remote functions have been placed behind subscriptions even after the hardware shipped. Secure gateways already stand between independent shops and full module access. Tie those gates to a data plan, and a lapse becomes a repair ban.
[P1.5] A vehicle built to last fifteen or twenty years is then hostage to a short subscription cycle.
[P1.6] Markets will not correct this after the sale. Buyers cannot comparison-shop terms that surface years later, and switching costs are the price of another car. Used buyers inherit the lock without ever hearing the sales pitch. Independent repair, the check on dealer pricing, collapses if the only key to the car's computers dies with the subscription.
[P1.7] If a company wants continuous control, it can lease. A sale should carry the practical right to keep the car running when the optional plan ends. Preserving core repair functionality does not punish innovation. It stops a sold car from being quietly converted into rented software.

[C1.1] The car is not a streaming account, but neither is it a perpetual service contract. A buyer owns the hardware and the software as delivered. Many so-called core repair functions, however, are not self-contained features sitting under the hood. They depend on live authentication, cloud-hosted calibration files, expiring security certificates, and automaker servers.
[C1.2] When a connectivity subscription lapses, the owner has stopped paying for that service. The motion says the automaker must keep running it anyway. That is not protecting ownership; it is mandating free lifetime service.
[C1.3] That mandate is costly and regressive. Backend access, credential management, security patches, and support for aging modules are not free. Requiring them after payment stops raises prices for everyone and makes subscribers subsidize people who opted out. If the service is genuinely optional, it must be allowed to end.
[C1.4] It can also make cars less safe. Connected modules use authenticated access for a reason. Forcing repair functionality to remain available after a lapse can mean stale firmware, unsupported tools, or credentials that never expire—creating new attack paths to immobilizers, braking, or driver-assistance systems. The claim that cybersecurity does not require hiding fault codes is a straw man; reading a code is not the same as reprogramming a safety-critical module.
[C1.5] “Core repair functionality” is also dangerously vague. Battery diagnostics? ADAS calibration? Immobilizer reflash?
[C1.6] A broad definition forces automakers to support old architectures indefinitely and punishes security updates. A narrow definition likely covers only what warranty and right-to-repair law already address.
[C1.7] The real fix is contract and competition: disclose what ends with the subscription, enforce promises, and target actual anti-competitive locks. But a lapse should not become a permanent obligation on the manufacturer. Ownership of a car does not entitle every future owner to perpetual connected infrastructure. Reject the motion.
