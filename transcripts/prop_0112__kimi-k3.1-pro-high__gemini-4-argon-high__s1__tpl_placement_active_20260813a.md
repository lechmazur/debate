[P1.1] Basic medical coverage has one fundamental job: to protect people when illness strikes. Employer-based insurance reverses that logic. It makes protection depend on remaining employed, often at a particular firm and for enough hours, while single-payer financing makes basic coverage a stable public guarantee. The choice is therefore between insurance that can disappear with a paycheck and insurance that follows the person.
[P1.2] That difference is most decisive at the worst possible moment. A worker diagnosed with cancer may reduce hours, leave work, or lose a job. Under employer-based coverage, illness can consequently threaten income and insurance together; temporary continuation may be expensive, and a new plan can mean new paperwork, providers, or cost-sharing. Single payer removes that second shock.
[P1.3] Losing a job should not also mean losing the ability to see a doctor.
[P1.4] Single payer is also better suited to insurance’s economic purpose. Health risks belong in a broad pool, not pools segmented by employers, where small firms face particular difficulty absorbing expensive claims and workers’ coverage changes as they change jobs. A universal basic plan can simplify enrollment and billing, reduce duplicated administration, and give the payer greater leverage over prices. Employers can stop managing a complex benefit they were never designed to provide, while workers need not stay in unsuitable jobs primarily to preserve coverage.
[P1.5] That is not merely convenience; it improves labor mobility, entrepreneurship, and bargaining power.

A single-payer system can distribute those costs according to ability to pay rather than employment luck.
