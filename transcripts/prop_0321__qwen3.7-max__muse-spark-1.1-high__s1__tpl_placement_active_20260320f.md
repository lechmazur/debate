[P1.1] The core of this debate is whether we allow tech platforms to strip-mine human labor to build competing commercial products without permission. [P1.2] When a commercial generative-AI company scrapes a digital artist’s portfolio, a journalist’s archives, or a musician’s tracks, they are not merely being inspired. [P1.3] A stronger default right to halt this training restores basic economic fairness, ensuring that the entities profiting from the data bear the cost of acquiring it.
[P1.4] Furthermore, the current paradigm relies on a fundamentally broken opt-out illusion. Platforms argue that creators can simply opt out of scraping. [P1.5] The default must protect the creator because the power asymmetry is so vast. If the default remains with the platforms, consent becomes a legal fiction.
[P1.6] Just because a painting hangs in a public gallery does not mean a corporation can mass-produce prints of it. Public access facilitates viewing and human engagement; it does not signal a surrender of commercial rights to automated, industrial-scale scraping bots. By establishing a stronger default right for creators to stop this training, we align digital property norms with basic principles of consent and fair compensation. The burden of permission must rest squarely on the corporation seeking to monetize the work, not the creator trying to survive.
