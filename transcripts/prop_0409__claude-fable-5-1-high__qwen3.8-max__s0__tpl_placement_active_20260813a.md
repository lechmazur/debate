[P1.1] A park is the only place in a city where a person's presence requires no purchase. That is not an incidental feature; it is the entire point. Everywhere else, from the café to the gym to the beach club, you must pay to stand still. The commons is the one exception, and the motion asks whether we should keep shrinking it.
[P1.2] Notice the word "increasingly." The question is not whether a park may host a coffee cart or a concert weekend. "Broadly free" tolerates that. The question is the direction of travel: whether the default should drift from open ground toward ticketed zones, members' lawns, and roped-off sand.
[P1.3] That trajectory has to be defended, and it cannot be.
[P1.4] Start with who actually loses. The wealthy already own the private version of everything a park offers: a garden, a pool, a club, a weekend house near the water. When a waterfront becomes a paid "experience," they lose nothing; they simply pay for what they were going to have anyway. The family in a two-room flat loses the only cool, green, open place they had.
[P1.5] Enclosure does not spread access; it removes access precisely from the people with no substitute. Walk certain Mediterranean beaches and you see the result: the sea is technically free, but the sand is fenced into paid loungers, and the public is squeezed into a thin, crowded strip nobody chose.
[P1.6] Then consider the ratchet. Once a park authority takes concession revenue, it becomes a landlord with a budget to protect. Each paid enclosure creates a constituency for the next one, and the free remainder becomes the leftover. Enclosure almost never reverses, because the fence has a business plan and the meadow does not.
[P1.7] Finally, the value being sold off is the mixing itself. Strangers of every income sharing one lawn is what makes a public space public, and it is what makes a city livable rather than merely inhabited. Sort people by wallet and you have destroyed the good you claimed to fund.

[C1.1] The proposition treats the entrance gate as the whole story. It is not. The real question is whether people can actually use, enjoy, and return to parks, beaches, and waterfronts. A space that is formally free but filthy, unsafe, eroded, or so crowded that families avoid it is not meaningfully open.
[C1.2] Conversely, a well-designed paid arrangement can expand usable public space, fund maintenance, and make access more reliable.
[C1.3] Our case: access should be judged by net availability and quality, not by the absence of a fee. First, many waterfronts and beaches need continuous investment: lifeguards, sanitation, dune restoration, lighting, transit, policing, waste removal. General budgets often fail these costs, especially in cities with unequal tax bases. Modest, transparent charges for premium or high-impact experiences—guided tours, managed beach clubs, event lawns, mooring berths—can produce the revenue that keeps the broader site clean and safe, with free or subsidized entry for residents, children, seniors, and low-income visitors.
[C1.4] A premium beach club or ticketed event can fund the promenade, restrooms, and lifeguards that make the free shoreline usable.
[C1.5] Second, paid management can protect fragile places from being loved to death. Unrestricted access to dunes, wetlands, or historic quays can degrade the very qualities people seek. Timed tickets, capped zones, or concession fees are not anti-public enclosure; they are conservation tools that preserve access over time.
[C1.6] Third, the poorest users are often the ones most harmed by abandoned commons. If a beach becomes unsafe, dirty, or inaccessible by transit, the wealthy simply buy private substitutes. The poor lose the option altogether. A publicly supervised paid partnership can require public transit links, free days, local hiring, and open sightlines in ways that purely public management often cannot afford.
[C1.7] The danger is not that every fee becomes a fortress. The danger is a romantic rule that bans the tools needed to keep public space usable. Vote against the proposition.
