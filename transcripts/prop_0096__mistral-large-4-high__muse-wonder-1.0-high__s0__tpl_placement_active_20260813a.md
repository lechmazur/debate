[P1.1] An actor's voice is not a transferable commodity like a prop or a set piece. It is a biometric signature, a piece of their physical identity, and often their entire livelihood. When a studio uses AI to dub that voice into languages the actor never performed, or to generate new dialogue for scenes filmed years later, they are not merely exercising a contract right—they are appropriating a living person's instrument and identity.
[P1.2] Broad boilerplate fails as consent because it is consent by ambush. Standard "work for hire" clauses and catch-all rights grants were drafted in an era before synthetic speech existed. An actor signing a standard agreement did not imagine their voice would be cloned to deliver lines in a documentary they never watched, dubbed into markets they never entered, or used to train models that generate speech indefinitely. True consent requires specificity: knowing exactly what technology will be used, on which projects, for how long, and with the ability to revoke permission when circumstances change.
[P1.3] The harms are concrete and immediate. Voice actors face economic displacement when studios clone a single performance and reuse it across dozens of projects without additional compensation. More troubling, synthetic voices can be weaponized—placed in contexts the actor would never accept, attached to political statements they oppose, or preserved in perpetuity after death without family approval. A boilerplate clause offers no meaningful guardrails against these dignitary and economic injuries.
[P1.4] Opt-in consent fixes this without halting innovation. Studios can still use AI dubbing efficiently, but they must ask first, negotiate fair compensation for the specific synthetic use, and honor a performer's refusal. That is the difference between a licensed tool and theft. We should not allow standard contracts to launder the unauthorized replication of a person's voice.

[C1.1] A signature on a negotiated entertainment contract is consent. Replacing that single, upfront bargain with opt-in permission for every AI dub is not protection — it is a perpetual veto that makes the finished work unreleasable, untranslatable, and unaffordable to distribute.
[C1.2] Modern boilerplate is not a trapdoor. Performers, usually through agents and unions, trade defined rights in their recorded performance for pay, credit, residuals, and healthcare contributions. Studios price and finance projects on that certainty. If any actor can later withhold consent language-by-language, cut-by-cut, or format-by-format, no distributor can safely invest in localization.
[C1.3] The rational response will not be endless renegotiation. It will be fewer dubbed releases, less accessibility for foreign and disabled audiences, and more replacement of the original cast with anonymous local voices — the very displacement opt-in claims to prevent.
[C1.4] AI dubbing is also materially different from inventing a new performance. Properly used, it carries the actor's actual delivery — timing, emotion, intent — into another language, replacing the older practice of erasing them entirely in favor of a dub actor. Treating that translation as a fresh appropriation confuses distribution with impersonation. The actor already consented to be heard by audiences they do not personally perform for; subtitles, traditional dubbing, editing, looping, and audio repair have always done this under the master agreement.
[C1.5] Abuse can be policed without an opt-in rule for all dubbing. Existing contracts, union rules, publicity rights, fraud and defamation law, and negotiated limits on new dialogue, political use, or posthumous exploitation target harmful uses directly. A blanket opt-in instead punishes benign translation, creates holdout rents for stars while leaving unknowns squeezed out of global exposure, and collapses when a performer is unavailable, incapacitated, or deceased despite the estate having sold distribution rights.
[C1.6] Consent was given at hiring, compensated, and collective. Undoing it for this one technology sacrifices audience access and production certainty for duplicative permission slips.

A performer signing standard boilerplate understands they are recorded for a project; they do not reasonably understand they are licensing a synthetic voice model that can generate new dialogue in languages they never spoke, outlive them, and be reused after the contract ends. [C1.6]’s “consent was given at hiring” proves the problem: it was given without knowledge of this use.
[P2.2] The accessibility argument is a false choice. Opt-in does not abolish dubbing; it requires studios to pay for the value they extract. If AI dubbing is, as CON says, merely translation that “carries the actor’s actual delivery,” then the actor is doing real work and deserves a real license. If it is valuable enough to replace local dubbing artists—the displacement CON warns about—then it is valuable enough not to be taken through a catch-all.
[P2.3] The rational response to opt-in is priced, negotiated dubbing, not fewer accessible releases. Studios already clear music, samples, likenesses, and archival footage; a voice is no different.
[P2.4] And the “not a new performance” claim collapses on inspection. Subtitles do not create a voice. Traditional dubbing hires another performer under a separate agreement. Looping repairs the actor’s own recorded lines for the same film.
[P2.5] AI dubbing builds a reusable synthetic instrument from the performance and makes it say what the project later chooses. That is closer to appropriation than translation.
[P2.6] Existing law is a remedy after harm, not consent before it. Publicity rights vary, defamation and fraud are reactive, union rules cover only some performers, and estates are exactly where boilerplate most often overreaches. Opt-in consent—negotiated once, with term, territory, pay, and revocation—protects performers without freezing distribution. It turns a hidden default into a visible bargain.
