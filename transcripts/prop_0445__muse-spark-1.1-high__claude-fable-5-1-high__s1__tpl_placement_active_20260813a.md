[P1.1] India does not have strong labor protection. It has narrow protection for a tiny insider class and deep insecurity for everyone else. Laws that require government permission to retrench in a 100-worker plant, mandatory permanency after 240 days, and heavy dismissal costs simply do not apply to 90% of Indians working informally. For them there is zero protection.
[P1.2] For formal employers, those same laws are a tax on hiring.
[P1.3] That tax kills jobs where India needs them most. Firms stay deliberately below thresholds, substitute informal contract labour for direct hiring, automate prematurely, or refuse to scale. A garment exporter in Tiruppur with a four-month surge order will not put 200 workers on permanent rolls it cannot later adjust; it will push the work to an informal job-worker who pays no PF, no ESI, no maternity benefit. Protection designed to save jobs ends up preventing formal jobs and pushing workers outside any protection at all.
[P1.4] Make contracts flexible and the logic flips. Allow easy fixed-term contracts, variable hours, and no-fault separation with advance notice and fair severance, and formal hiring stops being a life sentence. Then the employer has a reason to give a written contract, minimum wages, and pro-rata benefits. Entry barriers collapse for women, young first-time workers, and migrants whom firms currently see as risky permanent liabilities.
[P1.5] Flexibility only wins if insecurity is covered elsewhere. That is the bargain: trade job-tenure for universal, portable, state-backed social protection delinked from the employer. One Aadhaar-linked account covering PF, health insurance, accident cover, maternity, and a modest unemployment insurance that travels from a construction site in Kerala to a factory in Manesar. The state protects the worker's income, health and old age.
[P1.6] The firm decides headcount on demand.
[P1.7] Keep only job-lock and India keeps the worst of both worlds: few formal jobs and no safety net. Protect workers, not particular jobs.

[C1.2] This is not speculation. India has already run this experiment. Fixed-term employment was permitted nationally in 2018. The 2020 codes raised the retrenchment-permission threshold from 100 to 300 workers, loosened standing orders, and made strikes harder.
[C1.3] The flexibility half of PRO's deal is substantially on the statute book. The protection half, the Social Security Code's promise to gig, platform and unorganised workers, remains largely a registration portal and a set of welfare boards with thin funding and thinner payouts. E-Shram registered hundreds of millions of workers; ask any of them what benefit arrived. Flexibility is cheap and instant; universal protection is expensive and slow.
[C1.4] Bundling them means workers pay first and are promised later.
[C1.5] Second, the diagnosis is wrong, so the cure will not work. Ninety percent informality is not caused by Chapter V-B. The overwhelming majority of informal enterprises employ fewer than ten people; no dismissal law has ever touched them. They stay small because of credit, electricity, contract enforcement and demand, not severance rules.
[C1.6] Rajasthan raised its thresholds in 2014 and no formal-jobs boom followed. Remove tenure and you remove tenure. The jobs PRO promises do not follow.
[C1.7] Third, watch how flexibility actually operates in a labour-surplus economy with weak inspection. Fixed-term contracts become perpetual rollovers, exactly as contract labour did. No-fault separation becomes the quiet answer to anyone who joins a union or reports an accident. Delinking benefits from the employer does not "protect the worker"; it releases the employer from the one party with money and a legal address.
[C1.8] The right move is available without the trade: fund and enforce universal protection now, on the ground India already has. PRO asks workers to hand over security today for a promissory note. Reject the note.
