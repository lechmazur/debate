[P1.1] Picture the worst hour of a family's life. A neurologist has just told them their son is brain dead. Under opt-in, a coordinator then asks them to make a choice the son never made: decide, now, what he would have wanted. Roughly half of families, faced with that void, say no — not because they know he objected, but because refusal feels like the safe answer when you're guessing on behalf of someone you love.
[P1.2] Presumed consent changes exactly that moment. The question stops being "what do you want to decide?" and becomes "we have no record that he objected — do you know of any reason he would have?" That is a lighter burden, not a heavier one. Families in soft opt-out systems consistently report relief at not being made the author of the decision.
[P1.3] Distress falls; it does not rise.
[P1.4] Three things follow. First, transplant rates. Every serious behavioural finding on defaults points the same way: when the effortful path is registration rather than refusal, stated participation climbs dramatically, and countries that moved to presumed consent — Belgium, Austria, Spain — sit near the top of the deceased-donor tables while opt-in peers lag. The default is not the only factor, but the reform package that carries it — coordinators, registries, trained requesters — is what "shifting to presumed consent" means in practice.
[P1.5] Second, ethics. Autonomy is not violated by a default you can costlessly and permanently escape. Opting out takes minutes, is free, is legally binding, and every real-world system is soft: the family retains a veto. Nobody's organs are seized.
[P1.6] What actually violates autonomy is the status quo, where the large majority who tell pollsters they support donation die undocumented and their organs are buried by a hesitant relative's guess.
[P1.7] Third, "most patients." The motion doesn't claim universality. For the ordinary patient with no religious objection and no registered refusal, presumed consent tracks their wishes more accurately than opt-in does.
[P1.8] Better outcomes, better fidelity to actual preferences, less weight on grieving families.
