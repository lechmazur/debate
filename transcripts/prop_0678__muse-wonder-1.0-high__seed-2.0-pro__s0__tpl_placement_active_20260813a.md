[P1.1] When a company can watch a worker’s face at home and log every keystroke, it is not managing an office — it is entering a private home. That entry should require real, opt-in consent, and it should be strictly limited in purpose, scope, and retention. Without both constraints, legitimate management becomes continuous surveillance.
[P1.2] Video and keystroke monitoring are different from ordinary supervision. A manager can assess output, deadlines, and meetings without seeing a bedroom, a child walking past, a religious practice, or a medical condition. Keystroke logging can capture passwords, personal messages, health searches, and union organizing typed between tasks. AI then turns that raw intrusion into judgments about attention, emotion, idleness, or risk — inferences workers cannot see or reliably contest.
[P1.3] Disability, caregiving, slower typing, looking away to think, or a darker room can be scored as disengagement. Strict limits — work applications only, no off-hours capture, short retention, no continuous webcam, human review before discipline, and a ban on emotion inference — separate a narrow security tool from an opaque disciplinary machine.
[P1.4] Consent must be opt-in because employment consent is easily coerced. If refusing monitoring means losing a job, a promotion, or remote status others receive, “agree or leave” is not a choice. Opt-in means a genuine alternative with equivalent evaluation, no retaliation, and the ability to withdraw. That does not ban monitoring.
[P1.5] In genuinely sensitive roles — handling payment data, health records, or trade secrets — a company can explain the need, seek consent, limit capture to the secure system, and staff the role with workers who accept those terms.
[P1.6] Unlimited, imposed monitoring also backfires on employers. It rewards performative typing and mouse movement over deep work, drives evasion, raises stress, and pushes strong employees to quit. Clear output expectations and limited security audits deliver accountability without treating every home as a suspect workplace. Consent creates trust, limits create proportionality, and together they let companies use powerful tools only where the justification outweighs the intrusion.
