[P1.1] Fertility clinics market egg freezing as a way to "stop the biological clock," but their advertising systematically downplays how fast that clock actually runs. Age-based success rates are the most critical missing piece: a 35-year-old woman has roughly a 15-20% live birth rate per frozen egg, but this plummets to under 5% at 40, with uncertainty compounding due to individual biological variation. These stark age effects are rarely presented prominently; instead, ads use vague testimonials or cherry-picked success stories that obscure the steep decline in egg viability with age.
[P1.2] The omission is material because age is the overwhelming determinant of egg quality, and without this context, patients cannot accurately assess their personal odds or the likelihood of needing multiple expensive cycles. Uncertainty must be disclosed too—thaw survival rates, fertilization success, and implantation all introduce variability that clinics understand far better than their customers. Voluntary transparency has failed in practice; in a competitive market, clinics have every incentive to emphasize hope over statistics. Mandatory prominent display of age-stratified success rates and uncertainty ranges would correct this information asymmetry, ensuring patients understand they might spend $10,000-$15,000 on a procedure with no guaranteed child, and that many never return to use their frozen eggs.
[P1.3] Without regulation, the status quo allows clinics to exploit the most vulnerable—women in their late 30s facing acute time pressure—by selling a false sense of security. The requirement isn’t censorship; it’s essential consumer protection for a high-stakes, irreversible medical and financial decision.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes advertising is the right venue for statistical counseling. It is not. A billboard, a social media post, or a clinic homepage is an invitation to seek care, not a substitute for the doctor-patient relationship. Treating adult patients as incapable of seeking nuanced advice without government-mandated billboards is paternalism dressed up as consumer protection.
[C1.2] Mandating that every ad become a prominent actuarial warning conflates marketing with medical consent, and the result will be worse care, not better.
[C1.3] Age-stratified success rates matter, but they are context-dependent and easily misread when stripped from a clinical setting. A prominent banner declaring “under 5% at 40” sounds decisive, yet it flattens individual biology, clinic-specific protocols, and the number of eggs retrieved into a single fear-inducing number. Indeed, the per-egg figures often cited—such as 15 to 20 percent at thirty-five—can mislead precisely because they are not the per-cycle or take-home-baby rates that actually govern patient expectations. Women in their late thirties facing chemotherapy or other fertility threats need to know that freezing now is often far better than not freezing at all.
[C1.4] If regulators force clinics to lead with decline curves, the predictable result is deterrence—patients who would benefit from immediate action will self-select out before they ever sit down with a physician.
[C1.5] Worse, the requirement is inherently vague. Who decides what counts as “prominent”? A footnote in six-point type? A red disclaimer occupying half the screen?
[C1.6] Any bright-line rule will either be so weak it changes nothing or so heavy-handed it turns medical advertising into a public-health poster. That ambiguity invites bureaucratic overreach and litigation, not clarity.
[C1.7] Finally, fertility care already operates under professional oversight. Physicians, informed-consent protocols, and existing medical-board standards are built to handle statistical nuance. If patients are truly being misled, the failure lies in the consultation room and the consent process, not the advertisement. Fix that.
[C1.8] Don’t criminalize hope.
