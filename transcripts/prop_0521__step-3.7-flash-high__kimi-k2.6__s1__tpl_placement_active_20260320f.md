[P1.1] The status quo rule letting employers retain all value from employer-funded training and benefits when a worker leaves is unfair to workers and economically wasteful for entire sectors. When a worker spends after-hours studying for a certification their employer paid for, or uses paid time off to complete role-specific training, they are the ones investing the labor to turn that employer-funded resource into a usable, sector-valuable skill. If they take a new job in the same sector, the original employer gets an unearned windfall, while the worker loses access to benefits they helped build.
[P1.2] That is a raw deal. This hoarding also creates harmful lock-in that suppresses wages across industries. Workers stay stuck in bad jobs, low-paying roles, or toxic workplaces because they cannot afford to forfeit training account balances, tuition reimbursement funds, or sector-specific benefits their employer provides. For example, a nurse who completes employer-funded training in pediatric urgent care cannot transfer that certification funding if they move to another pediatric clinic in the same city, forcing them to either stay in a bad job or pay out of pocket for training their former employer already funded.
[P1.3] Portable training accounts and benefits fix both problems. They reward workers for their effort, reduce lock-in, and let skills flow to the roles where they are most needed across a sector, boosting overall productivity. Employers still capture the full value of improved worker performance during the period of employment, so their incentive to invest in training is not undermined. The only thing this policy ends is the practice of employers hoarding value that belongs to the sector, and to the workers who built it, for no good reason.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to treat employer-funded training accounts and sector benefits as the worker's personal property the moment they change jobs within the same industry. That is not fairness; it is a regulatory confiscation that forces firms to finance their own competition, and it will strangle the very training programs it claims to expand.
[C1.2] Employers do not fund certification courses, tuition balances, or specialized benefits out of charity. They invest because the expenditure generates returns during the period of employment and because structured programs help retain talent in a competitive market. When a worker walks across the street to a direct rival and takes the remaining account balance with them, the original employer is not "hoarding" value; it is being compelled to bankroll a competitor's labor costs. That is a textbook free-rider problem, and the rational response is obvious.
[C1.3] Firms will slash upfront training budgets, replace portable certifications with narrow firm-specific coursework of little transferable value, or eliminate reimbursement entirely and instead demand workers self-fund before they ever see a dime. The nurse described in the opening retains her pediatric skill and can command a higher salary with it anywhere. What she seeks to take is the unspent money and paid time off her original clinic provided. Forcing that transfer means the clinic either stops offering the program or locks every participant into draconian vesting and non-compete clauses that make the labor market less fluid, not more.
[C1.4] Workers already capture the value of portable human capital through higher lifetime earnings. What this proposition demands is portability of the financial vehicle itself—turning employer capital into a sector-wide entitlement. That does not liberate workers; it collapses the incentive to invest and invites employers to claw back value through lower starting wages and stricter contractual traps. The honest alternative is to let contracts, vesting schedules, and voluntary retention bonuses align risk and reward, rather than mandating that every firm subsidize the training of its rivals' workforce.
