[P1.1] A functioning democracy cannot survive as a collection of isolated parallel societies; it is a shared enterprise that requires a common operating system. Expecting immigrants to adopt core civic norms and the common public language is not an act of exclusion, but the essential foundation for genuine inclusion, social trust, and equal opportunity.
[P1.2] First, democratic self-governance demands shared civic commitments and communication. Core civic norms—such as equality before the law, democratic accountability, tolerance, and basic individual rights—cannot be treated as optional preferences. When these principles are made discretionary, the universal protection of vulnerable individuals within subcommunities is compromised. Simultaneously, a shared public language is the prerequisite for democratic deliberation.
[P1.3] Without a common tongue, citizens cannot debate policy, hold public institutions accountable, or build solidarity across cultural lines. Society fractures into insular enclaves, eroding the mutual trust that sustains public institutions and social safety nets.
[P1.4] Second, treating integration as optional actively harms newcomers. Language proficiency and civic literacy are the primary engines of socioeconomic mobility. When host nations adopt a hands-off approach under the guise of tolerance, they abandon immigrants to low-wage parallel economies, where they remain vulnerable to labor exploitation and reliant on insular intermediaries to access basic legal protections. Expecting and facilitating linguistic and civic integration ensures that newcomers possess the practical agency to assert their rights, advance economically, and participate in civic life as full equals rather than a permanent underclass.
[P1.5] Crucially, this expectation does not demand cultural erasure. A healthy pluralism encourages individuals to cherish their private cultural heritage, traditions, and faith. However, the public square requires a common civic grammar. Expecting immigrants to adopt that baseline is not cultural homogenization; it is the very mechanism that makes a diverse and open society durable, coherent, and capable of collective progress.

[C1.1] Notice what the proposition cannot mean. It cannot mean obeying the law: equality before the law, criminal codes, and individual rights already bind everyone in the country, immigrant or native, as enforceable obligation. Nothing about that is optional today. So the motion's real content must lie beyond the law: a social expectation, enforceable only through stigma, tests, or suspicion, that newcomers adopt "norms" and perform belonging on the majority's terms.
[C1.2] That demand fails three times over.
[C1.3] First, it is dangerously vague. Who defines "core civic norms"? In practice the majority does, and the target moves. Yesterday it was language; tomorrow it is dress, diet, how you greet a neighbor, whether your accent is too strong.
[C1.4] An expectation with no fixed content does not integrate anyone; it licenses employers, landlords, and officials to decide who looks sufficiently integrated — a standing invitation to discrimination against those who obey every law but look or pray differently.
[C1.5] Second, it misunderstands how integration actually happens. Integration is an outcome produced by work, schooling, and welcome — not a duty you can command into being. The United States had no official language and no integration mandate, yet wave after wave — Germans, Italians, Poles — were accused of forming unassimilable enclaves, and each assimilated within two generations because opportunity pulled them in. Mother-tongue enclaves were launchpads, not prisons.
[C1.6] Coercion, by contrast, breeds the resentment and withdrawal it claims to cure.
[C1.7] Third, on language specifically, the bottleneck is resources, not will. Immigrants overwhelmingly want to learn the public language; what they lack are class places, time between shifts, and patient institutions. An "expectation" adds stigma, not classrooms. Translation services and multilingual access don't delay integration — they let people use courts, hospitals, and schools today while they learn.
[C1.8] A confident society holds everyone to its laws and earns loyalty by invitation. The proposition offers an ultimatum where an open door works better.
