[P1.1] You cannot regulate what you have not measured, and you cannot measure a pristine ecosystem after you have strip-mined it. Under international law, the International Seabed Authority (ISA) must steward the deep seabed as the common heritage of humankind and ensure the effective protection of the marine environment. Yet it faces pressure to approve commercial exploitation in the abyssal ocean before science even knows what lives there, how those ecosystems function, or how they sustain the broader ocean.
[P1.2] Deep-sea mining targets formations like polymetallic nodules that take millions of years to grow. Unlike terrestrial habitats, abyssal ecosystems operate on geological timescales. When massive collector vehicles scrape the ocean floor, they obliterate irreplaceable habitats and kick up dense sediment plumes—both on the seabed and discharged into the midwater column—that can drift for vast distances, smothering filter-feeders, disrupting bioluminescent species, and threatening global carbon cycling and fisheries. Because recovery takes millennia, "learning by doing" is a euphemism for irreversible destruction.
[P1.3] That makes robust, independently verified baselines non-negotiable. A baseline is the only yardstick a regulator has to define serious harm, detect violations, or hold corporations liable. Currently, the ISA relies heavily on environmental data gathered by the mining contractors themselves—companies with billions of dollars riding on minimizing perceived ecological risks. Allowing profit-driven applicants to write the scientific benchmark against which their own damage will be judged is a fatal conflict of interest.
[P1.4] Nor does the green transition justify blind haste. Battery markets are already pivoting toward chemistries like lithium iron phosphate that require no deep-sea cobalt or nickel, alongside better recycling and terrestrial supply. Even if seabed minerals are eventually needed, a conditional pause is not a permanent ban; it simply requires turning the lights on before driving heavy machinery into the dark. Approving irreversible extraction before establishing independent baselines makes lawful regulation impossible.
