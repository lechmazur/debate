[P1.1] Universities should prioritize funding for in-person clubs, common spaces, and recurring social events over digital wellness and engagement platforms because embodied interactions deliver benefits that virtual alternatives cannot fully substitute. Students gain from the full range of nonverbal communication, physical proximity, and spontaneous group dynamics that foster deeper trust and collaboration. A recurring club activity held in a dedicated campus space, such as an art collective meeting or political discussion group, encourages attendees to contribute actively and follow up in person, leading to study alliances and emotional support systems that emerge naturally from shared presence rather than curated online profiles.
[P1.2] Digital platforms often exacerbate the very problems they aim to solve by encouraging fragmented attention and comparison culture. Students logging into wellness apps or virtual events do so from environments already saturated with screens, which can heighten feelings of inadequacy when seeing filtered representations of others' social lives. In-person funding, however, invests in neutral territory where participation does not depend on personal device ownership or comfort with public digital sharing, making it more inclusive across socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds.
[P1.3] This order of funding also promotes institutional efficiency, as common spaces and scheduled events can host multiple overlapping groups without the need for individualized platform maintenance or data management. Recurring in-person formats build institutional memory and student ownership, as participants invest time knowing the location and routine will persist. Digital initiatives, in contrast, frequently require ongoing updates and moderation that divert resources from the tangible improvements students notice most directly in their daily routines.
[P1.4] By placing physical social infrastructure first, universities equip students with the real-life connections that underpin academic persistence and personal resilience, ensuring that any digital tools introduced later complement an already vibrant on-campus community rather than attempting to manufacture one from behind a screen.

A recurring in-person networking event for pre-law students may build strong ties for attendees, but a digital engagement platform that lets all pre-law students access mentorship slots, wellness check-ins, and event recordings serves thousands of students at a far lower per-user cost than physical space renovations.

Expanding them first ensures no student is left behind by physical-first funding models that disproportionately benefit those already able to spend unstructured time on campus. Waiting until physical infrastructure is fully funded leaves these marginalized groups without support for years.
[C1.3] Physical spaces also carry hidden recurring costs PRO ignores [P1.3]: constant maintenance, security staffing, and accessibility retrofits drain budgets long after initial construction. Digital platforms, once deployed, can be updated at marginal cost to add new wellness modules or engagement features, reaching every enrolled student regardless of physical location. PRO’s efficiency argument [P1.3] also fails to account for the fact that digital tools can be iterated rapidly based on student feedback, while physical spaces require months of planning and construction to adjust.
