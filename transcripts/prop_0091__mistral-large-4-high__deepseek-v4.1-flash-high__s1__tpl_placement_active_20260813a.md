[P1.1] The car in your driveway contains thousands of dollars in hardware you purchased outright—yet automakers now demand monthly rent to activate it. This is not innovation; it is a fundamental breach of ownership that turns buyers into tenants of their own property.
[P1.2] When you finance a vehicle, you are purchasing the physical components: the heated seats, the remote starter, the cameras, the processors, the wiring harnesses. These are not hypothetical capabilities awaiting delivery. They are installed, tested, and sitting inside metal you own. Yet major automakers have begun locking already-installed features behind recurring subscriptions.
[P1.3] You pay for the hardware at the dealership, then pay again—every month, indefinitely—to access what you already own. The component never leaves the car; only the manufacturer's permission to use it expires.
[P1.4] This creates a perverse double payment that punishes consumers for buying upfront. A buyer might spend tens of thousands on a truck with ventilated seats, only to discover that the cooling function deactivates unless they maintain an active subscription. If they miss a payment or cancel, the hardware remains physically present in the vehicle but artificially disabled through software. The manufacturer has not removed the seats; they have simply installed a digital toll booth on property the owner legally possesses.
[P1.5] Beyond the economics lies a crisis of control. When access depends on a corporation's servers and billing infrastructure, the owner loses autonomy over their own property. A missed payment, a server outage, a corporate policy change, or even a company bankruptcy can strip functionality from a machine the consumer will be paying off for years. This transforms ownership into a perpetual lease, denying buyers the basic right to use their property without seeking ongoing permission from a distant boardroom.
[P1.6] The principle is straightforward: if the hardware is installed and paid for, access should be permanent. Ownership must mean ownership—not a temporary license subject to a manufacturer's quarterly revenue targets.

[C1.7] The genuine wrong is hidden lockouts—features disabled without clear disclosure.
