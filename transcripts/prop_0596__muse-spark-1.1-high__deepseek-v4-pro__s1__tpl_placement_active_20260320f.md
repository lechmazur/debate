[P1.1] Gene editing is different. When a one-time CRISPR cure for sickle cell or beta-thalassemia exists, charging $2 million plus is not a market price, it is a death sentence for 90% of the world where those diseases are most common. A patent monopoly that lets a company sit on a cure violates the very purpose of medicine.
[P1.2] That monopoly was built with public money. The core science behind CRISPR, the delivery vectors, the early clinical trials - overwhelmingly funded by NIH, universities, and public charities. Taxpayers took the riskiest early risk. A public-interest licensing pathway simply ensures the public gets a return: if a company refuses to supply an essential cure at an affordable price, government can compel a license to a qualified manufacturer while still paying the originator a reasonable royalty.
[P1.3] This is not radical. Countries already allow compulsory licensing for HIV drugs and COVID measures under TRIPS flexibilities, and that system did not destroy pharmaceutical innovation - it expanded treatment from thousands to tens of millions while industry profits grew. Gene-editing cures are even more suited: marginal manufacturing cost is far below the monopoly price, so a royalty-based license still rewards innovators handsomely while dropping price to a sustainable level. The pathway would only trigger after definition as essential, no alternative, and failed good-faith negotiation.
[P1.4] Without it, there is no negotiation. Companies have every incentive to hold out for the highest bidder. With it, governments have leverage to secure voluntary tiered pricing and regional manufacturing. The choice is not between innovation and access.
[P1.5] It is between innovation that saves only the wealthy, and innovation that is actually allowed to fulfill its promise.
