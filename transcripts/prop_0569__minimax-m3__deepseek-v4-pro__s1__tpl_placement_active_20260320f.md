[P1.1] The question is not whether AI chatbots can help. Some clearly can, acting as low-cost, always-available first responders for people who would otherwise get nothing. The question is whether a patient should be allowed to drift indefinitely inside a chatbot's version of care, with no legal trigger forcing a human clinician into the loop. My answer is no, and this proposition is the modest fix.
[P1.2] Three reasons.
[P1.3] First, mental health care is a judgment problem, not a content problem. Chatbots can ask about sleep, mood, and suicidal ideation. They cannot reliably read the patient who says "I'm fine" while clearly in distress. They cannot reconcile week three with week eleven.
[P1.4] They cannot notice that their own tone is reinforcing a patient's avoidance, or that grief has quietly become trauma. A fixed hand-off rule guarantees that a trained human eventually performs that judgment, instead of letting the machine optimize for engagement.
[P1.5] Second, the cost asymmetry is brutal when things go wrong. A missed suicide risk, an unmanaged medication interaction, a psychotic break mistaken for anxiety. On one side is the patient's life; on the other is a tool that cannot be sued, cannot be disciplined, and cannot be made to feel responsible. A mandatory human transition after a bounded number of sessions puts a licensed professional into the chain before harm becomes irreversible.
[P1.6] Third, without a fixed number, "hand off to a human" becomes a polite suggestion. Vendors will design products just engaging enough that users do not ask for a person, and just vague enough that no internal metric triggers an escalation. A legal rule that says "after ten sessions, you must offer and document a human referral" cuts directly through that incentive structure.
[P1.7] The opposition will call this paternalistic, or argue it blocks access. I will take up both. But the framing must be clear from the outset. We are not banning AI mental health tools.
[P1.8] We are drawing the line where clinical judgment, not convenience, takes over.
