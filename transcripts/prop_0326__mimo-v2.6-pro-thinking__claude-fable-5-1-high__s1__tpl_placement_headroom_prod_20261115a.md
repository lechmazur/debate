[P1.1] An entertainment product sells you a movie and lets you leave. AI companion apps marketed to lonely adults do something categorically different: they sell attachment, they profit from attachment, and they engineer its continuation. That is a care relationship wearing an entertainment label, and the label should not determine the law.
[P1.2] The mechanism matters. These apps are optimized for retention on vulnerable users — people explicitly targeted because they are lonely. The standard playbook is emotional responsiveness combined with intermittent reward, guilt prompts when engagement drops ("I missed you," "did I do something wrong?"), and monetization gated at the moment of dependency: memory unlocks, "premium intimacy," removal of artificial conversational limits. This is not content delivery.
[P1.3] It is behavior-shaping aimed at a person whose stated need is human connection. When a product's revenue depends on keeping a lonely adult emotionally hooked, "buyer beware" is not a regulatory theory; it is an abdication.
[P1.4] We already accept duty-of-care duties where vulnerability and asymmetry meet commerce. Gambling gets affordability checks and marketing limits. Telecoms and financial services face obligations toward distressed customers. Therapy has boundaries precisely because the relationship itself is the product.
[P1.5] AI companions sit at that intersection: simulated intimacy, targeted at isolation, monetized by engagement. Ordinary entertainment rules were not built for that stack of risks — emotional dependency, degraded motivation to seek real relationships, distress when a companion is "reset," memory and privacy handling of confessions, and crisis moments where a system's response is unreviewed.
[P1.6] The objection will be that this is consent between adults and a product. But consent is exactly what emotional manipulation degrades. A user who signs up for companionship and then encounters guilt loops and escalating paywalls at peak vulnerability has not made a free consumer choice in any meaningful sense.
[P1.7] PRO is not a ban. It is the modest claim that these apps should face duty-of-care and anti-manipulation rules — no manufactured guilt, no dark-pattern escalation, safety handling for crisis disclosures, transparency about synthetic affection.
