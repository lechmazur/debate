[P1.1] When you lose your job or are locked out of one, you lose income, healthcare, housing stability. You are entitled to know who — or what — judged you.
[P1.2] Today most candidates and workers don't. Large employers routinely use resume parsers that rank applicants, video-interview tools that score affect, and productivity monitors that auto-generate termination warnings for warehouse, call-center, and gig workers. The manager who signs the letter often has not reviewed your work, only the system's flag. Without disclosure, you can't question the data it used, correct an error, or even know the criteria.
[P1.3] That makes anti-discrimination law unenforceable. Automated systems replicate historic patterns: penalizing career gaps that correlate with motherhood, downgrading zip codes that proxy race, misreading accents or disabilities. We only learned about such failures when journalists or audits forced transparency. A worker who is never told automation was involved cannot file a meaningful complaint, cannot show pretext, cannot ask "what was wrong with my score?"
[P1.4] Disclosure costs almost nothing and does not require revealing code. "Meaningfully influenced" does not mean spellcheck; it means a score, ranking, or flag that a human defers to when deciding who gets hired or fired. We already handle this elsewhere: if a credit check costs you a job, the law requires an adverse action notice and a copy of the report. New York City already requires employers to tell candidates an automated tool was used.
[P1.5] Requiring a simple, timely statement — your application or separation was meaningfully influenced by an automated system, here's the vendor and factors considered, and how to seek human review — restores basic procedural fairness.
[P1.6] Left voluntary, employers hide flawed tools to avoid liability. Left secret, errors compound. A legal duty to disclose aligns power with accountability: you have a right to know when a machine meaningfully shapes your livelihood, and a chance to answer it. That right is not anti-technology; it is what makes using technology in employment legitimate.

[C1.1] Being rejected hurts, but a legal right to a label won't help you get hired. Losing a job is serious, which is exactly why we should not answer it with theater. Mandating disclosure whenever automation "meaningfully influenced" a decision promises accountability but delivers a boilerplate notice that tells a rejected applicant nothing usable.
[C1.2] The trigger cannot be defined. Modern hiring is layered automation: job boards rank, spreadsheets sort, scheduling tools filter, background checks score. When a manager glances at a top-50 list produced by software, was that meaningful? Employers will never know in advance, so lawyers will tell them to either over-disclose on everything — burying workers in meaningless pop-ups — or abandon helpful tools altogether.
[C1.3] And unlike the credit-check analogy, there is no report to hand over. A credit denial comes with a score you can check and dispute. An employment model has no such fix: telling a warehouse worker an HR vendor scored your productivity does not reveal what to correct, only whom to sue. New York City's early attempt proves the point: lots of vague postings about tools in use, almost no information a candidate could act on.
[C1.4] Worse, disclosure invites gaming and punishes fairness. If you publish the factors — tenure, keywords, attendance flags — savvy applicants will stuff resumes and coach behavior while honest, less-connected workers won't. Meanwhile the most biased decision-maker of all, the unchecked human manager hiring friends and penalizing accents and gaps himself, faces no notice requirement at all. We would regulate the auditable process and leave pure gut prejudice untouched.
[C1.5] If the outcome is discriminatory, it is already illegal. What workers need is testing of tools for bias and a fair chance to compete, not a mandated confession that chills small businesses from using standardized screening that often reduces nepotism. This law taxes transparency and subsidizes secrecy.
