[P1.1] Relying solely on domestic subsidies and regulations ignores the fundamental, undeniable reality of modern globalized trade: carbon leakage. If the U.S. strictly regulates or prices domestic carbon emissions, energy-intensive manufacturing industries simply relocate to countries with weaker environmental standards. We lose the domestic economic activity, and global emissions stay exactly the same—or even increase due to less efficient foreign production. Domestic policies alone are a leaky bucket that fails the global math.
[P1.2] A Carbon Border Adjustment effectively plugs that leak. By placing a fee on imported goods based on their embedded carbon, we level the economic playing field. More importantly, we weaponize the massive purchasing power of the U.S. import market. To avoid the border fee, foreign manufacturers in major exporting nations must rapidly decarbonize their supply chains.
[P1.3] We effectively export our climate standards globally, forcing worldwide emission reductions without waiting for sluggish, largely unenforceable international treaty negotiations.
[P1.4] The critical five-year horizon is where the border adjustment decisively beats relying on domestic subsidies. Building new subsidized green infrastructure—like battery gigafactories, green steel mills, or upgraded transmission grids—takes years of regulatory permitting, supply chain development, and construction. The emissions reductions from domestic subsidies are heavily back-loaded. In contrast, a border adjustment creates an immediate, unavoidable day-one financial penalty for dirty imports.
[P1.5] Foreign producers will instantly optimize existing operations, switch to cleaner fuel sources, and adopt available efficiency technologies just to protect their profit margins and maintain U.S. market access.
[P1.6] Domestic subsidies only clean up our own backyard while the rest of the world keeps polluting and undercutting American industry with cheap, dirty goods. A carbon border adjustment forces the entire global supply chain to adapt right now to survive. Because it stops leakage and leverages U.S. market power to drive immediate foreign decarbonization, it is the vastly superior tool for cutting actual global emissions over the next five years.

[C1.1] A carbon border adjustment sounds like a clever lever—until you look at how long that lever takes to build. PRO's entire case rests on immediacy: foreign producers facing a "day-one financial penalty" and "instantly optimizing" their operations. This is wishful thinking dressed as policy.
[C1.2] Implementing a CBA requires calculating embedded carbon for thousands of product categories from dozens of countries. The EU started designing its mechanism years ago and is still phasing it in gradually. The U.S. has no equivalent infrastructure, no consensus methodology, and no administrative apparatus. Within five years, we would be lucky to have a functional framework—let alone the kind of rapid emission cuts PRO promises.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, foreign producers have three responses to a CBA, and only one reduces emissions. They can decarbonize (good), pay the fee and pass costs to consumers (no global reduction), or redirect exports to markets without border adjustments (no global reduction). PRO assumes the first response dominates, but economic history says firms absorb modest tariffs rather than overhaul production, especially when the adjustment is phased and uncertain. The U.S. market is large, but it is not irreplaceable for exporters with growing alternatives elsewhere.
[C1.4] More dangerously, CBAs provoke retaliation. Developing nations—India, Brazil, South Africa—have already condemned border adjustments as protectionism in climate clothing. A trade war does not reduce emissions; it fragments supply chains and poisons the diplomatic ground for the cooperation that global decarbonization ultimately requires.
[C1.5] Domestic subsidies and regulations, by contrast, have a proven five-year track record. The Inflation Reduction Act drove massive investment in clean energy manufacturing and deployment within months of passage. Permitting reform and targeted regulation can accelerate further. These policies reduce emissions directly, domestically, and immediately—without requiring foreign compliance or risking trade conflict.
[C1.6] The real choice is not between a perfect international lever and slow domestic policy. It is between a demonstrated tool that works and a theoretical one that takes longer to build than the five-year window we have.
