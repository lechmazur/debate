[P1.1] Imagine a 13-year-old who lingered on videos about acne, dieting, or feeling left out. She told no advertiser anything. Yet by evening her feed sells skin products, weight-loss tea, and a game that lets her buy popularity. That is not ordinary advertising.
[P1.2] It watched her insecurities, inferred them, and timed its pitch for when she was least able to resist.
[P1.3] Children under sixteen are uniquely exploitable by that system. Early adolescents are highly sensitive to peer approval, rewards, and social comparison, while impulse control and recognition of persuasive intent are still maturing. Behavioral targeting profiles the individual child's anxieties, habits, sleep patterns, and location, then tests which message makes that child click, stay, or buy. Automated optimization aimed at a 12-year-old is not persuasion; it is manipulation personalised to the child.
[P1.4] Consent cannot fix this. A child cannot understand that play, searches, and pauses will become inferences about mood, vulnerability, and purchasing triggers, retained and recombined for years. A parent clicking a privacy banner does not cure that asymmetry, when even adults rarely grasp how profiles are built. We do not let incapacity to consent be cured by fine print elsewhere in childhood, and a profile created before a person can vote or sign a contract should not be the exception.
[P1.5] This ban is deliberately narrow. It prohibits behavioral targeting to under-16s, not advertising itself. Companies can still use contextual ads — a toy ad beside a cartoon, sports shoes on a football video — without tracking the child. That preserves funding for content while removing the incentive to surveil children ever more closely to sell their attention.
[P1.6] When profit depends on exploiting a child's weaknesses before she can understand the trade, the market is not informing choice. It is undermining the development of choice itself. That is where a clear age line belongs.
