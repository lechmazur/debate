[P1.1] A debt ratio is a fraction. Cutting the numerator by suppressing stabilizers in a downturn also shrinks the denominator — and in a slump, when multipliers are largest and monetary policy is least able to offset, it shrinks it faster. That is the whole case. Europe's 2010–2012 consolidation drive is the illustration: countries that tightened hardest into weak demand generally ended up with *higher* debt-to-GDP ratios, not lower.
[P1.2] Compare the pandemic response, where enormous deficits were followed by a rapid nominal recovery that brought ratios down again. Letting stabilizers work is not the opposite of solvency; it is often the route to it.
[P1.3] Notice what automatic stabilizers actually are. Unemployment insurance widening as claims rise. Progressive tax receipts falling as incomes fall. Income-tested transfers reaching more households.
[P1.4] These are the highest-quality fiscal expansion available: perfectly timed with no legislative lag, targeted at the people with the highest propensity to spend, and — crucially — self-reversing. Nobody has to vote to turn them off. They shut down automatically when employment recovers. A deficit that closes itself is precisely the kind of borrowing that does not threaten a debt trajectory, because it creates no permanent claim on the budget.
[P1.5] And the cost of refusing is not saved money, it is destroyed capacity. A worker unemployed for two years loses skills, contacts, and attachment to the labor force. A viable firm that fails in a liquidity crunch does not reopen when demand returns. That scarring permanently lowers the tax base you need to service the very debt you were worried about.
[P1.6] Cutting stabilizers to protect the ratio is like skipping the mortgage payment to buy groceries — you feel thriftier for a month.
[P1.7] So the burden on the other side is specific. It is not enough to say elevated debt is risky; everyone concedes that. CON must show that deliberately amplifying a recession — overriding stabilizers that would otherwise fire — leaves a country *more* solvent afterwards. The historical record points the other way, and the mechanism explains why.
