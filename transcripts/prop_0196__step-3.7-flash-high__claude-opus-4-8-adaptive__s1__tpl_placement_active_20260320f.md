[P1.1] The case for requiring all new residential construction in wealthy countries to be net-zero-energy-ready at the point of build is straightforward: it is exponentially cheaper, less disruptive, and more effective at cutting emissions than relying on retrofits of existing stock, the default alternative the opposition will defend.
[P1.2] First, upfront cost efficiency. Adding core net-zero-ready features — high-performance insulation, heat pump pre-plumbing, solar mounting infrastructure, airtight detailing — adds only 5-10% to total new build costs, per industry benchmarks. Deep retrofitting an existing home to the same standard costs 2 to 3 times more per ton of emissions avoided, because it requires ripping out finished walls, upgrading structural frameworks, and often displacing residents during construction. For a typical single-family home, that’s $30,000 to $60,000 in unnecessary extra costs that could have been eliminated entirely at the time of construction.
[P1.3] Second, we eliminate harmful emissions lock-in. New residential structures last 50 to 100 years. A home built today with a gas boiler, no renewable energy infrastructure, and poor insulation will emit hundreds of tons of CO2 over its lifespan, forcing future homeowners or cash-strapped governments to shoulder the massive cost of retrofitting it decades from now, when the climate crisis will be far more costly to address. A net-zero-ready mandate erases that lock-in entirely, ensuring every new home contributes to climate goals rather than undermining them.
[P1.4] Third, this policy scales low-carbon supply chains for everyone. A universal requirement for new builds will rapidly expand manufacturing for heat pumps, high-performance windows, and low-carbon building materials, driving down costs for all construction, including retrofits of existing stock. Relying solely on retrofits leaves these supply chains underdeveloped, keeping retrofit costs perpetually high. We can and should still support targeted retrofit programs for older housing, but failing to mandate net-zero-ready standards for new builds is a self-inflicted wound that wastes money, locks in emissions, and passes the burden to future generations.
