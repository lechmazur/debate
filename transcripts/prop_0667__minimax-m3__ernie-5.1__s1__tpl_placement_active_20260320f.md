[P1.1] The proposition asks two things: does mandatory nationwide E-Verify meaningfully cut unauthorized employment, and can it do so without large discrimination or labor-shortage costs? Yes to both — and the country has already run the experiment in pieces.
[P1.2] When states have required E-Verify for new hires, unauthorized employment has fallen substantially. Arizona's 2007 law produced a documented drop in its unauthorized workforce. Mississippi and Georgia saw comparable effects. The mechanism is plain.
[P1.3] Today, an employer can hire off the street, accept a folder of I-9 paperwork, and shrug if it turns out to be fraudulent. Mandatory E-Verify makes a name, date of birth, and document check take seconds, turning willful blindness into provable hiring of an unauthorized worker. It changes incentives at the margin that matters — the marginal employer deciding whether to look the other way.
[P1.4] The discrimination worry deserves a direct answer. Critics imagine employers rejecting Latino applicants out of fear they will fail the check. But E-Verify actually narrows that space. The system standardizes the question: does this document match this person in the database?
[P1.5] An employer can no longer legally reject an applicant on appearance, accent, or surname alone; if the system clears them, the record clears them. Profile-based rejection already exists in the no-E-Verify world. Mandatory E-Verify, paired with anti-discrimination enforcement, contracts it rather than expands it.
[P1.6] On labor shortages, the Arizona and Georgia record is that the feared collapses did not arrive. Agricultural employers mechanized, expanded H-2A visas, or raised wages, and domestic workers filled gaps. The right framing is wages, not shortages. Adjustment costs are real but bounded, and a temporary wage bump is not what the proposition contemplates as "large."
[P1.7] E-Verify works where it has been tried, the discrimination fear describes a problem that already exists, and the labor market has absorbed similar shocks before.

[C1.1] Mandatory nationwide E‑Verify will not deliver the promised cut in unauthorized hiring, and it will create exactly the discrimination and labor‑shortage harms the proposition pretends are avoidable.
[C1.2] First, the tool itself is blunt. E‑Verify matches a name and birthdate against government databases. For naturalized citizens, for people with misspelled names, for those whose records were entered incorrectly, the system flags a false positive up to several percent of the time. In Arizona, after the 2007 law, thousands of legal workers were locked out of jobs while employers waited for a manual review that could take weeks.
[C1.3] The result was not a surge in compliance but a surge in paperwork that pushed many small firms to stop hiring altogether. When the system cannot reliably separate the undocumented from the documented, the net reduction in unauthorized employment is modest—estimates from the Department of Homeland Security put the drop at under two percent of the total undocumented workforce, far short of “meaningful.”
[C1.4] Second, discrimination does not disappear; it migrates. Even if an employer cannot reject a candidate on appearance, the E‑Verify flag gives a legally safe excuse to screen out Latino‑looking applicants. Studies of state‑level mandates show a measurable rise in “document‑check” rejections that correlate with surname and accent, a classic disparate‑impact pattern. The standardized check does not erase bias; it gives bias a veneer of legitimacy.
[C1.5] Third, labor shortages are not a theoretical risk. Agriculture in Arizona lost an estimated ten‑to‑fifteen percent of its seasonal labor after the law, leading to unpicked crops and higher food prices. Construction in Georgia faced project delays as firms could not fill crews. These are not temporary wage bumps; they are real output losses that ripple through the economy.
[C1.6] Taken together, the policy’s modest impact on unauthorized work is outweighed by the concrete harms it inflicts on legal workers, minorities, and entire industries.
