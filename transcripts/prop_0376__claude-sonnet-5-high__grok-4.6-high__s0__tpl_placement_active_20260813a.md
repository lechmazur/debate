[P1.1] Credit card interest today isn't priced like risk—it's priced like extraction. Rates of 25%, 30%, even higher compound against people who are already struggling, turning a $2,000 emergency balance into a debt that can take a decade to escape even with steady payments. That's not a lending market functioning efficiently. That's a mechanism where the cost of being poor and unlucky gets multiplied, monthly, forever.
[P1.2] The core case for a legal cap is simple: markets don't self-correct when the harm is diffuse, delayed, and borne by people with the least bargaining power. A borrower facing a medical bill or a car repair isn't shopping three issuers and comparing APRs—they're taking whatever card will say yes. Lenders know this. That's exactly why the highest rates cluster on the riskiest, most desperate borrowers: not because 29% is the actuarially precise price of that risk, but because that's what the market will bear from someone with no alternative.
[P1.3] A cap forces lenders to price closer to real expected loss instead of exploiting captive demand.
[P1.4] Now the obvious objection: cap rates and some high-risk borrowers get cut off. I'm not going to pretend that's false—I'm going to argue it's the lesser harm, and often not even a harm at all. Being denied a 29% credit card is not equivalent to losing access to credit. It's losing access to a specific debt trap.
[P1.5] Many of those marginal borrowers are currently being extended credit that predictably makes their situation worse, not better—revolving balances that never shrink, minimum payments that mostly cover interest. Cutting off debt that primarily generates fees and compounding balances, rather than functioning as a bridge to solvency, is not an access problem. It's removing a harmful product from a market that has failed to self-regulate.
[P1.6] The comparison the rest of this debate should turn on is this: weigh the diffuse, uncertain harm of "some risky borrowers get fewer credit card offers" against the concrete, demonstrated harm of "millions of already-struggling borrowers pay compounding rates that entrench poverty." One of those harms is speculative [clipped]

[C1.3] PRO treats that lockout as the lesser harm—even as no harm at all—because a 29 percent card is a "debt trap." That is the error at the heart of the case. An expensive card is costly. No card is worse when the alternatives are payday shops, title loans, overdraft spirals, unpaid rent, or a broken car that costs someone a job.
[C1.4] Those substitutes do not come with grace periods, dispute rights, fraud protection, or a path onto cheaper credit later. Cutting the legal product does not erase the need for liquidity. It pushes that need into harsher markets.

[C1.6] It forbids the only price at which the loan can clear. Safer borrowers keep their cards. The people cut off are exactly the ones PRO says already have the least bargaining power.
[C1.7] That is rationing, not protection. A medical bill or a car repair does not become less urgent because a statute set a number. It becomes harder to finance legally. The excluded pay more elsewhere or go without.
[C1.8] Costly credit can still be a bridge. Denied credit is a wall. The comparison is not expensive credit versus no harm. It is expensive formal credit versus worse options or none.
