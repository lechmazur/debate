[P1.1] Universities collect tuition on day one. Students and taxpayers collect the risk for a decade.
[P1.2] When a loan defaults, the school has already been paid in full, the lender is backed by the public, and the only balance sheets ruined are the graduate's and the Treasury's. That is not a market. It is revenue without responsibility.
[P1.3] Requiring universities to bear part of the cost when a program repeatedly leaves graduates earning too little to repay their debt fixes that asymmetry with a simple principle: if you profit from a credential, you share the loss when that credential predictably fails to pay.
[P1.4] Repeatedly and relative to debt are the key safeguards. One bad graduation year, a recession, or a graduate who chooses part-time work does not trigger a penalty. A pattern does. When cohort after cohort borrows $30,000 or $40,000 for a certificate or degree that returns fast-food wages, that is not student misfortune.
[P1.5] It is institutional design: the price charged, the students admitted, the curriculum taught, and the career support not provided are all choices of the university.
[P1.6] Under the current system, those choices are rewarded anyway. A school can raise tuition because loans will cover it, expand enrollment in cheap-to-teach but low-return programs because loans will fund it, and advertise employability while bearing no cost when employment disappoints. Risk-sharing reverses each incentive. Programs that want to keep enrolling have reasons to lower prices, be honest about outcomes, admit students they will actually support to completion, build employer links, and shut down offerings that cannot justify their debt.
[P1.7] Opponents will call this unfair because universities do not control the economy or a student's effort. That is why they should bear part, not all, and only for repeated failure. They control enough — and currently answer for none.
[P1.8] Without a share of default costs, the cheapest way to grow is to sell debt-financed hope. With it, quality becomes financially necessary.

[C1.1] Universities are not banks, and making them share in loan‑default losses will not fix a broken market—it will break the mission that makes higher education worth defending. The proposition assumes that when a cohort earns too little, the school must pay a penalty. In reality, earnings are shaped by macro‑economic cycles, regional labor demand, and individual career choices that no curriculum can fully control. Holding schools financially liable for outcomes they cannot guarantee turns a complex social problem into a blunt accounting rule that punishes the wrong actor.
[C1.2] First, the cost will be passed straight to students. To absorb default penalties, institutions will raise tuition, shrink financial‑aid budgets, or both. Those price hikes fall hardest on low‑income borrowers—the exact group the policy claims to protect. A school that now must budget for potential defaults will treat every prospective student as a risk, tightening admissions and limiting enrollment slots for those who need help most.
[C1.3] Second, the “repeatedly” trigger will force universities to abandon or downsize programs that are socially valuable but statistically low‑paying. Humanities, social work, education, and many liberal‑arts degrees generate public goods—civic engagement, cultural literacy, community cohesion—that never show up in a salary spreadsheet. If a school fears that a modest earnings gap will trigger penalties, it will truncate curricula, narrow majors, and prioritize short‑term employability over long‑term societal benefit.
[C1.4] Third, the policy creates perverse incentives that outweigh any marginal gain in accountability. Schools may inflate reported earnings, game admissions data, or steer students toward higher‑pay but less‑suitable fields to avoid penalties. Existing mechanisms—accreditation standards, gainful‑employment rules, transparent reporting—already pressure institutions to improve outcomes without imposing direct financial liability.
[C1.5] In sum, the proposition misplaces risk, regresses costs onto the most vulnerable, and threatens to hollow out the breadth of higher education. The real solution is better information and stronger consumer protection, not making universities pay for a labor market they do not control.

[P2.4] Enrolling a poorly prepared student, collecting years of loan-funded tuition, then watching them drop out in debt is extraction. Under risk-sharing, helping students finish and find work becomes cheaper than repeatedly paying for failure.

[P2.6] Social work and the humanities create value, but that value does not grow when financed by ruining borrowers. If society wants to subsidize public goods, it should fund them directly — not conscript 22-year-olds into unpayable debt.
