[P1.2] The only question, then, is whether great apes, cetaceans, and elephants have interests of the kind that demand it. They plainly do.
[P1.3] Three facts drive this case. First, these animals possess the cognitive markers we treat as morally significant in ourselves: self-recognition in mirrors, long-term memory, problem-solving, planning for the future. Elephants mourn their dead and return to bones years later. Orcas live in matrilineal pods with distinct dialects and hunting traditions — culture, transmitted across generations.
[P1.4] Chimpanzees use tools, deceive, console, and cooperate.
[P1.5] Second, captivity inflicts on these specific beings something close to what solitary confinement inflicts on humans. Orcas that swim a hundred miles a day in the wild collapse their dorsal fins and gnaw concrete in tanks. Elephants in zoos develop stereotypic rocking and die decades early. This is not sentimental projection; it is documented, species-typical suffering in creatures who know they are suffering.
[P1.6] Third, the property framework has demonstrably failed. Welfare statutes regulate how you may exploit an animal, never whether you may. As long as Happy the elephant or a research chimpanzee is legally a thing, every protection is revocable the moment it conflicts with an owner's interest.
[P1.7] Note what the proposition does not claim: not voting rights, not full human equality, not personhood for all animals. It claims a narrow, evidence-bounded set of basic rights — against captivity and exploitation — for the handful of taxa where the scientific case is overwhelming.
[P1.8] So the burden is set. If personhood follows morally relevant capacities rather than DNA, this case is won. The alternative is that the law protects beings based on species membership alone — which is not a principle, just a prejudice with a statute.
