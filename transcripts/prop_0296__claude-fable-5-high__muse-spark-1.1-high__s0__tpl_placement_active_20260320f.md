[P1.1] A street name is not a history lesson. It is an honor, renewed every single day at public expense. When a city keeps a boulevard named for a slaveholder or a dictator, it is not preserving the past; it is making a present-tense statement that this person still deserves civic celebration. That is the core of the case: public naming is an ongoing act of endorsement, and governments should stop endorsing people whose defining legacy is subjugation.
[P1.2] Notice what the motion targets: figures who *primarily* honor colonization, slavery, or tyranny. This is not sandblasting every imperfect founder off the map. It is asking whether a child should attend a school named for a man who owned her ancestors, whether citizens should celebrate a holiday glorifying the general who crushed their grandparents' democracy. The narrow scope matters, and I will hold my opponent to it.
[P1.3] Three reasons renaming is the right policy. First, honesty. Museums, archives, and textbooks preserve history; street signs confer prestige. Removing a name from a school erases nothing—Germany did not forget Hitler by refusing to keep Adolf-Hitler-Platz.
[P1.4] Nobody argues Germans lost their history; they argue Germany gained its credibility. Second, equal citizenship. Public symbols belong to everyone. Forcing descendants of the colonized and enslaved to fund and salute their oppressors' names is a daily civic insult with real consequences for belonging and trust in institutions.
[P1.5] Third, precedent shows it works smoothly. Russia renamed Leningrad, post-Franco Spain stripped fascist street names, the United States renamed military bases honoring Confederates—mail still arrived, maps updated, life continued.
[P1.6] The continuity argument gets it backwards. Names have never been permanent; every honored colonizer displaced an earlier name, often an indigenous one. Renaming is not a break with tradition—it is the tradition. The only question is whether our era's contribution to that living record will reflect our values or merely our inertia.
[P1.7] Honors are choices. Governments should choose deliberately, and choose better.
