[P1.1] Defaulting to institutional elder care strips older adults of core autonomy before their needs are even assessed. For many older adults, the loss of their home is a significantly greater blow to quality of life than manageable physical limitations. When an elder can remain in their own home with appropriate support, they retain control over daily routines, social ties, and personal space—rights that institutions inherently limit, regardless of staff quality. The proposition’s "when possible" caveat excludes those requiring round-the-clock facility-level care, so this is not a blanket ban on institutions, but a rejection of automatic institutionalization for people who could thrive at home.
[P1.2] Home care delivers better health outcomes for eligible elders. Widely documented surveys show most elders in home settings report far higher life satisfaction than peers in facilities. Unfamiliar institutional layouts raise fall risks; shared living spaces increase exposure to contagious illness; lost environmental cues accelerate cognitive decline for those with memory issues. Staying in a familiar home mitigates all three: personalized care schedules avoid rigid institutional routines that disrupt sleep and nutrition, and familiar surroundings reduce confusion for dementia patients.
[P1.3] Shifting the default also addresses systemic inefficiency. Taxpayer funds tied to institutional beds could instead cover home care aides, meal delivery, and home modification for thousands more elders. Institutional care requires massive fixed costs for beds, staffing, and facilities often overused for elders who do not need 24/7 facility care. Most unpaid elder care already happens in homes; supporting formal home care formalizes that labor instead of forcing families to choose between unmanageable unpaid work and unwanted institutionalization.
[P1.4] Opponents will overstate the safety of institutions for all elders, but the proposition only applies when home care is viable. The current default clearly causes unnecessary harm, wastes resources, and violates elder dignity—all avoidable by centering home care first.

[C1.1] The romantic picture of staying home conceals the lived reality of how home-care-first systems actually function. The motion does not just ask us to like home care; it asks us to organize the entire system around it. That organizing choice has predictable, ugly consequences.
[C1.2] First, "when possible" is not a safety valve—it is the failure point. Whether home care is "viable" is not stamped on anyone's forehead; it is a judgment made by stretched budgets and overwhelmed families, and a system built around home care will systematically err toward declaring it possible. An aide visits two hours a day; the other twenty-two, the elder is alone with the stairs, the stove, the bathtub, and nobody watching. Decline at home is gradual and invisible—missed medications, malnutrition, untreated infections—until it surfaces as an emergency hospitalization.
[C1.3] Institutions catch problems at 2 a.m. Home care defaults discover them in the ER.
[C1.4] Second, the labor question. Formal home care never replaces family labor; it supplements it, and the gap lands overwhelmingly on daughters and wives who cut careers and exhaust themselves. A home-first default doesn't liberate families—it conscripts them, while letting governments claim the elder is "supported." Caregiver burnout is also where elder neglect and abuse flourish, behind closed doors, unsupervised.
[C1.5] Third, the economics invert at exactly the population that matters. For elders with moderate-to-high needs, round-the-clock home support costs far more per person than congregate care with shared staffing. The "efficiency" claim only works by rationing—giving many people a little help instead of some people enough.
[C1.6] Finally, organizing around home care residualizes institutions: they become underfunded warehouses of last resort, which then "proves" institutions are awful—a self-fulfilling prophecy.
[C1.7] The right system is option-neutral: rigorous needs assessment, well-funded congregate care that offers community and safety, and home support where it genuinely fits—not a default in either direction. The motion mandates a bias. Reject it.
