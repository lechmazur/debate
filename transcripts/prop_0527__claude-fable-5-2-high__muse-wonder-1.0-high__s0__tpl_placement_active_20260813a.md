[P1.1] A new antibiotic is the one drug we hope almost never to use. That is the entire problem. Good stewardship means locking the newest antibiotic in a cabinet and reserving it for infections nothing else can treat. So the better the drug and the more responsibly we use it, the less its developer sells.
[P1.2] The predictable result is that most large pharmaceutical companies have abandoned antibiotic research. A few small firms that did bring new antibiotics to approval went bankrupt shortly afterward. Achaogen is the well-known case. The science worked and the market didn't.
[P1.3] An advance market commitment fixes that precise failure. Governments promise in advance that if someone delivers an antibiotic meeting defined criteria against priority resistant pathogens, a market will exist. This is a pull incentive, so taxpayers pay only for success. If no drug arrives, the public spends nothing.
[P1.4] Similar logic helped bring pneumococcal vaccines to poorer countries years sooner than the ordinary market would have.
[P1.5] The motion asks whether countries should *permit* this tool even if unit prices remain high. They should, for three reasons.
[P1.6] First, unit price is the wrong metric. A reserve antibiotic is insurance, like a fire extinguisher. Nobody judges an extinguisher by cost per use. A high price on a few thousand courses a year is a tiny budget line.
[P1.7] Hip replacements, chemotherapy and caesarean sections all depend on working antibiotics, and losing that protection would cost vastly more.
[P1.8] Second, the alternative to a high-priced antibiotic is not a cheap one. It is no antibiotic. A patient with a pan-resistant bloodstream infection is not comparing prices. For that patient the only question is whether the drug exists.
[P1.9] Third, a high unit price on a reserve drug is not even perverse. It discourages casual overprescribing, the very behaviour that breeds resistance.

[C1.1] Permitting an advance market commitment that tolerates a high unit price is not buying insurance. It is signing a blank cheque for the next monopoly and surrendering the one moment the public has leverage.
[C1.2] An AMC does not just reward success. To lure firms, governments must promise revenue high enough to satisfy shareholders. Once set, companies can aim for the contract's minimum bar — another drug in an existing class with modest advantages — rather than risky, truly novel antibiotics. The taxpayer guarantees the return; the firm keeps the patent and pricing power, and can anchor that same high price for other buyers.
[C1.3] That price then undermines the purpose of a reserve antibiotic. Resistance does not respect borders, and the heaviest burden falls on poorer systems and underfunded hospitals. If a last-line drug is stocked only where budgets can absorb an exorbitant course price, it is effectively unavailable to most patients who need it, while clinicians delay or ration it as infections spread. [P1.6] calls price the wrong metric because few courses are used.
[C1.4] But scarcity is why access matters: each delayed course can be fatal, and a rich-country-only stockpile leaves the global insurance policy full of holes.
[C1.5] Nor does a high price create stewardship. Use should be decided by microbiology and guidelines, not a pharmacist's budget. High margins tempt manufacturers to market for broader use beyond the commitment, while deterring legitimate use where need is greatest — the worst of both incentives.
[C1.6] Better tools exist: delinked subscription payments or market-entry rewards that pay a fixed sum for value in exchange for affordable supply, licensing, and global access. Those reward innovation without enshrining high unit prices. Countries should use purchasing power to break the link between monopoly price and survival, not commit in advance to preserving it.
