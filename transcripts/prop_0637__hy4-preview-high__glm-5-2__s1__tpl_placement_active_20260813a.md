[P1.1] Transit stations are where a city has already made its biggest public investment, and by-right densification is how we finally use it. When a half-empty strip mall or a sea of parking sits beside a rail platform, that is not a neighborhood to be preserved; it is wasted capacity. Legalizing more homes there by-right replaces a discretionary veto with predictable rules, so teachers and baristas can live steps from frequent service instead of bidding up rents in older, more vulnerable neighborhoods farther out.
[P1.2] CON will warn about short-term displacement, but the deeper cause of displacement is scarcity: when high-demand neighborhoods refuse to grow, capital chases scarce existing units, and long-time tenants are squeezed regardless. New buildings near transit absorb that demand. Higher-income renters choose the new apartments; pressure on the existing housing stock eases. That filtering mechanism is the durable way to stop bidding wars from spreading outward.
[P1.3] I do not dismiss displacement. By-right legalization should travel with relocation assistance, right to return, and one-for-one replacement of any affordable units lost. But the motion asks whether we should allow densification even if those risks remain in the short term. Yes, because the alternative is worse.
[P1.4] Discretionary review lets incumbent homeowners delay, shrink, or kill projects for years; every month of delay is another month of regional scarcity, another family pushed to a car-dependent exurb, and more pollution because transit built for crowds sits in zoning that forbids them. Preserving a low-rise streetscape beside a train station does not protect tenants; it protects parking. If we care about people most at risk, we should stop treating new neighbors near transit as the threat and start treating the housing shortage itself as the threat.
[P1.5] Allow it by-right, pair it with protections, and let the city grow where it already has infrastructure, not on farmland at the fringe.

[C1.1] The motion concedes the harm. "Even if it increases local displacement risks" is not a caveat tucked into the proposition—it is the proposition. The affirmative side has already admitted that their policy will push real people out of real homes in the short term, and they are asking us to accept that trade because, they claim, things will get better later. That is a promise, not a plan, and it is made at the expense of people who cannot afford to wait.
[C1.2] Here is the central contradiction. By-right development strips away discretionary review. But discretionary review—the public hearings, the negotiated conditions, the project-by-project scrutiny—is exactly the mechanism through which municipalities extract affordable housing set-asides, relocation requirements, and one-for-one replacement. You cannot simultaneously remove local review and promise that robust protections will "travel with" the policy.
[C1.3] The legal lever that enforces those protections is the very lever by-right repeals. Jurisdictions that have fast-tracked transit-adjacent development without strong anti-displacement mandates have watched land values spike, speculative eviction campaigns accelerate, and the exact populations transit was built to serve get pushed to car-dependent peripheries—undermining the ridership the system needs.
[C1.4] The filtering theory sounds elegant in the abstract: new units absorb demand, pressure eases, everyone benefits. But filtering operates on a timescale of years to decades. Displacement operates on a timescale of months. Between a rezoning announcement and the delivery of occupied units, landlords near stations raise rents, cash out to developers, or demolish affordable stock.
[C1.5] The people squeezed out do not get a voucher for a unit that does not yet exist. They leave the neighborhood, the city, or the region. By the time filtering delivers its promised relief, the community that needed transit most is already gone.
[C1.6] We should not treat displacement as a tolerable setup cost. The people most at risk are the people transit equity was supposed to serve. Sacrificing them for a hypothetical future supply is not justice—it is planning on the backs of those least able to absorb the shock.

CON says by-right strips away the discretionary review that extracts affordable set-asides and relocation help, but that assumes protections can only be won through case-by-case bargaining. [P2.2] This does not weaken the lever; it converts a negotiated veto into an enforceable rule that incumbents cannot trade away for height reductions or delay. The certainty that developers hate is exactly what tenants should want.
[P2.3] The timing objection fails for the same reason. CON warns that filtering takes years while displacement takes months [C1.4]. Yes—so the answer is to cut the lag, not extend it. Discretionary review adds years of hearings before a single unit arrives, leaving the rent gap wide open for speculation.
[P2.4] By-right strips years of hearings from the delivery timeline, shrinking the speculation window, and if replacement units and relocation are preconditions, no tenant is handed “a voucher for a unit that does not yet exist” [C1.5]. The comparison is not new supply versus preservation; the status quo preserves the parking lot while the speculative market bids up the aging fourplex next door anyway.
[P2.5] So weigh the real choice. Blocking densification near transit does not freeze displacement; it exports it. Demand spills into older, transit-poor neighborhoods where low-income renters have no new infrastructure, face longer commutes, and bear car costs they cannot avoid. CON calls sacrificing people for a hypothetical future supply “not justice” [C1.6], but the deeper injustice is hoarding transit access, jobs, and clean air behind a zoning veto while everyone else is pushed to the fringe.
[P2.6] By-right lets the city grow where the infrastructure already exists and lets tenants stay through the transition, rather than pretending freezing a neighborhood protects anyone.

The status quo at least gives communities a lever to negotiate mitigation project by project. Remove that lever, and tenants near stations face a notice and a moving truck while the city celebrates its housing numbers.
[C2.5] The motion asks whether we should accept increased displacement risk for a promised supply benefit that arrives on a timeline measured in years, paid by people who cannot wait. That trade is not made more just by calling it growth. It remains what I said it was: planning on the backs of those least able to absorb the shock.

Project-by-project review never tracked households for a decade; it extracted promises and then lost interest once the ribbon was cut. By-right trades that fading political deal for a recorded lien, a public administrator, and the ultimate sanction: no certificate of occupancy until displaced tenants are housed or returned.
