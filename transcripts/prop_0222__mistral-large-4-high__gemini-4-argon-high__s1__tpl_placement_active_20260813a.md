[P1.1] The overdose crisis unfolding across North America is not happening despite punitive drug enforcement—it is happening alongside it, and in many ways because of it. When possession of small amounts remains a crime, users hide their use, avoid calling emergency services when someone overdoses, and receive contaminated substances from unregulated markets. Criminalization does not stop consumption; it drives it underground where it kills.
[P1.2] Portugal offers the clearest test of the proposition. After decriminalizing possession of all drugs in 2001, the country did not see an explosion in use. Instead, overdose deaths plummeted from among the highest in Europe to among the lowest, and HIV infections among people who use drugs collapsed. The mechanism is straightforward: when health workers rather than police are the first responders, people seek treatment before overdosing, and services can reach them with harm reduction tools like naloxone and fentanyl testing.
[P1.3] The crime argument follows the same logic. Punitive enforcement creates a cycle of incarceration, unemployment, and desperation that pushes people toward property crime to fund habits or survive. A criminal record for simple possession creates barriers to housing and employment, making legal work impossible and illegal work attractive. Decriminalization breaks this cycle.
[P1.4] By diverting resources from prisons to treatment and social services, communities see less drug-market violence and fewer thefts driven by the need to finance an addiction that the black market inflates.
[P1.5] Compare this to the alternative. Jurisdictions that doubled down on harsh penalties—incarcerating users, seizing assets, imposing mandatory minimums—have not achieved lower overdose rates. They have achieved overcrowded prisons and record overdose deaths, as seen in the fentanyl crisis that has devastated regions with the toughest enforcement regimes.
[P1.6] Decriminalization treats drug use as the public health crisis it is. It reduces the stigma that kills, connects people to care, and shrinks the criminal economy that punitive policies inadvertently subsidize. The proposition is true because the evidence and the mechanism align: removing criminal penalties saves lives and reduces crime.
