[P1.1] Emergency powers are easy to grant and almost impossible to take back. That asymmetry is the whole debate. In a crisis, legislatures hand over extraordinary authority within days. When the crisis fades, nobody has to act for those powers to continue, and the one actor who could surrender them is the one who benefits from keeping them.
[P1.2] Automatic sunsets flip the default: the power dies unless elected representatives, after a public hearing, affirmatively choose to keep it.
[P1.3] Three reasons that default should win.
[P1.4] First, inertia is not consent. The United States has had dozens of declared national emergencies in force at once, with one dating back to the late 1970s. No one decided those should last for generations; no one was ever forced to decide. Under a sunset rule, the executive must return and make the case that the emergency still exists and the powers still fit it.
[P1.5] If the case is strong, renewal is a brief vote. If it cannot be made in public, that tells us exactly what we needed to know.
[P1.6] Second, sunsets cost nothing when speed matters. The motion does not touch the initial response. The executive acts on day one and the review comes later. Britain's pandemic legislation required periodic parliamentary renewal, and France's parliament repeatedly voted on extending its post-2015 state of emergency.
[P1.7] Neither government was paralyzed. What they faced was a recurring obligation to justify themselves, which is the minimum a democracy should ask of anyone holding extraordinary power.
[P1.8] Third, hearings create information and accountability that would otherwise never exist. Officials must testify, data must be produced, and legislators must put their names on the record. [P1.9] The alternative is trusting executives to hand back power voluntarily, or trusting legislatures to muster the energy to repeal against a veto and a news cycle that has moved on. History rewards neither trust.

[C1.1] The fatal flaw in automatic sunsets is the assumption that emergencies have expiration dates. Terrorism alerts, economic stabilization measures, and public health preparedness often require sustained executive powers that cannot be neatly boxed into legislative calendars. Forcing a lapse of authority when threats persist guarantees self-inflicted wounds. New York’s experience with COVID-19 demonstrates the danger: when emergency powers lapsed mid-pandemic, the sudden legal vacuum disrupted contact tracing and vaccine distribution, turning a public health crisis into a governance failure.
[C1.2] The disruption was not a feature of accountability but a failure of design—sunsets created a new crisis where none needed to exist.
[C1.3] Even when emergencies are genuinely temporary, sunset votes degenerate into political theater. Britain’s pandemic legislation saw renewal after renewal become a stage for partisan posturing, with legislators prioritizing grandstanding over governance. The result was not enhanced oversight but needless distraction, as precious parliamentary time vanished into performative debate while the crisis raged on. PRO’s own example thus undermines its case: the sunsets did not produce better accountability; they produced spectacle and legislative gridlock.
[C1.4] Worse still, the mechanism solves a problem that better tools already address. Courts stand ready to invalidate executive overreach through judicial review. Independent inspectors general and oversight bodies can audit emergency programs continuously, without the drama of public votes. Legislatures retain the power to repeal at any time, and can do so with surgical precision rather than the blunt instrument of a blanket sunset.
[C1.5] Automatic sunsets add only the illusion of control, replacing targeted oversight with a calendar-driven guillotine that severs capability just when it may be needed most. The real risk is not that powers will persist too long, but that the sunset mechanism will force their premature withdrawal. When democracies tie their own hands with rigid deadlines, they guarantee paralysis at the moment they need agility most.
