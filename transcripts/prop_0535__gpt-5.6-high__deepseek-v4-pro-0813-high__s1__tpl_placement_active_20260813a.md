[P1.1] An advance directive is not a message from a stranger. It is the same person, exercising decision-making capacity, deliberately governing a future in which that capacity may disappear. Dementia can impair present choice without erasing personal identity or transferring ownership of the body to relatives or clinicians. We routinely respect competent consent across time: during anesthesia, unconsciousness, or other incapacity.
[P1.2] The principle should not collapse merely because incapacity is prolonged.
[P1.3] The directive protects what philosophers often call a person’s critical interests: the values by which they wanted their life understood as a whole. Someone may decide that preserving recognition, independence, or freedom from invasive treatment matters more than extending biological life. Later, that person may smile at music, enjoy dessert, or resist a needle. Those present experiences matter and should shape daily care, comfort, and humane treatment.
[P1.4] But they do not necessarily show a changed judgment about feeding tubes, resuscitation, or hospitalization. Momentary preference is not the same as a materially revised value. Otherwise, the very cognitive decline anticipated by the directive becomes the excuse for nullifying it.
[P1.5] “Strongly binding” is not blindly literal. A directive must be interpreted within its actual scope and circumstances. And the proposition includes the right safeguard: clear evidence of material change. A competent later revision, or sustained and coherent evidence that the person’s underlying priorities genuinely shifted, can supersede the earlier instruction.
[P1.6] What should not suffice is a surrogate’s discomfort, a clinician’s default preference for treatment, or selective observations of contentment.
[P1.7] Without this presumption, advance directives become promises honored only when convenient. Families face emotional pressure, institutions tend toward intervention, and people who most feared prolonged dependency lose control exactly when they can no longer defend themselves. A clear burden of proof also reduces conflict: follow the person’s stated choice unless genuine change is demonstrated.
[P1.8] The decisive principle is bodily self-determination over time. If competent people cannot reliably refuse future treatment, then planning for dementia is largely fictional. Strong binding force makes autonomy durable while the material-change exception keeps it responsive rather than rigid.
