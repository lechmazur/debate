[P1.1] Decarbonization is not an abstract engineering exercise; it is an urgent race against time and capital constraints. Every dollar and every year spent matters. The rapid, exponential deployment of renewables—chiefly solar and wind, backed by plunging battery costs and modern grid management—has rendered nuclear energy obsolete as a central pillar of national decarbonization strategies for most of the world.
[P1.2] First, consider the timeline. Building a conventional nuclear reactor is a multi-billion-dollar megaproject that routinely spans a decade or more, plagued by chronic delays and budget blowouts. Western flagship projects like Vogtle in the United States, Flamanville in France, and Olkiluoto in Finland all arrived years late and billions over budget. Solar arrays and wind farms, by contrast, deploy modularly within months to a few years.
[P1.3] When the climate crisis demands steep emissions cuts in this decade, locking up immense capital in projects that produce zero clean kilowatt-hours until the late 2030s actively hinders decarbonization.
[P1.4] Second, look at grid economics and flexibility. The traditional justification for nuclear was the need for round-the-clock "baseload" power. Modern clean grids, however, do not require inflexible thermal baseload; they require flexibility. Plummeting battery storage costs, expanded interregional transmission, and responsive demand management now handle intermittency far more cheaply than maintaining rigid nuclear plants.
[P1.5] Because nuclear reactors carry massive fixed capital costs, they must run continuously at full output to remain financially viable—putting them in direct economic conflict with abundant, near-zero-marginal-cost renewable power.
[P1.6] Finally, the motion specifies *most countries*. Civil nuclear power demands massive sovereign financing, decades of specialized engineering expertise, sophisticated safety regulators, and stringent non-proliferation oversight. The vast majority of nations have neither the fiscal capacity nor the institutional architecture to build a domestic nuclear fleet. For them, renewable power is not merely the cheaper alternative; it is the only scalable, practical path to net zero.
[P1.7] Nuclear is no longer central; it is a costly detour.

[C1.1] At best, the opposing case shows that renewable power is necessary. It does not show that nuclear is unnecessary. The real test is whether most countries can keep a zero-carbon grid operating through a dark, windless winter week, a monsoon lull, or an extended heat wave. On storage and demand flexibility alone, they cannot.
[C1.2] Batteries are excellent for shifting solar from afternoon to evening. They are not a substitute for multi-day or seasonal firm power. Hospitals, industry, freight, and always-on digital infrastructure cannot be curtailed whenever weather is bad, and most countries lack the hydro or geothermal resources to fill the gap. Nuclear is currently the only scalable, zero-carbon source that supplies large quantities of power on demand, in all weather, from a very small land footprint.
[C1.3] The cost comparison is also misleading. A solar farm’s panel price does not include the overbuilding, long-distance transmission, synchronous stability services, and days-to-weeks storage needed to make renewables reliable. A reactor’s capital cost buys system reliability, a lifespan measured in many decades, and the kind of dense output land-constrained countries such as Japan, Korea, Bangladesh, and the Netherlands cannot forgo.
[C1.4] Nor does weak construction performance in a few Western projects prove the technology is marginal. It proves those projects were badly governed. The grids that actually run near-zero carbon today—France, Sweden, Ontario—did so with nuclear or hydro at the center. Where nuclear was withdrawn while renewables grew, fossil gas and coal remained as backstops.
[C1.5] If most countries copy that pattern, they will decarbonize partway and then stall. Nuclear is therefore not a costly detour; it is the missing firm backbone of a credible decarbonization strategy.
