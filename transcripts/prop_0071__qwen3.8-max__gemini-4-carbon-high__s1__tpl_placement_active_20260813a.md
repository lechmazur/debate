[P1.1] The central question is whether a state can acknowledge a massive theft of labor and land while leaving its material consequences untouched. The proposition says no: if wealth was built through slavery and colonial extraction, justice requires direct reparations to descendant communities, not mostly words.
[P1.2] Our case rests on three points. First, the harm was economic, not merely moral. Enslaved people generated capital for ports, insurers, plantations, and factories; colonial rule extracted taxes, land, crops, and forced labor. After British abolition, for example, the state compensated slave owners, while formerly enslaved people received no comparable asset base.
[P1.3] That decision helped turn emancipation into formal freedom without wealth.
[P1.4] Second, the effects persist. Wealth compounds. When one generation is barred from owning land, accumulating savings, or accessing education, the disadvantage is passed down through housing markets, credit access, inheritance, and public investment. A symbolic apology may express regret, but it does not repair a racial wealth gap, restore lost land value, or fund businesses in communities excluded from later prosperity.
[P1.5] An apology changes a nation’s story about itself; reparations change what descendants can actually own, learn, build, and inherit.
[P1.6] Third, direct reparations are administratively possible. States already identify beneficiaries for veterans’ benefits, Holocaust restitution, indigenous land claims, and compensation for wrongful government seizures. Difficulties in tracing lineage or setting amounts are reasons to design careful programs—grants for education, homeownership, business capital, community trusts, and health investment—not reasons to substitute speech for repair.
[P1.7] The opposition will likely say this is too complex, divisive, or historically remote. But complexity is not injustice’s answer. If a country can build its treasury on coerced labor, it can build a remedy targeted to those still marked by that history. Apologies can matter as acknowledgment, but they become evasion when they replace the transfer of resources.
[P1.8] Vote PRO because the motion asks what should mainly follow extraction: symbolic regret, or material repair.
