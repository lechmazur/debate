[C1.1] PRO’s “where the money actually goes” test misses the threshold question: does this lever move the housing problem? A home may become “a hotel room” [P1.1], but high-demand neighborhoods are expensive for deeper reasons: scarce land, tight zoning, slow construction, and affluent demand for transit and schools. Restrictions can reallocate some units; they cannot build the missing housing. If narrow enough to spare genuine home-shares [P1.5], too few units return to bend rents.
[C1.2] If broad enough to matter, they suppress the most valuable visitor use where visitor-linked income is largest.
[C1.3] The pro story assumes each returned listing becomes an affordable long-term home. Often it does not: owners sell, use medium-term furnished lets, renovate for upscale tenants, or reserve units for family and seasonal use. A tourist flat is not automatically the right size or price for the household being displaced. Current tenants get no rent cut; future tenants still compete in a scarce market.
[C1.4] The housing gain is delayed and speculative; the income loss starts when bookings stop.
[C1.5] Those losses are not just owner profits. A whole-unit listing supports cleaners, repair work, groceries, taxis, restaurants, and supplemental mortgage or tax payments for local hosts. PRO says visitors “would have stayed somewhere anyway” [P1.1], but substitution leaks away: hotel districts capture more spending, trips shorten, and some visitors choose another city. The corner café in a residential neighborhood does not recover its margin because a downtown hotel sold a room.
[C1.6] Restrictions can also displace in the opposite direction. Remove that income, and small owners may sell to investors, service workers lose routes, shops lose customers, and tourist demand migrates into adjacent neighborhoods with cheaper housing and weaker protections—the same pressure, one block outward. The comparative claim fails at both ends: a thin, uncertain rent effect is bought with immediate local income loss, reduced visitor spending, and displacement shifted onto people with less cushion.
