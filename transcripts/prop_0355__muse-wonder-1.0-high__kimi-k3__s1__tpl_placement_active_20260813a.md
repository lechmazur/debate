[P1.1] For a homeowner, charging an electric vehicle is routine: hire an electrician, pull a permit, plug in overnight. For an apartment renter, the same decision can end with an unanswered email. That asymmetry is a legal default that gives landlords an effective veto over how tenants fuel a car they legally own, even when tenants are willing to pay.
[P1.2] That veto matters because home charging is where EVs become affordable and practical. Overnight charging is cheaper and more reliable than hunting for public chargers, paying higher prices, and leaving a car elsewhere at night. Without it, renters—disproportionately younger and less wealthy than owners—are pushed toward expensive public charging or locked out of the used EV market altogether. We ask renters to support cleaner transport while preserving the bottleneck that makes it impractical for them.
[P1.3] The market will not fix this because the incentive is split. The tenant gets the savings; the landlord gets paperwork and disruption, with no obvious rent premium today for saying yes. So rational landlords delay, demand repeated studies, or refuse over aesthetics and hypothetical future plans. Delay is denial when a lease lasts a year and a car is needed now.
[P1.4] A stronger right to request reverses that presumption without expropriating anyone. Tenants would pay for installation, electricity, maintenance, and insurance, use licensed installers, meet code, and restore the space on departure where reasonable. Landlords could impose sensible conditions on placement and timing, and refuse for a demonstrable safety problem, genuine capacity limit, or disproportionate structural cost. They could not block by silence, speculation, or preference alone, and would have to answer within a set period.
[P1.5] That balance also serves buildings. Tenant-funded wiring and conduit remain as infrastructure for future tenants who will increasingly expect to charge. The law should treat reasonable, tenant-paid access as the normal rule, not a favor landlords may stall indefinitely.

[C1.1] The homeowner comparison that opens the other side's case actually ends it. A homeowner can hire an electrician and plug in because she owns the panel, the fire risk, the insurance policy, and the consequences of a bad decision. A renter owns none of those. Control follows risk.
[C1.2] The same lease that blocks an EV circuit also blocks a tenant-funded bathroom remodel, and nobody calls that an effective veto over hygiene. Buildings are altered by the person who bears the downside when the alteration goes wrong.
[C1.3] That downside is real, and "tenant pays" does not erase it. EV charging is a continuous high-amperage load, often eight hours overnight, on wiring older buildings were never sized for. Panel capacity is a finite shared resource: the first three tenants who install chargers can consume the headroom the fourth tenant, the elevator, or the laundry room needs. When capacity runs out, the fix is not one tenant's charger—it is a five-figure service upgrade, building-wide.
[C1.4] Who pays then? The proposal has no answer, because the cost is systemic while the benefit is private.
[C1.5] Nor can owners offload the risk. If a tenant-installed charger starts a fire at 3 a.m., it is the landlord's building, the landlord's insurer, the landlord's liability—and the other tenants' lives. A requirement that tenants carry insurance is a paper promise no one verifies after move-in.
[C1.6] Then comes the enforcement trap. "Demonstrable safety problem" versus "speculation" will be litigated building by building, and small landlords will learn that the only legally safe answer is yes, whatever the engineering says. Delay is often diligence; a twelve-month lease does not change the physics of a hundred-amp panel.
[C1.7] Meanwhile, time and the market are doing the work. New construction codes increasingly require EV readiness; buildings advertise charging to win tenants; renters can choose those buildings at signing. This mandate falls hardest on the oldest, least-suited buildings—exactly where refusal is most justified.

[P2.1] The bathroom analogy gives the game away. A remodel tears out the owner's fixtures; a charger lets a tenant use a parking space she already rents to fuel a car she already owns, with removable equipment. Landlords do not get unlimited control over rented space just because they own it — they cannot dictate every appliance a tenant plugs in. Ownership justifies conditions, not an unreviewable veto.
[P2.2] Their capacity argument concedes the workable line. If a panel genuinely has no headroom, that is exactly the demonstrable, disproportionate-cost refusal allowed. What it does not justify is blocking the first tenant, where headroom exists, by pointing to a hypothetical fourth. And scarcity is often manageable: licensed installers can use load-sharing chargers that throttle at building peaks, lower-amperage circuits, or scheduled charging.
[P2.3] A landlord may require those tools. Under the current default, he never has to consider them — he just says capacity, and the inquiry ends.
[P2.4] Fire risk follows the same pattern. A permitted, code-inspected circuit by a licensed electrician is not a space heater on an extension cord, which landlords tolerate with far less oversight. Insurance is not unverifiable if approval is conditioned on it: require a certificate, name the owner as additionally insured, make lapse grounds to disconnect. Risk that can be inspected, insured, and switched off is a reason to regulate installation, not prohibit it.
[P2.5] Calling delay 'diligence' renames the problem. Diligence has an endpoint — a load calculation, an electrician's letter, a yes or a specific no. Indefinite silence and repeated studies are not engineering. A deadline with reasons reduces disputes because both sides know the test; the present veto invites arbitrary treatment with no record.
[P2.6] Finally, 'move to a building that allows it' is no answer in tight rental markets, for tenants mid-lease replacing a dying car, or for anyone priced out of new construction. New buildings helping tomorrow does nothing for the renter whose landlord will not reply today.

The tenant loses practical access to her car for her lease; the landlord administers conditions, can disconnect for non-compliance, and keeps tenant-funded infrastructure afterward.
