[P1.1] Homelessness is defined by one thing: not having a home. Everything else — the tent, the ER visits, the disorder on the sidewalk — flows downstream from that deficit. Treating homelessness mainly as a policing problem treats the fever while ignoring the infection, and cities pay dearly for the mistake.
[P1.2] Start with causation. Policing does not reduce the number of homeless people; it relocates them. A sweep clears a corner on Tuesday, and the same people reappear two blocks away by Friday, minus documents, medications, and the service appointments keeping them connected. [P1.3] Second, outcomes. Where cities lead with housing and services, homelessness falls. Houston cut its homeless population by well over half over the past decade through coordinated housing-first placements. Finland made Housing First national policy and is among the few countries where long-term homelessness keeps dropping.
[P1.4] No city has ticketed or swept its way to a comparable result, because that tool never touches the cause.
[P1.5] Third, cost. Enforcement-first is the expensive model. Chronically homeless people cycle through jails, ambulances, and emergency rooms at a public cost that commonly rivals or exceeds a supportive apartment. Housing is not the compassionate splurge; it is the fiscal consolidation.
[P1.6] And notice what this does to public order, the value the other side will raise. The only durable way to clear a sidewalk is to give the person on it somewhere to go. Encampments re-form after sweeps because displacement is not resolution; a housed person generates no recurring complaint, no recurring call for service, no recurring cleanup.
[P1.7] The motion asks only about primacy: housing and services as the lead strategy, enforcement in a supporting role when genuine safety demands it. That ordering is the one that actually shrinks the problem. The opposite ordering spends more, houses no one, and reproduces the same sidewalk week after week.
