[P1.1] Control of mass graves, atrocity sites, and sites of forced labor is not a courtesy extended to descendant communities. It is a substantive condition of truthful commemoration. I will defend that claim on three grounds: epistemic standing, prevention of instrumental abuse, and the reparative logic of consent.
[P1.2] First, descendant communities possess knowledge no outside expert can substitute. They carry the names, kinship structures, mourning rituals, and the meaning of specific objects and locations. A mass grave is not a data set; it is a relational site. When state archaeologists alone decide what to dig, what to display, and what story to tell, the resulting narrative is predictably flattened into something the state can tolerate.
[P1.3] The African Burial Ground in lower Manhattan was uncovered during construction of a federal building in 1991, and only sustained descendant and community advocacy prevented the site from being paved over and the dead from being reduced to an inconvenient footnote. That pattern repeats wherever descendant authority is absent.
[P1.4] Second, the record of these sites under external control is a record of exploitation. Mass graves have been mined for political legitimacy. Forced-labor camps have been rebranded as heritage tourism with the suffering airbrushed into palatable lessons. Descendant authority is the only durable check against this instrumentalization, because descendants have no professional or political incentive to sanitize what was done to their own people.
[P1.5] They are the constituency that loses if the story is softened.
[P1.6] Third — and decisively — excavation is itself an act of disturbance. To dig up the dead without the standing consent of those who loved them is to repeat, in a quieter register, the original violence. The state or faction that perpetrated the atrocity does not acquire, by the act of losing, the authority to narrate its victims afterward. Substantial control is therefore not a privilege for descendants; it is the minimum condition under which these sites can be handled at all without compounding the injustice.

The dead cannot consent; the state cannot own the dead; but neither can any living group claim full authority over crimes that implicate universal legal norms.
