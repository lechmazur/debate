[P1.1] The silent epidemic of metabolic dysfunction affects hundreds of millions worldwide, with insulin resistance often progressing for years before a diabetes diagnosis. When a user sees their glucose spike after a supposedly healthy breakfast cereal or crash during a sedentary afternoon, they gain actionable intelligence to adjust behavior before disease takes hold. [P1.2] This is preventive precision, not pathology.
[P1.3] Critics warn of anxiety and over-medicalization, but they mistake empowerment for illness. Watching your own physiological data is not the same as being treated for a disease; it is the ultimate form of health autonomy. The anxiety of ignorance—walking blindly toward metabolic syndrome—far outweighs the temporary discomfort of learning hard truths about one's diet. [P1.4] Early data prevents that tragedy.
[P1.5] Moreover, the population-level benefit dwarfs individual neurosis. If even a fraction of users reduce glycemic variability through simple dietary shifts informed by CGM data—swapping refined carbs for fiber, timing meals to avoid spikes, adding post-meal walks—we prevent cases of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver at scale. Over-medicalization implies unnecessary drugs and procedures. A CGM requires no prescription, no surgery, no pharmaceutical intervention—only data and personal choice.
[P1.6] Widespread adoption means a population that understands its metabolism, eats with intention, and intervenes early. That is a public health victory, not a medicalized dystopia.

A spike after oatmeal, a dip during a long meeting, a higher reading at 4 a.m.—these are normal physiology, not warning signs. Consumer sensors carry roughly ten percent error and lag behind blood by minutes.
[C1.3] So the typical healthy user sees hundreds of transient numbers they cannot interpret, with no clinician to contextualize them, and an app that labels perfectly ordinary readings "spikes." PRO's own example gives the game away: a smoothie producing a "diabetic-range" reading in a non-diabetic is not a discovery of hidden disease. [C1.4] That reframing is the engine of harm. Users learn to fear fruit, beans, and whole grains because they move the line, and to prefer bacon and cheese because they don't—optimizing a proxy at the expense of the outcome. We already have names for this pattern: orthorexia and food anxiety, and they thrive on continuous feedback. Then comes the cascade: a worried user books a visit, requests an HbA1c and an insulin panel, gets referred, buys the company's supplements and "metabolic coaching."
[C1.5] No prescription required, yet thoroughly medicalized.
[C1.6] Now weigh the benefit. There is no solid trial evidence that CGM use in non-diabetics reduces diabetes incidence or cardiovascular events. The people who buy sensors at over a hundred dollars a month are disproportionately the already-fit worried-well. [C1.7] Population health improves through boring, proven things: vegetables, walking, sleep, affordable screening. Widespread CGMs don't deliver those; they sell a stream of personalized noise, and noise plus profit motive produces anxiety, not prevention.
