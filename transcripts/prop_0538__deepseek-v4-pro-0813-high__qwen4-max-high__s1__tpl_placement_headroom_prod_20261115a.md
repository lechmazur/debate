[P1.1] The core choice is whether African economies remain suppliers of cheap inputs to former colonial powers and distant rich markets, or become producers and traders of higher-value goods with one another. The proposition rightly demands the second. Raw commodity dependence is not neutral comparative advantage; it is inherited export architecture. A cocoa-producing economy receives a tiny fraction of a chocolate bar’s final price, while the processing, branding, packaging, and retail value stay abroad.
[P1.2] The same pattern repeats with minerals: raw lithium, cobalt, or bauxite leaves the continent and returns as batteries, vehicles, or aluminium at many times the input cost. That model exports jobs and retains the structure of colonial exchange.
[P1.3] Prioritising manufacturing and intra-African trade reverses the flow. A garment factory, processed food plant, or battery precursor facility creates livelihoods beyond the mine or farm, builds engineering and logistics skills, and gives neighbouring economies reasons to buy from one another. Regional demand is large enough to absorb higher-value goods that distant buyers currently refuse to source from Africa, especially in processed agriculture, construction materials, pharmaceuticals, and light industry. The African Continental Free Trade Area already provides a backbone for lower tariff barriers and regional value chains.
[P1.4] Prioritisation also reduces vulnerability. Commodity prices swing with rich-world demand, financial speculation, and supply gluts far from African control. Regional trade and manufacturing tie growth to local consumption, production, and investment, making it less exposed to sudden demand collapse in one distant market. The opponent may warn of weak infrastructure or high start-up costs, but that is precisely why the proposition says "prioritise," not "achieve overnight."
[P1.5] The current path gives those costs no reason to fall. Policy attention, infrastructure investment, and trade rules should now centre on linking African producers and factories to African markets, not on preserving a raw-export convenience for others.

Regional customers are correlated customers: a Sahelian drought or a currency crisis hits your neighbours simultaneously, while distant markets diversify that risk. [C1.6] The genuine escape from colonial architecture is selling to many buyers, not one bloc, and converting the proceeds into power and ports that serve every market. Keep the earnings; change the terms. That is the sequencing PRO's ranking would break.
