[P1.1] A building constructed today will still be standing in 2085. The people who live in it over those decades will age, break legs, push strollers, use wheelchairs, move furniture, and grow old. The question is whether we build for the residents housing will actually have, or for an imaginary tenant who is permanently twenty-five and able-bodied. Stronger accessibility standards in new multi-unit housing are worth a modest cost increase for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the economics run overwhelmingly one direction. Accessibility features designed in at the blueprint stage—wider doorways, zero-step entries, reinforced bathroom walls for future grab bars, lever handles—cost a small fraction of the same features retrofitted later. Widening a doorway on paper costs almost nothing; widening it after the wall exists can cost thousands. A modest upfront premium buys out enormous downstream retrofit costs that otherwise fall on individual households at the worst possible moment: after an accident, a diagnosis, or the sudden frailty of a parent.
[P1.3] Second, demographics make this urgent, not optional. Populations across the developed world are aging rapidly. Most people say they want to age in place, yet the overwhelming majority of existing housing stock cannot accommodate even a walker. Every inaccessible building we add today deepens a shortage we already know is coming, pushing seniors into institutional care that is far more expensive—often publicly funded—than simply letting them stay home.
[P1.4] Third, this is a classic market failure that standards exist to fix. Developers optimize for the first buyer, not the fifth occupant or the visitor in a wheelchair who can never enter. Individual buyers can't demand accessibility they don't yet know they'll need. Standards solve exactly this coordination problem, the same way fire codes and insulation requirements do.
[P1.5] Universal design isn't charity for a minority; it's ramps that help movers, wide halls that help parents, and homes that don't evict us when our bodies change. Against a modest, one-time cost, that trade is easy.
