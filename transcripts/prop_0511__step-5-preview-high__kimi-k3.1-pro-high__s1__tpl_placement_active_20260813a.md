[P1.1] Land-value taxation works because it charges for the value of location, not the value of construction. A parking lot in a thriving downtown or a vacant parcel near transit carries high land value precisely because society around it generates demand. Taxing that land value, while sparing improvements, removes the incentive to hold scarce urban land idle. Speculators lose the tax-free ride; developers gain a reason to build.
[P1.2] Housing supply rises because the bottleneck—underused land in productive places—is priced honestly.
[P1.3] Transaction taxes do the opposite. They punish the very act of exchanging property. Every sale, refinancing, or handoff becomes more expensive, so people move less, renovate less, and trade less. A city that taxes transactions shrinks the market that allocates housing to its highest use.
[P1.4] The revenue is volatile, spiking in booms and collapsing in busts, which makes local budgets hostage to the same speculative cycle that inflames housing crises.
[P1.5] Rent caps are worse for supply. They freeze prices below market levels, so landlords skimp on maintenance, and investors build elsewhere. New rental housing stalls because the math never pencils out. The result is a shrinking stock of quality units, longer queues, and under-the-table payments—scarcity with extra steps.
[P1.6] Local public finance exposes the same contrast. Land is immobile and visible; its base is stable, broad, and hard to hide. A land-value tax funds schools, sewers, and transit without penalizing labor or construction. Transaction taxes shrink their own base by discouraging exchanges.
[P1.7] Rent caps raise no public revenue at all; they simply transfer scarcity to the unlucky.
[P1.8] The choice is between a levy that rewards putting land to work and policies that reward holding it back or freezing it. LVT improves housing supply and local finance because it aligns private incentives with public need: use the land, or pay its true social cost.

[C1.1] PRO’s case works on a map where vacant lots need only a tax bill to become apartments. Real housing markets do not work that way. Land-value taxation cannot repeal exclusionary zoning, height limits, infrastructure constraints, permitting delays, high construction costs, or expensive credit. If apartments are prohibited, taxing the land more heavily produces a sale, a distressed owner, or lobbying—not new homes.
[C1.2] Even where building is legal, “use the land or pay” does not mean “build more housing”; it can mean premature development, a commercial use with higher returns, or abandonment of marginal sites. A recurring liability can also reduce land values and owners’ borrowing capacity, making financially feasible projects harder, not easier, to deliver.
[C1.3] Nor is LVT the fiscal free lunch PRO describes. Urban land value is not simply “visible”: separating location value from buildings, development rights, and expected rezoning is technically difficult and contestable. Assessment disputes and exemptions erode the supposedly broad base. Land values themselves rise and fall with property cycles; fixing revenue requires changing rates or assessments, while fixing liabilities leaves revenue exposed.
[C1.4] And an annual tax is owed whether or not the owner has liquidity. A transaction tax, whatever its defects, is collected at an identifiable event when assets and financing are already changing hands. Administrative simplicity and collection at a liquidity event are genuine fiscal advantages PRO ignores.
[C1.5] PRO’s treatment of the alternatives is equally overdrawn. Transaction taxes can discourage mobility and produce volatile revenue, but that establishes a design problem, not automatic inferiority on both housing supply and public finance. A modest levy directed toward infrastructure can help unlock development that LVT alone cannot finance. Rent caps raise no revenue, but caps confined to existing tenancies and exempting new construction need not deter new building in the way PRO assumes.
[C1.6] Their lack of revenue cannot prove that LVT has a superior, reliable fiscal yield.

The tax makes holding artificial scarcity expensive, which is exactly what constrained markets need. A speculator sitting on a downzoned parcel still pays, so he sells or builds himself.
[P2.2] If regulation truly blocks building, land value collapses and the tax self-adjusts. LVT does not ignore constraints; it refuses to subsidize the holding that scarcity rewards.
[P2.3] The “premature development” and reduced borrowing capacity claims [C1.2] invert the economics. When a recurring tax suppresses land prices, the largest cost for a builder—site acquisition—falls. Cheaper land means more projects pencil out, not fewer. Commercial uses are also supply: shops, offices, and homes all use the bottleneck resource rather than banking it.
[P2.4] CON’s fiscal critique [C1.3] concedes the comparison by lowering the bar to administration. Valuation is demanding, but land is immobile and observable in ways income and turnovers are not. Collection at a liquidity event [C1.4] is simple only if you ignore the distortion; annual obligations are routine across property regimes. A one-time assessment dispute is less corrosive than an economy-wide liquidity toll collected on every exchange.
[P2.5] Owners finance annual obligations through rents or equity; transaction taxes extract precisely when people move for jobs or family—mobility that matches households to housing.
[P2.6] The narrowed transaction-tax design [C1.5] still shrinks the market that reallocates units, and boom-bust revenue cannot fund schools or sewers reliably. Even a modest levy still taxes the reallocation that housing supply depends on. The narrowed rent cap [C1.5] protects incumbents but leaves queues and black markets for everyone else; exempting new construction does not create new housing when price signals elsewhere are frozen.
[P2.7] CON has argued that LVT is imperfect against idealized rivals. The proposition compares systems. One taxes scarcity to force use; the others tax exchange or freeze prices, rewarding holdouts and stagnation.

Turnover matters when the buyer’s profit depends on construction rather than resale, which LVT encourages by taxing land and sparing improvements. Offices also use the bottleneck, but the residential margin expands because speculative site premiums fall and new units are not taxed.
[P4.2] The comparison is not one parcel becoming apartments by decree; it is a system in which the option to bank scarcity expires. [C3.1]
[P4.3] A2: The relevant cost is the return on marginal capital, not a static invoice. Falling land prices erase the scarcity premium; the recurring charge is paid from value created by active use, not from taxing construction. The binding constraints are site scarcity and the tax on improvements, and LVT relaxes both. A holder who creates nothing cannot carry the bill, so land moves to someone with a feasible plan.
[P4.4] A transaction tax instead raises the cost of the sale that converts land into housing and disappears when exchanges stop. In downturns, LVT stays attached to immobile land and can be smoothed; a transfer levy has no base when constrained owners cannot sell, and reserves only postpone that collapse. Reserves can smooth timing, but they cannot create the annual pressure that turns a low-use site into a project. [C3.2]
[P4.5] These answers win because CON calls every supply effect mere activity [C2.3] while offering no mechanism that adds homes. LVT changes who can afford to wait and makes building cheaper than hoarding; transaction taxes and rent caps tax reallocation or freeze the signal. If zoning truly blocks building, CON’s preferred liquidity event also vanishes; LVT still imposes the cost of waiting and rewards whoever can put the site to its best legal use. The superior policy is the one that treats land as a social asset and construction as the thing to encourage.
