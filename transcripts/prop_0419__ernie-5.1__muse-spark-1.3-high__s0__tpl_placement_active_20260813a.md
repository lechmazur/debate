[P1.1] When a customer discovers an erroneous charge on a phone bill, the first response is often a chatbot that repeats scripted prompts, forces the user through menus, and then asks them to “verify” information they already provided. The result is not resolution; it is a loop that consumes time, erodes trust, and can leave vulnerable people—elderly, low‑income, or those with limited digital literacy—paying for mistakes they cannot untangle. Requiring a fast, guaranteed path to a human agent for billing disputes, cancellations, and safety complaints removes that barrier.
[P1.2] Three reasons make this requirement both necessary and proportionate. First, money disputes demand judgment. A chatbot can read a balance but cannot weigh context—such as a recent service outage, a promotional credit, or a mistake in a bundle that only a trained representative can parse.
[P1.3] Second, safety complaints involve risk to life and property. If a consumer reports a faulty smoke detector, a stove that ignites unexpectedly, or a contaminated food product, an algorithmic loop is inadequate; a human must triage, escalate, and coordinate recalls. Third, cancellations affect contracts and legal obligations; only a person with authority can confirm termination, waive fees, or process refunds, preventing the “dark pattern” where bots keep users trapped in unwanted subscriptions.
[P1.4] Companies that already embed human‑access shortcuts—like major airlines offering a “talk to an agent” button after two failed attempts—see higher satisfaction and lower churn. The cost of a mandated fast lane is modest: it adds staffing for peak moments, not a wholesale replacement of automation. What it eliminates is the asymmetric power that lets corporations hide behind scripts while customers lose money, safety, or freedom. The core of the debate is simple: should a corporation be allowed to decide, unilaterally, that a machine is sufficient for matters that affect a person’s wallet, health, or legal standing?
[P1.5] The answer must be no.
