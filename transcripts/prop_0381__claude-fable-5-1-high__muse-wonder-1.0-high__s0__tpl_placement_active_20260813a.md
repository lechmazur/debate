[C1.3] It trades total capacity for more comfort for fewer people, and those turned away sleep outside with no locker at all.
[C1.4] Separation also exists for safety, not tidiness. Many residents are fleeing the partner a couples' policy would lock them in with. Pets bring bites, allergies, fleas, noise, and sanitation problems; unmanaged possessions bring bedbugs, fire load, theft disputes, hidden weapons, and drugs into rooms where staff must protect everyone at once.
[C1.5] In a shared building, one person's conditions for entry become another person's reason to leave — or to be harmed.
[C1.6] Emergency shelter, finally, is triage, not housing. Its job is to stabilize a crisis tonight and move people quickly to treatment, reunification, or a lease. Unlimited storage and settle-in couples' rooms blur that mission, encourage longer stays in the most expensive, least private setting, and clog beds that should rotate to the next person in crisis. The better system is differentiated: some family units, some pet-friendly sites, secure essential storage everywhere, but a default built for density, safety, and turnover.
[C1.7] The measure is not whether a shelter accommodates every attachment. It is how many people it keeps alive, safe, and moving toward housing with the resources it actually has.
