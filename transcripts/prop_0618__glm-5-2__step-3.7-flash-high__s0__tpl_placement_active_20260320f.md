[P1.1] Public funding for universities should produce public value. Enrollment alone measures only that students walked through a door. It tells us nothing about whether they left with a degree, whether that degree translated into economic mobility, or whether taxpayers got a return on their investment. Tying funding to graduation rates and post-graduation earnings shifts the incentive structure from access theater to access with consequence.
[P1.2] The current enrollment-first model creates a clear perverse incentive. A university can enroll thousands of students, lose most of them to attrition, and still collect the same per-head funding as an institution that graduates the students it admits. This rewards institutions for filling seats and punishes those that invest in the advising, mentoring, and academic support that actually drives completion. The students most harmed are the ones public universities exist to serve: first-generation, low-income, and underprepared students who enroll at high rates but drop out at higher ones when support is thin.
[P1.3] Graduation rates capture whether a university delivered on its core promise. Post-graduation earnings capture whether the degree was worth the time and debt. Together, they measure what students and taxpayers actually care about. A degree that leaves graduates unable to out-earn high school peers is not a public good regardless of how many students were enrolled to pursue it.
[P1.4] The objection that outcome metrics punish institutions serving disadvantaged students has it backward. Enrollment funding lets those institutions off the hook—they collect money for enrolling vulnerable students while facing no consequence for failing them. Outcome funding, properly designed, creates pressure to invest in the support systems those students need. It makes failing disadvantaged students expensive rather than profitable.
[P1.5] Public money should follow public results. Funding enrollment without funding outcomes subsidizes a system that profits from aspiration without delivering on it.

[C1.3] Post-graduation earnings metrics are even more destructive, as they punish universities for offering exactly the programs that sustain a healthy, functional society. Teacher preparation, social work, early childhood education, public interest law, and the arts all deliver immense public value but produce below-average starting salaries. If funding hinges on cohort earnings averages, universities will slash these programs entirely, hollowing out the civic and social infrastructure that public universities are uniquely positioned to uphold.

Tying funding to graduation speed and earnings averages will penalize universities for serving these students, creating a powerful incentive to cut support programs and reject applicants who do not fit the narrow, fast, high-earning mold PRO’s policy rewards.
[C1.5] Enrollment, by contrast, measures access—the core public promise of higher education. While no single metric is perfect, tying funding to graduation and earnings replaces a measure of opportunity with a narrow, mission-corrupting incentive structure that will leave public universities less accessible, less rigorous, and less responsive to the full range of public needs.
