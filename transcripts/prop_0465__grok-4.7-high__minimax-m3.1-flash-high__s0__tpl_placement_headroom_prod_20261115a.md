[P1.1] Rivers are not pipes and forests are not stockpiles. When Indigenous or local communities ask a government to recognize a major river or ecosystem as a legal person, they are demanding that the law stop treating living systems as free inputs for someone else's project. Personhood on request is the right answer because it turns unrepresented harm into a claim that courts and agencies must hear.
[P1.2] Legal personhood does not pretend a river is a citizen. Corporations already possess it without walking or voting. What it supplies is standing, guardianship, and enforceable duties. Representatives drawn from the requesting community can challenge pollution, diversion, and habitat loss that ordinary permits treat as acceptable externalities.
[P1.3] That is why the reform is worth the complication it creates for infrastructure. A dam, mine, or highway that must answer to the river's legal interests is forced to disclose its real costs before concrete is poured. Delay is not sabotage. It is the price of a constraint the status quo never imposed.
[P1.4] The request trigger matters. This is not a remote bureaucracy inventing rights for scenery. It is the people who know the water, carry the cultural loss, and will drink the silt or watch the fishery collapse. Denying them so a developer can move faster simply privileges one set of claimants over another.
[P1.5] Personhood levels that field without requiring every ecosystem on earth to be enrolled at once.
[P1.6] Where rivers have already been recognized, the practical result has been guardians and negotiated use, not a freeze on all building. Infrastructure continues, but under a duty of care that open liquidation never required. Governments should grant the status when communities ask, because calling irreversible damage development only works until the ecosystem can no longer sustain the community or the economy built on its ruin.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a useful intuition for a sound legal rule. Legal personhood would give a river or ecosystem standing, but it would not determine who speaks for it, whose interests count, or when its interests outweigh those of the people who depend on it. The request of one community is an inadequate warrant for granting that authority over a connected system affecting many others.
[C1.2] The strongest example is a major river crossing jurisdictions. Upstream communities may request recognition because the river sustains a fishery, sacred practice, or local economy. Downstream cities, farms, Indigenous nations, and Indigenous and other communities further along the river may support a dam, diversion, or flood-control scheme for different reasons. Personhood creates a single institutional voice, but it does not resolve this genuine conflict.
[C1.3] It merely transfers the decision from agencies capable of weighing regional claims to a guardian whose mandate would remain contested. A locally requested legal person could therefore become a new source of exclusion.
[C1.4] PRO also claims that personhood supplies enforceable duties, yet legal personhood does not itself create duties. Those must still be written, funded, and assigned to human institutions. If guardians are empowered to negotiate permits, approve development, or defend the ecosystem in court, their discretion becomes decisive. Without transparent appointment rules, conflict-of-interest safeguards, and reviewable standards, personhood risks producing opaque authority rather than accountable protection.
[C1.5] If the guardians merely advise, the added legal machinery may add little beyond existing environmental assessment and public-interest litigation.
[C1.6] The proposition is also asymmetric: it treats every infrastructure complication as a cost, but treats unclear representation, litigation, institutional conflict, and exclusion of affected communities as benefits in advance. Those are not minor administrative details; they determine whether recognition protects the ecosystem or merely changes the forum for disputes.
[C1.7] Existing statutes can protect rivers through water rights, habitat rules, public-interest standing, and designated guardians without pretending an ecosystem is a single legal actor. Governments should not grant full personhood merely because local communities request it. [clipped]

[C2.1] PRO’s strongest claim is that representation is merely a “design task.” It is not: it determines whether the new actor protects a river or converts one community’s preferences into institutional authority. Personhood creates standing, but substantive duties and powers must still be legislated. Once specified, statutes can impose those duties without inventing a second legal person [C1.4].
[C2.2] That exposes the additionality problem. PRO says personhood ends “booking ecological ruin as someone else’s residue” [P2.6]. But a statute protecting flows and habitat, combined with public-interest standing and reviewable guardians, can achieve that without giving the ecosystem one institutional voice. If the only difference is who sues, personhood may increase litigation without improving conservation.
[C2.3] If guardians control consent, competing ecological and human interests become harder to weigh, not easier.
[C2.4] PRO calls existing habitat law inadequate because ecosystems remain objects [P2.5]. Yet objects can have enforceable protection through species rules, water allocations, pollution controls, and cumulative-impact review. Human organizations can bring claims while remaining answerable to affected people. Personhood adds no ecological knowledge and creates no remedy by itself; courts still need evidence, justiciable interests, and standards.
[C2.5] The request trigger sharpens the problem. One community may seek recognition to strengthen its position against downstream users. PRO insists personhood does not crown that community sovereign [P2.1], but authority must be delegated because no government can determine a river’s single “interest.” Safeguards can reduce abuse; the proposition grants authority first and resolves who controls it later.
[C2.6] A workable regime should protect ecosystems through transparent duties, participation, and reviewable decisions. If rivers need legal standing, governments should create it carefully, not automatically grant personhood whenever a community requests it. Simpler mechanisms may deliver more protection with less concentrated, contestable power.

But the proposition leaps from “we want recognition” to “the government must grant personhood.” Neither the legal gap nor the river’s importance answers who defines its interests, whose claims may compromise them, or what standard courts apply. “Because the river cannot say no” is not a reason to approve a diversion; it is a reason to prevent any actor from receiving an automatic advantage.

Where those mechanisms are inadequate, governments should assess the particular ecosystem before conferring exceptional status.
[C4.5] The proposition instead makes the petition decisive while conceding that duties, representation, and review remain unresolved [P4.1]. That sequencing converts community pressure into a presumption of legal authority. The sounder rule is to grant legal standing or guardianship where a demonstrated legal gap and accountable design require it—not automatically whenever any community requests the more dramatic label.

Human communities retain their votes and claims, but the ecosystem acquires an additional institutional advocate whose mandate and influence need not answer to them.
