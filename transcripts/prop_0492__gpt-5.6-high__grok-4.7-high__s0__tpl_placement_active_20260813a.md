[P1.1] When a company replaces cashiers, drivers, or warehouse staff with machines, it captures the savings, while workers and communities absorb lost wages, retraining costs, and pressure on public services. That is not simply innovation; it is a transfer of transition costs from the beneficiary to everyone else. An automation tax corrects that externality: firms that create concentrated disruption contribute to repairing it.
[P1.2] The policy should target demonstrated labor replacement, not every purchase of software or machinery. Liability can be based on substantial workforce reductions linked to automation and calibrated to a portion of the resulting payroll savings. Revenue should be ring-fenced for practical adjustment: training connected to actual vacancies, wage insurance for workers forced into lower-paid jobs, relocation assistance, apprenticeships, and job matching. Credits can reward firms that retrain and redeploy their own employees.
[P1.3] This design taxes displacement, while protecting technology that complements workers.
[P1.4] Crucially, the tax improves the quality of automation rather than stopping it. A genuinely productive machine will still be worthwhile after a moderate levy. What becomes less attractive is marginal automation pursued mainly because the firm can pocket savings while taxpayers fund unemployment and workers bear ruined careers. Markets produce efficient choices only when decision-makers face the costs they create.
[P1.5] Requiring that contribution encourages gradual adoption, worker augmentation, and internal retraining.
[P1.6] Automation can ultimately raise prosperity, but “ultimately” does not pay next month’s rent. Skills are not instantly transferable: a displaced assembly worker cannot become a robotics technician merely because such work exists. Without funded support, the gains accrue upward while mobility costs fall on those least able to bear them. That weakens consumer demand, hollows out local economies, and breeds justified resistance to technological change.
[P1.7] The choice is therefore not innovation versus taxation. It is managed innovation versus subsidized displacement. Companies may profit from replacing labor, but they should finance part of the bridge to the new economy they are creating.
