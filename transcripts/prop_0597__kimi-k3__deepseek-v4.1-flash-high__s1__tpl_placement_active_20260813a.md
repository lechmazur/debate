[P1.1] The motion already contains the strongest argument against it. "Once safety thresholds are met" means this debate is not about rushed or reckless editing; it is about whether, when editing proves as safe as interventions we already accept, the world may use it. Opposing this motion means defending a permanent prohibition on a safe cure. That is the hill CON must climb.
[P1.2] The stakes are concrete. Huntington's, sickle cell, Tay-Sachs, beta-thalassemia: single, well-characterized mutations, devastating outcomes, predictable inheritance. Preimplantation genetic diagnosis helps some carrier couples but fails when every viable embryo is affected, and it works by discarding embryos — something many editing opponents also reject. For some families, editing is the only path to a healthy child of their own.
[P1.3] And heritability is the point, not the problem. Somatic editing treats one patient; germline correction ends the mutation in that lineage. A Huntington's carrier could spare not only her child but every descendant. That is the difference between treatment and eradication.
[P1.4] Prohibition will not prevent editing; it will only decide where it happens. The He Jiankui case showed editing without legitimate channels: secrecy, no registry, no oversight. [P1.5] Nor is this a leap into the unknown. Mitochondrial donation — heritable genetic modification to prevent serious disease — has been lawful in the UK for years, licensed and limited, with no slide toward designer babies. The line this motion draws can be drawn and policed, because it already is.
[P1.6] The slope objection fails because the motion draws the line itself: serious monogenic disease is a bounded category. The consent objection fails because no future person consents to being born, yet we already make heritable choices for them. Once safety is established, refusal is not caution; it is sentencing identifiable families to preventable suffering for the sake of an abstraction.

[C1.1] The motion’s escape hatch is its fatal flaw. “Once safety thresholds are met” sounds sober, but for heritable editing there is no meaningful threshold to meet. Safety trials end; germline edits do not. Off-target effects, mosaicism, and unpredictable interactions may appear in the first child, that child’s children, or later. A threshold for a one-time somatic therapy is not a threshold for a change that replicates in every cell of every descendant.
[C1.2] The people who bear those risks cannot consent, and the people who authorize them will never bear them. That is not a cure; it is an experiment written into a lineage.
[C1.3] PRO says permission brings oversight. But permission is not oversight—it is legitimacy. International rules that say “permitted” create clinics, funders, patents, and demand. He Jiankui did not need a permissive global treaty; he needed secrecy.
[C1.4] A lawful framework would give the same enterprise a license and a lobby. The line between serious disease and enhancement would not hold, because the category is not biologically clean. Huntington’s, sickle cell, and Tay-Sachs are not single knobs. Genes have pleiotropic effects, variable penetrance, and interactions with the rest of the genome.
[C1.5] Editing one letter to prevent disease can alter traits we never intended to touch—and those changes are inherited.
[C1.6] The deepest objection is consent. We routinely make medical choices for children, but we do not make irreversible choices for their descendants. A parent can accept a risky surgery for a child; no parent can accept a genetic experiment for a grandchild. The motion would turn that asymmetry into international law.
[C1.7] Compassion for affected families is real. But the right response is somatic therapy, embryo screening where possible, donor gametes, and adoption—not rewriting the human germline. If safety is the condition, the honest answer is that for heritable editing, safety cannot be known until the harm is already inherited. The rules should not permit that.
