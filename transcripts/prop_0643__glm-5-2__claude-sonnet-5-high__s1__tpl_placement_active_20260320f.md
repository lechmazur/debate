[P1.1] The case for mandatory worker board representation rests on a structural asymmetry shareholder-only governance cannot fix: workers bear firm-specific risk that shareholders diversify away. A shareholder unhappy with company direction sells and moves on. A worker whose employer makes a catastrophic strategic error loses income, accumulated expertise, pension expectations, and community stability—losses that cannot be hedged. Yet the boardroom, where those consequential decisions get made, gives workers no voice at all.
[P1.2] This is a governance defect that produces materially worse outcomes.
[P1.3] The defect is informational, not just moral. Workers sit closest to production, safety, customer experience, and operational bottlenecks. Boards excluding this knowledge systematically underweight implementation risk and overestimate the feasibility of top-down strategy. Germany's co-determination system, where large companies reserve roughly half of supervisory board seats for worker representatives, demonstrates the model is viable at scale—not utopian, just governance with a broader information base.
[P1.4] The predictable objection is that worker representatives face conflicts of interest or will paralyze boards. But shareholders and executives already have conflicts—over compensation, risk appetite, short-term returns—and we accept that as the cost of representation, managing it through structure and voting rules. Worker conflicts are no different in kind and arguably more manageable, because workers' long-term interest in firm survival aligns them against the most damaging corporate short-termism: sacrificing operational capacity for quarterly numbers.
[P1.5] Mandatory reservation matters precisely because voluntary experiments are rare and fragile. Companies optimize for shareholder control; competitive pressure pushes toward governance concentration, not sharing. A legal floor for worker seats is the cleanest fix for a market failure the market itself will not correct. The question is not whether workers deserve a seat—it is whether boards making billion-dollar decisions can afford to keep ignoring the people who actually know whether those decisions can work.
