[P1.1] Blanket pet bans punish responsible owners for risks they never create. The landlord's real concern is tangible: scratched floors, ruined carpet, complaints, liability. That is a financial risk, and financial risks call for financial tools. Standardized pet damage insurance or an earmarked deposit forces the pet owner to internalize the cost, while letting landlords recover without litigation.
[P1.2] Prohibition is simply overkill when compensation is available.
[P1.3] Bans also impose enormous hidden costs on communities. Pet ownership is normal, not niche — in many buildings a large share of applicants either have or want an animal. When every door says no, families face an impossible choice between housing and a family member. The predictable result is more surrenders to overcrowded shelters, more turnover as tenants keep searching, and more secret pets kept anxiously and destructively because owners fear discovery rather than managing openly.
[P1.4] For elderly tenants, families, and people living alone, that stability is health, not luxury.
[P1.5] This default is better for property owners themselves. An empty unit earns nothing. A ban shrinks the applicant pool, lengthens vacancies, and invites evasion that leaves damage unreported until move-out. Insurance reverses that logic: more eligible renters, faster lease-ups, and a dedicated fund for repairs.
[P1.6] A tenant who paid for coverage has every incentive to train, crate, and supervise, because claims follow them.
[P1.7] Finally, the word default does critical work. It doesn't force any building to accept dangerous behavior; it sets a reasonable presumption that can be rebutted for documented damage, aggression, or code violations. Allergies, noise, and shared spaces can be handled with leash rules, cleaning standards, and breed-neutral behavior requirements. What the default ends is arbitrary, all-or-nothing gatekeeping.
[P1.8] If money can make landlords whole, fairness demands we use money, not banishment.

[C1.1] Insurance can write a check for a chewed door. It cannot un-bark a baby at 2 a.m., un-trigger a neighbor's asthma in a shared hallway, or make a fearful tenant comfortable beside an unleashed dog in the elevator. That is the fatal gap: pets in an apartment are not a private risk between tenant and landlord. They are a shared living condition imposed on everyone behind thin walls and in common corridors.
[C1.2] A deposit may bring the landlord closer to whole after the fact. It does nothing for the night-shift tenant next door who chose that building and loses sleep while complaints accumulate. It does nothing for odor soaked into floors, allergen residue in carpets and ducts, fleas in common areas, or intimidation in a narrow stairwell. Those harms are real, diffuse, and largely uninsurable, and standardized cover will define them out through caps, exclusions, and narrow definitions of damage.
[C1.3] Standardization makes this worse. A chihuahua in a concrete high-rise and two huskies in a thin-walled walk-up are not the same risk, but a standard product treats paperwork as if it could flatten that difference. Set the deposit low and it will not cover subfloor urine damage or professional deodorizing. Set it high and landlords still chase insurers, document claims, fight exclusions, and absorb vacancy during repairs.
[C1.4] Insurance does not remove conflict; it bureaucratizes it.
[C1.5] A default to allow also reverses the burden. Instead of a clear rule before move-in, landlords and neighbors must endure noise, mess, or aggression, document a pattern, dispute whether a threshold is met, and only then act. Removing an established pet is far uglier than a clear no at the door.
[C1.6] Pet-free buildings are not arbitrary gatekeeping. They are legitimate housing diversity: quieter, cleaner, and safer for allergic or fearful tenants, and manageable for small owners. Let pet-friendly buildings compete openly. Do not force every shared home to gamble on a payout after the harm is done.

Babies cry, teenagers practice drums, cooking fills hallways, late shifts slam doors. We do not ban children or cooking; we set quiet hours, ventilation rules, and lease standards for conduct.
[P2.2] Pets belong in that same category: a manageable shared-living issue, not a unique contagion requiring exile.
[P2.3] Allergies, odors, and fear are real, but they argue for targeted rules, not building after building saying no. Leash and elevator requirements, waste and cleaning standards, vaccination and flea prevention, and breed-neutral behavior thresholds handle exactly the hallway and stairwell problems invoked. And genuine housing diversity cuts the other way: keep a truly allergy-sensitive building pet-free if residents need it, but stop letting pet-free be the lazy default everywhere that leaves responsible owners with nowhere to go. Choice means both options actually exist, not a market where bans choke off one side.
[P2.4] Nor does the default force neighbors to endure harm before acting. Every lease already lives or dies on documented behavior. A clear pet addendum — barking limits, waste, aggression as a lease violation — is faster and cleaner to enforce than today's regime: hunting for secret cats, proving an unauthorized animal, then pursuing a full eviction while damage festers unreported. Open pets are visible, accountable, and removable for cause; banned pets are hidden until move-out.
[P2.5] Finally, standardization does not mean pretending a chihuahua equals two huskies. Standardized means reliable minimum coverage, transparent terms, and prompt payout — like auto coverage, which is standardized yet still risk-rated. The fund need not cover every catastrophic subfloor in one check to change behavior; it guarantees first-dollar recovery and ties future cost to claims, which is why owners who pay train, crate, and supervise.
[P2.6] If money plus conduct rules can prevent most harm and repair the rest, a blanket ban is not protection. It is punishment of the many for the misconduct of the few.

[C2.8] Finally, risk-rated insurance is not the rescue offered. Price the huskies honestly and the deposit becomes a ban by wallet; price it flat and it underpays for severe damage. Either way, neighbors get rules and paperwork, not the home they chose.
