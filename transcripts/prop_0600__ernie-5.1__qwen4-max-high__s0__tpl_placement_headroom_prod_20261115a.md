[P1.1] Latin America stands at a historic inflection. Global supply chains are being redrawn, and the United States, its largest trade partner, is actively seeking reliable, nearby production hubs. Prioritising nearshoring‑focused industrial policy is not a luxury; it is a strategic necessity that outweighs the short‑term pain of protectionist tools and higher fiscal outlays.
[P1.2] First, the economic calculus favours upfront investment. Targeted tariffs and subsidies that nurture domestic assemblers of automotive parts, medical devices or electronics generate jobs that pay above the regional average and create multiplier effects across services and logistics. Mexico’s maquiladora boom, once a simple assembly line, now hosts higher‑skill manufacturing because of sustained policy support. Brazil’s recent push to localise battery production for electric vehicles shows how fiscal incentives can seed a nascent industry that would otherwise stay captive to Asian imports.
[P1.3] Second, the fiscal burden is an investment, not a drain. Infrastructure spending, vocational training and R&D grants raise productive capacity, expanding the tax base and reducing dependence on volatile commodity revenues. Historical experience shows that countries which ignored industrial upgrading—Chile’s reliance on copper, Venezuela’s oil mono‑economy—paid a far steeper price in terms of employment and social stability.
[P1.4] Third, controlled protectionism is a pragmatic tool, not an ideological end. Temporary import duties on finished goods paired with export‑oriented incentives keep domestic firms competitive while avoiding the blanket trade wars that harm consumers. The alternative—continuing to export raw materials while importing finished products—locks Latin America into low‑value chains. Moreover, the United States is already offering tax credits and preferential market access to firms that relocate production to allied nations, turning nearshoring into a geopolitical win‑win that reinforces diplomatic ties.
[P1.5] In sum, the gains in sovereign industrial capability, job quality and geopolitical leverage make nearshoring‑centred policy the rational choice, even when it demands measured protection and fiscal commitment.
