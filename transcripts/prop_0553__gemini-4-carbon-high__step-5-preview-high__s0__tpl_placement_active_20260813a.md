[P1.1] Across India, a smartphone or budget laptop is no longer a luxury; it is the infrastructure of daily survival, powering digital payments, gig work, and schooling. Yet when a battery degrades or a charging port fails, manufacturers increasingly trap consumers between two bad choices: pay near-replacement prices at an authorized center, or discard a mostly working machine. A national right-to-repair law requiring access to parts, tools, and documentation breaks this artificial monopoly.
[P1.2] That monopoly hits household budgets and smaller towns hardest. Authorized service centers cluster in major cities, ration spare parts, and routinely push whole-board replacements for minor component faults. Meanwhile, India already possesses a vast network of skilled independent technicians in nearly every town and local market. Giving consumers and independent shops fair access to genuine parts and diagnostic tools injects real competition into the repair market, cutting costs for working families and sparing small-town users long, expensive trips to metro hubs.
[P1.3] Legal access also makes repairs safer while upgrading domestic livelihoods. Manufacturers claim restricting repairs prevents battery fires, botched fixes, and intellectual property theft. That gets Indian reality backward. Price-sensitive consumers already rely on neighborhood technicians every day; manufacturer lockouts simply force those technicians to use unverified gray-market batteries and trial-and-error teardowns without schematics.
[P1.4] Requiring authentic parts, calibration tools, and official manuals eliminates the guesswork that creates safety hazards. Nor does it threaten innovation: a service manual explains how to safely unglue a battery or calibrate a replacement screen, not how to copy proprietary chip designs.
[P1.5] Finally, extending device lifespans confronts India’s surging e-waste problem at its source. Every repairable appliance or phone scrapped over a software-paired part adds toxic waste to overburdened municipal systems and informal recycling yards. Voluntary manufacturer portals cannot reform an industry that profits from premature replacement. Only a binding national law ensures that when Indians buy electronics, they truly own them.

[C1.1] Repair matters, but the motion is not a slogan; it is a national compulsion covering parts, tools, and documents for every consumer electronic. That breadth is the flaw. Before India surrenders proprietary diagnostic channels, it must weigh systemic safety, security, and innovation risks across an enormous, uneven repair economy. As written, the law fails that test.
[C1.2] PRO's own phone example proves the danger. A battery or charging-port replacement is not merely "ungluing" [P1.4]; it demands torque control, thermal protection, ESD-safe benches, and calibrated testing. A manual cannot supply those. A nationwide rule would push thousands of undertrained shops toward lithium cells where one puncture can trigger a fire in a crowded apartment.
[C1.3] The same official diagnostic access needed for repair can expose secure boot, component pairing, or anti-theft functions, creating channels for stolen-part laundering and device tampering. PRO's assurance that manuals are benign ignores that official documentation can encode boot modes, calibration keys, and pairing protocols. The motion contains no scope, certification, or security carve-out.
[C1.4] The e-waste and affordability claims also overreach. A right to buy a part is not a right to a cheap part: genuine components may be legally available yet still expensive, while compliance costs fall hardest on smaller Indian manufacturers. Nor does every extended-life repair reduce waste; repeated patching with gray batteries or mismatched boards can create hazardous failures and push toxic devices into informal recycling. A durable, safe repair market needs accountability, not just unlocked parts.
[C1.5] India deserves better than a binary between manufacturer lockout and compelled disclosure. Enforceable repairability scores, minimum parts-availability windows, certified technician standards, stronger warranty duties, and formal recycling rules protect consumers without forcing security-sensitive firmware or calibration keys into the open. PRO treats a blunt mandate as the only alternative to monopoly. It is not, and its hidden costs are too large.
