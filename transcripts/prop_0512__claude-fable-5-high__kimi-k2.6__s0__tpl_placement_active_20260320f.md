[P1.1] A hyperscale data center is a strange thing to subsidize. It consumes electricity on the scale of a small city, draws millions of gallons of water for cooling, and employs—after construction—perhaps a few dozen permanent staff. Yet states and municipalities routinely shower these facilities with tax abatements worth hundreds of millions of dollars, competing against each other in a bidding war whose winners are the world's wealthiest corporations and whose losers are local ratepayers.
[P1.2] The principle here is simple and conservative: public money should not pay companies to impose costs on the public. When a data center's demand forces a utility to build new generation and transmission, those costs flow into everyone's electricity bills. When it draws down an aquifer in a drought-prone region—as has happened in parts of Arizona and the American Southwest—farmers and households absorb the scarcity. Tax incentives layered on top of these externalities mean residents pay twice: once through forgone revenue, once through higher utility costs and strained resources.
[P1.3] Notice what this proposition does not do. It does not ban data centers, cap their growth, or punish the industry. It says only that the subsidy spigot should be conditioned on internalization—pay the true marginal cost of your grid connection and your water draw, and incentives remain available. That is textbook economics: subsidies are justified by positive spillovers, not as compensation for negative ones.
[P1.4] A firm that cannot operate profitably while covering its own resource costs is not creating value; it is transferring it from communities to shareholders.
[P1.5] The counterfactual matters too. Hyperscalers site facilities based on fiber, latency, land, power availability, and climate. Incentives mostly reshuffle locations between jurisdictions—a zero-sum race to the bottom that capping would help end collectively, the same logic behind global minimum corporate tax efforts.
[P1.6] The burden in this debate falls on anyone defending unconditional subsidies for trillion-dollar firms that shift their infrastructure costs onto neighbors. That position should be hard to hold. I intend to make it harder.
