[P1.1] A stabbing is not a random event. It is the last step in a chain that begins with a teenager deciding, that morning, to slip a blade into his waistband. Everything that follows—the argument outside a chicken shop, the flash of panic, the hospital, the funeral—flows from that one decision. Policy that only arrives after the funeral has already failed.
[P1.2] Preventive policing is the only tool that reaches the decision to carry.
[P1.3] Here is the mechanism, and it is simple. Young men carry knives mostly for perceived protection: "I carry because he carries." That is an equilibrium, and it is self-sustaining until something makes carrying costly. A credible chance of being searched in a known hotspot does exactly that.
[P1.4] The value of stop-and-search was never mainly the blades confiscated; it is the blades left at home because the walk to school now carries a real risk of a pat-down. Nobody carries a weapon that is more likely to get them arrested than to save them.
[P1.5] Expansion, properly understood, means wider authorisation in defined places and periods—a weapons-focused power triggered by intelligence or a recent attack, running for days, logged, filmed, published. That is a scalpel, not a dragnet. Glasgow's celebrated turnaround combined outreach with intense, visible enforcement; the "public health" half only worked because carrying became untenable first.
[P1.6] Weigh the costs honestly. A search is fifteen minutes of inconvenience, sometimes humiliation, and real safeguards can shrink both. A stabbing is irreversible. And the people bearing the stabbings are the same young, often minority men whom critics say searches burden.
[P1.7] Mothers on those estates are not petitioning for fewer police; they are asking who will stop the next boy.
[P1.8] Reactive policing is a choice to wait for the body. Expanded preventive powers, bounded and accountable, are the choice to reach the knife before it reaches a chest. Europe should make that choice.
