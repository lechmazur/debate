[P1.1] Noncompete clauses do not protect innovation; they hold talent hostage. When governments ban noncompetes only for low-wage workers while carving out broad exceptions for managers, high earners, or staff handling confidential information, the reform collapses in practice. Employers routinely stretch vague exceptions to cover ordinary workers, labeling shift supervisors as executives or claiming every salesperson holds vital secrets. Because few employees can afford massive legal fees just to test whether an exception applies to them, broad carve-outs preserve the exact chilling effect the law was meant to end.
[P1.2] Only a bright-line restriction covering nearly all workers—leaving narrow exceptions only for situations like selling a business—actually restores freedom to the labor market.
[P1.3] Employers claim broad exceptions are needed to protect intellectual property, client relationships, and training investments. That argument confuses protecting corporate property with owning human capital. Every legitimate business interest already has a targeted legal shield. Trade secret statutes, nondisclosure agreements, and specific non-solicitation clauses stop a departing engineer or executive from taking source code, formulas, or a client roster.
[P1.4] A noncompete does something fundamentally different and anti-competitive: it forbids a person from earning a living in their chosen field. If an employer invests in training a worker and wants to retain them, the proper market tool is competitive pay, not legal immobilization.
[P1.5] Finally, broad employer exceptions suffocate economic dynamism by locking down the exact workers most likely to drive innovation. When a seasoned researcher, specialized clinician, or software architect is barred from joining a challenger firm or founding a startup, consumers lose better products and wages stagnate. Dynamic industrial hubs flourish where skilled professionals move freely, cross-pollinating know-how and forcing incumbents to compete for talent. Protecting established corporations from competition is no excuse for trapping workers; restricting noncompetes for nearly all employees is essential.

[C1.1] The near-blanket noncompete ban PRO advances is a poorly targeted sledgehammer that confuses the well-documented problem of exploitative boilerplate noncompetes for low-wage, easily replaceable workers with the legitimate, pro-competitive purpose of protecting costly, risky employer investments that no existing legal tool can cover without placing unfair, unworkable proof burdens on wronged employers. Trade secret statutes and nondisclosure agreements only protect explicit, separable proprietary material—written source code, documented formulas, printed client rosters. They cannot police the embedded, uncodified know-how a worker gains when a firm sinks months of paid specialized training, trust-based client relationship building, or pre-launch product development into them.
[C1.2] Non-solicitation clauses cannot stop a senior account manager from retaining clients who choose to follow them based on rapport the employer invested years cultivating, nor can an NDA erase a lead startup engineer’s intimate knowledge of an unannounced product’s unpublicized design flaws, regulatory strategy, and launch timeline. PRO frames broad employer exceptions as an invitation for loopholes, but the solution to vague exception language is clear statutory guardrails within those carveouts, not a near-total ban that strips nearly all protections.
[C1.3] Reasonable noncompetes for covered senior, client-facing, or strategic roles—broad categories that align with legitimate business interests—are limited to short time windows and narrow industry scopes, so they do not bar workers from taking jobs entirely—they only bar immediate exploitation of employer-funded advantage to directly undercut the firm that invested in them. When firms know a rival can poach a fully trained senior team member weeks before a major launch and replicate their work for a fraction of the upfront cost, they will cut entry-level training budgets, slow high-risk, consumer-benefiting innovation, and hoard high-growth opportunities behind rigid internal barriers rather than giving junior workers the clear, supported chance to advance into skilled, well-compensated roles.
