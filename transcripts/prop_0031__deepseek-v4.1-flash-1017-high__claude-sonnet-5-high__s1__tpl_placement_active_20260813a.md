[P1.1] Single-family-only zoning is not a neutral preference for detached houses. It is a legal rule that makes every other home—duplexes, townhomes, small apartments—illegal, even where demand and infrastructure support them. The motion draws a clean line: cities may keep existing single-family neighborhoods, but they may not create new districts where only that form is allowed. Incremental density bonuses fail because they leave the exclusionary baseline untouched and treat inclusion as a discretionary favor.
[P1.2] That difference is decisive. A bonus is optional: a developer must ask, a council must grant, neighbors can organize, and the project can shrink or die. A city can therefore advertise “density bonuses” while approving new subdivisions that remain entirely detached. The exclusion is not incidental; it is the default, and the bonus is a narrow exception.
[P1.3] New housing lasts for generations, and zoning maps are hard to rewrite. Every new single-family-only district is a long-term commitment to exclusion.
[P1.4] Banning new single-family-only districts changes the default without mandating towers. It says that if a city opens new land or writes a new district, it must permit some diverse housing by right—at least duplexes, townhomes, or small multifamily. Existing homeowners keep their neighborhoods; future neighborhoods are not born segregated by income and household type. That is more predictable than bargaining over bonuses, and it prevents the most durable harm: locking in car-dependent sprawl and exclusion exactly when cities need more homes.
[P1.5] The choice is not between gentle density and radical upzoning. It is between an enforceable rule against new exclusion and a promise that exclusion will be softened only when someone asks nicely. Cities should stop minting new single-family-only districts.

[C1.1] The case for a blanket ban rests on a fantasy: that a legal rule, once passed, stays passed and actually changes what gets built. Reality is messier, and that messiness is exactly why incremental density bonuses are the better strategy.
[C1.2] First, durability. Zoning reform that strips local control outright invites immediate backlash—referenda, council turnover, and state-level reversal. We have seen this pattern before: aggressive one-shot upzonings generate organized opposition that incremental reforms don't, because incrementalism lets cities build a track record before asking for more. A ban passed in one election cycle can be gutted in the next, leaving nothing.
[C1.3] A density-bonus program that quietly delivers hundreds of new duplexes and ADUs each year builds political durability precisely because it doesn't trigger the all-or-nothing fight.
[C1.4] Second, loophole substitution. Banning "single-family-only" as a category doesn't abolish exclusion; it just relocates it. Cities that want to keep neighborhoods homogeneous will respond with large minimum lot sizes, strict floor-area ratios, parking minimums, and design review boards that make triplexes legal on paper and impossible in practice. PRO's own framing admits the bonus route "leaves the baseline untouched," but a ban leaves the far more powerful toolkit of covert exclusion untouched too.
[C1.5] Incremental bonuses, by contrast, are easier to pair with the complementary reforms—parking reform, permitting streamlining—that actually unlock units, because they don't require fighting the single big culture-war battle first.
[C1.6] Third, calibration. Infrastructure—water, sewer, schools, roads—varies block by block. A blanket ban treats a dense streetcar suburb and an exurb with a single well and septic system identically. Density bonuses let cities match new housing types to actual capacity, avoiding both NIMBY-driven outright blockage and uncoordinated sprawl into unprepared districts.
[C1.7] The question isn't whether exclusion is bad—PRO is right that it is. The question is which mechanism actually dismantles it over time. A rule that invites repeal and loophole evasion loses to one that keeps winning incrementally.
