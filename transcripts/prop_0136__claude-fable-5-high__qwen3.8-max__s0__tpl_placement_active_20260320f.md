[P1.1] A child in Seoul, Tokyo, or Shanghai costs roughly a quarter of a million dollars to raise — and that's before you count the extra bedroom in a housing market where a modest apartment can consume fifteen years of median income. Against that mountain, a one-off bonus of a few thousand dollars is a rounding error. Parents know it. That is why cash bonuses fail and structural policy is the only serious lever.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.3] First, the math of the decision. Having a child is a twenty-year financial commitment, and the binding constraints are recurring: rent or mortgage, childcare fees, and education costs. A lump sum arrives once and vanishes; it cannot change the answer to "can we afford this every month for two decades?" Affordable housing and subsidized childcare attack exactly the costs that surveys across Korea, Japan, and China consistently identify as the top reasons young couples delay or forgo children.
[P1.4] Second, the track record. South Korea has poured enormous sums into pronatalist spending, much of it in grants and one-time payments, while fertility collapsed to around 0.7 — the lowest ever recorded. Cash has been tried at scale and has demonstrably not worked; structural reform is the road not yet seriously taken.
[P1.5] Third, behavioral economics. Demographers repeatedly find that baby bonuses mostly shift birth timing — couples who already wanted a child have it slightly earlier — without raising completed family size. Structural policies change the underlying feasibility of family life itself, which is what moves cohort fertility.
[P1.6] The motion asks which approach raises birth rates *more*. One side treats children as a purchase to be discounted; the other removes the actual barriers parents name. When the obstacle is a mountain, you don't hand people a coupon — you build a road through it.
