[P1.1] Food-system resilience is not a market externality; it is a public good that global seed and agrochemical oligopolies systematically degrade. When a handful of corporations control the germplasm and chemistry that underpin most of the world’s calories, redundancy disappears. A single supply shock, cyber failure, or profit-driven portfolio cut can ripple across continents within a season. Governments should break up these concentrations because market power on this scale is inherently a structural threat to food security.
[P1.2] The mechanism is biological as much as economic. Dominant firms monetize uniformity: proprietary seed lines engineered for dependency on matching pesticides, and restrictive contracts that lock farmers into narrow genetic pipelines. That uniformity shrinks the genetic toolkit available to breeders and farmers just as climate volatility is rising. Drought in the Sahel or a novel blight in Southeast Asia demands rapid varietal adaptation, yet consolidated R&D funnels resources into a few blockbuster crops optimized for the largest markets, leaving regions and minor crops underserved.
[P1.3] Resilience depends on diversity—diverse germplasm, diverse regional suppliers, diverse research pathways. A consolidated industry suppresses all three because diversified systems are harder to patent and harder to monetize through bundled chemical sales.
[P1.4] Vertical integration amplifies the brittleness. The same conglomerates sell seeds, herbicides, and digital agriculture platforms, raising barriers that crowd out smaller breeders and alternative models. When farmers have no viable off-ramp, the system cannot adapt to sudden input shortages or price spikes. Breakups would separate seed development from chemical manufacturing and data services, forcing competition on performance and local suitability rather than lock-in.
[P1.5] Antitrust action here is preventive infrastructure, not punitive redistribution. Restoring a decentralized market structure creates the competitive conditions necessary for resilience: multiple R&D pipelines, regional supply buffers, and the farmer choice required to pivot when pests, drought, or conflict disrupt global flows. Without that structural diversity, we are gambling the food supply on the stability of a few boardrooms.
