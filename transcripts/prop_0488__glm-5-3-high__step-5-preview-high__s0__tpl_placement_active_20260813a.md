[P1.1] Resilience means a food system that keeps functioning when shocks hit—disease, drought, supply collapse, price spikes. By that standard, concentrated seed and agrochemical markets are not a neutral efficiency; they are a structural vulnerability, and governments should break them up.
[P1.2] Start with the concentration itself. Diverse, regionally adapted plant breeding is a public good that concentrated private firms systematically underprovide.

When one merger fails, when one company's flagship herbicide strategy falters—as Bayer's dicamba litigation and Roundup liabilities painfully demonstrated—the disruption ripples through an entire continent's planting decisions. [C1.1] Food resilience matters, but this motion mistakes corporate scale for the cause of vulnerability. Resilience depends on genetic diversity, adaptive research capacity, regulatory access, and reliable supply chains—not on how many corporate parents own breeding programs.
[C1.2] Concentration does create risks. Fragmentation, however, would not automatically diversify genes or regional adaptation. The 1970 corn blight came from shared Texas male-sterile cytoplasm, not from today's mega-firms; Ireland's famine came from dependence on a narrow potato variety long before the modern seed industry. Break up the companies and you still face the same gene pools, the same trait economics, and the same market pressures toward uniform crops.
[C1.3] You may simply duplicate fixed costs and balkanize the breeding networks that carry germplasm, trials, and resistance monitoring across borders.
[C1.4] Scale is not only pricing power. It funds capacities that shocks require: multilocational trials, seed-quality systems, regulatory dossiers, chemistry pipelines, and rapid distribution. A small breeder can improve one region's line; detecting and answering a new rust, fall armyworm, or herbicide-resistance outbreak across continents requires shared data and reach. A breakup could leave weaker pieces dependent on private buyers or licensing thickets, not a robust public resilience system.
[C1.5] The opportunity cost is severe. Untangling seed lines, chemical registrations, R&D programs, and data takes years while pests and climate stress do not pause. Farmers may face disrupted varieties, lost tools, and higher prices, especially where public distribution is weak. The actual remedies are targeted: public breeding, gene-bank access, open germplasm licenses, faster approval for gene-edited traits, mandatory resistance data-sharing, and enforcement against abusive licensing.
[C1.6] Bayer's liabilities, for example, call for accountability, not demolition of the capacity to respond. Breaking up the dominant firms weakens adaptive capacity while leaving the real bottlenecks intact. Fragmentation is not resilience.

[C2.5] Finally, “decentralized mistakes” is not resilience when the mistake is common-mode. Many small firms can converge on the same short-term fix.
