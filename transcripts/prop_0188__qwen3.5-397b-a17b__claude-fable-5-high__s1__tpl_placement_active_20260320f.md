[P1.1] Public funding demands public accountability, yet the current scientific incentive structure systematically rewards novelty over truth. Researchers face immense pressure to produce statistically significant findings to secure tenure and future grants. This creates a perverse incentive to engage in p-hacking, tweaking models until data yields a publishable result. Consequently, null results vanish into file drawers, distorting the literature and misleading policymakers.
[P1.2] We see this in medicine, where ineffective treatments appear viable because negative trials remain hidden, and in social science, where flashy claims frequently fail to replicate. This is not just inefficiency; it is a betrayal of public trust.
[P1.3] Preregistration stops this gaming by locking in methods and hypotheses before data collection, separating genuine confirmation from exploratory fishing. However, registration alone is insufficient if journals still reject null findings based on outcome bias. That is why result-blind review is essential. If a study is methodologically sound, the outcome should not dictate its value.
[P1.4] A well-executed study finding no effect is scientifically more valuable than a flawed study finding a spurious one.
[P1.5] Making this mandatory for public funds aligns researcher incentives with the public interest. Taxpayers should not fund research designed to fail silently. When negative results are published, other researchers do not waste millions repeating dead ends, accelerating genuine discovery. This policy transforms science from a lottery of significant findings into a cumulative record of evidence.
[P1.6] Crucially, journal commitment to result-blind publishing removes the final barrier to transparency. Without it, researchers will simply avoid preregistration or seek loopholes. The alternative maintains a system where career advancement depends on statistical manipulation rather than rigorous inquiry. We must choose integrity over convenience to ensure public money buys reliable knowledge, not just publishable headlines.
[P1.7] The cost of changing this system is administrative; the cost of maintaining the status quo is the erosion of scientific credibility itself.

[C1.1] Strip away the rhetoric and this motion contains two distinct mandates, each defective. First: compulsory preregistration for every publicly funded study in clinical and social science. Second: journals contractually bound to publish those studies regardless of outcome. The first is a one-size-fits-all rule imposed on fields where it doesn't fit; the second is unworkable and actively corrosive to quality.
[C1.2] Start with scope. Enormous swaths of valuable social science are exploratory by nature: ethnography, qualitative interviews, secondary analysis of administrative data, natural experiments where the "hypothesis" emerges from the event itself. You cannot meaningfully preregister a study of how communities respond to an unforeseen disaster. A mandate forces researchers into one of two pathologies: faking confirmatory structure onto exploratory work—performative preregistration that launders the very fishing PRO fears—or abandoning exploratory research because it's no longer fundable.
[C1.3] Either outcome makes science worse, not cleaner.
[C1.4] Now the journal commitment, which is the motion's fatal clause. Publishing "regardless of outcome" guarantees publication at the moment of registration, before anyone knows whether the study was competently executed. That is moral hazard in its purest form: a guaranteed publication removes the incentive for careful data collection, adequate recruitment, and rigorous analysis. It also drowns the literature.
[C1.5] Funders sponsor tens of thousands of studies; compelling journals—independent entities funders don't control—to absorb all of them converts peer review from a quality filter into a rubber stamp.
[C1.6] Here's the deeper problem: PRO's diagnosis doesn't require PRO's cure. The file-drawer problem is solved by results-reporting requirements—deposit your findings in a public registry, as clinical trial rules already demand. P-hacking is addressed by optional registered-report tracks, which already exist and work precisely because they're voluntary and selective. The genuine fix is enforcement of disclosure, not conscription of journals and bureaucratization of discovery.
[C1.7] Accountability is the goal we share. This motion delivers ritual compliance, gamed registrations, and a flooded literature instead.
