[P1.1] A seat at a selective university is one of the scarcest opportunities a society hands out. Legacy and donor preferences award it for who your parents are and what they can pay. That is indefensible, and universities should sharply limit it.
[P1.2] First, these preferences reward nothing the applicant has done. [P1.3] Picture the first-generation student with the same grades, who worked a job through high school, getting the thin envelope so an inherited claim could be honored. [C1.1] The motion sounds like a clean referendum on fairness, but “sharply limit” is the operative phrase, and it is a blunt instrument aimed at a marginal practice. Legacy and donor preferences are not quotas filling half the class; at most selective campuses they are small, discretionary factors in holistic review. The question is not whether birth should trump achievement—it shouldn’t—but whether a mandated sharp cap improves admissions or merely replaces accountable judgment with a political formula.
[C1.2] Universities have legitimate, competing obligations: enrolling a strong class, sustaining alumni loyalty, raising money for financial aid, honoring donor intent, and preserving institutional independence. A legacy tie-breaker can help a campus retain multi-generational communities; a donor relationship can fund scholarships that open doors for students with no alumni parent. Sharply limiting both treats those goods as radioactive. The likely result is not a windfall for first-generation applicants but lost philanthropic support and a shift toward other discretionary preferences—athletics, geography, legacy-like networks—that are harder to see and harder to justify.
[C1.3] The examples [P1.4] cites prove less than advertised. MIT, Caltech, and the UC system are atypical: extraordinarily wealthy, public, or tech-focused institutions with unusual donor cultures. They do not show that every university can cut alumni and donor ties without cost. And the motion’s own modesty—“sharply limit, not abolish”—cuts against it: if the problem is a heavy thumb, the remedy is transparency, caps, and case-by-case review, not a sharp limit that forbids proportionate judgment.
[C1.4] Finally, legitimacy is not built by pretending money and community do not matter. It is built by disclosing how they matter and bounding their influence. A sharp mandate substitutes symbolic purity for the harder work of calibrating competing goods—and the students who need aid may pay the price.
