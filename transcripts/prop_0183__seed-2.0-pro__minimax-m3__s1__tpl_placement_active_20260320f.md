[P1.1] Digital works rot faster than any medium in human history. Most public websites that existed in 2000 are already permanently gone. This is not just forgotten memes: early pandemic public health bulletins, defunct local newspaper archives, small press poetry collections, disabled community support forums, tens of millions of works that were never archived anywhere else. No museum, no university, no government is saving most of this.
[P1.2] Only nonprofit digital libraries are even trying.
[P1.3] Traditional copyright was built for physical media, where scarcity was the core problem. Digital preservation has the exact opposite problem: if no one makes a verified copy right now, the work vanishes forever. There is no second chance.
[P1.4] Right now, every time these libraries attempt to save at-risk material, they face takedowns, lawsuits, and ruinous liability. They are not selling copies. They are not undercutting working authors. They are holding material that cannot be purchased anywhere, that no rightsholder is even maintaining, that most rightsholders have long forgotten exists.
[P1.5] This is not an abolition of copyright. The exemption would apply only to registered nonprofits operating as libraries, only for non-commercial preservation access, and only for works not currently available through normal commercial retail channels. Nobody is arguing libraries should give away new bestsellers.
[P1.6] You cannot negotiate permission to preserve a work when the rightsholder is dead, dissolved, vanished, or simply never replies. That is the situation for nearly all at-risk digital material right now.
[P1.7] If we reject this exemption, twenty years from now we will look back and realize we allowed most of the cultural record of the early internet age to dissolve forever, because we applied 20th century rules to a problem no one could have imagined when those rules were written. That is the choice before us.

[C1.2] Yes, digital preservation matters. Conceding that immediately: the question is whether carving out a blanket copyright exemption for self-declared nonprofits is the right instrument. It is not, and the PRO case actually makes the strongest argument against itself by admitting how narrow the proposed limits must be in [P1.5]. If the rule only works when carefully fenced by non-commercial use, by registration, by unavailability in retail channels, and by library purpose, then what is being proposed is not a general exemption at all but a new statutory license with restrictions.
[C1.3] We already have those. Library safe harbors, fair use, Section 108, orphan-works schemes, and extended collective licenses in many jurisdictions all exist precisely to handle preservation without nullifying the underlying property right. The problem PRO identifies in [P1.6] - dead, dissolved, non-responsive rightsholders - is what orphan-works regimes were built to address. They route permissions through collecting societies, not by voiding the right.
[C1.4] The deeper issue is portability. A "registered nonprofit carrying out preservation" exemption is a porous border in practice. The Internet Archive's own controlled digital lending program began as a narrow pandemic response for already-purchased library books and then expanded, repeatedly, into lending of contemporary commercial titles until publishers sued and won. That pattern suggests the limits in [P1.5] are aspirational.
[C1.5] Once access to a digital copy is created, controlling its downstream scope against determined users is somewhere between very difficult and impossible.
[C1.6] Finally, the motion asks this debate to treat the cost as abstract cultural loss. It is not. The cost is concrete: authors, small publishers, photographers, and rights organizations whose surviving revenue comes from licensing and controlled reproduction. They have already absorbed decades of uncompensated digital scraping, platform piracy, and AI training ingestion.
[C1.7] Telling them that a final, formal carve-out from enforcement is justified by the urgency of preservation transfers the burden of "heritage" from the public to those who create it. [clipped]
