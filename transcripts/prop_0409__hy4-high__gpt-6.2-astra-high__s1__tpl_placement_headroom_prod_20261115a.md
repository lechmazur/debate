[P1.1] Parks, beaches, and waterfronts are not merely amenities; they are the parts of a city where public life is practiced without a purchase. A free path by the water lets a child see the horizon, an older resident walk safely, a teenager meet friends, and a low-income family rest without first calculating whether the afternoon is affordable. Those uses look small, but together they are the social infrastructure of a shared place. Put a gate around them, and the loss is not just a few acres.
[P1.2] The public is taught that the most beautiful common assets are really private rooms for people who can pay.
[P1.3] The enclosure case will be dressed as care: cleaner lawns, better lighting, curated events, less crowding, new investment. But those goods do not require selling the threshold. A city can tax, maintain, permit cafés, charge for equipment rentals, reserve limited areas for ticketed performances, or require private operators to keep a continuous free promenade. [P1.4] Paid extras may sit inside the commons, but the commons itself may not be converted into the extra.
[P1.5] Paid private experiences also distort the public realm before anyone reaches the turnstile. The city stops planning for neighbors and starts planning for customers; residents who once belonged by being there must now belong by booking, spending, or fitting the brand.
[P1.6] Revenue cannot justify public spaces if they no longer belong to everyone.
