[P1.3] Allow lawful employment early and that same person pays for housing, buys groceries, and pays payroll and sales taxes on the books. This is not speculation about work ethic; it is removing a legal prohibition on self-support. The jobs asylum seekers typically enter — hospitality, construction, food service, care work, warehouses — have persistent entry-level demand and quick onboarding. [P1.4] The fraud objection mistakes a temporary permit for a green light to stay. Authorization within 60 days would not grant asylum, lawful residence, or protection from removal if the claim fails. Someone filing in bad faith still faces interviews, a hearing, scrutiny, and eventual denial and removal — now with a recorded identity, address, and job history that makes evasion harder, not easier.
[P1.5] Nor is four months of earlier lawful work the decisive lure for fraud. The real draws, if any, are the U.S. labor market itself, diaspora networks, and a multi-year backlog before final adjudication — all of which exist whether the permit arrives on day 60 or day 180, and all of which already allow informal work. Shifting work from the shadows to a taxed, documented job does not create a new magnet; it changes whether an already-present applicant is self-reliant or publicly supported while the case is decided.
