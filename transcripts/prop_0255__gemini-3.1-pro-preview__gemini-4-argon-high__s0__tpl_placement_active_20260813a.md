[P1.1] Digital privacy fundamentally hinges on consent. When a private citizen clicks "delete" or sets a profile to "private," they are exercising their explicit right to control their digital footprint. Data brokers who archive and sell these deleted or non-public traces are not providing a legitimate service; they are operating a surveillance economy built on the active subversion of user autonomy. We must prohibit this practice because it strips individuals of the right to define their own identities and exposes them to profound, unconsented harms.
[P1.2] First, the commodification of deleted data destroys the right to rectify mistakes or evolve over time. If a user deletes an ill-advised post, removes a sensitive photo, or clears a search history, they are attempting to correct their digital record. Data brokers circumvent this by scraping and permanently archiving the information before the deletion fully propagates, freezing the user's past in a hidden "shadow profile." This renders the user's delete button functionally meaningless, trapping them in a permanent record they can no longer control, edit, or even see.
[P1.3] Second, trafficking in non-public traces poses an unacceptable risk to personal security. Non-public digital traces often include intimate metadata: location pings, unlisted contacts, and behavioral habits never intended for public consumption. When brokers aggregate and sell this invisible data to the highest bidder—whether to advertisers, private investigators, or undisclosed third parties—they create massive vectors for stalking, predatory profiling, and discrimination. The citizen is entirely defenseless, as they are completely unaware the data was collected, let alone monetized.
[P1.4] Finally, we cannot rely on self-regulation. The data broker industry thrives in the dark. Individuals cannot meaningfully negotiate with or opt out of databases operated by companies they do not know exist. The market actively incentivizes brokers to hoard the most hidden, deleted, and sensitive information precisely because it is rare and therefore valuable.
[P1.5] Because the industry profits directly from violating explicit user intent, an absolute prohibition on archiving and selling deleted or non-public traces is the only mechanism capable of restoring digital self-determination.
