[P1.1] Picture a ten-year-old at 3:15 on a Tuesday. She has spent six hours in a classroom, much of it now on a laptop. She will spend her evening on a phone. The question is what the school offers in between: another screen, or a field, a stage, a paint table, and a playground full of other kids.
[P1.2] We say the school should open the gym before it adds another login.
[P1.3] This motion is about sequencing, not banning. Digital enrichment can wait its turn. Three reasons it should.
[P1.4] First, marginal value. Children are not short on screen time. They are short on movement, face-to-face play, and the unstructured freedom to invent games, argue over rules, and resolve conflicts without an adult or an algorithm. Those experiences build physical health, attention, and social competence, and kids cannot get them from a device.
[P1.5] Another coding module adds a little more of what they already have plenty of. An afternoon of soccer or theater adds what is scarce. When budgets are limited, a school should buy the scarce thing first.
[P1.6] Second, equity. Affluent families already purchase club teams, music lessons, and dance studios. Low-income kids mostly get these through school or not at all. Digital enrichment is the opposite.
[P1.7] Much of it is free or cheap online, and a school-issued device can carry it home. So expanding digital programs first mostly duplicates what is already accessible. Expanding sports, arts, and play gives the students with the least a door that would otherwise stay closed.
[P1.8] Third, the school's unique assets. Schools own gyms, fields, art rooms, and playgrounds that sit locked and empty every afternoon. They have trusted adults and a building parents already send children to. That makes schools unusually well placed to host physical and communal programs, and no better placed than any app store to deliver digital ones.
[P1.9] Expect the other side to say digital is cheaper and scales. It is cheaper precisely because it strips out the human, physical, shared part that made enrichment worth funding. Scale is only a virtue [clipped]

[C1.2] The evening course in music production or engineering is the only enrichment that ever reaches their hands.
[C1.3] That is the hole in the sequencing rule. In-person programs are gated by transportation, caregiving duties, disability, part-time jobs, and parents who can't do a 6 p.m. pickup. Digital enrichment is the one format that follows a child home. So when the other side says physical programs open a door that would otherwise stay closed, ask *for whom*.
[C1.4] For the kid with a ride, yes. For the kid without one, the opposite is true.
[C1.5] The second inversion is the "it's already free online" claim. Free content is not enrichment. What affluent families actually buy is *guided* digital access — tutors, mentored robotics, structured online coursework, someone who checks the work. What a low-income kid gets on an unsupervised device is an algorithm selling him outrage.
[C1.6] The modern divide isn't hardware; it's whether an adult organizes the screen into something that compounds. A school-run digital program is precisely that organizing hand, and pushing it to the back of the queue leaves poor kids with the worst version of the internet and no alternative.
[C1.7] Third, sequencing rules are the wrong instrument. Some districts have three fields and no one who can teach advanced math or Mandarin. A blanket "sports and arts first" tells that district to add a fourth team before it offers a course no local adult can teach. Need is local; the motion hard-codes a national ranking.
[C1.8] Fund what reaches the unreached child. Sometimes that's a soccer ball. Often, it isn't.
