[P1.1] Three arguments carry this debate: a right that only exists when convenient is not a right; the empirical fear driving anonymity is weaker than advertised; and we already settled this question everywhere else in law.
[P1.2] First, the right itself. Every adopted person in most Western jurisdictions has long had pathways to their origins. Donor-conceived adults differ only in the accident of how they were separated from their genetic parents. The interest at stake is not curiosity—it is medical history that becomes life-or-death when a doctor asks about familial cardiac disease or cancer risk, and it is identity information that donor-conceived people themselves, in growing numbers, describe as central to who they are.
[P1.3] A legal regime that says "we knew who your father was, we wrote his name in a file, and we will burn it before you see it" is a regime that has chosen to protect an adult's past promise over another adult's lifelong need. That choice is indefensible once the child is an adult and the state is the one holding the file.
[P1.4] Second, the supply argument is the entire CON case, so weigh it honestly. Jurisdictions that abolished donor anonymity—including Sweden, the Netherlands, the UK, and Victoria in Australia—did not see the collapse of donation services that critics predicted. Supply dipped in some places and recovered; in others it barely moved. Donors adapt, registries and known-donation programs grow, and clinics recruit differently.
[P1.5] And even where supply falls, note what the concession actually is: we are being asked to manufacture children through a system premised on permanently withholding their origins, because otherwise we would manufacture fewer of them. A shortfall in one preferred production method is not a reason to bind a person's identity rights.
[P1.6] Third, the temporal logic of the motion does the work. Anonymity promises the donor something about a third party's entire life. No one can meaningfully promise that. What clinics can promise is that the donor will not bear parental duties—and that promise survives disclosure.

[C1.1] The motion asks the state to break binding promises. When a man donates sperm or a woman donates eggs under an anonymity agreement, they are not making a suggestion—they are entering a contract with specific terms: I will give you the chance to create life, but you will not expose my identity to that life. The donor-conceived adult's interest is real, but it is an interest, not a trump card that overrides the donor's settled expectation of privacy.
[C1.2] Consider the reliance interest. A donor who gave gametes in 1995 under a clinic's explicit promise of anonymity reasonably structured his life around that promise. He may have told no one, built a family assuming separation, or simply valued the boundary between his genetic material and his personal life. Retroactively voiding that promise is a taking of his autonomy, not merely a policy adjustment.
[C1.3] The supply concern is not a scare tactic—it is arithmetic. Donation is an act of generosity that many people will not perform if the terms change after the fact. When Sweden and the UK moved to identifiable donation, donor numbers did drop initially, and clinics had to recruit harder, often shifting toward older, altruistic donors or known donation—changing who gets to become a parent. Every lost donor is a real couple, often LGBTQ+ or facing infertility, who loses their chance at biological children.
[C1.4] Most importantly, the motion's strongest argument—medical necessity—has a narrower solution. Clinics can and do provide updated medical histories, genetic screening information, and health alerts without revealing the donor's name or contact details. The donor-conceived person gets the cardiac history, the cancer risks, the psychiatric family history—everything that matters for their health—while the donor keeps the privacy they were promised.
[C1.5] This is not adoption. Adoption dissolves a parental relationship; donation creates a genetic one without a parental contract. The state should not rewrite the terms of a gift after the gift has been given. We can honor both interests: full medical information for the child, and the privacy that made the child possible.

[C2.1] The claim in [P2.2] that contracts cannot bind third parties mistakes how contract law works. Contracts routinely bind non-signatories—life insurance beneficiaries, trust beneficiaries, and third-party beneficiaries enforce rights they never negotiated. More importantly, the anonymity promise was never a private pact; it was a term embedded in a state-licensed medical procedure. When legislatures revise medical privacy rules, they do not "void" contracts; they change the regulatory baseline all future participants must accept.
[C2.2] The donor knew he was operating inside a state framework, not a lawless zone.
[C2.3] The adoption analogy collapses under inspection. Adoption involves the state severing an existing parent-child bond, creating a new legal parentage. Donation involves no parental relationship at all—only a genetic link and a contractual gift. Sealed adoption records were opened because the state had actively manufactured the separation and thus owed the separated parties a remedy.
[C2.4] Here, the state is being asked to retroactively break a privacy promise made to induce a gift. That is a fundamentally different transaction with a fundamentally different moral hazard.
[C2.5] On medical information [P2.5]: PRO concedes too much by demanding identity when a clinic intermediary could update health data dynamically. Clinics can maintain living registries that push updates to donor-conceived adults when new diagnoses emerge—without ever revealing a name. If the worry is a diagnosis at fifty, the clinic can notify the offspring. What cannot be routed through an intermediary is the demand for a relationship, or at least a confrontation—and that is a psychological interest, not a medical necessity.
[C2.6] Finally, on supply [P2.7]: The motion admits anonymity protects supply, then argues the cost is acceptable. That is a values choice, not a rebuttal. When Sweden's numbers fell, real patients waited longer and some never conceived; when clinics recruited older, altruistic donors to fill gaps, the nature of the donor pool changed. The state should not manufacture fewer children, or manufacture them through broken promises, simply to satisfy an adult's curiosity about a stranger who gave a gift on the condition of anonymity.
