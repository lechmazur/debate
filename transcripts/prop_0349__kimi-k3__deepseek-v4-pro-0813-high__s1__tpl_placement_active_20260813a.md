[P1.1] Millions of people with severe depression, PTSD, or terminal illness exhaust everything modern medicine offers — SSRIs, ECT, years of therapy — and remain sick. For them the status quo is not caution but a slow catastrophe: treatment-resistant depression carries markedly elevated suicide risk, and veterans with PTSD keep dying despite decades of pharmacology. The question is whether governments may open one more supervised door, or must keep it locked until the evidence is perfect.
[P1.2] First, the evidence already justifies access for this narrow population. Psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression and MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD have produced large, durable gains in rigorous trials — remission lasting months after a handful of sessions, not daily maintenance pills. The mechanism is increasingly understood: psychedelics reopen windows of neural plasticity, letting therapy rewrite entrenched trauma and despair. Results strong enough to earn breakthrough-therapy designations should not stay trapped inside trial-enrollment lotteries most patients will never enter.
[P1.3] Second, medicine already accepts this logic. Compassionate-use pathways exist because terminal and refractory patients cannot wait for certainty; we accept informed risk when the alternative is certain decline. A dying ALS patient may try an unproven drug, yet a patient dying of suicidal depression may not try psilocybin under psychiatric supervision. That asymmetry is bureaucratic, not scientific.
[P1.4] Third, regulation is the safety argument, not a concession to it. The motion requires psychosis screening, trained facilitators, controlled dosing, licensed supply. Australia already permits authorized psychiatrists to prescribe psilocybin and MDMA under such controls — a working model, not a thought experiment. The real choice is not therapy versus no therapy; it is supervised therapy versus the underground retreats and street sources desperate patients already use, with no screening, no dosing standards, no emergency protocol.
[P1.5] CON will say: wait for more data. But waiting is not neutral; it has a body count, counted in suicides among people with nothing left to try. When the condition is severe, the alternatives exhausted, and the safeguards real, denial is the reckless position.

[C1.1] PRO's central move is to make denial bear all the risk while regulated access gets all the benefit. That hides the harms. Psychedelic-assisted therapy is not a drug with a known side-effect profile; it is a destabilizing procedure. In severe treatment-resistant depression and PTSD, acute panic, dissociative terror, post-session mood crash, or newly activated suicidal ideation are not rare possibilities—they occur in exactly the population where decompensation is hardest to manage.
[C1.2] The strongest trial results rest on screening out psychosis, mania, high acute suicide risk and unstable cardiac disease. Those exclusions mean the evidence does not transfer to the broader severe group this motion would open.
[C1.3] The phrase "carefully regulated" does the safety work for PRO. But regulation outside trials rarely replicates trial conditions. It can license the molecule and the dose; it cannot easily license the preparation, hour-by-hour containment, integration sessions, or clinician judgment needed to prevent harm. A bad session can produce weeks of flashbacks, insomnia, or persistent perceptual changes, and without mandatory long-term registries, regulators will not see whether they have approved a treatment or exported harm.
[C1.4] The compassionate-use analogy [P1.3] strengthens the case for restraint, not expansion. Compassionate use is an exceptional, monitored bridge for terminal illness when no licensed option exists. The motion instead creates a permanent parallel system for chronic psychiatric illness, with clinics, advertising, and financial incentives to widen eligibility. Australia's authorization may prove that licensing is possible; it does not yet prove that long-term safety is established.
[C1.5] Broad approval outside trials also undermines the trials themselves. Patients who can receive therapy in a clinic have less reason to enter randomized comparisons, so key questions get answered more slowly. Rare harms such as psychosis, cardiovascular events, or persistent perceptual disturbances would be scattered across private offices, hidden from systematic follow-up. The prudent path is rigorous monitored access within trials, not licensing before validation.

A facilitator can recognize acute panic; recognition does not stop post-session suicidality days later. [C2.2] [P2.2] creates an inescapable dilemma. If severe suicidal patients are excluded as in trials, then the policy abandons the very group PRO invokes to make the moral case. If they are included, the evidence no longer transfers. Either the motion fails the people whose deaths supposedly justify it, or it is unsafe for them.
[C2.3] The ECT and ketamine analogy [P2.3] hides the key difference: ECT and anesthesia have objective endpoints, measurable dose-response, and decades of training. Psychedelic-assisted therapy depends on set, setting, and integration—subjective features regulators are least able to standardize. Ketamine clinics already show the realistic drift: rapid expansion, off-label dosing, and uneven monitoring.
[C2.4] PRO also treats mandatory registries as free [P2.4]. They are not. An adverse-event report after a bad session is numerator without denominator; it cannot show causation or whether the treatment beats expanded trial access. Meanwhile, once patients can obtain the therapy outside trials, fewer enroll in controlled comparisons, slowing exactly the validation PRO says we should not wait for.
[C2.5] The real comparator is not only the underground. Governments can expand trial sites, compassionate access, and observational cohorts while still requiring evidence. That is not certainty-first absolutism; it is the ordinary standard for a destabilizing psychiatric procedure before it becomes a permanent commercial pathway.
