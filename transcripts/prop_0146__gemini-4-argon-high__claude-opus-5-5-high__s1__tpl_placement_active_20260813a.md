[P1.1] Traditional privacy law assumes parents act as gatekeepers protecting their children from public exposure. Family vlogging shatters that assumption by turning a child’s private life into a parent’s commercial product. A toddler cannot consent to having a tantrum, a medical issue, or a bedtime routine broadcast to strangers for ad revenue. When that child reaches adolescence—an age when they are forming an independent identity and navigating the social minefield of middle or high school—they should not be permanently bound by a publicity bargain they never had the capacity to make.
[P1.2] This proposition targets names, routines, and embarrassing moments because those categories inflict acute, ongoing harm on teenagers. Publishing a child’s real name alongside their daily routines—such as school habits, bedroom layouts, or athletic schedules—creates a dangerous blueprint for stalkers and parasocial strangers. Meanwhile, archiving embarrassing moments like hygiene accidents, emotional meltdowns, or punishments hands permanent ammunition to peers. An adolescent should not have to walk into a classroom knowing every classmate, and eventually every college admissions officer or employer, can search their name and watch their most vulnerable childhood struggles on repeat.
[P1.3] A stronger legal right to request removal is essential because current law leaves adolescents powerless. Copyright and platform rules heavily favor the account holder: the parent. Because the parent held the camera, the parent owns the footage, creating a profound conflict of interest when that parent also profits from the views. Relying on voluntary family discussion fails in the exact cases where protection is needed most—when parents refuse to delete lucrative videos despite their teenager’s distress.
[P1.4] Granting adolescents a direct legal right to compel removal does not ban home videos or punish ordinary parenting. It simply establishes a vital baseline: a parent’s desire to broadcast the past cannot override a young person’s right to own their future.
