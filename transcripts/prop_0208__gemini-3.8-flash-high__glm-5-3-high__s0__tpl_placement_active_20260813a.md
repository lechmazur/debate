[P1.1] Higher education’s promise of upward mobility is broken by a simple, devastating reality: debt without a degree. Across the country, public universities maintain programs where fewer than half—and often fewer than a third—of enrolled students ever finish. When a degree pathway consistently fails to graduate the majority of its matriculants, it ceases to be an engine of opportunity. It becomes a financial trap.
[P1.2] Students leave after two or three years burdened by non-dischargeable debt, diminished credit, and no credential to show for their labor.
[P1.3] Public universities, sustained by taxpayer subsidies and public trust, have an obligation to fund programs that reliably benefit the students who enroll in them. Shifting resources away from chronic, low-completion degree programs toward shorter vocational and technical pathways directly rectifies this failure. Programs in allied health, cybersecurity, clean energy technology, and advanced manufacturing offer clear, highly structured curricula. Because they take months or two years rather than four or six, they drastically compress tuition costs and forgone earnings.
[P1.4] Crucially, they lead directly to high-demand, family-sustaining careers.
[P1.5] Some will argue that public universities should remain exclusively dedicated to traditional four-year degrees, leaving vocational training to community colleges or private trade schools. But this division has allowed predatory for-profit institutions to exploit the vocational gap, while community colleges often lack the resources to meet regional workforce demand. Public universities possess the regional scale, modern laboratory infrastructure, and institutional credibility to deliver rigorous, stackable technical credentials. Integrating these pathways elevates their status and gives working-class students viable alternatives within the public flagship system.
[P1.6] Higher education policy must be judged by the tangible outcomes of real students, not institutional prestige. Funneling vulnerable students into programs where the statistical norm is dropping out into debt is indefensible. Reallocating funding toward shorter, high-completion technical pathways restores the public university’s core mission: providing accessible, dependable routes to economic security.

Programs with open access—programs serving first-generation students, working parents, students from under-resourced schools—will always show lower graduation rates than selective ones. Cut them, and you haven't fixed anything; you've simply expelled the students the public university exists to serve.
[C1.2] The failure isn't the philosophy major with a 35% completion rate. It's the poverty and under-preparation that walk in the door with those students. The remedy is advising, financial aid, and structured support—not defunding the program and handing the student a wrench.
[C1.3] Second, the "shift resources" framing hides a shell game. Vocational pathways at public universities already exist where demand justifies them—in nursing, engineering technology, agriculture. Where they don't exist, the reason is usually that community colleges already provide them more cheaply and closer to home. Diverting money from academic programs to duplicate that infrastructure means subsidizing universities to do worse what community colleges do better, while gutting the one thing flagships uniquely provide: bachelor's-level education.
[C1.4] Third, look at what actually gets cut. Low-completion lists are dominated by fields like education, social work, and the humanities—careers that are essential, locally anchored, and chronically underpaid, not useless. A state that defunds its teacher-education program because future teachers are often working-class students who take six years to graduate has solved its completion-rate statistic by manufacturing its teacher shortage.
[C1.5] Finally, credentials are not interchangeable. A technical certificate delivers real value—but it caps earnings earlier, narrows mobility, and tracks sixteen-year-olds toward decisions that harden into ceilings. Vocational pathways should be expanded as an addition, funded as their own case. The motion doesn't say "add."
[C1.6] It says shift—take from the students with the least and call it rescue. It isn't. It's triage on the wrong patient.
