[P1.1] Mass graves and atrocity sites are not mere archaeological puzzles. They are the resting places of people—often people whose descendants are still alive, still grieving, still fighting for recognition. When we talk about who controls how these sites are excavated, displayed, and explained, we are talking about who gets to define the meaning of suffering. And that authority belongs, first and foremost, to the communities that inherit it.
[P1.2] Consider what happens when outsiders take charge. Around the world, we have seen mass graves opened without consultation, remains displayed with clinical detachment or sensational framing, victims' stories flattened into abstract numbers. The history of colonial-era anthropology is littered with cases where indigenous remains were excavated, catalogued, and exhibited without consent. This is not ancient history—it is a living pattern of harm that descendant communities are still confronting.
[P1.3] Descendant communities hold something no outside researcher can replicate: living memory, oral tradition, cultural understanding of how death and mourning work, and a personal stake that gives them moral legitimacy to decide what happens to their ancestors. They know which stories matter, what language is respectful, what forms of commemoration honor rather than exploit.
[P1.4] This does not mean rejecting science. It means ensuring that excavation follows ethical standards set by those most affected, that displays center dignity rather than spectacle, and that explanation reflects the community's own understanding—not just an outsider's academic framework. The difference between memorialization that heals and memorialization that reopens wounds often comes down to who holds the pen.
[P1.5] The alternative—outsider control as the default—has proven itself a recipe for erasure and re-victimization. The burden of proof belongs to those who would take this authority away.

[C1.1] Mass graves and atrocity sites are not mere archaeological puzzles; precisely because they matter, they cannot become the property of any living group. They are graves, crime scenes, evidence archives, and public warnings. Grief gives descendant communities a strong claim to be heard. It does not give them substantial control over excavation, display, or public explanation.
[C1.2] “Substantial control” turns a moral relationship into jurisdiction. Excavating a mass grave can decide whether remains are identified, whether perpetrators are prosecuted, and whether missing families get answers. Those tasks require independent forensic standards, transparent custody of evidence, and review by institutions answerable beyond one constituency. A family may rightly request a ritual pause; it should not choose which experts enter, which samples are taken, or which findings are too inconvenient to publish.
[C1.3] The claimed alternative, “outsider control as the default” [P1.5], is a false choice. The real alternative is independent public authority under enforceable duties: notice, consultation, ceremonial participation, access to records, protection from sensational display, and independent oversight with sanctions. Colonial-era abuses teach that unchecked power needs rules and accountability, not that another group should own the story. Relocating primary power to descendant leadership repeats the defect.
[C1.4] Descendant communities are also not single moral persons. Who speaks for the dead when survivors disagree, when diaspora and local families differ, when religion, class, politics, or disputed membership divide the group? At a forced-labor camp, victims may come from several populations; some have no identifiable descendants; others may be claimed by rival national narratives. Control will empower the best-organized faction and silence minority memories within the community itself.
[C1.5] The question, “who holds the pen” [P1.4], is wrong. The pen must be constrained by evidence and shared: forensic reports, survivor testimony, descendant memory, archives, and dissent. Substantial control risks authorized memory—dignified perhaps, but selective and useful to present-day politics. The dead deserve neither spectacle nor ownership; they deserve an account no constituency may rewrite.

The burden remains on those who would keep power out of the hands of those who bear its weight.
