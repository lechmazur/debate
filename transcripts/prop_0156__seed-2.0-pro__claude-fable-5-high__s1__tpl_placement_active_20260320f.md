[P1.1] The eurozone did not just manage a debt crisis after 2010. It turned routine economic stress into a moral blame game, and the political distrust it created has already outlasted any temporary solidarity that kept the currency intact.
[P1.2] First, the response destroyed the baseline assumption that European governments stand with each other in crisis. When Greece, Ireland, and Portugal needed support, loans were tied to austerity cuts that no northern government would ever impose on its own people. Pensions were sliced, public hospitals cut back, youth unemployment peaked above 50% in the worst hit regions. This was never shared sacrifice: all the pain landed on debtor populations, all the security flowed north to protected bondholders.
[P1.3] Second, it broke political trust within every member state too. National politicians told voters they could no longer control their own budgets, that critical decisions were being made behind closed doors in Frankfurt and Brussels. It did not matter that the currency did not break apart. People stopped believing European institutions were neutral, or that they worked for ordinary citizens.
[P1.4] Solidarity is not just that the union did not collapse. Solidarity is the quiet belief that you will be treated fairly when you are weak. That belief was broken permanently. No amount of later recovery funds can erase the fact that when the real test came, the eurozone chose to punish rather than carry one another.
[P1.5] The distrust visible in every European election, every populist surge, every deadlocked negotiation today is the direct legacy of that choice.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to weigh two things: distrust deepened against solidarity preserved. Judged that way, the post-2010 measures were the largest peacetime act of cross-border solidarity in European history, and the evidence shows it held the union together rather than tearing it apart.
[C1.2] Consider what actually occurred. Creditor-country taxpayers — German, Dutch, Finnish, Slovak — put hundreds of billions of euros at risk to lend to countries their own voters had no treaty obligation to save. The EFSF, then the ESM, then the ECB's "whatever it takes" backstop were built from scratch, against fierce domestic opposition in the north and legal challenges in Karlsruhe. Northern governments paid a political price to extend that support.
[C1.3] That is what solidarity under strain looks like: not costless warmth, but help given when refusing was the easy option.
[C1.4] And it worked. Ireland and Portugal exited their programs, returned to markets, and remain governed by emphatically pro-European parties. Greece, even at the depth of the crisis, consistently chose to stay — Greek voters wanted the euro throughout, which is impossible to square with the claim that trust was "broken permanently." Eurozone membership grew during and after the crisis; no one left.
[C1.5] Most decisively, the crisis built the habits of solidarity that PRO says it destroyed. Banking union, the ESM, and ultimately joint EU borrowing for the pandemic recovery fund are direct institutional descendants of 2010-12. When COVID hit, Europe responded with grants and mutualised debt — precisely because the earlier crisis taught it how to act together.
[C1.6] Yes, austerity was painful and trust in institutions dipped. But populism surges in Britain, the US, and non-euro states too; the crisis response cannot carry that blame alone. The eurozone faced an existential test, paid real costs, and emerged more integrated. That is solidarity preserved.
