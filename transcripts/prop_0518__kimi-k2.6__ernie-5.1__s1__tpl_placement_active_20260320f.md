[C1.1] The proposition asks us to ban noncompetes for almost everyone and reject any broad employer carve‑outs. That is the wrong prescription because it discards the very tool that lets firms protect the investments that make modern economies work. Noncompete clauses are not a blanket leash; they are a narrow contract that compensates employers for the cost of training, for the risk of losing trade secrets, and for the capital they pour into specialized knowledge. When governments wipe them out for “nearly all” workers, they also wipe out the incentive for firms to invest in that training.
[C1.2] A small aerospace supplier that spends two years teaching a machinist proprietary tolerance stacks will simply stop hiring apprentices if it cannot recoup that outlay. The result is fewer skilled jobs, not more mobility.
[C1.3] Carve‑outs are not the loophole the opposition fears; they are the precise, market‑driven filter that separates genuine proprietary roles from ordinary labor. A senior engineer who knows a patented alloy formulation, a sales director who holds client relationships built over a decade, or a chef who has created a signature menu are the cases where a noncompete protects real value. Broad employer exceptions let legislators protect these categories without dragging every line‑cook or warehouse clerk into a one‑size‑fits‑all ban. The alternative—universal restriction—creates a perverse incentive: firms will replace noncompetes with even more restrictive confidentiality agreements that are harder to enforce and give workers less transparency about what they can and cannot do.
[C1.4] Moreover, the economic evidence from jurisdictions that have tried blanket bans shows mixed results. States that kept limited carve‑outs for high‑skill workers retained higher rates of R&D spending, while those that eliminated noncompetes entirely saw a dip in startup formation among former employees. The proposition’s “nearly all” language ignores this nuance and forces a choice that harms both employers and the workers who depend on the training those employers provide. The smarter path is to preserve broad, well‑targeted employer exceptions, not to impose a blanket prohibition that chokes investment and innovation.
