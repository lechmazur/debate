[P1.1] Subscription businesses routinely make joining a service simpler than leaving it. Sign-up may require two clicks, while cancellation can be buried behind menus, phone calls, retention offers, or a “are you sure?” maze. This asymmetry is not merely irritating; it converts a voluntary transaction into a trap. Consumers pay recurring money and surrender attention or data, yet firms make exit artificially costly.
[P1.2] Subscription fatigue amplifies the problem: each hidden renewal adds another obligation that households must monitor, remember, and cancel.
[P1.3] The consumer harm is concrete. A customer who believes they have canceled can still face charges, debt collection, or loss of access to already purchased services. Even when cancellation succeeds, friction imposes time and stress on people with the least capacity to spend it. Many firms also use cancellation flows to push replacements, discounts, and additional subscriptions, exploiting the very moment a customer has chosen to leave.
[P1.4] This is especially troubling when the service is financial, health-related, or essential to work.
[P1.5] The proposed rule closes an obvious gap in consumer protection. If cancellation must be as easy as sign-up, firms cannot preserve easy acquisition while shifting the burden of exit onto consumers. The requirement also turns a vague ethical aspiration into an enforceable performance standard: a business should not profit by making unwanted services difficult to stop.
[P1.6] Objections about implementation miss the core principle. A simple standard need not dictate one interface for every company. Businesses may retain menus or phone options, but they must disclose the route clearly, make access comparable, avoid unnecessary obstacles, and ensure cancellation actually ends the charge. Firms can also preserve legitimate customer support and targeted retention while removing deceptive or obstructive steps.
[P1.7] This policy directly targets the mechanism causing the welfare loss. It does not regulate prices, dictate product quality, or prevent legitimate subscriptions. It simply ensures that consent to buy can be followed by an equally effective consent to leave. When firms profit from difficulty rather than customer value, the law should make exit as straightforward as entry.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes asymmetry for harm and symmetry for a remedy. Concealed renewals, false cancellation claims, and charges after a valid request deserve punishment. But “as easy as sign-up” is not a coherent consumer-welfare standard: easy in clicks, time, identity checks, channels, or cost? PRO calls this an enforceable performance standard while leaving every decisive term undefined.
[C1.2] That ambiguity matters because exit is not always the mirror image of entry. Cancelling a shared work account can erase files; ending insurance, security monitoring, or a child’s phone plan can interrupt protection. Cancellation may require establishing authority, explaining consequences, and preserving access already paid for. A rule obsessed with equivalent friction pressures firms to strip safeguards—or simply make sign-up slower so both sides match.
[C1.3] Neither outcome benefits consumers.
[C1.4] Nor would this rule cure subscription fatigue. Fatigue comes from accumulating payments, fragmented services, and the effort of comparing household commitments. Easier exit helps at the margin, but parity does not reduce the number of subscriptions, reveal their total cost, or prevent consumers from signing up again. It treats the interface at departure as though it were the whole market failure.
[C1.5] The compliance design also favors incumbents. Large platforms can build compliant flows, document equivalence, and litigate ambiguous standards. A small software firm or local gym faces the same formal duty with fewer staff. Those costs can mean higher prices, fewer trial offers, or reduced entry, while genuinely deceptive firms simply find new wording around an interface rule.
[C1.6] The better principle is effective, honest exit: clear renewal disclosure, a readily accessible cancellation method, prompt confirmation, and no charges after cancellation, with safeguards proportionate to the service. Enforce those outcomes directly. The motion instead mandates a vague symmetry that can weaken protection, burden competition, and leave real deception to be pursued anyway.
