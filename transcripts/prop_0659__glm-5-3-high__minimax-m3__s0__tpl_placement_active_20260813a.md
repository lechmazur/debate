[P1.1] Emotion-recognition systems claim to read inner states from faces, voices, and posture. That claim collapses under basic science, and it collapses hardest for the people with the least power to refuse: job applicants and workers.
[P1.2] Start with the science. The assumption that a smile means happiness, a furrowed brow means hostility, or a flat tone means disengagement is folk psychology dressed in machine-learning packaging. Decades of psychological research on emotional expression show that facial movements map only loosely, if at all, onto felt emotions; expressions vary enormously across cultures, contexts, and individuals. A system trained on one population's faces will systematically misread another's.
[P1.3] We already know this pattern from facial recognition: error rates that spike for women and people with darker skin. Emotion-recognition inherits that failure mode and adds a new one, because it pretends to detect something invisible—a feeling—rather than merely identify a face.
[P1.4] Now layer on the power imbalance. In hiring, the applicant cannot opt out without losing the job. The candidate with a neurological condition, the interviewee from a culture where animated expressions read differently, the worker masking grief after a loss—each gets scored as "low affect" or "poor fit" with no explanation and no appeal. Inside the workplace, the stakes worsen: continuous monitoring that turns every sigh into a performance metric, chilling speech, organizing, and complaint—the very activities labor law exists to protect.
[P1.5] An emotion score is a permanent, unseeable personnel file entry.
[P1.6] The market will not fix this. Employers face pressure to adopt whatever tool promises filtering efficiency, and vendors have every incentive to market confidence their models cannot support. Only a prohibition realigns incentives. And note what the ban does not touch: employers remain free to use skills tests, structured interviews, work samples, and outcome-based evaluation.
[P1.7] What it removes is one thing: unverifiable inferences about workers' inner lives, made by machines, at scale, with career consequences.
[P1.8] A tool this inaccurate, this biased, and this coercive should not merely be regulated—it should be kept out of the hiring room and off the shop floor.
