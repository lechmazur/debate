[P1.1] Every year, thousands of students graduate with debts they cannot repay because their degrees didn’t lead to sufficient earnings. Universities profit from these loans through tuition, yet bear no cost when programs fail graduates. This one-sided system lets institutions offer low-value degrees while students and taxpayers absorb the defaults. When programs repeatedly produce earnings too low relative to debt, the school that designed, marketed, and profited from that program must share the burden.
[P1.2] The current arrangement creates a moral hazard where universities collect revenue regardless of outcomes. This explains the proliferation of programs with poor return on investment: art therapy master’s degrees with six-figure debt but median salaries below $50,000, or for-profit IT programs that leave graduates with loans they can’t service on entry-level wages. If universities knew they’d cover a significant portion of defaults from programs with a track record of poor outcomes, they’d have a direct incentive to reform or discontinue them.
[P1.3] The for-profit college sector’s history—where institutions aggressively recruited vulnerable students into high-cost programs with dismal job placement, leading to mass defaults—demonstrates how desperately incentives need realignment.
[P1.4] Opponents argue this would reduce access, but the status quo already limits access by saddling students with unaffordable debt. Shared default costs would encourage universities to focus on programs with genuine value, which ultimately expands real access. It’s also a matter of fairness: universities design the curricula, set the prices, and market these programs to students who often lack the information to evaluate their true value. In any other industry, sellers share responsibility for defective products; higher education should be no different.
[P1.5] This isn’t punishment—it’s accountability, ensuring that when students invest in their education, the return justifies the cost, and that taxpayers aren’t left footing the bill for institutional failure. It would also improve the overall quality of higher education by incentivizing universities to prioritize student outcomes over enrollment numbers.
