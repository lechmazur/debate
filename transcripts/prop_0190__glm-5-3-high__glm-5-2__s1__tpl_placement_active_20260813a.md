[P1.1] Insurance began as mutual aid: a community pools its money so that when misfortune strikes one member, the burden doesn't crush them. Actuarial fairness inverts that purpose. It sorts people by their predicted misfortune and charges the soon-to-be-unlucky the most — pricing them toward exclusion precisely when they need the institution most. For essential coverage like health, basic life, and mandatory auto or home protection, this inversion is a moral defect the law should correct.
[P1.2] The core problem is that risk classifications overwhelmingly track factors people did not choose: genes, age, sex, postcode, disability, the illness they were born with. A system that makes the sick pay more for health insurance is not managing risk; it is converting bad luck into debt. Social solidarity means precisely that we do not let the brute facts of birth determine access to essential protection. Unlimited actuarial pricing quietly dismantles that commitment while keeping the vocabulary of fairness.
[P1.3] Notice also that "actuarial fairness" is a misnomer on its own terms. It is fairness as calculated by the insurer, not fairness lived by the insured. What it produces for the vulnerable is the certainty of higher costs and the ever-present threat of becoming uninsurable. If your genes mark you as high-risk, no amount of prudence can save you from the premium.
[P1.4] Choice-based arguments — that risky behavior deserves higher prices — apply, at most, to a narrow slice of classifications, and even there essential coverage argues for moderation, not abandonment.
[P1.5] The remedy is not utopian. Community rating, limits on rating factors, and risk-equalization pools already exist in many systems, and they work: they keep coverage affordable for those the market would otherwise discard, while spreading costs so widely that each individual's burden stays modest. The market's efficiency in risk-sorting is real — and it is exactly the thing we should refuse to buy when the commodity is basic security.
[P1.6] The proposition stands: for essential coverage, the law must put solidarity above the spreadsheet.

[C1.1] Actuarial fairness is not the enemy of social solidarity — it is the mechanism that makes solidarity sustainable. A mutual aid pool that ignores risk cannot survive without either constant external subsidy or silent redistribution that its members never consented to. Risk-based pricing is what keeps the pool solvent, predictable, and honest about what protection actually costs.
[C1.2] Consider what happens when law bans risk-rating for essential coverage. The young and low-risk pay more than their expected cost; many rationally exit or underinsure. The pool skews toward high-risk members, premiums spiral, and the system contracts — hurting exactly the vulnerable people the policy meant to protect. This is not theory: community rating without robust risk-equalization has repeatedly produced premium shocks, insurer exits, and coverage gaps.
[C1.3] The mechanism is simple. If you tell insurers they cannot price risk, they find other ways to manage it — narrower networks, higher deductibles, or simply leaving the market. The poor risk signal doesn't vanish; it migrates into worse coverage for everyone.
[C1.4] The proposition frames actuarial fairness as "charging the unlucky more." But that misdescribes what insurance does. Insurance charges everyone a price calibrated to expected loss so that the actual loss, when it strikes, is covered. Eliminating the price signal doesn't eliminate the loss — it just ensures the system has less money when it arrives.
[C1.5] Social solidarity has two requirements: that everyone can access essential protection, and that the system funding it remains viable. Actuarial pricing serves the second requirement directly. The first is properly addressed through subsidies, public reinsurance, and targeted support — explicit transfers that society debates and funds deliberately. Smuggling redistribution into the premium schedule, by contrast, hides who pays whom, destabilizes markets, and calls the result fairness.
[C1.6] The proposition asks us to limit actuarial fairness by law. But limiting the tool that keeps insurance functional doesn't produce solidarity — it produces fragility dressed in moral language. The right answer is to let risk pricing do its job and handle affordability through transparent, accountable means.
