[P1.1] Treat these sites as they truly are: not deposits of data but ancestral remains, crime scenes, and sacred ground. For descendants, forced-labor barracks and mass graves are not abstract heritage; they are family, unfinished grief, and continuing harm. That is why control cannot rest mainly with distant ministries, universities, or tourism boards.
[P1.2] Start with re-victimization. Atrocity begins by declaring a group less than human; excavating and displaying their relatives without their consent repeats that logic in the name of science. Indigenous repatriation struggles showed the damage when outsiders dug up ancestors as specimens. The corrective is not sentimentalism: it is consent, co-design, and the power to refuse voyeuristic excavation or trophy displays.
[P1.3] Substantial control does not mean shutting down inquiry; it means descendants set the terms under which inquiry is legitimate, as institutional review boards do for living subjects.
[P1.4] Second, descendants make the work better, not narrower. Archaeologists bring method; descendant communities bring oral history, burial custom, and knowledge of who is likely buried where. Ignoring that has real costs: sites have been misdated, misgendered, or destroyed because community memory was treated as folklore. Co-stewardship turns rumor into hypothesis and taboo into protocol, improving accuracy and protecting evidence at the same time.
[P1.5] Third, public meaning needs authorship, not just access. A display that angers the community whose ancestors are in the ground is not neutral; it is another assertion of power over them. Visitor numbers or academic freedom are poor metrics if the narrative denies the community’s lived truth. The right measure is whether the site’s excavation and interpretation repair dignity and advance truth as the descendant community understands it.
[P1.6] So the clash is simple: should the people most bound to these dead and most harmed by these crimes have only a seat at someone else’s table, or real authority? If memory is a matter of justice, the answer is the latter.

[C1.1] Mass graves are not family plots. They are crime scenes, court exhibits, and warnings to humanity. Their primary value is not private closure but public proof: who killed whom, how, on whose orders. That work demands independence from anyone with a stake in the story — including victims’ kin.
[C1.2] The very idea of “the descendant community” collapses under pressure. After mass violence, descent is disputed, diaspora differs from locals, rival families and political entrepreneurs all claim to speak for the dead. Substantial control means handing a veto to whoever organizes loudest or wins government recognition, letting states and factions launder censorship as respect. Consultation welcomes memory; control invites capture.
[C1.3] Worse, the power to refuse excavation and display destroys evidence. Forensic teams often must disturb remains, photograph trauma, take DNA, and show disturbing images to prove genocide and identify perpetrators in court. A grieving family’s understandable wish to leave loved ones undisturbed or to ban graphic display is human — but if that wish becomes authority, bodies stay unidentified, killers go free, and denialists fill the silence. Justice cannot depend on permission from pain.
[C1.4] The same holds for explanation. Dignity is vital, but history that must advance truth “as the descendant community understands it” is not history. Communities, like nations, sometimes need heroic ancestors, not complicated ones — suppressing evidence of collaboration, intra-group killing, or inconvenient perpetrators. Professionals, open archives, and adversarial peer review exist precisely to resist that temptation.
[C1.5] A museum that angers no descendant may simply be flattering myth.
[C1.6] The just model is consultation without veto: seek oral history, respect ritual where possible, hire community liaisons, explain decisions — but leave final authority over digging, preserving, and interpreting with independent scientists, prosecutors, and curators accountable to the public and the record. The dead deserve truth more than they need approved storytelling.
