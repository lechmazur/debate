[P1.1] Total bans on cross-border commercial surrogacy never eliminate demand: they push transactions into unregulated gray markets where the most vulnerable parties bear every risk. Without legal guardrails, agencies routinely recruit low-income women with no independent legal advice, withhold pay if a pregnancy ends in miscarriage, pressure surrogates to undergo dangerous multi-embryo transfers or selective terminations they would not otherwise consent to, and cut corners on prenatal and post-partum care to pad profits. Intended parents face sudden custody battles when local courts refuse to recognize their parental status, leaving children stranded without citizenship or legal guardians for months or years.
[P1.2] This proposition does not endorse an unregulated free-for-all: it allows cross-border commercial surrogacy to remain legal only for agencies and clinics that meet binding, enforceable standards across three non-negotiable domains. Anti-exploitation rules require independent, separate legal representation for surrogates, guaranteed compensation regardless of pregnancy outcome, prohibitions on coercive recruitment of people in extreme economic precarity, and no contractual clauses stripping surrogates of authority over their own medical care. Clear, cross-recognized parentage rules eliminate statelessness for children and end the limbo of contested custody before a pregnancy begins, not after a child is born.
[P1.3] Health-protection standards mandate evidence-based prenatal care, limits on embryo transfers to avoid life-threatening high-risk multiple pregnancies, and coverage for long-term physical and mental health complications from surrogacy.
[P1.4] This is the only position that rejects the false choice between two unacceptable extremes: blanket criminalization that punishes vulnerable people seeking to build families or earn stable, life-changing income, and unregulated commerce that treats surrogates as disposable vessels and children as legal afterthoughts. Enforceable standards turn a system ripe for abuse into one that delivers on its promises for every party.

[C1.1] Cross-border commercial surrogacy should not remain legal under a compliance label. The wrong is not merely bad clinics; distance is the product. A wealthy household buys in one system a pregnancy performed on a poorer woman in another, while brokers, payment, records, and recognition scatter. “Independent advice” cannot equalize that bargain when the agency controls translation, housing, doctors, and papers needed to sue. A ban on recruiting women in “extreme precarity” is either porous or removes the low-cost pool destination markets need.
[C1.2] The proposition treats exploitation as optional defects: withheld pay, multiple embryos, missing aftercare. Remove them and, supposedly, choice remains. But the core is a paid pregnancy in which clients hold interests in medication, miscarriage management, selective termination, delivery timing, and transfer of the child. Health rules can lower risks; they cannot make pregnancy safe, erase pressure to carry to term for a paying family, or provide care years later when the agency is gone and the clinic closes.
[C1.3] “Enforceable” parentage is the fatal gap. A clinic cannot order a foreign court to recognize parenthood, a consulate to grant citizenship, or a home state to ignore its own public policy. Children are stranded because family status and nationality are sovereign decisions, not paperwork defects. Real standards would require sending, destination, and recognition states to harmonize licensing, status, nationality, follow-up care, and remedies—coordination the offshore model exists to evade.
[C1.4] Weak standards launder that risk through licensed agencies.
[C1.5] Persistent demand does not answer that. Prohibition is imperfect, but law need not lend contracts, courts, and respectability to markets built on unequal leverage. Legal status gives brokers enforceable contracts and buyers a state-approved route into poorer jurisdictions; refusal shrinks the market, punishes recruitment, denies enforcement, and makes each link legally toxic. The choice is not elegant rules versus chaos.
[C1.6] It is whether to authorize an industry exporting bodily risk and legal uncertainty to those least able to refuse. It should not.

Regulated programs require advance parentage orders recognized by both sending and receiving jurisdictions before an embryo transfer, exactly the cross-border coordination opponents claim is impossible—coordination that never happens when transactions are pushed underground.
[P2.3] Finally, the claim that bans shrink the market [C1.5] ignores the well-documented pattern that criminalization does not eliminate demand, it only eliminates accountability. A ban does not stop a wealthy intended parent from hiring a broker; it stops that broker from being inspected, sued, or stripped of a license when they cheat a surrogate or abandon a child. The opposite of exploitation is not prohibition—it is rules that actually work.
