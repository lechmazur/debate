[P1.1] Death does not turn a person’s intimate identity into public domain salvage. We already require consent from the deceased or their next of kin before harvesting organs, publishing private letters, or licensing a likeness. Simulating someone’s voice and intimate personality—generating new words in their exact cadence—goes much further. It does not merely preserve a memory; it puppeteers a person who can no longer object.
[P1.2] Requiring consent from the deceased or their estate sets the essential boundary between honoring the dead and hijacking them.
[P1.3] First, unauthorized simulation violates posthumous dignity. Many people find the prospect of an algorithm wearing their voice, hallucinating opinions they never held, or fabricating intimate conversations deeply disturbing. If someone rejects digital resurrection while alive, overriding that wish to soothe the living reduces the dead to emotional property.
[P1.4] Second, a clear gatekeeper prevents devastating abuse by third parties. "The bereaved" are not a single, harmonious actor. Without a consent mandate, nothing stops an obsessive ex-partner, an estranged relative, or a stranger from feeding old voicemails and texts into a commercial platform to build a compliant replica of someone who wanted nothing to do with them.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, the surviving spouse or children must watch their loved one’s intimate identity cloned without recourse. Worse, uploading voice and personality data hands a private company a permanent biometric model. Only the deceased or their legal estate should have the authority to greenlight that risk.
[P1.6] Crucially, this standard is compassionate and workable because it allows consent from the deceased or their estate. When tragedy strikes suddenly without prior directives, the surviving family acting as the estate can still authorize a memorial bot. This requirement never blocks legitimate family healing; it simply ensures that a person’s most intimate traits belong to them and their legal stewards, not to whoever happens to hold an old voicemail and a credit card.

[C1.2] The dead do not sit in the chat and suffer. What can be wronged are the living, and the settled wishes of people who actually recorded them. A spouse replaying a cadence she already knows, or a child finishing a conversation cut off in a hospital room, is not harvesting a corpse. Unlike an organ, that voice is not cut from a body and reassigned.
[C1.3] “Or their estate” is not a compassionate escape. The promise that family can simply authorize collapses the moment love and legal control diverge. An estate is an office for debts and titles, often contested or held by the relative least fit to judge intimacy. The partner with the voicemails may not be the executor.
[C1.4] The executor may be an estranged sibling, a distant heir, or nobody. Silence, the ordinary fact of sudden death, becomes a ban. The rule tracks paperwork, not love or likely wishes.
[C1.5] Abuse is real and answerable without this gate. An obsessive ex, a fraudster, or a firm selling a compliant replica should meet harassment law, fraud liability, and limits on commercial likeness and data retention. A universal consent mandate instead blocks the private mourner who already lawfully holds the recordings, while a determined abuser runs the model off-platform. Estate sign-off does not delete a biometric file.
[C1.6] Purpose limits and deletion rules do.
[C1.7] Consent recorded in life is a worthy option. It is a bad precondition for grief. Access for those who shared the life, plus sharp bans on harassment and commercial exploitation, honors both the dead and the people left behind. A probate lock does neither.
