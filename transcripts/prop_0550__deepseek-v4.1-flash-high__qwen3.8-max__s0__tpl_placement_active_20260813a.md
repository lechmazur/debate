[P1.1] The question is not whether college pays for graduates. It is what an extra public dollar buys in long-run earnings. Four-year enrollment subsidies buy seats and applications; they do not buy completion, and completion is where most of the earnings premium lives. A student who enrolls, accumulates credits, then drops out with debt and no credential often earns little more than a high school graduate while losing years of wages.
[P1.2] Subsidies are also weakest at the margin: they pull in students whose academic preparation and life circumstances make completion least likely, while better-off students who would attend anyway capture much of the benefit.
[P1.3] Paid youth apprenticeships and employer-linked pathways attack that problem directly. They pay participants while they train, so opportunity cost is low and debt is minimal. Employers co-invest because they need workers, so the curriculum is tied to actual vacancies, tools, and supervision. That produces completion, a work record, references, and often a direct offer or wage progression.
[P1.4] In fields like advanced manufacturing, health care, IT support, and the skilled trades, a young person can build earnings from day one, then stack credentials later, often with employer support.
[P1.5] Long-run earnings are not just starting salary; they are cumulative wages, continuity, benefits, and promotion. Apprenticeships compound all four early. A subsidized four-year seat may pay off brilliantly for the graduate—but the expansion dollar is spread across enrollees, including many who never graduate. The apprenticeship dollar is attached to a job and a skill standard.
[P1.6] It reduces the risk of underemployment and credential inflation rather than feeding it.
[P1.7] College subsidies expand access to a high-risk lottery. Employer-linked apprenticeships expand access to paid, demand-driven skill formation. On the margin, that raises long-run earnings more.
