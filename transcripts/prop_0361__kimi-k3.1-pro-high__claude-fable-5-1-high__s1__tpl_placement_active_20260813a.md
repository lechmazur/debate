[P1.1] A meaningful theatrical exclusivity window is not a pledge to keep films off streaming for months regardless of circumstances. It is a commitment to let major movies have a real theatrical run before home availability teaches audiences to wait. That distinction matters: studios should choose a window that fits the film, not sacrifice every film’s long-term value to the next subscriber target.
[P1.2] First, exclusivity protects the revenue and attention that make major movies possible. A blockbuster earns through tickets, premium formats, repeat viewing, and the publicity surrounding a shared release. Those revenues support ambitious production budgets; the theatrical campaign then gives the film an identity when it reaches streaming. A movie that debuts as one thumbnail among thousands has missed its best chance to become an event. *Barbie* and *Oppenheimer* showed how theatrical success can generate conversation far beyond the cinema.
[P1.3] Their eventual home availability did not require erasing the theatrical stage that helped make them cultural phenomena.
[P1.4] Second, rapid streaming is often a weak substitute for sustainable subscriber growth. A major film may produce a brief sign-up spike, but subscribers can watch it and leave. Meanwhile, immediate availability trains habitual cinema customers to postpone purchases, weakening the very releases studios depend on to market their platforms. Trading several durable revenue streams and months of cultural visibility for easily cancelled subscriptions is not necessarily growth; it can be value destruction wearing a growth metric.
[P1.5] Third, theatrical windows preserve consumer choice and creative diversity. CON may invoke affordability and access, but sending films straight to a subscription service does not make entertainment universally affordable—it merely changes who controls access. A window followed by digital rental, purchase, and streaming offers multiple routes over time. The alternative risks leaving studios financially dependent on a few giant-budget, platform-friendly properties, while mid-budget dramas and original films lose the theatrical proof of demand that can sustain careers and future commissions.

[P2.1] The alleged contradiction between “meaningful” and “flexible” is CON’s central move, and it fails. A meaningful window means a real theatrical run, not an identical number of days for every release. Its length can fit the film; zero days is not a shorter window but abandonment of one. The motion opposes treating rapid streaming as the default instrument of subscriber growth, not sensible exceptions.
[P2.2] A presumption with exceptions remains a presumption.
[P2.3] Calling exclusivity a “subsidy to theater chains” assumes exhibitors capture value while studios receive none. But studios receive ticket revenue, premium-screen demand, word of mouth, and publicity that CON simply prices at zero. Splitting a dollar earned is not worse than keeping every cent of a dollar never earned. Nor does direct billing settle the comparison: streaming has acquisition, retention, technology, and marketing costs, while rapid home availability can cannibalize tickets and rentals.
[P2.4] The survivorship-bias objection also misses the claim. *Barbie* and *Oppenheimer* do not prove every film succeeds theatrically; they show what a major film can gain before streaming. A theatrical flop might also flop at home, with less revenue and cultural residue. Citing hit series changes the product: an established series accumulating viewers over episodes is not a test of how to launch a major movie. Streaming can create events; that does not show streaming-first maximizes a film’s value.
[P2.5] CON’s pipeline argument proves too much. If a steady supply of expensive major films is required merely to prevent cancellation, that is a continuing content obligation, not evidence that sacrificing each film’s theatrical returns is profitable. Subscriber counts omit whether newcomers stay, what they cost, and which other revenues disappeared.
[P2.6] Finally, access justifies calibrating windows and improving affordable theatrical options, not erasing every other stage. Streaming-first is not “everyone at once”: it requires a subscription, broadband, and compatible equipment. A bounded delay followed by rental, purchase, and streaming broadens access over time. CON compares temporary inconvenience with hypothetical platform growth while ignoring the value destroyed in between.
