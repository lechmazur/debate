[P1.1] School choice should be judged by whether families are freer and better served, not by a single test-score average—and on that standard, vouchers earn expansion even with mixed evidence on academic gains.
[P1.2] Start with what "mixed" actually means. Large-scale voucher research shows no consistent catastrophe and no consistent miracle. Small gains for some students, no measurable harm for most. If a program costs roughly what we already spend, satisfies parents, and doesn't harm learning, that is not an argument against expansion—it is an argument for it.
[P1.3] We don't halt other public programs, from tutoring to technology initiatives, simply because their test-score effects are mixed. Demanding certainty only from vouchers is a double standard.
[P1.4] Second, academic test scores are not the only output parents value. Families choose schools for safety, discipline, values, special programs, and fit. In cities like Milwaukee and Washington, D.C., where vouchers have operated for decades, parental satisfaction and demand remain persistently high—revealed preference that no regression can erase. When low-income parents line up for vouchers, telling them "the evidence is mixed" while their assigned school fails them is a policy that protects institutions, not children.
[P1.5] Third, the "lost resources" objection is weaker than it sounds. Vouchers are typically funded at a fraction of per-pupil spending—often half or less. When a student leaves with a partial voucher, the public system retains the remainder while serving one fewer student. Per-pupil resources can actually rise.
[P1.6] And competition gives public schools a reason to improve that decades of pure funding increases haven't produced.
[P1.7] Finally, this is about who decides. Wealthy families already have school choice through housing markets. Vouchers extend that same dignity to families who can't buy their way into a good district. Mixed evidence on test scores is a reason to keep studying vouchers—not a reason to deny poor families the exit that rich families take for granted.
[P1.8] Expand the programs. Measure honestly. But don't ration freedom to a bar no public policy has ever met.

[C1.1] The motion does not ask whether some voucher experiments are worth studying; it asks states to expand them while admitting academic evidence is mixed and public systems may lose resources. That is a recipe for scaling an unproven intervention and shifting risk onto the students who can least absorb it.
[C1.2] Mixed evidence is not neutral evidence. Expansion means committing real budgets and disrupting real schools before we know whether benefits persist, whom they reach, or whether early gains fade. Voucher programs can select motivated families and produce gains for some while leaving comparable students behind. If the best case is “no consistent catastrophe,” that is a reason to run careful pilots with transparent evaluation—not to expand statewide.
[C1.3] The fiscal objection cannot be waved away with the claim that vouchers cost “half or less.” Public-school budgets are full of fixed costs: buildings, transportation, special education, debt, and staff contracts. A student leaving does not let a district close a classroom or a bus route proportionally. The remainder may stay in the budget, but the district still must serve the highest-need students left behind. In rural districts, losing even a handful of students can hollow out a school.
[C1.4] And vouchers can cream-skim students whose families can navigate applications and supplement costs, leaving public schools with more concentrated needs and fewer resources.
[C1.5] Parental demand and satisfaction are not enough. Families may value safety, values, or fit—legitimate goals—but vouchers are public money, and the public is entitled to know whether the program improves outcomes and serves all children, including those with disabilities and the hardest-to-serve. The wealthy already have choice; a partial voucher does not create equal choice for a family that cannot cover tuition gaps or transportation. Expanding on mixed evidence is not extending freedom; it is asking taxpayers and public-school students to underwrite an experiment whose costs are certain and whose benefits are not.
[C1.6] States should expand evidence first, not vouchers.

[C2.1] The double-standard argument proves too much. Preschool and class-size reforms may have mixed evidence, but they are public-system changes: budgets, enrollment, and accountability stay inside the same democratic structure, and when they fail, districts can adjust. Vouchers move public money to private providers and create a constituency that makes reversal politically costly. That is exactly why “scale first, evaluate later” is uniquely reckless here.
[C2.2] The fixed-cost answer is not reassuring. “Gradually” is cold comfort to a rural school losing a handful of students; it still cannot shrink a building, a bus route, or a special-education program proportionally. Per-pupil arithmetic can look better while total capacity and services shrink, and the students left behind are disproportionately the ones private schools cannot or will not serve. My opponent treats “money left behind” as a concession; it is only true on average, and it ignores total revenue and the cost of concentrated need.
[C2.3] A district with a management problem can hire a consultant; a district with a voucher-created structural deficit cannot conjure back the students or the revenue.
[C2.4] Cream-skimming “runs backwards” only if you assume the parents most likely to leave are surplus. They are not; they are often the engaged families who make a school work. Telling them to stay as infrastructure is precisely the inequity: a partial voucher selects families who can cover tuition gaps and transportation, not the poorest. Public schools then concentrate need.
[C2.5] And the Milwaukee/D.C. line cannot carry expansion. Long-running programs show demand and satisfaction, but the motion itself concedes academic evidence is mixed. Demand tells us some families prefer exit; it does not tell us the public system, or the children left in it, can absorb the cost. “Modest gains” for some plus high satisfaction is a case for targeted, well-funded pilots—not statewide expansion while public schools lose resources. The side that bears the downside must not be asked to fund an experiment whose upside is unproven.
