[P1.1] Buying a smart device today means buying a ticking clock without being told the time. You can purchase a smart thermostat, security camera, or television whose physical hardware would easily last a decade, only to have the manufacturer quietly halt security updates or shut down essential servers two years later. Overnight, an expensive product becomes either a cybersecurity hazard on your home network or a useless plastic brick.
[P1.2] This happens because of a classic market failure: information asymmetry. Today, support commitments are left deliberately vague or buried in digital terms of service that buyers only see after taking the product home and opening the box. Requiring a guaranteed minimum support lifespan on the physical packaging gives consumers the truth before they pay. We do not let grocers sell perishables without an expiration date.
[P1.3] In a smart device, software is what keeps the hardware alive; hiding when that support expires is deception by omission.
[P1.4] Printing this guarantee directly on the box also transforms industry incentives. When companies can obscure short support windows, they profit from software-driven planned obsolescence—abandoning working hardware to force early upgrades. That dynamic drains consumer wallets, leaves unpatched home devices exposed to hackers, and generates mountains of preventable electronic waste. Yet if a company must print a meager two-year guarantee on a package sitting right beside a competitor promising six years, longevity instantly becomes a visible point of competition.
[P1.5] This rule does not ask manufacturers to predict the impossible. It simply requires a binding minimum floor for software and security, much like a standard hardware warranty. Whether printed as a fixed calendar date or a guaranteed number of years from first activation, a physical label on the box cannot be buried in fine print or quietly altered online after the sale. Consumers deserve to know if they are buying a durable product or a short-term rental before they reach the register.

[C1.1] An expiration date works for milk, not for software support. Milk spoils because time and bacteria act on it; a smart speaker dies when a company decides—or is forced—to stop funding servers, or when an upstream library, chip supplier, or cloud API breaks. A smart doorbell can easily outlive its startup's cloud budget; no honest carton can promise otherwise. That is a contingent business decision, not a physical property knowable at the factory.
[C1.2] Forcing manufacturers to print a guaranteed support lifespan on the box demands a promise about a future they do not control.
[C1.3] The result will not be the longevity competition described in [P1.4]. A printed guarantee is a liability, not a feature. Risk-averse firms will standardize on the safest, shortest number that reputational pressure allows—often the same uneventful “two years” across an entire lineup—because promising six years invites breach claims if a vulnerability or cloud shutdown makes patching impossible. Competition collapses into a lowest-common-denominator label rather than a race to the top.
[C1.4] Nor will the physical box deliver the promised clarity. The rule requires only that the lifespan be displayed; it does not dictate font size, placement, or plain language. A tiny footnote—“minimum security support: 2 yrs from activation; see terms”—is compliance, and the actual coverage will still live online. The claim that such a label “cannot be buried in fine print” [P1.5] ignores how packaging works: cartons are already dense with regulatory icons and asterisks; one more line changes little.
[C1.5] Most damning, the mandate targets the one surface many buyers never see before paying. A large share of smart devices are bought online, where the physical package arrives after the purchase, not before. A dynamic online disclosure or enforceable after-sale duty would inform the checkout decision; a static carton cannot do so. A label that under-promises, misleads, and arrives too late is not consumer protection—it is a compliance sticker.
[C1.6] Reject the mandate.
