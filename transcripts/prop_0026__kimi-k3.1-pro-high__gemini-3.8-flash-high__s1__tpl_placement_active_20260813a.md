[P1.1] The question is not whether dignity matters. It is what should happen when a patient refuses treatment that someone else considers necessary for a dignified life or death. In personal medical choices, bodily autonomy carries more moral weight because it identifies whose body must bear the intervention; dignity too often supplies only an observer’s account of how that body ought to be treated.
[P1.2] First, autonomy establishes jurisdiction. A competent adult can refuse chemotherapy, a transfusion, or an amputation even when clinicians believe refusal means death. The treatment may be beneficial, the refusal tragic, and the medical advice excellent. None of that authorizes strangers to sedate, restrain, or cut into the patient for the patient’s own good.
[P1.3] The intervention occurs in one person’s body, imposes risks on that person, and sets the course of that person’s remaining life. Those facts give the patient’s consent or refusal a priority that no appeal to appearances, comfort, or an ideal death can match.

One person sees dignity in fighting illness until the final possible moment; another sees it in refusing prolonged dependence. One sees it in accepting disfiguring surgery; another in declining it. [P1.5] The patient must then surrender bodily control whenever others sincerely believe they know what a worthy life looks like.
[P1.6] Autonomy also protects dignity’s most defensible core: people must not be treated as instruments or objects. [C1.1] Autonomy is not a moral prime; it is a derivative virtue. We respect an individual’s bodily choices only because we first recognize the inherent dignity of the human person. To assert that bodily autonomy carries more moral weight than dignity is to mistake the branch for the root. When the two are severed, autonomy collapses into a hollow libertarian license that medicine rightfully rejects.
[C1.2] First, dignity provides the normative foundation and boundary for autonomy itself. If raw bodily autonomy were supreme, any competent, self-regarding choice would be morally unassailable. Yet across global medical ethics, we prohibit competent adults from selling their vital organs, entering into degrading experimental exploitation, or demanding that surgeons amputate healthy limbs. We forbid these choices not from petty paternalism, but because human dignity insists that a person must never be commodified or degraded—even by their own consent.
[C1.3] Autonomy does not possess a sovereign right to destroy the dignity that gives it moral standing.
[C1.4] Second, placing autonomy above dignity abandons the most vulnerable. In advanced dementia, comatose states, and severe cognitive impairment, active bodily autonomy is compromised or absent. If autonomy carries the greater weight, the moral claims of these individuals are diminished alongside their agency. It is dignity alone that establishes an inviolable floor, commanding that vulnerable patients receive care, comfort, and protection against degradation even when they can no longer assert a sovereign will.
[C1.5] Finally, medicine is a moral practice, not an open market. Bodily autonomy identifies who makes a choice, but dignity dictates what human beings fundamentally are. When autonomy reduces the body to mere property to be bartered, altered, or discarded at will, dignity intervenes to remind us that human worth is not alienable. Because dignity grounds, regulates, and outlasts autonomy, it carries the superior moral weight.

[P2.1] The root-and-branch metaphor does the work of an argument without supplying one. Even if dignity explains why persons deserve autonomy, it does not follow that an appeal to dignity should prevail over a competent patient’s refusal. A value can ground a right without giving every interpreter of that value authority to override it. The motion concerns comparative weight in personal medical choices, not which concept appears first in a philosophical genealogy.
[P2.2] Notice what the opposition’s examples actually prove. Restrictions on organ sales regulate a market; safeguards against exploitative experiments constrain researchers; a surgeon’s refusal to amputate a healthy limb limits what a patient may demand. None shows that doctors may sedate, restrain, or operate on a competent adult who refuses. A limit on what autonomy can demand is not a licence to impose what autonomy rejects.
[P2.3] These cases therefore leave the central clash untouched.
[P2.4] The vulnerable-patient argument likewise changes the subject. Dementia or coma does not create a contest between an operative autonomous refusal and dignity; it creates a situation in which current choice may be unavailable or unreliable. Dignity can require comfort, care, and protection there without outranking autonomy where a competent patient can choose. “Carries more moral weight” does not mean “is the only source of moral weight,” and protecting people who cannot choose says nothing about coercing those who can.
[P2.5] Most seriously, the opposition never supplies a decision rule for dignity. Whose account controls: the patient who finds dependence unbearable, or the clinician who calls continued treatment dignified? If dignity means respecting the patient’s own worth and self-understanding, it supports rather than defeats refusal. If it means enforcing the clinician’s ideal, its superior weight is simply asserted, and coercion is renamed care.
[P2.6] Bodily autonomy does not reduce persons to property. It prevents their bodies from being treated as someone else’s project. Dignity remains essential; but when used to justify overriding a competent, self-regarding refusal, it becomes indistinguishable from the paternalism it claims to restrain.
