[P1.1] Digital heritage is uniquely, catastrophically fragile. Roughly two-thirds of all web content created before 2010 is already gone: Geocities neighborhoods erased when the platform shut down, local news archives deleted when papers folded, indie games and personal blogs lost when domains lapsed, small-press books and zines discarded when their publishers closed with no succession plan. Physical media can survive centuries in a dry attic; digital files vanish overnight when a server is turned off, a file format becomes unreadable, or a platform purges old data to cut costs.
[P1.2] Traditional copyright enforcement was never built to address this crisis. It is structured to police commercial exploitation of in-commerce works, where enforcing rights puts money in creators’ pockets and incentivizes new work. But applied wholesale to nonprofit digital libraries, it fails to deliver that core benefit. For the vast majority of at-risk digital material, there is no active rightsholder monetizing the work, no licensed copy available for purchase, no existing commercial path for the public to access it at all.
[P1.3] Enforcing copyright here does not compensate a creator. It just imposes an impossible, unmeetable demand: track down every anonymous forum poster, every defunct 90s zine maker, every dissolved small business to secure explicit permission before saving a copy, even when no one will lose a dollar from preservation. Nonprofit digital libraries like the Internet Archive do not compete with commercial media markets. They rescue the unprofitable, abandoned, overlooked material that makes up the vast majority of human digital experience: the fan works, the community oral histories, the amateur tutorials, the niche cultural artifacts no corporation has any incentive to save, because they will never generate meaningful ongoing revenue.
[P1.4] Exempting their preservation work from traditional copyright enforcement does not undermine creators—it stops copyright from becoming a tool of permanent, irreversible cultural erasure.

[C1.1] PRO paints a romantic picture of saving abandoned websites and obscure zines, but that framing obscures the real conflict. True archival preservation — saving digital bits, maintaining dark archives, and migrating dying file formats — rarely triggers legal disputes. The actual conflict arises because nonprofit digital libraries demand something vastly more radical: the unilateral right to engage in unrestricted, global public distribution of copyrighted works under the guise of preservation. Saving vulnerable history does not require abolishing the legal rights of working creators.
[C1.2] Exempting these institutions from traditional copyright enforcement shatters the economic foundation of creative labor. Copyright is not an obstacle to human culture; it is the vital legal architecture that allows living authors, independent publishers, and digital creators to earn a living. When a digital repository unilaterally scans commercial books, mirrors proprietary software, or disseminates creative catalogs without licenses, it directly cannibalizes the primary market for authorized sales and established library lending agreements. Traditional public libraries respect licenses and compensate rightsholders.
[C1.3] Granting digital platforms blanket immunity creates an unaccountable loophole that devalues creative labor and subsidizes mass aggregators at the direct expense of working artists.
[C1.4] Furthermore, an absolute exemption creates a disastrous enforcement vacuum. If nonprofit status and an archival mission confer total legal immunity, any entity can establish a server, brand itself a digital heritage library, and distribute contemporary media worldwide without authorization. We do not need to dismantle copyright to protect fragile digital history. Targeted legal doctrines like fair use and statutory preservation exceptions already permit the conscientious archiving of orphan works and defunct platforms without eviscerating commercial rights.
[C1.5] Stripping creators of enforcement mechanisms does not rescue human heritage; it dismantles the very economic ecosystem that makes creative production viable. We cannot save yesterday’s culture by destroying the livelihoods that produce tomorrow’s.
