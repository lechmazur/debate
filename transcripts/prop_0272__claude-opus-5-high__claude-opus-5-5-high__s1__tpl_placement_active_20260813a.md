[P1.2] And the answer is obvious. The average American child is not suffering from a shortage of screens. They are suffering from a shortage of gyms that stay open, coaches who know their name, a band room with a door unlocked, and an hour of unsupervised, unscheduled play with other kids. Digital enrichment is the one input already saturating their lives, delivered free by phones, YouTube, and the home.
[P1.3] In-person, embodied, adult-supported activity is the scarce good. You expand the scarce thing first.
[P1.4] Second, the after-school window is fundamentally a supervision problem, not a content problem. Between roughly 3 and 6 pm, kids are alone, and that's when risk concentrates — injury, delinquency victimization, loneliness. A licensed coding platform does not notice a seventh-grader stopped showing up. A track coach does.
[P1.5] When a school opens the gym, it is buying custody, mentorship, and belonging in the same transaction. A digital program buys content only, and content was never the bottleneck.
[P1.6] Third, equity cuts hard my way. Affluent families buy club soccer, private violin, travel teams. They do not struggle to get their child an educational app. So when a district funds sports and arts, it is filling precisely the gap the market leaves for poor kids.
[P1.7] When it funds purely digital enrichment, it subsidizes the one resource that is already closest to universal — and with voluntary online programs, the children who persist tend to be the ones with quiet rooms and pushy parents. It widens the gap it claims to close.
[P1.8] Finally, the fields, gyms, and instruments are already purchased and sitting idle. The marginal cost is a stipend for an adult. Digital platforms bring recurring licenses, devices, and refresh cycles.
