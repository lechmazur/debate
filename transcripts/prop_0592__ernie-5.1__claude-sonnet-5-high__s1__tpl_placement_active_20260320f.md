[P1.1] The European Union's foreign-policy sanctions regime is broken not by design flaws in the sanctions themselves, but by a voting rule that lets a single member state hold twenty-six others hostage. Unanimity means one country with cozy energy ties to Moscow or Tehran can freeze the entire bloc's response for weeks, months, or indefinitely. This is not a theoretical risk—it has happened repeatedly. Hungary's repeated obstruction of Russia sanctions packages, Cyprus slowing measures against Iran, and similar holdouts have left the EU looking sluggish while aggressors calculate that time is on their side.
[P1.2] Qualified majority voting fixes this structural paralysis without abandoning democratic legitimacy. QMV already governs the vast majority of EU foreign-policy decisions, including trade measures and enlargement. Extending it to sanctions is a logical, incremental step—not a revolutionary leap. It still requires broad support: roughly fifteen of twenty-seven members representing at least 65 percent of the population.
[P1.3] That is a high bar, far from a rubber stamp.
[P1.4] The core trade-off is clear: speed versus veto comfort. But veto comfort for one reluctant state cannot justify strategic paralysis for the entire union. When Russia invaded Ukraine, the world watched the EU scramble to overcome its own internal blockages while Kyiv burned. Sanctions imposed weeks late are sanctions imposed too late.
[P1.5] Credibility in foreign policy is not built on careful deliberation alone—it is built on the demonstrated capacity to act decisively when aggression demands it.
[P1.6] The EU's founders designed unanimity for a smaller, more homogeneous community. Today the Union faces threats that do not wait for consensus. Replacing unanimity with qualified majority voting is the single most effective reform to make EU sanctions a credible instrument of deterrence rather than a slow-motion gesture.

Aggressor states don't fear a EU that votes fast; they fear a EU that is unified.
