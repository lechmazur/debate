[P1.1] Tenants sign leases based on a promise of a livable home. When that promise is broken by party noise, constant theft, or a building plagued by short‑term‑rental chaos, the cost falls on the renter, not the landlord who chose to stay silent. Disclosure is the minimal, fair correction to an information asymmetry that currently lets landlords profit while tenants bear hidden risk.
[P1.2] Consider a concrete scenario: a young professional signs a lease for a downtown apartment, only to discover that the floor above is an Airbnb that hosts loud gatherings every weekend. Packages vanish from the lobby, and the building’s management does nothing. The tenant either endures sleepless nights or breaks the lease, paying penalties and losing a deposit. That is not a market failure; it is a contract failure caused by withheld facts.
[P1.3] If the landlord had disclosed the pattern of short‑term‑rental complaints and theft, the tenant could have chosen a quieter building or negotiated a lower rent. The market would self‑correct without any government mandate beyond the disclosure requirement.
[P1.4] Disclosure also protects landlords from liability. When tenants later sue for constructive eviction or breach of quiet enjoyment, courts increasingly look at what the landlord knew and failed to reveal. Proactive disclosure converts a potential lawsuit into a simple lease clause, saving legal costs for both parties.
[P1.5] Opponents will claim that disclosure burdens landlords or invades privacy. But the information is already public in many jurisdictions through noise complaints and police reports; the landlord merely aggregates it. The burden is a one‑time statement, not ongoing monitoring. Privacy concerns vanish because the data concern building‑wide patterns, not individual tenant behavior.
[P1.6] Therefore, the core principle is simple: a lease is a contract, and contracts require material facts. Recurring party noise, chronic theft, and short‑term‑rental disruption are material. Requiring disclosure aligns incentives, reduces disputes, and respects tenants’ right to make informed choices. The proposition should be affirmed.
