[P1.1] When unemployment rises, incomes fall before any legislature can agree on a rescue. That delay is the mechanism that turns a shock into a spiral. Households cut spending immediately, businesses lose customers, and more people lose jobs. Automatic cash transfers that expand as unemployment rises interrupt that spiral at the start, without waiting for a vote or a coalition.
[P1.2] The case is not that debt is free. It is that temporary borrowing costs less than an unnecessarily deep downturn. Unemployed people spend a transfer on rent, food, and bills, and that spending keeps other workers employed. Output never produced is lost for good.
[P1.3] Workers who stay attached to the labor market avoid the lasting wage and skill losses that follow long unemployment. A recession that governments refuse to cushion can raise the debt ratio anyway: tax revenue collapses and the economy shrinks. Supporting demand protects people and the economy that has to carry the debt. Cash reaches households in weeks, faster than public works.
[P1.4] Automatic design also answers the fear of permanent looseness. The same rule that expands transfers when unemployment rises should shrink them when it falls. That is discipline with a trigger, not a blank check. Discretionary packages arrive late, pick up unrelated priorities, and are politically hard to unwind.
[P1.5] A pre-committed stabilizer is faster, better targeted, and more likely to recede.
[P1.6] Picture a factory town after a demand shock. If support waits for a special session, families miss rent, shops close, and the next round of layoffs is already underway. If transfers scale with unemployment, those families keep spending and the town rides out the shock instead of amplifying it. Governments should adopt that rule even when it adds to public debt, because the alternative is slower recovery, deeper hardship, and a weaker fiscal position earned the hard way.

[C1.1] Automatic cash that expands whenever unemployment rises is not insurance. It is a pre-committed debt machine keyed to a lagging, noisy number. The motion asks governments to spend first, borrow anyway, and trust that the same trigger will later shut the tap. That is the part that fails.
[C1.2] Unemployment is a poor master switch. It moves after output has already fallen. It also rises for reasons a check cannot fix: an energy shock, a sector that will not return, a skills mismatch, a pandemic that strangles supply. In those cases the transfer does not save a factory town.
[C1.3] It bids up prices, slows necessary reallocation, and still adds to the debt. A rule that cannot tell a demand slump from a supply shock will fire at the worst moments—and the proposition says fire even then.
[C1.4] The promised unwind is a paper promise. Unemployment can stay high long after the original blow. If the trigger remains elevated, “temporary” transfers become a standing program. Legislatures that would have struggled to pass a rescue will struggle even more to admit the formula is wrong.
[C1.5] Expansion is automatic; retreat is political. Interest, however, compounds either way.
[C1.6] Ordinary stabilizers already move with the cycle: income taxes fall, jobless insurance pays people who actually lose work. What this motion adds is extra cash scaled to a headline rate, financed by borrowing, without a vote. That severs the last check on size, duration, and targeting. For high-debt governments, and for any government that does not issue a reserve currency, the rule itself is a signal: deficits will widen on a schedule.
[C1.7] Borrowing costs can jump immediately. Then the supposed cushion becomes the next round of forced cuts.
[C1.8] A town in structural decline does not need a formula that keeps cash flowing until an average moves. It needs room to adjust, and a treasury that still has dry powder when a true emergency arrives. Pre-committing to debt-financed cash whenever unemployment rises buys speed by sacrificing judgment. The bill arrives whether the spiral was real or not.

[P2.1] Unemployment is not a faulty forecast. It is lost paychecks already in motion. By the time the rate rises, households are cutting spending and the spiral has a head start. A transfer keyed to that fact does not pretend to diagnose the first shock.
[P2.2] It stops the second one: the local demand collapse that turns an energy disruption, a sectoral blow, or a mismatch into wider layoffs. Refusing cash does not restore supply or force cleaner reallocation. It empties shops, exhausts credit, and makes the next job harder to reach.
[P2.3] The supposed inflation risk assumes slack has already vanished. When unemployment is rising, it has not. Transfers then replace missing income rather than bidding over a fully employed economy. Where a pure supply squeeze never produces joblessness, the trigger simply does not fire.
[P2.4] The unwind objection reverses the design. Expansion does not require a vote, and neither does retreat: the same formula shrinks payments as unemployment falls. That is more reliable than discretionary packages, which arrive late and then accumulate riders legislators will not repeal. If the rate stays high, the hardship has not ended.
[P2.5] Continuing support is the rule working, not a paper promise failing. Ending it while joblessness remains elevated is how a temporary shock becomes a permanent scar on wages and skills.
[P2.6] Ordinary stabilizers are incomplete. Falling tax receipts do not pay rent. Jobless insurance is partial, delayed, and leaves many workers out. Headline-linked cash closes that gap without waiting for a coalition.
[P2.7] The debt comparison is not borrowing versus a free lunch. It is a visible, reversible rise in debt against a deeper downturn that shrinks the tax base and can worsen the debt ratio anyway. Markets punish governments that let recessions metastasize as readily as those that borrow to contain them. A pre-set rule is a clearer signal than panic legislation.
[P2.8] Speed with an automatic sunset beats judgment that shows up after the town has already amplified the shock.

[C2.3] Refusing extra cash does not restore supply, and sending checks does not either. They do not reopen a closed plant. They can keep a town spending against a future that is not returning, while the debt is booked in full. The motion still expands even then.

Retreat needs no vote, they say, because the formula shrinks as unemployment falls—unless the rate stays high, in which case continuing support is “the rule working.” That is duration, not discipline. [C2.5] Interest does not recede with the formula.
[C2.6] Ordinary jobless pay already follows lost work. Extra cash scaled to a headline rate follows a lagging aggregate and lasts as long as that average stays ugly. That is not better targeting. It is a bigger switch with fewer brakes.
[C2.7] The comparison is not reversible debt versus a deeper hole. A downturn can worsen the ratio; so can years of formula-driven cash after the shock has changed. It is a pre-committed deficit that widens on a noisy number, including where borrowing buys the least recovery. A signal of scheduled deficits is not cheaper than panic.
[C2.8] Speed that cannot refuse a bad trigger is not insurance. It is financing the wrong spiral in advance.
