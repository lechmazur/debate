[P1.2] Start with the marginal student. College enrollment subsidies don't recruit the future engineer who was going anyway. Roughly two in five students who start a four-year degree don't finish within six years, and the non-completion rate is concentrated exactly among the marginal entrants a subsidy pulls in.
[P1.3] Someone with two years of debt, two years of forgone wages, and no credential is often worse off than if they'd never enrolled. The average return to a degree is not the return to the person the policy actually moves.
[P1.4] Second, incidence. Tuition subsidies flow into a supply-constrained sector that can raise sticker prices and absorb them. Institutions capture a real share of any expansion — in administrative growth, in amenities, in list-price creep. Apprenticeship dollars can't be captured that way, because the employer is co-paying and paying a wage.
[P1.5] Employer money on the table is a costly signal that the skill is genuinely in demand; no such signal exists behind an underenrolled humanities seminar filled by voucher.
[P1.6] Third, the earnings mechanism. An apprentice earns from day one, accumulates continuous tenure, and — critically — is embedded in a firm's internal labor market from age eighteen. Long-run earnings are driven less by credential possession than by employer attachment, promotion ladders, and networks. Germany and Switzerland don't have low youth unemployment because their teenagers are unusually clever; they have it because a plumbing, mechatronics, or bank apprenticeship at seventeen is a real ladder, not a consolation prize.
[P1.7] Fourth, the counterfactual. Every dollar is fungible. Compared with subsidizing the fourth year of a degree the student was 80% likely to finish anyway, subsidizing employer-linked pathways reaches young people currently getting nothing — and buys wages, not just seat-time.

[C1.3] Four-year graduates enter managerial, professional, and technical tracks where compensation accelerates through their thirties, forties, and fifties. Sacrificing general human capital for immediate wages caps a worker's lifetime ceiling.
[C1.4] Second, adaptability in a dynamic economy. Employer-linked training is designed around the immediate production needs of a specific sponsor. [C1.5] The greatest asset of a bachelor’s degree is the option value and resilience it provides over four decades of economic transformation.
[C1.6] Third, employer monopsony and portability. Tying early training directly to specific employers restricts labor mobility and depresses bargaining power. A bachelor’s degree is a transparent, nationally portable credential recognized across the entire economy, allowing graduates to capture wage gains by switching firms. Furthermore, apprenticeship availability is pro-cyclical; when recessions hit, private firms slash training slots precisely when young workers need them most.
[C1.7] Expanding four-year college subsidies opens the door to the single most reliable engine of long-term wealth creation. Subsidizing college invests in broad capabilities that outlast any individual firm or technology. For maximizing total lifetime earnings, expanding four-year degree attainment remains the decisive path.
