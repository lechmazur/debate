[P1.1] The decisive issue is not whether a summer evening feels pleasant, but which time regime best protects public health, safety, and social coordination across all seasons. Permanent standard time wins because it is the only stable option that keeps clocks reasonably aligned with the sun.
[P1.2] Twice-yearly clock changes impose a repeated, society-wide disruption comparable to mild jet lag. When clocks shift, people lose or gain an hour, sleep schedules drift, and mornings become temporarily darker or lighter at the wrong moment. The costs appear in familiar forms: groggy commuters, more scheduling errors, and vulnerable groups—children, elderly people, shift workers, and people with sleep disorders—bearing the burden first. Even if each adjustment is modest, it recurs indefinitely and affects everyone at once.
[P1.3] A government should remove a predictable, avoidable risk rather than preserve it for marginal benefits.
[P1.4] Permanent daylight saving time may sound appealing because of brighter summer evenings, but it locks societies into a misalignment that worsens in winter. A permanent summer clock means darker mornings for months. In many countries, children would travel to school before sunrise, and early-shift workers would begin work in darkness. Human circadian systems are not adjusted by preference for after-work light; they respond to morning light and darkness at night.
[P1.5] Standard time, by keeping mornings closer to solar time, gives people better access to the light that stabilizes sleep and alertness.
[P1.6] The conservative, health-protective choice is therefore not a perpetual summer experiment but a single, simple clock: end the switches and keep standard time. It reduces disruption, preserves morning daylight when it matters most, and avoids forcing all public life onto an artificially shifted schedule. For that reason, countries should abolish seasonal clock changes and adopt permanent standard time.
