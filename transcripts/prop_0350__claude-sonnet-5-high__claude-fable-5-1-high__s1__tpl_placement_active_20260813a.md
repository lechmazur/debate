[P1.1] Fifty years of "eat less, move more" campaigns have run alongside a fivefold rise in obesity rates across the industrialized world. If individual behavior campaigns worked, we would expect to see them bend that curve. They haven't, and the reason is structural: you cannot out-educate an environment engineered to defeat willpower at scale.
[P1.2] Consider the actual mechanism of modern obesity. It is not primarily ignorance. Nearly everyone alive today knows sugary drinks and ultra-processed snacks are unhealthy. What they face instead is an environment where the cheapest, most heavily marketed, most convenient calories available at every gas station, school hallway, and phone screen are engineered for overconsumption - hyper-palatable combinations of sugar, fat, and salt, sold in ever-larger portions, advertised specifically to children who have no defense against persuasion.
[P1.3] A campaign telling a parent to "choose healthier snacks" does nothing to change the fact that the healthy option costs three times more and isn't stocked in the corner store that parent actually shops at.
[P1.4] This is why the food environment framing wins on the metric that matters most: population-level effectiveness at reasonable cost. Behavior-change campaigns rely on an individual absorbing a message, retaining it, and then exercising sustained willpower against an environment actively working against them, meal after meal, ad after ad, for decades. That is an enormous behavioral burden placed on people with the least time, money, and resources to resist it - which is exactly why obesity tracks so heavily with poverty.
[P1.5] Environmental interventions - restricting marketing to children, taxing sugary drinks, requiring calorie labeling at the point of purchase, zoning against food deserts - change the default for everyone simultaneously, without requiring anyone to be more disciplined than they already are.
[P1.6] The comparison is not campaigns versus regulation in a vacuum. It's asking people to swim against a current versus turning the current off. Policy that focuses resources on the environment is policy that actually matches the scale of the problem to the scale of the intervention.
