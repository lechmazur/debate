[P1.1] The menu is the restaurant’s price promise. If a mandatory service charge appears only on the bill, the restaurant is presenting an incomplete price and relying on the customer to discover the true cost later. The rule should be straightforward: every mandatory charge used to determine the diner’s final bill must be reflected in the menu price at the point of ordering.
[P1.2] Consider a £30 meal advertised on the menu, followed by a £4.50 service charge and an additional £1.50 “administrative” fee at checkout. The customer cannot make a genuine comparison between restaurants or judge whether the meal fits their budget because the initially advertised prices are not the prices they will actually pay. Including the mandatory charges from the outset converts the menu into a reliable guide to spending.
[P1.3] This does not mean eliminating itemisation. A restaurant may display the food price and the mandatory service charge separately, provided their sum is prominently visible and the advertised menu figure includes both. Nor does it prohibit voluntary tips for exceptional service. The objection applies only to charges the restaurant says are unavoidable.
[P1.4] Mandatory charges also create responsibility without transparency. If the customer believes the menu price is the total but a charge appears later, the restaurant can treat the difference as an ordinary part of the bill. The customer, meanwhile, may feel deceived even if the charge was technically disclosed on a receipt. Upfront incorporation prevents that mismatch by aligning the displayed offer with the amount actually due.
[P1.5] Some operational differences exist, but they do not justify surprise pricing. Restaurants already vary menus by location, date, and occasion; they can publish different prices or visibly list different components. What they should not do is advertise one figure and reveal the mandatory amount only after the customer has committed.
[P1.6] Transparent menu pricing therefore improves budgeting, permits accurate comparisons, reduces checkout disputes, and protects the customer’s trust. It gives the diner control before ordering rather than merely information after payment is due.

[C1.1] PRO frames this as honesty versus surprise, but that is a false choice. The real question is whether restaurants must fold every mandatory service charge into the displayed menu price, or whether clear pre-commitment disclosure is enough. It is the latter. A conspicuous notice at the host stand, a prominent line on a digital ordering screen, or a server’s plain statement before the first order is placed gives the diner the same budget certainty without forcing every menu into a single all-inclusive arithmetic figure.
[C1.2] PRO’s own concession that the components may be shown separately proves the point: the goal is visibility before commitment, not menu-price incorporation.
[C1.3] Mandatory service charges are also frequently conditional rather than universal. A twenty percent fee for parties of six, a weekend surcharge, or a holiday levy cannot be honestly compressed into one menu price without either littering menus with asterisks, tiered totals, and confusing conditional pricing or creating pressure to eliminate the charge altogether. That second outcome would harm the very staff the charge is meant to pay. The checkout line item is not a trick; for a conditional cost, it is often the clearest possible presentation.
[C1.4] Moreover, folding service into menu prices reduces genuine transparency. A diner comparing two restaurants should see which charges more for the food and which imposes a heavier service load. If one hides an eighteen percent surcharge inside the menu price and another itemizes it, the itemizing restaurant is arguably more honest about cost structure. PRO mistakes a pricing convention for a moral imperative.
[C1.5] The customer’s legitimate interest is knowing the full cost before becoming committed to the meal. That interest is fully served by mandatory disclosure at the point of ordering. It does not require a rigid mandate that every menu price be a total inclusive figure. Regulate the timing of disclosure, not the arithmetic of display.
