[P1.1] Buy-now-pay-later is credit with the label filed off. When a shopper who cannot pay $200 today promises to pay it over the next weeks or months, sometimes with interest and always with consequences for missing a payment, that is borrowing. It performs the same function as a credit card — advance purchasing power now, collect later — except BNPL fragments the debt across apps and hides it behind a cheerful checkout button.
[P1.2] That disguise is why treating BNPL as a mere checkout feature is dangerous. Credit-card rules exist because lenders profit by extending credit faster than borrowers can judge it. BNPL faces the same incentive, often with weaker affordability checks, less standardized disclosure of late fees and total cost, and no single statement showing what a person owes in total. A consumer can stack three or four plans for shoes, a phone, and groceries before any one provider sees the full picture, turning supposedly manageable installments into a monthly bill larger than rent money.
[P1.3] Regulating BNPL much more like credit cards would close that blind spot without banning the product. It would mean a real check that repayments are affordable on the customer's income, clear disclosure up front of fees, interest, and what happens after a missed payment, periodic statements, the same right to dispute a faulty purchase that cardholders have, and responsible reporting so debts are visible rather than shadow obligations. If a firm wants to profit from revolving consumer demand at checkout, it should carry the same duties as any other consumer lender.
[P1.4] Saying many plans are short or interest-free does not change the substance. Credit cards also offer grace periods and introductory zero-interest deals, and no one argues they should therefore escape lending law. The risk to the household is identical when the payment comes due. Regulation should follow what a product does, not what marketing calls it.

[C1.1] BNPL and credit cards may both involve deferred payment, but that is where the resemblance ends, and regulation should track risk, not vocabulary. The credit-card regime was built for a specific danger: open-ended revolving lines that let a balance compound indefinitely at high interest, often 20 percent or more, with minimum-payment traps designed to stretch debt for years. BNPL is structurally different — a fixed four-payment plan on a $120 purchase, closed the moment it is paid off, usually with no interest at all.
[C1.2] Forcing the credit-card toolkit onto that product is like requiring a bicycle to meet highway crash-test standards because both are vehicles.
[C1.3] That mismatch has real costs. Credit-card regulation assumes profit from revolving interest, so it builds in underwriting and reporting infrastructure sized for years-long exposure. Importing that wholesale onto a $75, six-week plan means compliance costs that dwarf the transaction itself. Providers will respond by raising minimum purchase thresholds, charging fees to offset underwriting costs, or exiting the lower end of the market entirely — the exact consumers BNPL currently serves because they lack credit history or want to avoid a card's interest trap.
[C1.4] The people pushed out don't disappear; they land on actual credit cards or payday loans, both carrying higher effective costs than the free installment plan they left behind.
[C1.5] None of this means BNPL should be unregulated — it should face rules fitted to what it actually does: capped late fees, clear total-cost disclosure, and limits on stacking multiple plans. Call that "ordinary checkout feature" regulation if you like, but it is still real regulation, just proportionate. The proposition doesn't ask us to choose between full credit-card law and nothing — but PRO's own framing does: it says BNPL should be regulated *much more like* cards. That overshoots the actual risk and will make credit harder to get, not safer to use.
