[P1.1] A machine should never be permitted to select and kill a human being on its own. Killing in war is not a technical calculation; it is a moral and legal judgment about necessity, distinction, and proportionality. Only a human can understand surrender, fear, civilian confusion, or a crowded marketplace. Delegating that judgment to software erases responsibility: when an autonomous system commits a war crime, there is no commander who decided, no operator who pulled the trigger, only code and diffusion of blame.
[P1.2] These systems will also fail predictably and catastrophically. Pattern recognition is brittle. Systems trained to hunt vehicles, launchers, or uniformed figures mistake school buses for transports, farmers with tools for fighters, journalists' cameras for weapons. In signal-jammed, smoky, fast-moving combat, sensors degrade and algorithms guess.
[P1.3] A human pilot can abort at the last second when a child runs into frame; an autonomous drone optimized to engage within seconds will not. The result is not surgical precision but automated error at scale.
[P1.4] Strategically, full autonomy makes war more likely and less controllable. If killing requires no risk to one's own soldiers, leaders face less restraint. If cheap autonomous drones proliferate, militias, cartels, and terrorist groups obtain hunter-killer swarms no arms control can recall. And when two autonomous forces interact at machine speed, misinterpretation triggers escalation faster than diplomats or generals can intervene — a flash war started by glitch, not decision.
[P1.5] A ban on deployment is targeted and workable. It does not outlaw artificial intelligence in surveillance, navigation, cyber defense, or even missile interception with human oversight. It bans one function: selecting and engaging human targets without meaningful human control, much as the world banned blinding lasers, chemical and biological weapons before they became normal. Waiting until autonomous massacres are routine ensures they can never be restrained.
[P1.6] States should prohibit them now.

[C1.3] Every state, including the most cautious, will claim compliance while the systems look identical on the battlefield. A ban that cannot be verified by inspection isn't a ban — it's a diplomatic statement that binds only the states willing to be bound, which are precisely the states least likely to abuse the technology.
[C1.4] That asymmetry is the whole problem. Democracies with legal accountability, oversight committees, and war-crimes tribunals will actually comply. Authoritarian states, insurgencies, and criminal networks will not, because there is no enforcement mechanism — no autonomous-weapons IAEA, no inspectors who can walk into a drone factory. The result of a ban is not fewer autonomous weapons; it's autonomous weapons concentrated in the hands of actors with the least restraint, facing opponents who unilaterally disarmed.
[C1.5] That is strategically dangerous, not stabilizing.
[C1.6] Second, the moral framing in the opening speech assumes human control is reliably better. It isn't. Humans in combat are exhausted, frightened, and vengeful. Mass-casualty events — a platoon massacring a village after losing a comrade, a pilot strafing fleeing civilians in panic — are human failures, not machine ones.
[C1.7] A well-designed automated targeting system, restricted to defensive or narrow discrimination tasks like intercepting a missile or engaging a tank in open terrain, doesn't get angry and doesn't panic. Existing systems like shipboard missile defense already select and engage targets faster than any human can react, and no one proposes banning those.
[C1.8] The honest policy path is not prohibition but calibrated regulation: testing standards, context-limited deployment, human override requirements — flexible tools that a blanket ban cannot deliver.
