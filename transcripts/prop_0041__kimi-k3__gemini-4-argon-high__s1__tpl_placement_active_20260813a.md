[P1.1] Every market rests on one quiet rule: the person beside you pays what you pay for the same loaf of bread. Personalized algorithmic pricing deletes that rule. It reads your browsing history, your device, your ZIP code, your purchase record, computes the maximum you specifically can be squeezed for — and charges exactly that. That is not commerce; it is extraction.
[P1.2] First, the business model targets vulnerability, not value. Willingness to pay is usually a proxy for desperation: the parent buying medicine at midnight, the stranded traveler rebooking a flight, the elderly shopper who cannot comparison-shop. An algorithm trained to maximize willingness to pay is an algorithm trained to find the buyer with the fewest options and charge them most. We ban price gouging in emergencies for exactly this reason.
[P1.3] Personalized pricing is gouging that never ends.
[P1.4] Second, it guts competition itself. Markets discipline sellers only when prices are visible and comparable. When every shopper sees a different number, there is no market price to undercut and nothing to shop against. You cannot bargain with a price you cannot see or a profile you cannot audit.
[P1.5] This is not speculative: Amazon tested charging different customers different prices for the same DVDs and backed down under outrage; retailers have varied online prices by location and device. The practice survives only in the dark.
[P1.6] Third, it launders discrimination. Willingness-to-pay models rebuild protected traits — income, race, age — from correlations. Charging more because a model inferred your demographic is discrimination even if no human typed in the category. Only a ban works; you cannot audit every black box in every checkout.
[P1.7] Note what the motion leaves untouched: surge pricing applied to everyone, sales, posted student discounts. Those are visible, uniform, contestable. We ban only the invisible, individualized number — because a price that exists for you alone is not a price. It is a trap.

No algorithm can force you to pay more than a competitor’s price one click away. Instead, algorithms let retailers lower prices for budget-conscious shoppers without losing margin on affluent buyers who gladly pay full price and never bother comparison-shopping.

Personalized grocery app coupons, cart-abandonment deals, and retention offers when you try to cancel a subscription all rely on algorithms estimating your price sensitivity. My opponent protects student discounts [P1.7], yet student and senior discounts are explicitly based on perceived ability to pay. Extending that same flexibility to a working-class family that lacks a student ID is a lifeline, not a trap.

Targeted laws already prohibit emergency price gouging and protected-class discrimination, and we can regulate essential medicines directly. Furthermore, personalized pricing sharpens competition [P1.4]. Uniform, visible prices make it easy for dominant retailers to tacitly match each other's high sticker prices; personalized discounting lets rivals aggressively undercut the market to poach price-sensitive buyers.
[C1.6] Do not outlaw the mechanism that makes goods affordable for families who need a break.

[C2.2] Personalized pricing operates below that ceiling through targeted promo codes, grocery-app rebates, and retention offers. Rivals see the public ceiling, which caps prices, while private discounts let retailers undercut that ceiling to win budget shoppers.
[C2.3] This also flips their working-mother example [P2.4]. My opponent confuses a physical monopoly with an online checkout. Online, that mother has the same store options as a millionaire. How does a retail algorithm actually measure inelasticity?
[C2.4] By shopping behavior. The affluent buyer who uses one-click checkout on the latest smartphone without comparison-shopping signals high willingness to pay—and pays full list price. The working mother who sorts by "price: low to high," buys generic brands, and leaves items in her cart overnight signals fierce price sensitivity. In retail, wealth creates inelasticity; a tight budget creates hesitation.
[C2.5] Outlawing algorithms that respond to that hesitation forces the working mother to pay more so the affluent one-click shopper can pay less.
[C2.6] Furthermore, demanding visible categories where buyers show proof like a student ID [P2.6] is a cruel standard for working families. A struggling parent should not have to upload a tax return to prove poverty just to get twenty percent off winter coats. Inferring price sensitivity from cart behavior delivers relief without humiliation. Nor does individualized pricing stop enforcement [P2.8]: regulators do not compare consumer screenshots; they subpoena corporate pricing logs and audit the code directly.
[C2.7] Keep the public ceiling, and let working families keep their private discounts.
