[P1.1] Gene-editing therapies, including CRISPR-based cures for sickle cell disease and other severe genetic disorders, can rewrite a patient's future from lifelong suffering to full health. Yet launch prices frequently exceed a million dollars per patient, placing them out of reach for most individuals and for the budgets of even wealthy health systems, let alone poorer ones. Without a required public-interest licensing pathway, high prices turn medical breakthroughs into permanent engines of inequality. Countries should therefore require this pathway so that governments can compel access to essential gene-editing therapies at affordable prices.
[P1.2] Intellectual property properly rewards risk and investment, but monopoly rights stop short of absolute power over essential lives. When a therapy is essential—offering a durable cure for a devastating condition with no close alternative—public need must be able to compel licensing on fair terms. The mechanism is straightforward: a transparent process to designate essential status, attempt voluntary negotiation, then authorize production or importation with reasonable royalties paid. This remains limited, reversible, and non-expropriatory.
[P1.3] History shows the approach works. Compulsory licenses for HIV antiretrovirals and for certain pandemic treatments scaled access dramatically while industry continued investing and innovating. Gene editing rests heavily on publicly funded discoveries; the social contract already includes a return. Absent the pathway, a child with beta-thalassemia in a lower-income country faces preventable death while the patented cure remains unused.
[P1.4] That result is indefensible.
[P1.5] Claims that investment will evaporate ignore the safeguards. Royalties preserve returns, narrow criteria prevent abuse, and expanded volume markets create new revenue. More lives saved means more demand for improved therapies. Equity strengthens rather than starves innovation.
[P1.6] We face a defining choice between exclusive gene editing for the privileged and shared medical progress. Requiring the public-interest licensing pathway makes that choice for the latter. Affirm the proposition.
