[P1.1] A plane ticket has always meant moving a person with the minimum to function for a few days. Not a seat rental for your body while the rest of you is billed separately. Airlines now pretend the seat is the product and you are the add-on.
[P1.2] Charging $40-$70 each way for a normal cabin roller - the bag the overhead bin was literally built to hold - isn't unbundling. It's stripping the baseline to fake a low fare. A family sees $99 each way, then pays $240 extra roundtrip to bring clothes. That is classic drip pricing.
[P1.3] It makes real price comparison impossible and punishes anyone who doesn't treat the fee menu like a full-time job.
[P1.4] It also makes flying worse. When the basic bag is penalized, people try to compress everything into an undersized "personal item," overstuff it until it doesn't fit, argue with gate agents, and get stopped for on-the-spot fees. Boarding slows, bins fill chaotically, last-minute gate checks pile up, flights delay, and flight attendants are turned into baggage police. The airline saves nothing and everyone pays in time and conflict.
[P1.5] There is no cost defense here. Checked bags require ramp crews, conveyors, sorting, liability, real fuel burn. A cabin bag you carry yourself and stow yourself has trivial marginal cost. No light traveler is subsidizing a heavy one when the bin already exists above their head.
[P1.6] Requiring a reasonable baseline - one underseat personal item plus one standard cabin bag, roughly 22x14x9 - does not kill choice or low fares. Airlines can still set their own fares and still charge for checked bags, a second cabin bag, overweight, or priority bin access. It just says the advertised price must include what any normal person understands as travel, and stops the race to the bottom where competition means competing to make the basic product unusable.

[C1.1] Mandating a carry‑on baseline forces airlines to bake a cost they would otherwise leave to the consumer into the headline fare. The immediate result is higher sticker prices that hurt the very travelers the proposition claims to protect. Low‑cost carriers survive by stripping everything that can be unbundled—check‑in, seat selection, even a modest cabin bag—so they can advertise sub‑$50 one‑way fares that open travel to people who could never otherwise fly. If regulators require a “reasonable” bag in every ticket, those carriers must raise the base fare to cover the marginal cost of a bin‑sized roller, erasing the price advantage that makes air travel accessible to students, gig workers, and families on tight budgets.
[C1.2] The proposition also freezes a one‑size‑fits‑all definition of “reasonable.” Travelers differ wildly: a business traveler may need a larger laptop‑friendly case, a backpacker may be fine with a duffle, and a senior may prefer a compact personal item. By legislating a single bag size, we eliminate the ability of airlines to price‑differentiate and let passengers choose the bundle that matches their needs. The market already does this: airlines that charge for a cabin bag attract price‑sensitive flyers; those that include it target convenience‑oriented customers.
[C1.3] Regulation collapses that spectrum into a blunt instrument that benefits neither end.
[C1.4] Furthermore, imposing a baseline creates compliance costs and legal ambiguity. Airlines would need to redesign fare structures, renegotiate contracts with airports, and defend against endless disputes over what qualifies as “reasonable.” Those overheads inevitably flow back to passengers, not to shareholders.
[C1.5] Finally, the consumer‑harm narrative overstates the problem. Drip pricing is already curbed by transparent‑fare rules in many jurisdictions, and competition among carriers—especially in deregulated markets—has already driven many to offer free cabin bags on higher‑fare tickets. The real issue is price transparency, not the existence of a paid‑bag option. A mandatory baseline solves a symptom while raising fares, reducing choice, and adding regulatory drag—exactly the opposite of what travelers need.
