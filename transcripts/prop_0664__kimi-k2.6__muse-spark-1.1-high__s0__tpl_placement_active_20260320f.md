[P1.1] The surest way to prevent a war is to make winning it impossible. Forward-deployed anti-ship and air defense assets in the Western Pacific do exactly that: they raise the military cost of a cross-strait operation to prohibitive levels, confronting Beijing with a credible, immediate, and geographically fixed barrier that remote economic threats simply cannot replicate. A power surveying invasion must see defeat in advance; forward deployment makes that vision unavoidable.
[P1.2] Economic deterrence is fundamentally reactive. Sanctions, trade restrictions, or financial isolation promise pain after the fact, not before it. History shows that states preparing for strategic breakthroughs routinely discount future economic penalties, banking on fait accompli and asymmetric resilience. Beijing has spent years insulating its financial and energy systems precisely to weather such post-invasion costs.
[P1.3] A threat that arrives too late, and that rivals can bargain over or outlast, is not deterrence; it is a delayed consolation prize.
[P1.4] Forward deployment, by contrast, hardens intentions into geography. Missile batteries in the Ryukyus, integrated air defense networks stretching from Japan through the Philippines, and allied sensor coverage turn the Taiwan Strait from a potential highway into a contested kill zone. These weapons are defensive in posture but devastating in effect: they deny an aggressor the sea and air control required for even a minimal invasion window.
[P1.5] Crucially, allied participation—Tokyo, Canberra, Manila deepening interoperability—transforms a bilateral standoff into a multilateral lock. Beijing must then gamble not against ambiguous rhetoric, but against a distributed, manned, and visible wall.
[P1.6] Taiwan’s survival hinges on denial measured in days and weeks, not fiscal quarters. Amphibious assault is among the most complex operations in modern warfare, extraordinarily vulnerable to layered coastal defenses. Economic levers do not sink transports, suppress airfields, or close sea lanes; missiles and interceptors do. Because deterrence collapses the moment an adversary senses a window of military success, only tangible, forward-deployed capability can make that window unmistakably and permanently shut.
