[P1.1] Imagine ten landlords in a city meet weekly, pool their private lease terms, occupancy, and planned concessions, and let one consultant set all their rents, discouraging discounts to keep prices high. No one would doubt that deserves antitrust scrutiny as direct coordination. Replacing the consultant with software and the meeting with a data feed does not change the conduct — it only launders it.
[P1.2] That is how shared rent-setting tools work at their most concerning. Competing landlords feed the same platform non-public, competitively sensitive information — what tenants actually pay, when leases expire, which units sit empty — and in return receive a recommended rent generated from that pooled data. Each user knows rivals are receiving guidance from the same brain, using the same private data, toward the same goal of maximizing revenue for participants as a group. The independent center of decision-making that antitrust protects is gone.
[P1.3] The competitive harm follows predictably. Ordinary competition forces a landlord with vacancies to cut rent or offer a month free to steal a tenant. A shared algorithm can do the opposite: tell everyone to hold price, accept emptiness, and wait, because it sees rivals will hold too. When adoption is dense in an apartment market, renters face not many competing offers but one coordinated price presented many times.
[P1.4] Negotiation disappears, concessions shrink, and rents can stay above what rivalry would produce without anyone ever undercutting.
[P1.5] Treating this as direct coordination does not ban pricing software or punish a landlord using public data alone. It asks the questions antitrust would ask of a smoke-filled room: Did competitors pool private information? Did they delegate pricing to a common agent knowing others did too? Did the system discourage deviation?
[P1.6] If so, efficiency claims should be tested, not taken as a shield. Technology should not create a loophole where the most effective cartel is the one no human has to attend.

[C1.1] The proposition collapses a crucial legal distinction: the difference between conscious parallelism and agreement. Antitrust law has long recognized that competitors may independently respond to market signals without conspiring. When landlords each use software analyzing publicly available rental listings, they are not coordinating prices; they are independently discovering what the market will bear. To treat this "as if" they were coordinating directly erases the requirement of concerted action that anchors competition law.
[C1.2] Algorithms are not common agents unless landlords have actually agreed to follow their recommendations. A tool that suggests a rent based on comparable listings is no different from a landlord checking a rental website or local classifieds. The software processes information; it does not bind anyone to a price. Without evidence of an agreement to adhere to algorithmic outputs—or to exchange non-public, competitively sensitive data—there is no cartel, only parallel conduct in an efficient market.
[C1.3] Moreover, the premise that algorithmic pricing inherently harms competition ignores its pro-competitive potential. Dynamic pricing tools allow landlords to respond rapidly to vacancy rates and demand shifts, potentially lowering rents when supply increases and raising them when demand justifies. They reduce information asymmetries that previously sustained inefficient pricing. Treating all such tools as presumptive conspiracies would chill beneficial innovation in housing markets precisely when transparency and efficiency are most needed.
[C1.4] Antitrust scrutiny should target actual agreements to fix prices or exchanges of private, forward-looking data that facilitate collusion. But a categorical rule that algorithmic rent-setting equals direct price coordination would punish independent, efficient business conduct and stretch antitrust doctrine beyond its foundations. The law distinguishes between the smoke-filled room and the spreadsheet; the proposition asks us to pretend they are the same.
