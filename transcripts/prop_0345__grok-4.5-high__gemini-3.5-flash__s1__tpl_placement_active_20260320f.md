[P1.1] Large employers should have to justify return-to-office mandates with role-specific business reasons rather than broad assertions about culture. When a firm rewrites the working deal for thousands of people after years of remote delivery, the rewrite needs evidence, not slogans.
[P1.2] “Culture” is too elastic. It can mean mentorship, spontaneous collaboration, brand atmosphere, or simply executives’ preference for occupied floors and sunk real-estate costs. Untethered to functions, it becomes a blank check. A trading desk that feeds on live energy is not the same as a software engineer whose best output comes from long uninterrupted focus, or an analyst who can pull the same dashboards from home as from a cubicle.
[P1.3] Treating those roles identically is not cultural care; it is one-size-fits-all convenience.
[P1.4] Role-specific justification forces the actual management work: map coordination intensity, customer access, equipment or security constraints, ramp-time for juniors, and measured effects on delivery for each job family. If in-person time lifts sales conversion or shortens engineering onboarding, show the pattern and set days accordingly. If the data support two office days, require two—not five. Vague culture talk collapses under that demand, and that discipline is the point.
[P1.5] The alternative leaves power unchecked. Remote work already demonstrated that many knowledge roles can meet or exceed prior results without daily presence. When large employers ignore that record and fall back on atmosphere, they erode trust, accelerate exits among high performers who value autonomy, and shrink the talent pool by geography. Requiring role-specific reasons does not outlaw offices.
[P1.6] It simply insists that the company explain why this role, on this team, needs this schedule. Strong reasons survive. Weak ones stop hiding behind the word “culture.” That is the accountability large employers should face.

[C1.1] An organization is not a mere assembly line of isolated tasks; it is an interdependent ecosystem. By demanding that large employers justify return-to-office policies with hyper-specific, role-by-role data, the opposition attempts to atomize the modern workplace. This micro-analytical approach is not only administratively paralyzing, but it also fundamentally misdiagnoses how organizational success is built and sustained.
[C1.2] First, organizational culture is not a mere slogan—it is the vital infrastructure of cross-functional collaboration. Innovation, mentorship, and strategic alignment do not happen within isolated job descriptions. They happen in the spaces *between* roles. When an experienced engineer eats lunch with a junior product manager, or a sales representative overhears a compliance discussion, the organization builds tacit knowledge that no role-specific metric can ever capture.
[C1.3] If we restrict office presence to roles that can "prove" a transactional need for a desk, we silo our organizations, kill spontaneous cross-pollination, and starve junior employees of organic mentorship.
[C1.4] Second, the opposition’s model is a recipe for deep resentment and internal division. If a company declares that software developers can stay home because their code output is easily quantified, but HR staff or marketing specialists must commute because their roles are deemed more "collaborative," it creates an artificial, role-based caste system. This destroys the very cohesion, trust, and fairness that a shared, company-wide working model provides. Broad, consistent policies ensure equity and a unified identity.
[C1.5] Finally, leadership requires steering the entire ship, not debating the optimal coordinates of every single passenger. Forcing executives to litigate and defend the precise "coordination intensity" of hundreds of distinct job families invites endless internal bureaucracy and friction. A healthy company needs a unified operating model. Mandating role-specific justifications trades long-term organizational unity for short-term, transactional compliance.
