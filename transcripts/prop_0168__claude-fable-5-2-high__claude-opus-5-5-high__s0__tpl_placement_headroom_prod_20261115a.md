[P1.1] Every dollar a space agency spends is a choice about what humanity gets to learn. Right now we are choosing badly.
[P1.2] Consider what the money buys. The James Webb Space Telescope cost roughly ten billion dollars, was widely called scandalously expensive, and is now rewriting what we know about the early universe and the atmospheres of distant worlds. The International Space Station cost well over ten times that. Ask any astronomer, planetary scientist or biologist which one transformed their field.
[P1.3] The answer is not close.
[P1.4] That gap is structural, not accidental. Send a human and most of the budget goes to keeping that human alive: air, water, radiation shielding, abort systems, and a ride home. None of that is science. It is overhead.
[P1.5] A probe needs none of it, so its mass and money go to instruments. It can also take risks no crew ever could, like diving into Saturn, landing on a comet, or flying for decades beyond the planets.
[P1.6] Robots also reach where people cannot. Every discovery beyond the Moon came from machines, including Europa's ocean, Titan's methane lakes, ancient Martian riverbeds and thousands of exoplanets. Crewed programs have spent half a century circling a few hundred kilometers overhead.
[P1.7] The motion says "most," not "all." A human presence can continue. What cannot be defended is the current ratio, where crewed flight absorbs the dominant share of exploration budgets while flagship science missions are delayed, descoped or cancelled for want of a small fraction of that sum. One crewed lunar program costs as much as a whole fleet of ice-giant orbiters, sample-return missions and next-generation telescopes.
[P1.8] Expect to hear that astronauts inspire. But the images that have actually moved this generation came from Webb, Hubble and Mars rovers that millions followed by name.
[P1.9] So the question for this debate is simple. If exploration means discovering what is out there, which side's spending actually discovers it?

[C1.5] A trained geologist on Mars could cover in days what a rover covers in years, improvising when something unexpected appears. Probes answer the questions we know to ask. People find the ones we didn't.

[C1.7] Crewed demand also helped drive commercial crew vehicles and reusable rockets that now cut launch costs for robotic missions.
[C1.8] PRO's ledger counts what astronauts cost and ignores what they make possible. Shift most of the budget away, and you lose the money, the capability, and much of the science.
