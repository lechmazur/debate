[P1.1] The case for raising the minimum firearm purchase age to 21 rests on a simple empirical reality: the 18-to-20 age group is disproportionately dangerous both to others and to themselves, and firearms are uniquely lethal in ways that substitution cannot match.
[P1.2] Start with suicide. Suicide attempts are frequently impulsive, and the time between decision and attempt is often measured in minutes. Firearms convert that impulse into death roughly nine times in ten; other common methods fail far more often. The 18-20 cohort sits in a developmental window where impulsivity peaks and the prefrontal cortex is still maturing.
[P1.3] Removing the easiest, fastest access to the most lethal tool saves lives that wouldn't be saved by substitution, because a failed overdose or cutting is a survivable event in a way a gunshot is not. Most young people who survive a suicide attempt do not go on to die by suicide—they get help. Guns close that window.
[P1.4] On homicide, the same age group accounts for a disproportionate share of both victims and perpetrators. Raising the purchase age disrupts the legal supply at exactly the point where impulsive interpersonal violence—arguments, altercations, escalations—turns fatal. The illegal-acquisition alternative sounds plausible in the abstract, but for most 18-to-20 year olds, the legal purchase is the path of least resistance. They lack the money, connections, and criminal infrastructure that older buyers might have.
[P1.5] The black market is not frictionless; it carries risk, cost, and unreliability that will deter some and slow others.
[P1.6] Even where illegal acquisition occurs, the decisive comparison is lethality. Firearms turn arguments into funerals. The substitute weapon a frustrated 19-year-old reaches for instead is likely to be less lethal—meaning fewer deaths even if the underlying conflict still happens. The proposition wins on the balance: large reductions in the most lethal outcomes, partially offset by smaller increases in less deadly ones.
