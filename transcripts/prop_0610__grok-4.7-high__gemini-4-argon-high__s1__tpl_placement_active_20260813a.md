[P1.1] The European Union cannot honor its climate commitments if carbon-intensive production simply moves abroad. When European steel, cement, aluminum, and chemicals pay a carbon price and identical imports do not, emissions do not fall. They relocate. Plants close, jobs leave, and the atmosphere is no better off.
[P1.2] A carbon border adjustment on most imported industrial goods ends that arbitrage. It applies to imports the same carbon cost domestic producers already face, and it can rebate exports so European firms are not handicapped in markets that still treat pollution as free.
[P1.3] Retaliation will come. Major partners may answer with tariffs of their own. That risk is not a reason to abandon the policy. It is the price of refusing to let foreign free-riding dictate European climate law.
[P1.4] Without a border measure, the carbon price becomes politically unsustainable: industry will demand carve-outs, or production will shift to jurisdictions with no price at all. Either path destroys the policy while handing competitors a permanent cost advantage. A trade fight is preferable to a hollow climate regime and a hollow industrial base.
[P1.5] The adjustment is also leverage, not a wall. Partners that impose a comparable carbon price can be exempted. Market access then rewards climate action instead of punishing it. Europe’s market is large enough that many suppliers will adapt rather than walk away, and those who retaliate still face the cost of losing preferential access over time.
[P1.6] Temporary friction is manageable. Structural leakage is not.
[P1.7] Limit the measure to industrial goods where carbon content is measurable and leakage is acute. That scope captures the core problem without pretending Brussels can carbon-account every consumer import overnight. Impose the adjustment, mirror the domestic price, and proceed even if partners retaliate. A climate policy that stops at the border invites the world to free-ride while Europe deindustrializes.
[P1.8] That is the outcome this motion rejects.
