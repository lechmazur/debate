[P1.1] Housing affordability is ultimately a math problem: when cities add jobs faster than homes, households must outbid one another for scarce shelter. Streamlining zoning and permitting directly resolves this scarcity. Across advanced economies, exclusionary zoning—like single-family mandates, parking minimums, and multi-year discretionary reviews—caps supply and imposes a heavy regulatory tax on construction. Removing these bottlenecks lets builders replace detached houses with duplexes and apartments quickly and predictably.
[P1.2] When supply grows to meet demand, landlords must compete for tenants rather than tenants fighting over scarce units. Even new market-rate construction triggers a vital filtering effect, absorbing wealthier households who would otherwise bid up and gentrify older, naturally affordable neighborhoods. This is why permissive, by-right zoning in places like Tokyo and Auckland sustains broad affordability, whereas supply-choked cities like San Francisco or London suffer relentless price spirals.
[P1.3] Conversely, rent control and aggressive tenant protections treat the symptom while worsening the disease. Capping prices creates zero new bedrooms; instead, it actively shrinks the rental pool. Faced with capped returns and costly lease restrictions, property owners defer maintenance or pull units off the rental market altogether, converting them into high-end condominiums for sale.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, expansive tenant protections heighten the financial risk of a bad lease, prompting landlords to protect themselves upfront. They impose stricter credit checks, hefty guarantors, and higher income minimums, systematically screening out the exact low-income and vulnerable renters these policies claim to help.
[P1.5] Ultimately, you cannot price-control your way out of a physical shortage. Rent control and rigid tenant rules merely ration scarcity, rewarding lucky, incumbent tenants—regardless of their income—while locking out young workers, growing families, and newcomers who face a smaller, pricier shadow market. Because true affordability requires enough homes for everyone who needs one, unleashing supply through permitting and zoning reform is far more effective.
