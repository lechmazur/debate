[P1.1] Picture a woman with advanced motor neurone disease. Her mind is entirely clear. She knows what comes next: losing the ability to swallow, then to speak, then to breathe on her own. She has weighed it, talked with her family, seen palliative specialists, and decided she does not want her final weeks dictated by suffocation.
[P1.2] Today, in most of the world, the law tells her she must either endure it, starve herself, travel abroad while she still can, or ask someone she loves to risk prosecution. That is the status quo CON must defend.
[P1.3] My case rests on three points.
[P1.4] First, autonomy over the end of one's own life. We already let competent adults refuse ventilators, feeding tubes, and dialysis, knowing death will follow. We permit sedation into unconsciousness until death. So the question is not whether patients may choose to die.
[P1.5] They already can. The question is whether the only lawful routes must be slow, painful, and indirect. A law that permits death by dehydration but forbids a peaceful, chosen death is not protecting life. It is rationing mercy.
[P1.6] Second, prohibition does not stop assisted dying; it drives it underground. Desperate patients die violently and alone. Some doctors quietly hasten deaths with no oversight, no documentation, and no check for coercion. A legal framework replaces that darkness with scrutiny: confirmed diagnosis, repeated requests, independent assessments of capacity, screening for pressure and treatable depression, and mandatory reporting.
[P1.7] Regulation is the safeguard. Prohibition is the absence of one.
[P1.8] Third, safeguards demonstrably work. Jurisdictions such as Oregon have run terminal-illness laws for over two decades. Use remains rare, patients are typically educated and insured rather than marginalized, and a notable share who obtain medication never take it. The prescription itself relieves terror, giving control back to someone whose disease has taken everything else.
[P1.9] CON will warn of slippery slopes. But the motion is narrow: competent adults, unbearable terminal or degenerative suffering, strict safeguards. Hypothetical abuse elsewhere cannot justify certain, present cruelty here. Let her choose.
