[P1.1] Democratic control of lethal force is not a slogan. It is the requirement that the public can authorize war, see its costs, and punish abuse. Private military contractors in combat roles break that requirement. They let governments kill through firms whose duty runs to a contract and a client, not to voters, and whose deaths, methods, and failures are easier to bury than those of a national force.
[P1.2] Accountability needs three things: clear authorization, visible consequences, and enforceable responsibility. Combat contractors fail all three. Legislatures argue over troop numbers and casualty lists. Contractor deployments hide in supplemental budgets, classified task orders, and deals with foreign governments.
[P1.3] A soldier’s death moves elections. A contractor’s death usually does not. That gap is how leaders run bloodier, longer wars with less consent. The public cannot restrain a conflict it is not fully allowed to see.
[P1.4] Responsibility collapses the same way. A soldier sits inside a chain of command that, however flawed, answers to civilian authority and military law. A contractor answers to a company, an insurer, and a statement of work. When force is abused—reckless fire, civilian harm, quiet mission creep—inquiry hits corporate secrecy, jurisdictional fog, and the political convenience of calling the scandal a vendor problem.
[P1.5] Impunity is the predictable result of renting the power to kill.
[P1.6] Profit makes it worse. Firms are paid to supply force, not to end the need for it. Renewals, expansions, and prolonged deployments can be good business. War powers exist to resist exactly that incentive: lethal force should be a last resort under public control, not a revenue stream.
[P1.7] Support contracts can be policed. Combat cannot be outsourced without outsourcing the decision to take life. Ban private military contractors from combat roles in international conflicts, or treat democratic accountability over the use of force as optional.

[C1.1] Banning combat contractors would not restore democratic accountability; it would push lethal force into darker shadows while hamstringing law-abiding states. Every failure in [P1.2] and [P1.4], including buried budgets, uncounted deaths, and legal loopholes, reflects negligent legislation, not contracting itself. Parliaments can mandate public contractor casualty reporting, extend military criminal jurisdiction, and bind contractors directly to a state chain of command. When you ban regulated firms instead, casualty-averse leaders do not suddenly hold transparent votes for troop surges.
[C1.2] They turn to local proxy militias, foreign irregulars, and covert intelligence assets. A democracy can audit a contract, fire a vendor, and prosecute an employee in domestic court; it cannot subpoena a proxy warlord.
[C1.3] Democratic self-rule also means nothing if an elected government lacks the capacity to defend its voters. Not every international conflict is a superpower’s war of choice. When a smaller democracy faces sudden invasion, it may lack the standing forces or specialized combat operators needed to hold the line while mobilizing. Hiring vetted combat specialists under sovereign authority lets elected leaders protect the public without relying solely on coercive conscription or slow allied intervention.
[C1.4] Worse, an international ban works asymmetrically: autocracies routinely disguise state-backed mercenaries as volunteers or advisors, meaning a ban disarms only transparent democracies that take legal limits seriously.
[C1.5] Finally, conceding that support contracts can be policed [P1.7] unravels the ban. On modern battlefields, guarding fuel convoys, defending embassies, or operating air-defense and targeting networks becomes combat the instant an enemy attacks. Outlawing combat roles while permitting support does not create clarity; it drives governments into semantic evasion, mislabeling frontline fighting as "defensive security" and hiding real rules of engagement from voters. Nor does profit justify prohibition [P1.6]: private arms makers profit from prolonged war too, yet democracies regulate procurement rather than banning factories.
[C1.6] Real accountability comes from explicit authorization, open reporting, and criminal liability, not a ban.
