[P1.1] Across Europe, a quiet arbitrage is hollowing out the social contract. When a large company hires a cleaner, a driver, or a warehouse worker directly, it pays the full weight of pensions, unemployment insurance, sickness cover, parental leave. When the same company routes that same work through a platform app or a four-layer subcontracting chain, the social bill shrinks dramatically — sometimes to nothing, because the worker is reclassified as "independent," or because the liability sits with a thinly capitalised subcontractor that vanishes the moment inspectors arrive.
[P1.2] That is the problem this proposal fixes. Require the lead firm — the one that actually captures the value — to pay a higher share of social contributions when work reaches it through platforms or subcontracting layers. Three reasons this is the right rule.
[P1.3] First, fairness in competition. A bakery that hires its delivery rider directly should not be undercut by a multinational that books the identical rider through an app and pays a fraction of the social cost. Today's regime rewards the firm that restructures away from employment, not the firm that is more productive. A higher contribution share neutralises that perverse incentive.
[P1.4] Second, fiscal honesty. European welfare states are financed largely by payroll. As work migrates into platforms and cascading subcontracts, the contribution base erodes while the social risks — accidents, illness, old age — remain and fall back on taxpayers. Making lead firms internalise those costs is simply asking them to pay for the risks their business model generates.
[P1.5] Third, enforcement realism. Joint and several liability up the chain is the only mechanism that actually works. Chasing a shell subcontractor in another member state is hopeless; the lead firm has assets, reputation, and a compliance department. Put the obligation where it can be enforced.
[P1.6] The motion does not ban platforms or subcontracting. It prices them honestly. If a business model only survives by offloading social costs onto the public, it is not efficiency — it is a subsidy. End the subsidy.

By forcing large companies to pay a higher share of social contributions simply because they use external partners, the EU would incentivize inefficient insourcing. [C1.4] Second, this policy would devastate the very entities it claims to protect: small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and independent contractors. [C1.5] Third, the proposal is a legal and administrative nightmare. Social security is a jealously guarded national competence of EU member states, each with vastly different funding models. Imposing a top-down, punitive rate on specific organizational forms violates the principle of subsidiarity and creates immense legal uncertainty.
[C1.6] Instead of pricing "honestly," this policy arbitrarily punishes modern, flexible business models. It constructs walls around large corporations, isolates SMEs, and drags down European competitiveness. We must reject this blunt, counterproductive intervention.
