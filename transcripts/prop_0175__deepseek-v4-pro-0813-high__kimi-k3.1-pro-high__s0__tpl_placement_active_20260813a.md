[P1.1] Licensing in interior design, hair braiding, and similar low-risk trades behaves like a guild permit, not a safety check. The mismatch between stated risk and actual requirement reveals the purpose. A hair braider can be forced to complete hundreds or thousands of hours of cosmetology training, plus tuition and exam fees, even though natural hair braiding uses no chemicals, no heat, and no cutting. Almost none of that curriculum is braiding; it is time and money paid to existing schools, whose licensed graduates often sit on the boards that set the required hours.
[P1.2] The effect is predictable: fewer new braiders, less competition, higher prices for consumers, and little or no reduction in harm. Basic hygiene can be taught in a short targeted course. A full cosmetology license is a fence around the market, not a public-health intervention.
[P1.3] Interior design follows the same pattern. Licensing commonly requires years of formal education, supervised practice, and a professional exam. But interior design decisions—color, furniture, finishes, space planning—are primarily aesthetic and functional. Actual structural and fire-safety responsibility is handled separately by architects, engineers, building codes, permit review, and inspections.
[P1.4] Requiring a design license does not make buildings safer; it makes it harder for new designers to compete on price. Incumbent designers get legal protection from competition, while consumers lose access to lower-cost alternatives without gaining safety.
[P1.5] The same formula appears across low-risk trades: mandatory classroom hours, fees, exams, and apprenticeships whose gatekeepers are current licensees. This is exactly how incumbents raise rivals’ costs and create a price umbrella. If safety were the real object, government would regulate specific risky acts, require insurance or bonding, and inspect premises.
[P1.6] Instead these laws regulate the right to work itself. That distribution of burdens and benefits—closed entry for outsiders, protected income for insiders—is the definition of a barrier to entry, not a consumer safeguard.

[C1.2] Take hair braiding. Even chemical-free braiding involves repeated close contact, shared equipment, sanitation, scalp and skin conditions, product reactions, and excessive tension that can damage hair and scalp. Individual services may be low-risk, but licensing addresses repeated exposure across many clients, including clients poorly positioned to assess hygiene or complain effectively. A short, targeted course may well be better policy than full cosmetology training.
[C1.3] But identifying a cheaper safeguard does not prove that the existing safeguard is primarily a racket; it proves that its design may be disproportionate.
[C1.4] PRO also reduces interior design to “color, furniture, finishes, space planning,” then assumes away the safety content. Finishes and furnishings can affect fire behavior and smoke; layouts affect egress and accessibility; material selection can affect indoor air quality. Building codes, permit review, and inspections are complements to competence, not substitutes for it. Codes supply rules, designers apply them before mistakes are built, and inspectors cannot redesign every deficient project at the end.
[C1.5] The information problem matters. A consumer can readily judge whether a room looks attractive; that consumer cannot easily judge whether a finish is suitable for its location or whether salon implements were properly disinfected. Insurance compensates after harm, inspections sample only some conduct, and regulation of individual acts cannot always supply baseline competence. Ex ante screening is therefore a plausible safety mechanism, not merely a “fence.”
[C1.6] Finally, incumbent expertise on boards is ambiguous evidence, not proof of capture. Expertise and self-interest can coexist; PRO needs to show that particular requirements lack reasonable safety justifications, not simply that licensees help administer them.
[C1.7] The proper principle is proportionality: remove irrelevant coursework, recognize practical training, and create exemptions where risks genuinely warrant them. [clipped]
