[P1.1] Central city road space is the scarcest thing a city owns, and we give it away for free at the moment of use. The price is still paid — in idling, in buses stuck behind single-occupant cars, in ambulances crawling, in children breathing exhaust. Congestion pricing doesn't invent a cost; it takes a cost already imposed on strangers and makes the person creating it face it. That is the whole case: rationing by delay is worse than rationing by price, because delay destroys the resource while price recycles it into transit.
[P1.2] Strong driver opposition is expected, and it is not a veto. The people harmed by gridlock are diffuse — bus riders, couriers, pedestrians — while the people who benefit from free entry are concentrated, organized, and vocal. Stockholm is the instructive pattern: fierce resistance before the trial, grudging acceptance after, and a vote to keep it once people had actually breathed the difference. Preferences measured before experience are weak evidence about a policy's merits.
[P1.3] Equity runs our way too. Today's center is rationed by who can afford to sit in traffic and who owns a car at all; the majority arriving by bus, bike, or foot subsidize the minority driving in. Pricing with revenue spent on buses, and exemptions for disabled drivers, transfers advantage toward the people with fewest options.
[P1.4] The alternative is not free movement; it is more lanes and more parking that refill as fast as we build them. Ask what the opposition offers a nurse on a cross-town bus. If the answer is patience, that is a policy of protecting the queue — and the queue is the most regressive toll ever invented, paid in hours by whoever cannot buy their way out.

[C1.1] The motion is not whether road space is scarce. It is whether a city should ram a charge through over the strong, sustained objection of the people who will pay it. That clause is the whole fight, and the answer is no, because strong opposition is not noise to be overridden; it is the policy failing a test it needs to pass.
[C1.2] Look at who actually drives into a city centre at peak. Not mostly executives in sedans. It is electricians with vans full of tools, hospital shift workers whose shifts end when the last bus has gone, parents in outer neighbourhoods where the train comes twice an hour. When these people oppose a charge fiercely, they are telling you the alternative does not yet exist.
[C1.3] Pricing them before the alternative is built does not ration the road efficiently; it just moves the cost from their time to their rent money. Rationing by price only beats rationing by delay when the priced-out have somewhere to go.
[C1.4] Then there is what imposition actually produces. Edinburgh put a scheme to its residents and it was crushed. Manchester did the same and lost badly. New York's plan, designed over years, was abruptly paused by a governor who read the polls, and survives only in a scaled-back form.
[C1.5] Stockholm, PRO's own example, is the exception precisely because it was not imposed: the city ran a trial, held a referendum, and earned consent before making it permanent. That is the opposite of the motion.
[C1.6] Charge over opposition and you get the worst version: a scheme hollowed by exemptions, revenue that never reaches the buses because the next council runs on repealing it, and a decade of poisoned trust for every transit reform after. The nurse on the cross-town bus needs a funded bus. Funded buses come from durable politics, and durable politics come from consent, not conquest.
