[P1.1] The case for this motion is order without rigidity. Universities and employers currently face the worst of both worlds: high demand for talent and discretionary visa systems that make hiring slow, uncertain, and politically vulnerable. A guaranteed allocation gives a research team the confidence to bring in a specialist, lets a manufacturer plan shifts, and lets a care provider recruit without waiting months for a decision that may still be no. That predictability is not a bonus; it is the difference between a functioning institution and a cancelled programme.
[P1.2] Guarantees alone, however, are politically and socially fragile. The plausible failure of migration policy is not that a foreign worker arrives, but that they arrive into a town where housing, primary care, and school places are already exhausted. Tying allocations to housing and local service capacity converts these visible strains into approval criteria. Where there is headroom, arrivals are cleared; where there is not, the cap binds.
[P1.3] This protects receiving areas and makes the system self-restraining.
[P1.4] The cap and guarantee work together. A cap without guarantee is a discretionary lottery that invites lobbying, delay, and regional arbitrariness. A guarantee without a cap invites uncontrolled concentration in the same few expensive postcodes. The motion’s pairing creates a known, transparent ceiling, so migration is planned like any other scarce resource.
[P1.5] The local-capacity link also moves policy toward devolution. Universities and employers become sponsors with an interest in housing delivery and service planning, because their allocation rises only as local capacity does. A city that builds more student or workforce housing can credibly accept more students and staff; one that does not cannot simply import more pressure. That is a better incentive than either a points system or a freeze.
[P1.6] Ultimately, the proposition is not an argument for unlimited entry. It is an argument for making entry legible, predictable, and matched to real community capacity.

[C1.1] The proposition promises tidy planning, but in reality it creates a catastrophic economic trap: it chokes off talent precisely where it is most productive. Tying migration visas to local housing and service capacity treats the failures of municipal planning as a valid reason to paralyze national progress.
[C1.2] First, this policy punishes dynamism. High-value clusters—pioneering research universities, tech hubs, and major hospitals—are almost universally located in high-demand, capacity-strained regions. Under this motion, a laboratory cannot recruit an oncology researcher and a hospital cannot hire critical nurses simply because the surrounding area has a tight rental market or crowded clinics. Starving these institutions of talent does not build a single home; it merely degrades public services and throttles the innovation and tax revenues needed to fund infrastructure expansion.
[C1.3] Second, the claim that employers will simply build capacity is an illusion. Universities and enterprises are not municipal planners. Conditioning essential recruitment on local infrastructure delivery holds vital institutions hostage to local zoning restrictions, planning delays, and NIMBY vetoes. A hospital or manufacturing plant cannot unilaterally build sewer systems, GP surgeries, or transit lines just to unlock its workforce.
[C1.4] Third, the geographic link is unenforceable. People are not cargo. Unless the state enforces authoritarian restrictions on internal movement, workers sponsored in an area with excess service capacity will commute from adjacent, overcrowded districts, completely severing the link between sponsorship and local impact. Labor markets simply do not stop at borough borders.
[C1.5] Finally, "guaranteed" allocations entrench an incumbent cartel. Guaranteed quotas privilege established corporations and legacy universities that can lobby councils and warehouse permits, while shutting out dynamic startups and emerging sectors.
[C1.6] Migration policy must respond to real-time skill needs, not municipal bottlenecks. The answer to a housing shortage is to build infrastructure, not to turn visa policy into a mechanism of geographic stagnation.
