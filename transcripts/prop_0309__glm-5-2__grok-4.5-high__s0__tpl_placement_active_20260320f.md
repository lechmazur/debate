[P1.1] Rural hospital closures are not an efficiency story. They are a sentence passed on communities too small to clear a returns-based threshold for staying alive.
[P1.2] The core issue is time. Stroke care, cardiac arrest, obstetric emergencies, and trauma all have windows measured in minutes, not hours. When the nearest facility jumps from 15 to 90 minutes away, the relevant metric is not aggregate efficiency, it is avoidable death. No consolidation model erases the drive time.
[P1.3] Telemedicine does not stabilize a bleeding patient or deliver a breech baby.
[P1.4] Second, the population most harmed is the population least able to adapt. Rural residents skew older, poorer, and sicker. They are less likely to have reliable transportation, flexible work, or savings to absorb overnight stays in regional cities. Telling a 72-year-old with heart failure to drive two hours for specialist access is not a reform.
[P1.5] It is abandonment with a spreadsheet.
[P1.6] Third, closures produce cascading harm beyond healthcare. A rural hospital is often the largest local employer. Its closure removes not just care but the tax base, nursing homes, pharmacies, and the community's viability. The downstream effect is depopulation, which further erodes service feasibility, creating a spiral no market corrects on its own.
[P1.7] Market logic optimizes for throughput per dollar. But healthcare is not retail. Government has a legitimate interest in preserving access where concentrated need meets sparse population. That is not market distortion, it is the floor below which market language stops making sense.
[P1.8] The proposition does not ask governments to prop up every facility at any cost. It asks them to treat medical access as infrastructure, like roads and fire service, whose value is not captured by their own revenue line. When the market says consolidate, government should ask who is being consolidated away, and whether a civilized society gets to make that call on a profit-and-loss basis.
