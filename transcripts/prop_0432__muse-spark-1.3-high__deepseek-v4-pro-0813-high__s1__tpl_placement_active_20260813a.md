[P1.1] Blanket pet bans don't prevent damage. They prevent honesty. When a large share of renters live with cats or dogs, a building that bans them outright doesn't become pet-free. It becomes full of hidden pets, forged assistance letters, and constant low-level warfare between management and tenants.
[P1.2] That is the worst outcome for landlords too: uninsured risk, resentment, and turnover.
[P1.3] A default of allowing pets backed by standardized damage insurance or deposits solves the actual problem. Landlords have one legitimate core objection: cost. Scratched floors, chewed trim, stained carpet, complaints that take staff time. A standard financial instrument prices that risk and guarantees recovery without intrusive policing.
[P1.4] The tenant who can cover the harm gets housing; the landlord who suffers harm gets paid. That is far more rational than banning the responsible majority because of a careless minority.
[P1.5] Standardization matters. Ad hoc pet fees today are arbitrary, sometimes extortionate, sometimes trivial, and they invite discrimination. A uniform, transparent requirement — proof of coverage or a capped refundable deposit — treats all tenants alike, discourages frivolous claims, and creates a clear incentive to train, crate, and clean up. Responsibility is rewarded instead of presumed impossible.
[P1.6] The gains go beyond fairness. Pet bans are a quiet driver of shelter surrender, forced moves, and housing instability, especially for lower-income renters who cannot shop among rare pet-friendly buildings at premium rents. Keeping families intact reduces surrenders and euthanasia pressure. For owners, it means longer leases and fewer vacancies, because pet owners move less when they finally find a welcoming home and they work harder to be good neighbors to keep it.
[P1.7] Default allow does not mean anything goes. Buildings can still enforce leash rules, noise standards, waste cleanup, and liability for aggression. The difference is the starting point: proven responsibility plus financial backing opens the door, rather than a blanket no slamming it shut.

[C1.1] The motion treats an apartment building as if the only relevant harm from an animal is a scratched floor that can be priced. But most reasons for pet-free housing are not repair bills. They are sleep lost to barking, allergens that migrate through shared ventilation and hallways, odors, waste left in common areas, fear of strange dogs, and conflict among neighbors. No deposit compensates a tenant’s asthma attack or two years of noise disputes.
[C1.2] A default allow makes those losses the expected cost of renting, while only the owner’s financial exposure is softened.
[C1.3] Standardized insurance or deposits would also replace judgment with payment. A landlord should be able to ask whether a particular animal suits a dense building, an elevator floor, a shared HVAC system, or a building with young children and elderly tenants. Under the motion, a destructive or disruptive pet qualifies as long as its owner carries the right paperwork. That is pay-to-play, not responsible screening.
[C1.4] In fact, the opponent’s hidden-pet problem [P1.1] cuts the other way: if tenants already hide animals or fake assistance letters, they can also fake or let lapse insurance certificates, leaving the landlord with an uninsured animal and a harder eviction fight.
[C1.5] Coverage itself is weaker than the motion assumes. Security deposits are often legally capped and may not cover repeated common-area damage. Insurance disputes take time, exclude many types of harm, and do nothing for non-financial nuisances. The landlord still absorbs management time and neighbor turnover.
[C1.6] Finally, pet-free housing is a legitimate promise, not a loophole. Owners should be able to offer quiet, allergen-controlled, or simply no-animal buildings. Defaulting to pets shrinks that choice for tenants who depend on it and privileges pet owners over existing communities. The right answer is enforcement of lawful pet restrictions where chosen, not making pet ownership the default.
