[P1.1] Companies may forecast demand, staffing needs, and workplace risks. They should not turn a named worker into a prediction: likely slacker, likely organizer, likely quitter. That distinction is the heart of this ban. It restricts a particular use of power, not AI or workforce planning generally.
[P1.2] These predictions are dangerous precisely because they precede the conduct they claim to describe. A worker flagged as a quitting risk can be quietly denied training or promotion, making departure more likely. A worker predicted to underperform can receive fewer opportunities, then “confirm” the model through the resulting record. Unlike an evaluation of completed work, the employee cannot simply point to the future and disprove it.
[P1.3] The employer holds the data, the model, and the consequences; the worker often does not even know a score exists.
[P1.4] Unionization prediction makes the illegitimacy unmistakable. Organizing is a protected collective choice, not a defect for management to anticipate and neutralize. Even without explicit retaliation, a risk score can direct surveillance, schedule changes, or attention toward suspected organizers. No promise that the model is “accurate” makes that purpose acceptable.
[P1.5] Quitting and productivity scores invite softer language—retention, coaching, personalization—but softer purposes do not remove the coercive machinery. Such systems can infer vulnerability from proxies involving health, caregiving, age, finances, or workplace dissent. A manager who sees “high flight risk” has a ready reason to withhold investment while insisting the algorithm, not the manager, made the judgment.
[P1.6] CON’s likely answer is consent, transparency, and auditing. But consent under an employment relationship is not meaningfully voluntary when refusal may cost opportunity. Transparency merely tells someone which opaque inference harmed them. And accuracy audits cannot cure biased labels, feedback loops, or illegitimate purposes.
[P1.7] The workable boundary is prohibition at individual prediction. Companies can still use aggregate attrition forecasts, improve conditions, ask employees what they need, and assess actual performance through accountable processes. If better management is available without pre-judging identifiable workers, efficiency is no justification for giving employers an unchallengeable claim on their futures.

[C1.1] Companies already decide who will thrive, who may leave, and who is restless enough to organize. They do it in reviews, skip-levels, and hallway impressions. This ban would not stop those judgments. It would only forbid making them with a model that can be tested, documented, and constrained.
[C1.2] That is a ban on accountability wearing the costume of worker protection.
[C1.3] Productivity and quitting forecasts are how scarce investment gets placed before the outcome arrives. A flight-risk score can trigger a retention offer, a raise, or schedule flexibility rather than a quiet write-off. A productivity forecast can flag who needs coaching while the trajectory can still change. Treating every pre-conduct score as a trap assumes lazy or malicious managers.
[C1.4] That failure is not built into prediction. We do not ban medical risk scores because a negligent clinician might withhold care; we punish the withholding.
[C1.5] The unionization clause does not salvage the package. Labor law already forbids retaliation and anti-organizing surveillance, whether the suspicion comes from a model or a supervisor’s hunch. Banning the model leaves the hunch untouched and drives the same inference underground, where no one can demand an explanation or an audit. Consent and accuracy are not the whole moral question.
[C1.6] The question is whether foreseeable misuse justifies prohibiting forecasts that can also keep people, train them, and fix the conditions that make them want to leave.
[C1.7] Aggregate attrition charts and backward-looking reviews cannot tell a manager which named employee is about to walk or which one will flounder without support. If the harm is adverse action on a hidden score, regulate the action and require disclosure. Do not outlaw the forecast. A ban this broad withholds help as surely as it blocks harm, and it does so by pretending the worst use is the only use.

[P2.6] Existing labor law also does not answer the unionization point.
