[P1.1] Bodily autonomy is the claim that a competent person decides what happens to their own body. Appeals to dignity are claims about worth, appearance, or the story others want that body to tell. In personal medical choices, the first has more moral weight because it is tied to the only person who must inhabit the consequences.
[P1.2] Dignity is too elastic to govern the scalpel. It has been invoked to block abortion and to demand it, to forbid assisted dying and to sanctify it, to resist gender-affirming care and to require it. A standard that can be flipped to justify opposite invasions of the same body cannot outrank the right of the person inside that body.
[P1.3] Autonomy is not vague in the same way. It asks who consents, who bears the pain, who lives with the result. A Jehovah’s Witness refusing blood, a terminal patient stopping chemotherapy, a person declining an implant or accepting an experimental trial: the moral center is their control over their flesh, not whether observers find the choice elevated or degrading.
[P1.4] If dignity carried more weight, third parties could overrule a competent refusal because the refusal looked “undignified,” or force a procedure because living without it seemed humiliating. That converts medicine into moral theater. Patients become symbols. Their bodies become public property dressed up as honor.
[P1.5] The spectator’s sense of dignity does not absorb the nausea, the scar, or the extra months of suffering.
[P1.6] Dignity, at best, is something autonomy makes possible. You cannot have a dignified life if others may cut, fill, or sustain you against your will in the name of an ideal you reject. When the two conflict, the person whose blood, breath, and remaining time are at stake must decide. Anything else is paternalism with better vocabulary.

[C1.1] Bodily autonomy matters, but it is not the heaviest value; it is the gate through which moral claims enter medicine. Dignity is the reason the gate must exist. We protect consent because persons have worth, not the reverse. If autonomy alone carried greater weight, every competent preference would have near-final authority, even when it reduces the patient to an instrument of despair, identity rejection, or another person's demands.
[C1.2] PRO treats dignity as a spectator's etiquette. That is too thin. In medical choices, dignity usually names the patient's own claim not to be degraded, abandoned, or turned into a problem to be managed. A depressed patient refusing lifesaving treatment, an exhausted elder requesting death mainly to avoid burdening family, a disabled patient whose “choice” reflects stigma more than settled values: autonomy records the request, but dignity asks whether honoring it respects the person.
[C1.3] When the answer is no, dignity has greater moral weight.
[C1.4] PRO says dignity is elastic. Autonomy is just as contested: capacity, coercion, information, maturity, ambivalence. Medicine already limits autonomy through these concepts. The real dispute is what guides those limits.
[C1.5] Dignity provides a substantive standard: protect equal human worth against fear, shame, market pressure, and dehumanizing institutions. Autonomy only tells us who speaks; dignity tells us whether the speech is free enough, informed enough, and worthy of the person speaking. Without it, consent becomes a stamp, not a safeguard.
[C1.6] Nor does dignity license third-party control. Properly understood, it disciplines paternalism by requiring the patient’s perspective, not merely observers’ discomfort. It supports refusal of humiliating treatment, privacy, and care that does not objectify. The danger PRO warns about comes from abandoning dignity: a system that says any choice is respectable because it was chosen, even when the choice expresses self-hatred or social cruelty.
[C1.7] The morally weightier value is the one that keeps autonomy human.
