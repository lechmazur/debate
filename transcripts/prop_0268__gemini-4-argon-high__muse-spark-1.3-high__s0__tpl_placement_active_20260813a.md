[P1.1] Markets only work when buyers know what they are paying for. Shrinkflation—reducing product weight while keeping the headline price and front-of-pack dimensions nearly identical—is engineered to exploit human psychology. Shoppers notice immediately when a $4.00 cereal box jumps to $4.50. They rarely notice when the $4.00 sticker stays put, the box stays just as tall, and ten percent of the food quietly vanishes.
[P1.2] Standard unit-price labels do not fix this blind spot. Pointing to a tiny per-ounce figure on a shelf tag ignores how people actually shop. No consumer pushing a cart or scrolling a food app memorizes last month’s per-gram cost across dozens of weekly staples. A static unit price shows what something costs today, but hides that you are getting less than yesterday for the exact same headline price.
[P1.3] Only clearly flagging the size drop and the resulting unit-price increase closes that information gap.
[P1.4] That gap damages both household budgets and fair competition. For families on tight budgets, buying their usual grocery list on an app for the same checkout total creates a false sense of stability—until meals run short mid-week. Meanwhile, honest brands that maintain full package sizes and transparently adjust prices are punished on the shelf next to rivals hiding behind stealth downsizing. Clear disclosure restores honest price competition.
[P1.5] Compliance is also straightforward because supermarkets and food apps already track the necessary data. Whenever a manufacturer shrinks a product, the net weight updates in the retailer’s inventory database. Flagging when weight falls without a proportional price cut is a simple automated calculation—easily displayed as a bold notice on a printed shelf tag or a badge on an app screen.
[P1.6] This requirement does not ban smaller packages. Companies can resize products whenever they choose—they simply lose the ability to disguise a price hike.
