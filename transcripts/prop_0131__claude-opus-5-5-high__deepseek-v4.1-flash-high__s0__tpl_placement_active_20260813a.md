[P1.1] Unpaid care is not free. It is the largest long-term care system most countries have, and governments have been running it on credit: borrowing the careers, pensions and health of daughters, spouses and parents who were never asked whether they could afford the loan. The question is not whether to spend money on care. It is whether we keep hiding the bill inside families until those families break.
[P1.2] Picture a fifty-two-year-old woman whose mother develops dementia. She cuts her hours, then quits. She stops paying into her pension, stops paying income tax, and spends years on sleep deprivation and isolation. When she finally collapses, her mother goes into residential care at public expense, and a decade later the daughter herself retires into poverty.
[P1.3] The state paid anyway. It simply paid late, paid more, and lost a worker along the way.
[P1.4] That gives PRO three reasons to win.
[P1.5] First, the assumption is failing. Families are smaller, more women are in paid work, relatives live farther apart, and people live longer with conditions that need years of care. The pool of unpaid carers is shrinking while demand rises. A policy built on "someone will cover it" plans for a supply that is disappearing.
[P1.6] Second, generous payment is the prudent choice. Residential care and hospital stays are the most expensive places to look after someone, and most older and disabled people would rather stay home. Paying carers enough to keep going, backed by assessment, training and respite, prevents burnout and delays institutional care. Token allowances worth a fraction of a minimum wage for full-time work do not.
[P1.7] Third, justice. Today the burden falls hardest on women and on poorer households. Wealthy families buy help. Everyone else sacrifices earnings.
[P1.8] The objection that payment "commodifies love" gets this backwards. We already pay foster parents, and nobody thinks that cheapens their devotion. Payment does not replace love. It stops love from bankrupting the person who gives it.
[P1.9] CON must defend a system that works only because we never total up what it costs.

[C1.3] Start with what care requires. Dementia, disability, incontinence, night-time risk: these need skills, equipment, backup, and accountability. A daughter who lifts her father badly can injure them both. A son managing psychosis may need crisis teams, not cash.
[C1.4] Higher caregiver payments supply none of that. They fund a private arrangement with no supervisor, no shift relief, and no guaranteed standard. [C1.5] Then ask who gets the money. It flows to whoever is already expected to sacrifice: disproportionately women, poorer families, people with few alternatives. [C1.6] It can also give one relative control over another's money and care—a serious risk for disabled people whose autonomy is fragile.
[C1.7] Finally, the fiscal illusion. If payments are high enough to prevent burnout, they are expensive enough to compete with formal home care and respite. If they are low enough to be affordable, they fail PRO's own standard. That dilemma is the motion's, not ours.
[C1.8] The decisive point is simple: the motion mistakes cash for care. Do not assume unpaid care will continue—but do not answer that failure by subsidizing it more generously. Build real care. Reject the motion.
