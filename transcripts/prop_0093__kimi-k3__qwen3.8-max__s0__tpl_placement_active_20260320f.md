[P1.1] A city's curb is some of the most valuable public land it owns, yet most of it still goes to storing private cars—often for hours, nearly free. The motion asks something modest: stop protecting that majority and give more curb to uses that move people and goods. Note the wording: "more," not "all." CON must defend freezing most curb space for parking even where it clearly underperforms.
[P1.2] Start with arithmetic. A parking space serves one vehicle, which sits empty most of the day. The same curb as a bus lane carries dozens of passengers every few minutes; as a loading zone with ten-minute turnover, it serves dozens of deliveries daily—packages, food, supplies keeping homes stocked and storefronts open. As a bike lane it moves a stream of people in a fraction of the space.
[P1.3] By people and goods served per square meter, car storage is the worst-performing use of the curb.
[P1.4] Second, safety and reliability. Protected bike lanes are among the most proven tools for cutting cyclist deaths and serious injuries. Bus lanes let transit run on time; unreliable buses push riders into cars, worsening the congestion drivers complain about. Everyone, including drivers, wins when trips shift to lanes that work.
[P1.5] Third, deliveries. E-commerce and food delivery are how cities now function. Without legal loading space, drivers double-park—blocking the bus, the bike lane, and the general lane at once. Reserving delivery zones doesn't punish drivers; it ends the chaos that punishes everyone.
[P1.6] The CON case will lean on habit: people are used to parking at the door. But habit is not a principle for allocating scarce public space. We don't ration transit by who boarded first decades ago; why ration the curb by who parked there last? When a full bus waits behind a row of empty parked cars, the city has already made a choice—just the wrong one.
[P1.7] Time to make the right one.

[C1.1] The proposition treats curb space as a throughput contest, but cities are not pipelines; they are places where people must stop. On-street parking is not merely storage. It is the access point that lets a plumber fix a leak, a parent drop off a stroller, a disabled resident reach a pharmacy, a restaurant receive produce, and a shopper carry goods home. If you convert most parking to moving lanes without first proving each street can lose that access, you do not create efficiency; you push those needs into alleys, garages, sidewalks, and double-parked lanes.
[C1.2] The key flaw in PRO’s metric is that it counts movement past a curb, not service to the curb. A bus lane may carry many people by, but if the bakery cannot receive flour because its loading bay became a bike lane, the street loses more value than throughput gains. Delivery zones sound good, but without constant enforcement they become bus stops, ride-hail pickup, or expanded bike storage. The proposition asks us to trade a flexible, widely used access system for dedicated uses that often need enforcement, physical redesign, and political maintenance.
[C1.3] This is especially true for residents with the least flexibility. In many neighborhoods, transit is unreliable, biking is unsafe or impractical, and car ownership is how households manage work, children, and medical care. Removing parking raises rents for off-street spaces, pushes cars onto side streets, and burdens older and disabled people who need door-to-door access. The burden falls hardest where alternatives are weakest.
[C1.4] PRO cannot win by pointing to one congested corridor; the motion asks for a citywide preference. A city can modernize its streets without starving them of access. The curb should be managed street by street, not reassigned by ideological arithmetic. Until the city can replace parking’s access function, protecting most on-street parking is the safer, fairer default.
