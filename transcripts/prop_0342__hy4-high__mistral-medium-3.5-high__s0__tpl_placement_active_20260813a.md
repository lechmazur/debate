[P1.1] The issue is not whether children can operate a chatbot. It is whether schools can teach reading, history, science, and citizenship while ignoring systems that generate, rank, and personalize what students encounter. A pupil can cite invented sources from a fluent model, mistake a synthetic clip of a local official for evidence, or be led by a recommendation feed from curiosity to outrage before any adult names the mechanism. That is not a gadget skill; it is the input side of learning.
[P1.2] Core status is the minimum institutional response. A core subject has sequenced lessons, trained teachers, assessment, and timetable protection: early source checks, later probabilistic reasoning, privacy, bias, prompt discipline, synthetic-media skepticism, and practice naming emotional hooks. Students would not just be told to “verify”; they would compare a model’s answer with primary material, trace who benefits from a viral frame, label uncertainty, and ask what an elegant summary omits. Those habits strengthen every course because knowledge needs trustworthy inputs.
[P1.3] An optional module fails by design. It is too short for durable judgment, too late for children using these tools, and too easy to drop when exams press. It deepens inequality: well-resourced families coach skepticism at home; others get an assembly. Rapid change is not a reason to marginalize the subject; it is a reason to teach transferable principles—provenance, incentives, uncertainty, feedback loops, and the gap between fluency and truth—instead of chasing apps.
[P1.4] Nor does this crowd out substance. A core subject in AI literacy and manipulation-resistant media literacy is not more screen time; it is close reading for today’s information environment. Schools taught students to interrogate print because print could mislead. Now a source can be generated, tailored to a reader’s fears, and amplified for engagement.
[P1.5] Treating that as optional defends yesterday’s curriculum. Treating it as core gives students repeated, assessable practice in refusing manipulation and using powerful tools without surrendering judgment.

The solution is deeper integration across existing curricula, not the creation of yet another silo that will compete for scarce time, teacher attention, and budget resources.
[C1.2] Would we really trade Shakespeare for chatbot tutorials, or hands-on biology labs for lessons on prompt engineering?
[C1.3] Moreover, AI technology evolves far too rapidly for a stable, assessable core curriculum. By the time textbooks are printed and teachers certified, the platforms and models they describe have already changed. Most teachers lack specialized training in AI systems, and requiring every school to develop this expertise would strain already stretched resources and divert attention from their primary subjects. A core subject requires multi-year planning, standardized assessments, and uniform teacher training—all of which assume a stability that simply does not exist in this fast-moving field.
[C1.4] The result would be a curriculum that is either outdated on arrival or in constant flux, neither of which serves students well.
[C1.5] The optional module model is not a failure but a necessary flexibility. It allows schools to adapt content to local needs, student maturity levels, and emerging threats without displacing foundational knowledge. It prevents the dilution of core academic standards with skills that can be taught contextually across existing subjects. Most critically, it avoids the mistake of elevating transient technological fluency to the level of enduring intellectual disciplines.
[C1.6] Students must understand how to use tools, but they need even more to grasp the timeless principles those tools serve. Chasing every new platform as a core subject would leave schools with a mile-wide, inch-deep curriculum that serves neither tradition nor genuine progress.
