[P1.1] A child who learns only that her country liberated others, never that it enslaved, expelled, or interned its own, has not learned history. She has learned advertising, paid for by the state that committed the acts.
[P1.2] Public schools are where a democracy reproduces its citizens. If government can use compulsory schooling to celebrate itself while omitting its worst crimes, it has turned education into self-exoneration. A requirement to study major atrocities and abuses by one's own country is the minimum safeguard against that abuse of power.
[P1.3] First, self-government requires self-knowledge. Students will become voters, jurors, police officers, soldiers, and officials asked to judge claims of national necessity. A student taught only achievements and victimhood — that harm always comes from foreigners, never from her own institutions — is trained to excuse the next emergency detention, forced removal, or abusive war as unthinkable here, therefore necessary. A student who has studied slavery and lynching in the United States, residential schools in Canada, colonial famines and massacres under European empires, or Japanese wartime atrocities, learns a harder democratic skill: that decent nations, legal orders, and ordinary people can do terrible things when fear and hierarchy go unchecked.
[P1.4] Second, honest history strengthens, rather than weakens, attachment. Pride built on suppression is brittle; it shatters in adulthood when the omitted facts are discovered, and breeds cynicism that everything taught was propaganda. Pride that survives the full record — that can say we built institutions worth preserving precisely because we know what happens when they fail — is durable. Germany's mandated confrontation with the Holocaust did not make civic loyalty impossible; it made postwar democracy more credible at home and abroad.
[P1.5] This is not a demand for self-hatred or equal time for every grievance. It is a factual floor for major, well-documented state abuses, taught age-appropriately alongside achievements. A government confident in its country should not fear its own archive.

[C1.1] Study, yes. Government requirement, no. That distinction decides this debate.
[C1.2] PRO assumes the state can mandate honesty about its own crimes without capturing the lesson. But the power that writes the celebratory textbook is the same power that will write the confessional one. P1.2 warns that compulsory schooling lets government shape the national story for its own ends; the motion answers by handing government a new chapter to author. Whoever holds power defines "major atrocities."
[C1.3] In Turkey, the state curriculum presents the Armenian genocide as a fabrication. In Japan, state textbook approval has repeatedly softened accounts of the Nanjing massacre and forced wartime prostitution. In the United States, the same machinery swings between the 1619 framing and the 1776 framing with each administration. The mandate does not remove propaganda; it changes its sign — and every tool PRO builds will be inherited by the government PRO fears.
[C1.4] Second, compulsion corrodes the goal. Complex moral history taught because the law requires it becomes compliance theater: unit plans, checkbox tests, and parents who conclude the school is indoctrinating rather than educating. That backlash does not stay in the classroom; it elects the school boards and governors who rewrite the mandate. A curriculum that survives only as long as one coalition is not a safeguard against self-exoneration — it is self-exoneration waiting for its turn.
[C1.5] Third, Germany proves the opposite of P1.4. Its reckoning rests on independent courts, historians, journalists, and memorials — institutions no election can switch off. The requirement works there because civil society, not the state, holds the pen. Where that ecology is absent, a requirement is theater; where it is present, it adds little.
[C1.6] Students should study the full record — through independent scholarship, professional teacher standards, open archives, and a press the government does not control. A democracy protects truth by dispersing the power to tell it; this motion concentrates that power and calls the concentration a safeguard.

A factual floor for major, well-documented abuses restricts that control: deleting slavery, forced removal, or residential schools becomes a visible breach, not a quiet editorial choice.

[P2.5] Germany is a complement, not a counterexample. Courts, historians, journalists, and memorials made its school reckoning credible; required teaching made it universal, reaching children whose parents or locality would have avoided it. CON would leave her exposed, and call that freedom.
