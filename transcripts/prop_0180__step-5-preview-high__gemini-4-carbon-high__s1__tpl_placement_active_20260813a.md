[P1.1] The motion wins because it compares outcomes per dollar, not good intentions per dollar. Most active reforestation spends heavily before a single carbon unit is stored: site preparation, nursery-grown stock, planting, fencing, irrigation or replanting. Rewilding abandoned farmland largely removes those costs. Natural succession supplies its own seeds, spacing, species mixing, and self-thinning.
[P1.2] The main spending becomes protecting the site, restoring missing processes such as grazing or beaver, and controlling invasive species. That is a far cheaper way to buy a functioning ecosystem.
[P1.3] The carbon case is stronger than it looks. Abandoned cropland moves from annual root turnover and soil disturbance toward perennial root systems, leaf litter, woody debris, and recovering soil organic matter. A mixed native woodland stores carbon in biomass, roots, litter, deadwood, and soil, while many planted stands are simplified, even-aged, and vulnerable to drought, fire, pests, or eventual harvest. Active planting can create fast canopy cover, but it does not reliably rebuild the complex carbon economy of an old-growth-like system.
[P1.4] The biodiversity case is even clearer. Abandoned fields do not simply become plantations. They become mosaics: grassland, shrubland, young trees, mature woodland, wetlands, and deadwood habitat. A reverting New England field can move through brush and sapling stages toward native hardwood forest while also supporting snags, vernal pools, and beaver ponds.
[P1.5] In Europe, abandoned upland and marginal farmland can regenerate scrub, oak and pine woodland, and riparian habitat rather than uniform rows of one species.
[P1.6] Most active reforestation programs optimize planting numbers and survival rates. Rewilding optimizes ecological assembly. It buys more habitat structure, more species niches, more soil recovery, and more long-term carbon stability for each dollar.

[C1.1] Rewilding rests on a romantic fiction: walk away from farmland, spend pocket change on upkeep, and watch a rich native woodland assemble itself [P1.1]. Unlike nineteenth-century New England pastures [P1.4], modern abandoned agricultural land in Europe and North America suffers from compacted soils, excess nitrogen, depleted fungi, and fragmented landscapes cut off from heavy-seeded oaks and beeches, while overabundant deer devour the saplings that do sprout. Left unplanted, ex-cropland routinely stalls in arrested succession, overrun by buckthorn, multiflora rose, cheatgrass, or coarse pasture grasses.
[C1.2] That neither sequesters forest-scale carbon nor restores native biodiversity; it buys invasive scrub.
[C1.3] Worse, the remedies listed in [P1.2] blow up rewilding’s cost advantage and cannibalize its carbon claim. Fighting entrenched weeds across sunlit open fields for decades costs far more than planting a dense native canopy that shades invasives out early. Securing private European or American land rights and reintroducing large grazers or beavers near farms and towns brings steep fencing, permitting, ongoing management, and flood-liability bills. Ecologically, those processes undercut carbon storage: herbivores that maintain open grassland and scrub [P1.4] suppress woody biomass, while beaver ponds flooding nutrient-loaded cropland emit potent methane.
[C1.4] To win, rewilding must deliver both more carbon and more biodiversity per dollar, yet the very disturbances used to build open-mosaic habitat sacrifice forest carbon.
[C1.5] Finally, active reforestation is not monoculture timber farming destined for "eventual harvest" [P1.3]. Modern restoration programs plant mixed, climate-adapted native trees, often in targeted islands, to bridge seed gaps and keep pace with warming zones that passive dispersal cannot outrun. Above all, take the motion as written: it pits rewilding in Europe and North America against most active reforestation programs worldwide. A vast share of active reforestation operates in the tropics and subtropics, where year-round growing seasons, unmatched species richness, and far lower land and labor costs deliver dramatically more carbon and biodiversity per dollar than expensive, slow-growing temperate farmland ever could.

CON calls planting a one-time shade canopy [C3.1], but establishment is multi-year and survival-dependent even under active budgets.
[P4.2] Targeted control is a bounded investment around bottlenecks, not a decades-long field-wide weed war. If a site truly cannot recover passively, that is an exception, not a reason to price every hectare as if planting were cheap. Meanwhile unplanted hectares begin accumulating root, litter, soil, and deadwood carbon immediately; a failed planting is a sunk cost with no canopy.
[P4.3] A2: Most mosaic structure comes from the abandoned landscape itself—wet hollows, thin soils, old hedgerows, field edges, and existing scrub [P1.4]—not from universal grazers or beavers. [P4.4] Most reverting fields move toward closed-canopy native woodland, while a minority remain grassland, scrub, and wetland because biodiversity is half the motion.
