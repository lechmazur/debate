[P1.1] Traditional antitrust was built for an era of smokestacks and factory output, where competitive harm was measured through immediate price hikes. But dominant digital platforms operate in zero-price ecosystems governed by extreme network effects, high switching costs, and self-reinforcing data moats. In these environments, harm manifests not through immediate sticker prices, but through degraded privacy, reduced choice, and suppressed innovation. Above all, competition is rarely *in* the market; it is *for* the market.
[P1.2] Dominant platforms protect their hegemony through preemptive acquisitions, buying nascent rivals long before they achieve threatening scale. Under the status quo, antitrust authorities face an impossible evidentiary hurdle: they must prove, with predictive certainty, the unwritten future of an early-stage startup. Because regulators cannot easily prove what an emerging disruptor would have become five years down the road, incumbents absorb potential challengers with near impunity, extinguishing competition in the cradle.
[P1.3] A rebuttable presumption corrects this systemic failure in two decisive ways.
[P1.4] First, it resolves a crippling information asymmetry. The acquiring platform alone possesses the internal communications, user telemetry, and strategic rationale explaining why a target was pursued. If a transaction is motivated by authentic technical integration rather than threat suppression, the platform already has the evidence to demonstrate it. Placing the burden on the party holding that information is both economically efficient and procedurally fair.
[P1.5] Second, it protects dynamic competition without prohibiting valuable deals. The motion does not impose an outright ban; it establishes a conditional test. When an acquisition genuinely serves users—leveraging global infrastructure to expand and improve a superior tool—the platform can readily substantiate that consumer benefit. What it halts is the frictionless buyout of future rivals to insulate existing moats.
[P1.6] When digital markets tip, they stay tipped. Presuming anti-competitive intent unless substantial consumer benefit is proven is the only mechanism capable of keeping the digital economy contestable.

[C1.1] A presumption of illegality will not keep digital markets contestable. It taxes the exit that finances the next challenger, and it replaces evidence with a forecast no firm can certify.
[C1.2] Startup formation here runs on acquisition. Most new products never reach public markets. Founders and backers accept that risk because a larger platform may buy the product, fund it, and put it in front of users the startup cannot reach. If every such purchase starts anti-competitive unless the buyer proves substantial consumer benefit, the exit stops being a plan and becomes a gamble.
[C1.3] Capital avoids the markets where a dominant platform is the natural buyer. The motion promises to save nascent rivals. It starves the pipeline that creates them.
[C1.4] Shifting the burden does not fix the uncertainty. An acquirer may know its motive. It cannot prove that consumers will enjoy substantial benefit years later, any more than an agency can prove the target would have become an independent rival. Benefit is not a memo in the file.
[C1.5] It is a contested judgment about quality, price, privacy, and innovation, often in tension. The claim that the platform already has the evidence [P1.4] confuses strategic emails with consumer welfare. A rebuttal that requires certainty about an unknowable future is not a conditional screen. It is a ban enforced by delay, and competitors will file to run out the clock.
[C1.6] The presumption is too wide. Platforms buy talent, tools, and complements far more often than they buy a firm about to displace them. Merger law already reaches deals that may substantially lessen competition, including potential competition. It should not treat dominance as guilt or force the buyer to disprove a story.
[C1.7] Consumers gain when a small product inherits security and scale, and when one ecosystem buys in order to pressure another. Freeze those deals and you entrench today's structure while calling it contestability. Require likely harm. Do not presume it.

[P2.2] Far from killing the pipeline, ending frictionless buyouts redirects capital toward durable, independent competitors that contest the market.
[P2.3] CON next claims that proving substantial consumer benefit is an impossible forecast that acts as a de facto ban [C1.4, C1.5]. This conflates speculative market trajectories with concrete integration plans. Dominant platforms do not spend billions on blind faith; they possess rigorous product roadmaps, capacity metrics, and infrastructure timelines. If an acquisition genuinely confers scale, security, or superior functionality onto a small tool [C1.7], the buyer can readily document those tangible operational gains.
[P2.4] What an acquirer cannot prove is consumer benefit when the underlying motive is defensive—paying a premium solely to neutralize a nascent threat. Calling a requirement to demonstrate real consumer value an "impossible standard" simply concedes that many platform buyouts generate no consumer value at all.
[P2.5] Finally, we must weigh the asymmetric cost of error. CON insists existing law is sufficient [C1.6], but modern digital history proves the opposite. Regulators could not prevent the monopolization of social media, digital advertising, or mobile ecosystems because predicting an early-stage app's ten-year trajectory was impossible under traditional standards. The harms of false negatives here are permanent: once a digital market tips, self-reinforcing network effects prevent new entry.
[P2.6] If a merger is wrongly delayed, the startup remains viable to scale or partner with other firms. If an anti-competitive buyout slips through, monopoly is cemented forever. Dominant platforms must justify their acquisitions.
