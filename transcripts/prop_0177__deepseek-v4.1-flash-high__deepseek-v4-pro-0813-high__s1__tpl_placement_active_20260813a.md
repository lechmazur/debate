Nothing in the current record supports that jump. Routine transplantation must be judged not by early function but by long-term graft survival, infection risk, and predictable management of rejection.
[C1.2] Those endpoints do not yet exist.
[C1.3] PRO’s body-count argument assumes the intervention is ready to scale safely. It is not. The main unresolved problem is chronic cross-species rejection. Gene editing removes some known triggers, but xenotransplantation still activates coagulation, innate immunity, and antibody responses through incompletely understood pathways.
[C1.4] Nonhuman primate recipients often survive only months even with aggressive immunosuppression, and the few human compassionate-use cases have not shown durable success. Fifteen years is barely enough to run sequential trials and observe five- and ten-year outcomes—not to declare routine use.
[C1.5] Accelerated pathways now would make this worse. For a living, lifelong-monitored organ, an early safety failure is not like recalling a drug. A porcine pathogen, a cluster of catastrophic rejections, or unusual infections in immunosuppressed patients would injure recipients and likely halt the entire field. The correct regulatory response is rigorous, carefully monitored trials with survival and safety endpoints.
[C1.6] Speed is not a safety standard.
[C1.7] Finally, routine use would require biosecure animal facilities at enormous scale, genetic stability across herds, organ perfusion and transport logistics, and payment systems that do not simply deliver pig organs to the wealthy while the shortage continues for everyone else. PRO treats these as details that fall with scale. They are not; they are preconditions for routine care, and none is close to solved. The honest answer to a desperate shortage is not to promise a fifteen-year miracle and fast-track before the evidence exists.
[C1.8] It is to demand that xenotransplantation prove it can keep people alive for years, not weeks.
