[P1.1] Unanimity in EU foreign-policy sanctions gives every one of twenty-seven governments a veto over the entire bloc's response to aggression. That is not a design flaw at the margins—it is the central mechanism by which aggressor states neutralize European power. Qualified majority voting fixes this, and the case for it rests on three pillars: speed, credibility, and precedent that this can be done safely.
[P1.2] Speed first. When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, sanctions moved with unusual unity, but that unity was the exception bought by shock, not the rule. In ordinary times, single states have repeatedly slowed or diluted sanctions packages—Hungary alone has held up or watered down multiple rounds of Russia sanctions, forcing carve-outs on energy and individual oligarchs, and delaying renewal votes that require repeated unanimous consent every six months. An aggressor state only needs to cultivate one capital, through energy contracts, loans, or simple diplomatic flattery, to freeze the entire mechanism.
[P1.3] QMV removes that single point of leverage: an aggressor would need to buy off nine or ten member states to block action, which is a vastly harder and more expensive task than buying one.
[P1.4] Credibility follows directly. Deterrence works only if the threat of a response is believed in advance. A veto-based system tells every autocrat contemplating aggression that Europe's response is negotiable state-by-state—find the weak link, and the whole structure stalls. QMV converts sanctions from a diplomatic favor one government can withhold into a standing collective capability, which is what actually deters.
[P1.5] Finally, this is not a leap into the unknown. The EU already uses QMV for trade policy, competition enforcement, and most internal-market decisions—areas just as sensitive to national interest. If member states can trust majority rule to set tariffs against China or regulate energy markets, they can trust it to sanction the aggressor of the month. The proposition simply extends a proven tool to the area where delay costs the most: lives on the ground in states being invaded right now.
