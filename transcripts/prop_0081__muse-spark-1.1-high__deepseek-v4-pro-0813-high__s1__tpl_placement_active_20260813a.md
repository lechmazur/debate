[P1.1] Safety is not a premium upgrade. A dating app is not a streaming service where paying removes ads; it is a service whose core function is arranging meetings between strangers in private. When you are in the business of introductions, baseline protection is part of the product itself, not an add-on.
[P1.2] That baseline includes three non-negotiables. Identity verification - a selfie match, phone or ID check - stops the easiest vectors for catfishing, underage profiles, and serial banned users returning. Unlimited block and anti-circumvention tools stop harassment and stalking from continuing after a user says no. Rapid scam-response - scam pattern detection, one-tap reporting, freezing chats with suspected fraud accounts - stops romance scams that drain bank accounts.
[P1.3] Put those behind a paywall and you create exactly the wrong system. The people least able to pay - younger users, lower-income women, LGBTQ users in less tolerant areas - are statistically most targeted for harassment and scams. You tell them: pay or accept more risk.
[P1.4] Meanwhile the user who pays for verification still swims in an unverified pool, because protection only works if everyone can participate. One unverified scammer can message hundreds of free users a night.
[P1.5] It also inverts incentives. If safety earns subscription revenue, the platform financially benefits from remaining unsafe for free users. That is indefensible when block buttons cost essentially nothing to run and verification is now automated for under a dollar.
[P1.6] Dating apps already internalize other costs of scale - moderation, hosting - because they are necessary to operate. Safety is the same. Make verification, blocking, and scam response free by default, and monetize real luxuries: super likes, boosts, advanced filters. You can sell advantage without selling safety.
[P1.7] That standard protects users, restores trust, and treats physical safety as a right, not a perk.

It requires document authentication, biometric matching, manual fraud review, secure data retention, and legal compliance. Scam response is not a one-tap report; it requires fraud teams, account-freeze judgment, victim escalations, and chargeback work.
[C1.2] These are recurring adversarial costs, not buttons that cost nothing.
[C1.3] If the best versions of those tools cannot be priced, apps will not simply absorb the expense. They will throttle investment, automate poorly, or recoup the money through more surveillance advertising and data extraction. That is a worse safety outcome, not a fairer one. A free feature that barely detects scams is not real protection.
[C1.4] The identity proposal also cuts against its stated goal. If verification is optional, scammers and serial abusers will not opt into it; the free pool remains just as unverified as before. If it becomes effectively mandatory, it creates its own exclusion: people without government ID, those whose documents do not match their gender presentation, undocumented users, and privacy-conscious people. Making it free does not remove that harm; it pressures everyone into it.
[C1.5] The incentive argument points the wrong way. Subscription revenue from verification and scam response funds fraud teams and anti-circumvention engineering. Once safety becomes a pure cost center, the rational platform underfunds it. Smaller or niche apps would be hit hardest, which would entrench large incumbents and reduce the specialized LGBTQ or community-focused spaces the proposition claims to protect.
[C1.6] Free reporting, basic block, and scam warnings already exist as baseline tools. Charging for enhanced assurance is not selling safety; it is funding the capacity that makes safety possible. The goal should be well-resourced protection, not a free checkbox.
