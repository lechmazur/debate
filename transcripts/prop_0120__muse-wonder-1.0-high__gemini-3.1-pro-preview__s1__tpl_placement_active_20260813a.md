[P1.1] A surgeon earning a high salary and an heir living off a vast inherited portfolio can look identical to the income tax in a quiet year, and identical at the checkout under a consumption tax. They are not identical in ability to pay, security, or power. That blind spot is why wealthy households should face an annual tax on large net worth, above a high threshold, instead of leaving the system to tax mainly what people earn and spend.
[P1.2] First, income and consumption taxes miss accumulated advantage. Wealth compounds through unrealized appreciation and assets that can be borrowed against rather than sold. Wage earners are taxed on every paycheck and shoppers on every purchase, while the largest fortunes can grow for years with little current tax and sometimes pass to heirs with much of the gain never treated as income. A modest annual levy on net worth above, say, tens of millions closes that timing loophole: it taxes the stock of command over resources, not just the moments when money moves.
[P1.3] Second, that stock matters for fairness and democracy. Wealth is far more concentrated than income, and it buys more than consumption: resilience against shocks, cheap credit, influence over markets and politics, and a funded head start for the next generation. Relying mainly on income taxes overburdens visible labor; relying on consumption taxes falls hardest on households that must spend most of what they get. A high-threshold wealth tax asks most from those with the greatest margin, without raising the price of bread or the tax on an extra hour of work.
[P1.4] Third, at a low rate it can improve the economy: it broadens the base, supplying revenue to ease rates elsewhere or fund infrastructure and education, and it nudges capital toward productive returns rather than rewarding idle land and dynastic warehousing.
[P1.5] Taxing only income and spending leaves the central fact — a self-perpetuating fortune — untaxed.

[C1.1] The proposition relies on a fantasy where wealth is a static pile of cash easily measured and scooped up. In reality, an annual wealth tax is an administrative nightmare that destroys productive enterprise, which is why nearly every advanced economy that has tried it has ultimately abandoned it.
[C1.2] First, an annual wealth tax fails on basic mechanics. As PRO notes in [P1.2], wealth heavily involves unrealized appreciation. But unrealized wealth is largely illiquid—tied up in private businesses, startup equity, and real estate. Valuing these complex, privately held assets annually requires an army of auditors and invites endless litigation.
[C1.3] More destructively, because the wealth is illiquid, an annual tax forces owners to liquidate portions of successful, job-creating enterprises year after year simply to pay the IRS. PRO argues in [P1.4] that this nudges capital toward "productive returns," but forcing founders to sell off equity to cover a tax on paper gains actually drains capital directly away from productive, growing businesses.
[C1.4] Second, wealth is highly mobile. When capital is taxed merely for existing, it flees. This is not theoretical; the OECD experience provides a clear empirical track record. Numerous European nations experimented with wealth taxes in recent decades, and almost all repealed them.
[C1.5] They discovered that the taxes raised dismal revenue, spurred massive capital flight, and cost nearly as much to administer as they collected. Driving investment across borders doesn't just annoy billionaires; it starves the domestic economy of the capital necessary for innovation, infrastructure, and wage growth.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO’s core grievance—that the wealthy can borrow against assets or pass them to heirs untaxed—highlights a flaw in the current income tax code, not a mandate for a wealth tax. We can easily close loopholes, such as eliminating the stepped-up basis at death, without abandoning the realization principle. Relying on income and consumption taxes ensures we tax actual, measurable cash flow rather than phantom, fluctuating paper valuations. We should fix the tax code we have, not import a structurally broken system with a proven track record of failure.

Local governments value real estate annually for property taxes; estates value private businesses at death. Difficulty is real for some assets, but it is a reason for clear valuation rules, deferral until sale for genuinely illiquid holdings, and payment plans — not for exempting the largest fortunes altogether.
[P2.2] Nor does a modest tax force productive firms to be dismantled. If net worth above the threshold is taxed at around one or two percent, a diversified fortune can normally pay from liquid holdings or borrowing capacity, and a fast-growing business earns far more than the levy. For a truly illiquid founder, the state can accept deferred payment with interest or a small equity claim payable on sale. What CON calls “draining capital” [C1.3] is usually a small ownership transfer, not a factory closure: plant, workers, and operations remain.
[P2.3] Europe proves less than claimed. Many repealed taxes were leaky designs with relatively low thresholds, sweeping exemptions, and easy movement within an integrated region — a recipe for low revenue and avoidance. That argues against a badly carved-up tax, not against a broad, high-threshold tax in a large economy backed by reporting and an exit charge on renouncing residence. Some European jurisdictions have kept wealth taxes — administration is possible.
[P2.4] Finally, CON concedes the central abuse, then offers a patch that arrives too late. Ending stepped-up basis at death [C1.6] still lets a fortune compound, fund borrowing, and confer power untaxed for decades before any realization event. If they also tax deemed gains at death, they have accepted taxing paper wealth — just after a lifetime of deferral. A small annual levy reaches the stock while it is conferring advantage.
[P2.5] The choice is not perfect valuation versus perfect simplicity. It is manageable imperfection versus permanently ignoring accumulated wealth.

This guarantees the taxpayer actually has the liquidity to pay, completely avoiding destructive forced liquidations, massive capital flight, and unprecedented government encroachment into private enterprise.
