[P1.1] Every pretrial system predicts. The only question is whether the prediction is written down, tested, and open to challenge, or whether it lives in a judge's gut and a defendant's bank balance. Today, in most American courtrooms, a man with a stable job and a serious record of violence posts bond in an afternoon, while a woman charged with shoplifting sits for weeks because she cannot find five hundred dollars. That is risk-based detention already, just with the risk defined as poverty.
[P1.2] The proposition fixes the variable. Detain on validated evidence of serious violent reoffending, not on cash and not on hunches. Validated means the tool has been tested against real outcomes, its error rates are known, and its inputs can be examined in open court. A judge's intuition offers none of that.
[P1.3] You cannot cross-examine a hunch. You cannot audit it for racial bias. You cannot appeal it on the record. A scored instrument gives the defense something to attack, which is more due process, not less.
[P1.4] Now the hard part: yes, detention rates may rise. The rate is the wrong metric. Serious violence is concentrated in a small share of defendants, and the people it lands on are overwhelmingly poor, Black, and brown residents of the same neighborhoods reformers claim to protect. A domestic abuser released on Friday because his charge was "only" a misdemeanor is a foreseeable, preventable harm to a specific woman.
[P1.5] If a validated score would have flagged him and did not because the court was forbidden to use it, that woman paid for an abstraction about aggregate rates.
[P1.6] The honest comparison is not detention versus freedom. It is structured, contestable, correctable risk assessment versus the opaque system we already run. Broader use of validated tools lets courts release the low-risk majority with confidence precisely because it identifies the high-risk few accurately. Rates may shift.
[P1.7] Who is detained shifts more, and in the right direction.

[C1.2] First, the math. Serious violent reoffending during the pretrial window is rare. When you predict a rare event, even a well-validated tool flags far more people who would never hurt anyone than people who would. "Validated" means the tool ranks groups better than chance.
[C1.3] It does not mean the person in front of the judge is dangerous. Even in the top risk bands, most flagged defendants are not rearrested for violence. PRO's phrase "even if it increases detention rates" is a commitment to jailing a majority of false positives to catch a minority.
[C1.4] Second, what the tool actually measures. These instruments learn from arrests, not violence, and arrests reflect where police patrol and whom they stop. [C1.5] "Contestable" is a courtroom fiction. A public defender with minutes per hearing cannot rebut a number, and judges defer to scores that look scientific. The gut PRO fears does not disappear. It gets a decimal point.
[C1.6] Third, detention itself does damage. A few days in jail can cost someone a job, an apartment, custody of a child. Detained defendants plead guilty more often because pleading is the fastest way out. And people destabilized this way are more likely to reoffend later.
[C1.7] Broader detention can manufacture the crime it claims to prevent.
[C1.8] Finally, PRO's domestic abuser. Courts can already detain a specific person after an individualized hearing on evidence of specific threats. That is targeted. The motion lowers the bar to a statistical label.
[C1.9] We may punish people for what they are proven to have done, not for what a model predicts people like them might do.
