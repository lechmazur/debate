[P1.1] One-off baby bonuses treat fertility like a transaction, while housing and childcare support treats it like a life decision. In East Asia's high-cost urban centers, a lump sum payment vanishes against decades of rent, tuition, and lost wages. Parents in Seoul or Tokyo face housing costs that consume a crushing share of income; a one-time check cannot offset the mortgage burden that makes starting a family feel financially reckless. The money gets absorbed into savings or daily expenses, leaving the structural barrier untouched.
[P1.2] Pronatalist cash schemes across the region have repeatedly disappointed precisely because they mistake the symptom for the disease.
[P1.3] By contrast, affordable housing and subsidized childcare attack the recurring costs that actually deter parenthood. When governments cap childcare fees or prioritize public housing for young families, they reduce the monthly outflow that forces couples to delay children. This is not a marginal incentive; it is a permanent reduction in the price of raising a child. Singapore's housing grants for newlyweds and Japan's childcare expansion show that when the state lowers ongoing costs, fertility intentions shift more durably than when governments mail checks after birth.
[P1.4] Moreover, structural support changes expectations in ways cash cannot. A one-off bonus signals a handout, not a partnership. Affordable housing and childcare signal that the government will share the burden throughout childhood, which matters in societies where grandparents can no longer provide informal care and dual-income households are the norm. The decision to have a baby is forward-looking; parents need confidence that costs will remain manageable at age five and fifteen, not just at birth.
[P1.5] They are deciding whether to commit to a twenty-year project, not whether to accept a signing bonus.
[P1.6] Cash bonuses are a down payment on a debt that structural support would actually retire. East Asian governments will raise birth rates more by making family life affordable than by offering parents a receipt for the first month.

[C1.2] Make housing cheaper in a tight urban market and much of the gain can capitalize into higher prices; expand childcare and the benefit may mainly let mothers work more, not have another child. The state pays either way, but the birth effect is weaker.
[C1.3] Cash also targets the marginal parent. East Asia’s fertility crisis is concentrated in delayed first births and couples who are almost ready. A salient, immediate payment can tip timing and first parity; a twenty-year childcare system cannot. [C1.4] That is why places with ambitious childcare and housing commitments can still post very low fertility: they reduce costs without changing the immediate decision.
[C1.5] Implementation matters too. Housing affordability is a macroeconomic problem with slow supply chains and local vetoes; childcare expansion takes years to train staff and build places. A baby bonus can be legislated, delivered, and adjusted now. It can be targeted by income or parity, and it is more politically feasible than remaking housing markets.
[C1.6] The motion asks which instrument will raise birth rates more. The answer is the one that reaches the decision point with less leakage and less delay. Structural support may make family life better; it does not follow that it raises births more than cash parents can actually use before the baby arrives.
