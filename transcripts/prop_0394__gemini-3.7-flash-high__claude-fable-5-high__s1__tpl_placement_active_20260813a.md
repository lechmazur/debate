[P1.1] Higher education should evaluate a student’s intellectual ability, not their administrative stamina. When institutions treat captions, recordings, and accessible course materials as exceptional accommodations granted only through bureaucratic hurdles, they disadvantage the very students who need support most. Establishing accessibility as the default baseline transforms the university into an equitable, modern learning environment.
[P1.2] First, the traditional accommodation model imposes a severe structural penalty on disabled and neurodivergent students. Requiring students to repeatedly disclose medical diagnoses, secure costly documentation, and negotiate individual permissions class by class causes pervasive delays, social stigma, and academic disruption. Shifting the presumption ensures immediate equity. A student with hearing loss, mobility challenges, or a chronic illness receives full access from day one, rather than falling behind while waiting for administrative clearance.
[P1.3] Second, universal design improves pedagogical outcomes for the entire student body. Captioned recordings and accessible materials are not remedial crutches; they are powerful learning tools. They enable non-native speakers to grasp nuanced vocabulary, allow working students and caregivers to manage competing demands, and give all learners the opportunity to review dense, complex material at their own pace. Multimodal access reinforces retention and deepens conceptual mastery.
[P1.4] Crucially, this policy is not an inflexible mandate. The resolution explicitly includes a vital safeguard: materials are presumptive *unless* there is a strong pedagogical reason not to. If a course centers on sensitive personal disclosures, or an interactive performance lab genuinely requires unrecorded spontaneity, faculty retain full academic freedom to opt out and explain that rationale.
[P1.5] The central question is where the burden of proof belongs. The status quo forces vulnerable students to justify why they need access. A presumptive standard simply asks educators to justify why withholding access serves a genuine educational goal. That shift honors academic nuance while ensuring no learner is arbitrarily excluded from the classroom.

[C1.1] A “presumption” in institutional life is never just a nudge. Once accessibility becomes the default and deviation requires justifying yourself to administrators, risk-averse compliance offices will treat every exception as a lawsuit waiting to happen. The escape hatch PRO celebrates — “strong pedagogical reason” — will be adjudicated by lawyers, not teachers. In practice, this proposition is a mandate wearing a disclaimer.
[C1.2] That mandate does real damage. First, it chills the classroom. Seminars on abortion, race, religion, or clinical case discussions depend on students speaking half-formed, risky thoughts aloud. When every session is recorded by default, students self-censor and faculty flatten their teaching into clip-proof scripts.
[C1.3] The professor who wants an unrecorded discussion must now file a justification and defend it — the surest way to guarantee most won’t bother, and candor quietly dies.
[C1.4] Second, universal compliance is ruinously expensive, and the predictable response is deletion, not access. When UC Berkeley faced demands to caption and remediate its vast library of free public lectures, it removed tens of thousands of videos rather than absorb the cost. Presumptive rules don’t conjure resources; they force institutions — especially underfunded community colleges — to strip content, cut adjunct-taught courses, or produce garbage auto-captions that actively mislead deaf students. Everyone gets equal access to less.
[C1.5] Third, blanket recording degrades learning. Instructors widely observe that lecture capture hollows out attendance, and passively re-watching video is among the weakest study methods — it feels like learning while replacing the active struggle that produces it.
[C1.6] The real choice is not access versus exclusion. It is between targeted, well-funded accommodations — captioners and materials directed precisely at students with documented need — and a diffuse universal mandate that spreads resources thin, chills speech, and shrinks the curriculum. Fix the accommodation process where it is slow or stigmatizing. Don’t answer bureaucratic failure by building a bigger bureaucracy and pointing it at every professor in the country.
