[P1.1] A pack of crisps goes from 200 grams to 165. The price tag doesn't move. The shopper walks out believing nothing happened. That is not a small gap in information — it is a price rise deliberately routed around the one number people actually check.
[P1.2] The whole justification for letting prices float freely is that shoppers can see them and respond. Competition works through comparison. Shrinkflation is engineered to defeat comparison: the headline price stays anchored, the size change sits in small print on the front of a redesigned pack, and the per-unit cost — the only figure that tells you what you're really paying — is either absent, printed in inconsistent units, or displayed in six-point type on a shelf edge. When Carrefour began flagging shrunk products on its shelves in France a couple of years ago, the reaction was telling: manufacturers objected loudly, which is not how firms behave about information they think consumers would shrug at.
[P1.3] So the motion asks for something modest and precise. Where a package shrinks without a proportionate headline price cut, show the unit price change clearly and, for a defined window, flag that the size has fallen. Not a ban. Not a price control.
[P1.4] Not a judgement about whether shrinking a pack is legitimate — it often is, given input costs. Just: don't let the change be invisible.
[P1.5] The mechanism is already sitting there. Apps compute price-per-100g automatically because they hold the data; supermarkets already run unit pricing schemes and increasingly use digital shelf labels that can be updated centrally. The marginal cost of honest display is a line of code and a label template.
[P1.6] Which puts CON in an awkward place. To win, they must defend an arrangement where the seller knows exactly what changed, has the technical capacity to say so at near-zero cost, and the buyer is structurally prevented from noticing. That isn't free-market pricing. It's a price signal with the volume turned down, and the people it silences are the ones counting pennies at the till.

[C1.1] Notice what the crisps example actually proves. The shelf edge already carries a price per 100 grams. That number moved the day the pack shrank. The shopper who "walks out believing nothing happened" walked past the disclosure.
[C1.2] So the problem is not that the information is hidden; it is that people do not read it. A mandate to display it "more clearly" does not cure inattention — it just adds another line to the same label nobody is reading.
[C1.3] Second, the motion aims the gun at the wrong party. Retailers and apps do not shrink packs; manufacturers do. The proposal makes the shop legally responsible for detecting, dating and flagging thousands of packaging decisions made upstream — including reformulations where a smaller pack contains a concentrated product, recipe changes, multipack rejigs, and relaunches under a new barcode. Every one of those needs a rule: what counts as a "proportionate" cut, how long the "defined window" runs, whether a 2% change triggers a flag.
[C1.4] That is not "a line of code." It is a permanent compliance department, and independents and corner shops pay it as a fixed cost while the big chains absorb it. The mandate concentrates the very market PRO says it wants to make competitive.
[C1.5] Third, and decisively: flag the shrink and you redirect the cost pass-through into the places nobody can see. A manufacturer facing a mandatory "this pack got smaller" sticker has three alternatives — raise the headline price, which the motion does not flag; relaunch as a "new" product to reset the window; or hold the pack size and quietly swap in cheaper ingredients. Pack size is the one cost cut that is measurable and printed on the label. Punish it selectively and you get quality cuts instead, which are truly invisible.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO's own evidence undercuts the mandate. Carrefour flagged shrinkflation voluntarily, because it wins customers. Apps compute unit prices because it wins users. Where shoppers value the signal, the market already sells it — no statute required.
