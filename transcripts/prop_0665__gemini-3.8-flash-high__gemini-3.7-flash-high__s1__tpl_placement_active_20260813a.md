[P1.1] Severe inherited mitochondrial diseases are among the most harrowing conditions in medicine. Disorders such as Leigh syndrome and MELAS starve the body’s cells of energy, subjecting infants and young children to progressive neurodegeneration, intractable seizures, multi-organ failure, and premature death. Because mitochondrial DNA is inherited maternally, carrier mothers face the agonizing prospect of passing severe, incurable illness to every child they conceive. Mitochondrial replacement therapy (MRT) offers the only clinical mechanism for these women to have genetically related children free of that devastating transmission.
[P1.2] Public health systems must fund it.
[P1.3] First, public funding aligns directly with the core mandate of healthcare: preventing catastrophic, fatal disease. MRT is not an elective enhancement; it is vital preventive medicine. By transferring maternal and paternal nuclear DNA into an enucleated donor egg containing healthy mitochondria, the technique simply replaces defective cellular powerplants. Withholding public coverage forces families into an intolerable gamble: risk conceiving a child condemned to severe suffering, or surrender the possibility of having a biologically related child.
[P1.4] Second, funding MRT is a fiscally sound investment. Managing severe mitochondrial diseases imposes enormous lifelong costs on public systems, demanding frequent intensive care admissions, mechanical ventilation, surgical interventions, and multidisciplinary palliative care. A single patient’s lifetime medical expenses easily run into the millions. A funded cycle of MRT requires a one-time preventive expenditure that permanently breaks the cycle of disease, liberating healthcare resources and sparing families profound grief.
[P1.5] Finally, public funding safeguards equity and clinical governance. If left to private out-of-pocket markets, MRT becomes an exclusive privilege for the wealthy, while middle- and low-income families are left behind or driven toward unregulated cross-border clinics with poor safeguards. Integrating MRT into the public health framework ensures rigorous patient selection, long-term monitoring through standardized registries, and democratic access governed by clinical need rather than ability to pay.

[C1.1] Public health systems operate under finite budgets to maximize collective well-being, uphold strict safety standards, and fund proven medical care. The proposition conflates preventing disease with subsidizing a preference for genetic relatedness, while ignoring the severe biological risks and unjustifiable opportunity costs of state-funded germline modification.
[C1.2] First, MRT is an experimental and biologically fraught technology. Far from a simple replacement of cellular powerplants, MRT disrupts delicate mito-nuclear interactions, risking incompatibilities between donor mitochondrial DNA and parental nuclear DNA that can impair cellular function. Crucially, the phenomenon of "reversion" remains a persistent hazard: carried-over mutant mitochondria can preferentially replicate in the developing embryo, ultimately causing the very fatal diseases the procedure aimed to prevent. Because MRT alters the female germline, these unpredictable mutations and epigenetic perturbations will be permanently inherited by future generations without their consent.
[C1.3] Public health systems cannot responsibly finance heritable genetic experimentation.
[C1.4] Second, public funding cannot be justified as the only preventive path. Safe, established alternatives—such as conventional egg donation and adoption—already completely eliminate the risk of transmitting mitochondrial disease. The only unique benefit MRT offers is fulfilling a parental desire for partial genetic relatedness. While emotionally compelling, public health systems do not exist to guarantee biological lineage.
[C1.5] Scarce collective resources must prioritize medical necessity and health outcomes, not expensive, high-risk reproductive engineering.
[C1.6] Finally, funding MRT carries severe opportunity costs. Funneling millions into specialized, resource-intensive fertility protocols diverts critical capital away from developing treatments for living mitochondrial patients and funding cost-effective frontline healthcare. Because MRT carries profound biological risks, solves a genetic preference rather than an unpreventable health crisis, and misallocates scarce public funds, public systems must not fund it.
