[P1.1] A safe-third-country rule lets a state refuse even to hear an asylum claim on the premise that another country will provide protection instead. Shedding that duty without examining the danger someone fled is justified only when protection is genuinely transferred, never dumped out of sight. Bare survival behind a fence is not safety. Unless the destination state guarantees enforceable rights to appeal, work, and family unity, transfer becomes abandonment by proxy.
[P1.2] Each right is indispensable. Without an enforceable right to appeal, one rushed or biased official in the destination state can reject a valid claim and deport a refugee straight back to persecution. Offloading people into an unreviewable screening system does not prevent refoulement; it launders deportation through a middleman.
[P1.3] Without an enforceable right to work, physical shelter decays into constructive expulsion. Aid rations shrink while backlogs stretch for years. Banning lawful employment forces refugees into black-market exploitation or destitution severe enough to pressure them back toward danger. Conversely, legal work lets displaced people support themselves and contribute locally instead of fueling resentment in host communities.
[P1.4] Without an enforceable right to family unity, transfers punish families for fleeing responsibly. Families rarely escape conflict intact; often one parent flees first to spare young children a lethal journey. Trapping that parent in a third country that bars reunification with a spouse or child, whether still in danger or already in the transferring state, inflicts indefinite separation and drives desperate people back to smugglers.
[P1.5] Above all, these rights must be legally enforceable, not left to diplomatic assurances or administrative grace. Political moods turn quickly against outsiders. If transferred refugees lack a binding remedy when officials deny work permits, block family reunification, or order summary removal, paper promises vanish once scrutiny fades. States may share responsibility for refugees, never outsource them into limbo.
[P1.6] Strip away any one of these enforceable rights, and a safe-third-country rule loses all justification.

[C1.2] Consider the practical realities of global displacement. The vast majority of displaced people find haven in developing and middle-income nations. Many of these states provide genuine physical sanctuary, shelter, healthcare, and international oversight, yet navigate fragile economies and high domestic unemployment. Conditioning safe-third-country arrangements on an enforceable right to work forces these host nations into an impossible bind: grant open labor market access to non-citizens or be declared unfit to provide refuge.
[C1.3] Treating physical safety as illegitimate without formal employment rights discourages regional partners from offering shelter at all, even when basic needs are fully met through state support and humanitarian aid.
[C1.4] Likewise, safe-third-country cooperation does not require destination countries to duplicate the protracted judicial appeals or expansive family reunification statutes of wealthy Western states. Effective protection against refoulement can be reliably secured through streamlined administrative reviews, independent monitoring, and UNHCR oversight, while family unity is frequently coordinated transnationally rather than through domestic courts. Demanding an identical, enforceable institutional basket everywhere mistakes legal form for substantive safety.
[C1.5] When safe-third-country rules are prohibited under this absolutist test, the alternative is not broader rights. The alternative is overloaded asylum systems, hardened borders, and desperate secondary journeys that force families into the hands of human smugglers. Safe-third-country rules are justified when they secure genuine safety from persecution and prevent return to danger. Conditioning protection on an exhaustive checklist makes the perfect the enemy of survival.
