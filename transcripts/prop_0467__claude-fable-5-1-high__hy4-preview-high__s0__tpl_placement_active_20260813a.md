[P1.1] A missile that knocks out a city's power grid in January is an armed attack. Nobody disputes it. [P1.3] The case rests on one principle: law should judge attacks by their effects, not their delivery mechanism. International law has never asked whether harm arrived by bomber, artillery, or sabotage. It asks what happened to people and property. When a cyberattack disables water treatment, grounds air traffic, or takes a hospital network offline for weeks, the human consequences are indistinguishable from kinetic destruction.
[P1.4] Pretending otherwise rewards the attacker for choosing a cleverer weapon.
[P1.5] Consider what the current ambiguity has produced. Ukraine's grid was taken down by hackers in winter. A single piece of malware spread across the world, crippling shipping companies and medical systems. Ransomware shut a major American fuel pipeline.

[P1.8] The framework is the one states have used for decades; the motion simply refuses to exempt one weapon class from it.
[P1.9] The deepest point is about deterrence and miscalculation. Attackers escalate when consequences are unclear. A bright, declared rule—cross this line against civilians and you have committed an armed attack—forces adversaries to weigh costs before they act. Ambiguity does not prevent war.
