[P1.1] Heritage is finite. Tourism demand, at peak season, is not. When those two curves collide at a fragile site, somebody has to be cut from the queue. The only serious question is who.
[P1.2] The motion answers that correctly: residents first. Three reasons.
[P1.3] First, stewardship. Sites like Venice's basilicas, the Acropolis, and Angkor's temples are physically consumed by footfall — worn stone, humidity from crowds, structural vibration. The damage is cumulative and largely irreversible. Residents bear that loss permanently; tourists absorb it for a week.
[P1.4] If the asset is ruined, residents lose their cultural patrimony while the tour bus moves on to the next stop. Priority booking is not a perk. It is partial compensation for the structural damage residents absorb on behalf of the rest of us.
[P1.5] Second, carrying capacity. At peak demand, rationing is inevitable — the only variable is whether it is done openly or by price alone. Pure price rationing has already failed in Dubrovnik, Barcelona, and Venice: cruise-ship day-trippers still arrive, locals are simply priced out, and the damage continues. Resident priority is a second axis of rationing that does what pricing alone cannot — it protects the asset while keeping the host community inside the experience they underwrite.
[P1.6] Third, precedent and principle. Museums already run members' hours; national parks charge residents less; many fragile sites cap daily entry. The motion just extends that logic to peak-season pressure at the worst-affected places. It does not exclude visitors.
[P1.7] It orders access.
[P1.8] The framing the opposition will offer — "discrimination against tourists" — inverts the actual inequality. The current default discriminates against the people who pay for, live with, and inherit the damage from these places. Overtourism is a stress test. The proposition is the rational triage.
