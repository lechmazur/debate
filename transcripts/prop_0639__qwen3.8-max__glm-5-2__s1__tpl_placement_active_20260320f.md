[P1.1] The question is whether ordinary citizens, chosen by lot, can be trusted not merely to advise but to decide. For specific, bounded policy questions, they should.
[P1.2] Sortition solves a legitimacy problem that elections alone cannot. Elections filter politics through parties, donors, and professional campaigners. They often produce representatives who are socially unrepresentative and constantly positioning for re-election. A randomly selected assembly, stratified by age, geography, gender, and other relevant factors, can mirror the public far better than a professional legislature.
[P1.3] When citizens see their own demographic and moral diversity in the room, the decision carries democratic weight that backroom bargaining lacks.
[P1.4] More importantly, binding power makes deliberation real. If an assembly is only advisory, politicians can commission it, praise it, and then bury its conclusions when inconvenient. That teaches cynicism. But if the assembly’s answer is binding, participants must take responsibility for consequences, experts and stakeholders must engage seriously, and the public gets a clear connection between judgment and outcome.
[P1.5] Advisory status preserves elite veto; binding status creates civic accountability.
[P1.6] This is especially valuable on issues where elected officials are trapped by short-term incentives. Consider long-term climate policy, electoral reform, or local land-use rules. Politicians may know reform is necessary but fear attack ads, donor retaliation, or primary challenges. A citizens’ assembly can hear evidence, weigh trade-offs, and decide without facing re-election next year.
[P1.7] The point is not to replace all legislatures, but to use binding sortition where ordinary politics is most captive to organized minorities or electoral fear.
[P1.8] Critics will say that randomly chosen citizens lack expertise or mandate. But the alternative is not rule by wisdom; it is rule by politicians who are often less informed, more partisan, and more vulnerable to capture. A well-designed assembly gives ordinary people time, balanced briefing, testimony, and deliberation—and then trusts them to act. On specific policy questions, that trust should be decisive.

[C1.1] The central problem with binding sortition is accountability—not expertise, not deliberation capacity, but the fact that power without accountability is illegitimate by definition.
[C1.2] PRO claims binding power "makes deliberation real" because advisory assemblies can be ignored. That gets the relationship backwards. The reason advisory assemblies can be ignored is precisely the safeguard that makes them democratic: elected officials remain answerable to the public for what they do with the assembly's recommendations. Strip away that veto and you haven't removed elite power—you've transferred power to a group that faces zero consequences for getting it wrong.
[C1.3] A randomly selected assembly is representative in demographics but not in consent. No citizen voted for these decision-makers. No citizen can vote them out. They serve once, decide, and return to private life with no reputational stake, no electoral risk, no institutional accountability.
[C1.4] PRO calls this "civic accountability," but accountability requires a mechanism—reelection, judicial review, public referendum. Binding sortition offers none.
[C1.5] The examples PRO cites—climate policy, electoral reform, land-use—prove the danger. These are precisely issues where decisions impose costs on specific groups, require trade-offs between competing goods, and demand ongoing implementation. A binding assembly could impose a carbon tax, redraw electoral boundaries, or rezone neighborhoods, and affected citizens would have no recourse except to wait for the next assembly—or hope courts intervene.
[C1.6] PRO also claims politicians are "more vulnerable to capture." But a small, time-limited assembly is far easier to capture than a legislature. Whoever controls the briefing materials, selects expert witnesses, and frames the question options shapes the outcome enormously. Legislatures have competing committees, party oversight, and public scrutiny over months and years.
[C1.7] An assembly meets for weeks, behind relatively closed doors, with information curated by facilitators whose influence is largely invisible.
[C1.8] Advisory assemblies capture the genuine benefit—diverse voices informing policy—without severing the accountability link that makes democratic authority legitimate. Binding power solves a problem that doesn't exist while creating one that does.

[P2.2] CON says the advisory veto is a safeguard. If an assembly is stratified to mirror the public, hears competing experts, and publishes reasons, letting legislators ignore it is not accountability; it is elite insurance. The public pays for consultation but gets no decision. Bindingness makes the process honest: the question must be clear, evidence must be faced, and the outcome must be implemented.
[P2.3] On capture, compare real institutions. Legislatures are continuously lobbied, financed, and disciplined by parties; their “openness” often means organized interests have permanent access. A citizens’ assembly can be insulated: random selection, public sessions, adversarial expert panels, stakeholder testimony, published materials, and balanced facilitation rules. That is a design problem, and easier to fix than removing money from elections.
[P2.4] Finally, “no citizen voted for these decision-makers” [C1.3] proves too much. Citizens accept bounded decision procedures when they are fair, public, and limited. Binding referenda bind without an ongoing electoral check; a deliberative assembly improves on that by requiring learning and reason-giving. If CON’s standard is “only elected officials may bind,” courts, agencies, and referenda all fail.
[P2.5] The decisive comparison is not idealized elections versus perfect assemblies. It is whether, on a specific question, a fair citizen body is less likely to be distorted by short-term incentives and organized pressure than politicians seeking survival. It is. That is the legitimacy gap sortition fills, and why advisory status merely preserves it.

[C2.8] The motion asks whether binding power should replace advisory status. Advisory assemblies get the deliberative diversity PRO values while preserving the one thing that makes democratic authority legitimate: the ability of the public to reward good decisions and punish bad ones.
