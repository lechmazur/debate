[P1.1] Paying the same for less is a price rise wearing a disguise. When a chocolate bar drops from 200 to 180 grams, or cereal from 750 to 680, while the headline price holds steady, shoppers are being charged more per bite without being told. That is not innovation or competition. It is profit through confusion, and it only works if people do not notice.
[P1.2] Defenders will say the weight is printed on the pack and unit prices already exist. In theory yes. In practice no. Net weights change in small print on redesigned packaging meant to look identical. Unit-price labels are tiny, low-contrast, sometimes missing, and calculated in shifting units that make comparison exhausting.
[P1.3] On food apps the problem is worse: photos stay the same, quantities hide below the fold, and subscription reorders arrive smaller with no warning. Expecting busy shoppers to catch every quiet gram-shaving across thousands of products is absurd.
[P1.4] A clear notice requirement fixes this asymmetry at minimal cost. Supermarkets and apps already track barcodes, weights, and prices to the decimal. Requiring a short, time-limited flag — smaller pack, higher price per 100 grams — when size falls without a matching price fall simply makes the truth salient at the moment of choice. It bans nothing.
[P1.5] Manufacturers remain free to resize for costs, health, or waste reasons, and shoppers remain free to buy. It only prevents the extra margin from depending on inattention.
[P1.6] This matters most for those who can least afford the trick. Wealthy shoppers may shrug at a few missing grams. Families stretching a weekly shop, pensioners on fixed incomes, and anyone comparing value across brands pay the price. A fair market rewards better products and lower costs, not stealthier packaging.
[P1.7] If a price has effectively gone up, the tag should say so plainly.

[C1.1] Mandatory shrinkflation warnings solve the wrong problem by confusing a product’s history with its current value. Suppose Brand A hikes the price of its 500-gram pasta by twenty percent, while Brand B keeps its headline price flat for families on a strict budget, trimming the weight to 450 grams instead. Both raise the unit price, yet under this proposal, Brand A gets a clean shelf while Brand B gets a warning flag—even if Brand B remains cheaper per gram. Singling out size reductions while ignoring straight price hikes misleads shoppers, steering them away from affordable price points toward costlier competitors.
[C1.2] Nor is this "minimal cost," as [P1.4] claims. Supermarkets and apps do not manufacture the thousands of items they sell. When a supplier resizes a pack, it typically arrives with a brand-new barcode, often alongside recipe changes or different size tiers. Retailer databases do not automatically know whether a 680-gram box is a "shrunk" 750-gram box or a distinct product line.
[C1.3] Forcing grocers and apps to manually audit supplier catalogs, link historical barcodes, and police physical shelf flags creates heavy compliance costs. In low-margin grocery retail, that overhead goes straight into higher food prices.
[C1.4] Worse, the mandate triggers perverse incentives that hurt the low-income households [P1.6] wants to protect. Manufacturers often shrink packs to preserve affordable entry prices during supply shocks. If regulators slap a public penalty on resizing, brands will not magically absorb rising commodity costs. They will either push through blunt headline price hikes—pricing out shoppers on fixed weekly budgets—or turn to "skimpflation," quietly swapping quality ingredients for water, palm oil, or cheap fillers.
[C1.5] Shoppers can read gram weights today; they cannot easily spot degraded nutrition.
[C1.6] Consumers need clear, standardized unit pricing to compare products on the shelf right now, not noisy historical warnings that distort competition and drive grocery bills higher.
