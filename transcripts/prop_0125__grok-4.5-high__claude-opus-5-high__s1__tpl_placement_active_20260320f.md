[P1.1] Rural broadband is not a luxury product waiting for a profit model. It is basic infrastructure in an economy where jobs, schooling, healthcare, banking, and civic life already run online. When private carriers refuse sparse areas because returns are thin, that is not a verdict on social value—it is a classic market failure. Markets price private revenue.
[P1.2] They ignore the external benefits of connecting farms, clinics, small towns, and remote workers to the same digital commons cities already take for granted.
[P1.3] Government subsidy is the correct response precisely because carriers are right about pure commercial math and wrong about public purpose. Electricity cooperatives and rural road networks faced the same logic: density too low, fixed costs too high, private capital too impatient. We did not shrug and leave those regions dark or disconnected. We treated access as a condition of equal citizenship and national productivity.
[P1.4] Broadband belongs in that category now. A student who cannot stream a class, a farmer who cannot use precision tools or sell into wider markets, a patient who loses telehealth, and a small business locked out of e-commerce are not “inefficient customers.” They are people being systematically excluded by a pure-profit test.
[P1.5] The objection that subsidy distorts markets misses the point. Distortion already exists: urban density cross-subsidizes itself while rural demand is left stranded. Targeted public support closes the gap carriers will not. It can be structured with build-out requirements, open-access rules, and performance checks so money buys coverage rather than windfalls.
[P1.6] The alternative—waiting for viability that never arrives—cements a permanent digital underclass and drags national growth, emergency response, and democratic participation with it.
[P1.7] Commercial viability is a useful screen for private investment. It is a terrible constitution for who gets to participate in modern life. When the market walks away from sparse places, government should step in and finish the network.

Any firm that understands that will learn to declare non-viability everywhere. That is the whole history of high-cost broadband funds: incumbents claiming coverage they hadn't built, banking support for speeds that were obsolete by the time the money landed, and returning years later to be paid again for the same homes.
[C1.3] Subsidy conditioned on a company's pessimism is not correcting a market failure; it is purchasing a lobbying position.
[C1.4] Second, the arithmetic. The last few percent of households are not slightly expensive — they are the tail where trenching a single mountain or prairie connection can cost tens of thousands of dollars per home. Meanwhile the people most likely to be offline are not remote farmers but low-income urban households who have service on the pole outside and cannot afford it, or lack a device or the skills to use it. The same dollar buys one fiber drop at the end of a dirt road, or a hundred families actually online.
[C1.5] My opponent's student who cannot stream a class is far more often a city renter than a homesteader.
[C1.6] Third, technology has quietly overtaken the premise. Low-earth-orbit satellite and fixed wireless now reach exactly the terrain that made fiber uneconomic, at a fraction of the capital cost. Locking public money into buried plant for scattered dwellings risks subsidizing the buggy whip while the substitute matures.
[C1.7] And the alternatives are cheaper than cash: release spectrum, fix pole-attachment and permitting delays, mandate dig-once conduit, enable co-ops and municipal networks that build for their own members rather than shareholders.
[C1.8] The rural electrification comparison flatters the wrong actor. Farmers built those co-ops. We did not simply write cheques to the utilities that had walked away.

[P2.2] Their incentive scare collapses once subsidy is tied to verified build-out rather than a carrier’s press release. Nothing in the proposition forces money to the same firm that walked away. Conditioned public capital is how investor-owned utilities’ refusals were answered before: finance and cooperative structure finished what pure returns would not. Farmers built those systems because public policy made capital available; romanticizing the co-op while denying the money that capitalized it is selective history.
[P2.3] The urban-affordability pivot is the softest dodge. Households with a live plant outside and no wallet face a price and device problem. Farms, clinics, and towns with no plant face a construction problem. A voucher cannot light fiber that was never trenched.
[P2.4] Treating the two as interchangeable “same dollar” choices pretends access and affordability are one bottleneck. They are not. Leaving rural plant unbuilt so cities can be better subsidized simply freezes a second underclass in place.
[P2.5] Satellites and fixed wireless are useful tools and should compete for any support. They do not erase latency, weather, and capacity limits that still leave dense commercial uses and multi-user homes needing terrestrial builds. “Wait for the substitute” is how regions stay permanently one generation behind.
[P2.6] Spectrum, dig-once, and pole reform lower costs; they do not conjure private returns where density never will. When carriers correctly say the math fails, the remaining choice is public finish-work or permanent exclusion.
