[P1.1] The current system taxes work and shopping, but lets entrenched fortune sit untouched. That is backwards.
[P1.2] Taxing only income and consumption lets the richest opt out. A founder with $10 billion in stock who borrows $100 million against it to fund his lifestyle has huge economic power but reports little "income." Wage earners cannot do that. When the tax base is flow, those who live off stock get a permanent discount.
[P1.3] A tax on large net worth closes that loophole at its root.
[P1.4] Wealth is not just delayed income; it is different. $50 million in the bank buys security, political access, the ability to buy competitors, to endure any shock. Two households can earn $300k a year, but if one holds $50 million and the other holds nothing, they are not equally able to pay, and not equally powerful. Ability-to-pay demands we tax the stock, not just the flow.
[P1.5] An annual tax on large fortunes is also more efficient. Under income tax, idle land, parked art, dormant shell companies are rewarded because they generate no taxable event. A modest 2% tax on wealth above, say, $10 or $50 million pushes capital out of vaults and into productive use - investors must earn a return to cover the tax or sell to someone who will. It counteracts rentier accumulation, where wealth grows simply because it already exists.
[P1.6] Finally, unchecked dynastic wealth corrodes democracy. When a few thousand families can fund campaigns, buy media, and shape markets forever, meritocracy dies. Income tax never breaks that cycle because fortunes compound untaxed for decades.
[P1.7] We can design this narrowly: high thresholds, exemptions for operating businesses until sale, deferred payment for illiquid assets. The principle stands: if you own vastly more, you should contribute annually on what you own, not just on what you happen to realize.

[C1.2] First, wealth is inherently difficult to value annually. Unlike a wage or a sales receipt, the market value of privately held businesses, unlisted equity, intellectual property, real estate, and fine art fluctuates unpredictably. Forcing governments to appraise millions of complex, illiquid assets every single year invites endless litigation, valuation gaming, and arbitrary enforcement. Notice PRO’s immediate retreat in [P1.7] to exemptions and deferred payments for illiquid assets.
[C1.3] The moment you carve out operating businesses or defer private assets, you create the mother of all accounting loopholes, punishing transparent savers while rewarding sophisticated tax shelters.
[C1.4] Second, taxing net worth penalizes capital formation and productive risk. An annual wealth tax demands cash payments regardless of whether an enterprise turned a profit or generated liquidity. A founder scaling a capital-intensive startup or a family manufacturer would face forced equity sales just to pay taxes on paper valuations, depressing domestic enterprise and pushing investment abroad. France’s wealth tax triggered an exodus of thousands of millionaires and billions in capital before its repeal.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO’s critique of the status quo in [P1.2] targets genuine loopholes—like borrowing against untaxed assets—that do not require a wealth tax to solve. Equalizing capital gains, closing step-up in basis, and progressive consumption taxes capture economic power at transaction points without building a bureaucratic appraisal state. We should fix the income base, not embrace a failed tax on unrealized net worth.
