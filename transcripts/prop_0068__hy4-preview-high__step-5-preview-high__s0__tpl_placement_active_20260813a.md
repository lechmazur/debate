[P1.1] Labels for AI-generated content address only the artifact. The harder case is an AI-optimized persuasion campaign: thousands of message variants tested by models, then delivered to the micro-group most likely to click, donate, or vote. Each recipient sees one tailored message; no one sees the experiment. Platforms should have to label that campaign, not merely the synthetic post it finally shows you.
[P1.2] A label saying a machine wrote the words does not reveal that the sentence you are reading was selected, from a pool you cannot see, because a model predicted it would exploit your loneliness, anger, or financial stress. That is forgery versus manipulation. A human can write one clumsy appeal; an optimizer can run variants overnight and serve the one that best exploits a new mother's exhaustion. The campaign does not argue in the public square; it builds a private persuasive environment for each micro-group, away from journalistic and political scrutiny.
[P1.3] Voters are then steered by arguments engineered to bypass reflection. Without notice, consent is fictional.
[P1.4] This is administrable. Major platforms already record targeting criteria, optimization objectives, and delivery data; they already run "Why this ad?" tools and political ad archives. A label such as "Part of an AI-optimized persuasion campaign tailored to users like you" bans no speech and forces no disclosure of proprietary code. It turns back-end transparency into a front-end warning.
[P1.5] Opponents will call it vague, costly, or chilling. Voluntary disclosure will not fix this; users rarely open ad libraries. Vagueness is manageable: platforms already define campaigns and audiences; the trigger is AI optimization of message selection for a defined group. Cost falls on firms that profit from optimization, while the cost of silence falls on users experimented on without consent.
[P1.6] We should not tag a counterfeit image yet ignore the machine deciding which message most efficiently picks your pocket.

[C1.2] The specific warning PRO wants—‘tailored to users like you’—would lose all informational value the moment it appears everywhere.
[C1.3] This is not a narrow transparency measure; it is a prior restraint on ordinary advocacy. A nonprofit testing donation appeals, a small business targeting local customers, or a candidate refining get-out-the-vote messages all rely on automated optimization. Under this rule, each must carry a stigma label implying hidden exploitation. That chills exactly the speech a free society should encourage, while doing nothing to stop genuinely deceptive synthetic media.
[C1.4] The label also fails its own consent logic. Users already know platforms target them; a vague warning that a model helped choose the wording does not restore agency. [C1.5] Treating every A/B test as psychological manipulation inflates the warning until it means nothing. A real consent framework would require understanding the optimization’s actual goal, not a badge that tells users only that software was involved.
[C1.6] CON wins because the proposal mistakes the machinery of modern personalization for forgery. If PRO cannot draw a line between standard targeting and manipulative campaigns, the mandate either breaks digital advertising or becomes a meaningless badge on everything. The burden falls on every speaker, while the promised benefit—seeing through manipulation—remains unproven. Either outcome makes users less informed, not more.
