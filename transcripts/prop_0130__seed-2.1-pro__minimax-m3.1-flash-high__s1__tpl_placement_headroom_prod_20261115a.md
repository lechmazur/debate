[P1.1] The claim that foreign museums can better preserve looted colonial artifacts is a circular, paternalistic justification that can never override the fundamental moral and legal right of communities to reclaim their stolen heritage. First, the world-class preservation capacity those Western institutions cite was built in no small part from the wealth extracted through the same colonial projects that seized the artifacts in the first place. It is a cruel perversity to use the dividends of plunder to argue the plundered are permanently unfit to care for their own cultural legacy.
[P1.2] Second, “preservation” cannot be reduced to preventing physical decay in a distant, climate-controlled vault. The core purpose of cultural artifacts is not to sit as curated trophies of imperial adventure for foreign audiences, but to anchor living community memory, ritual practice, and intergenerational connection. The Benin Bronzes, ripped from their sacred and civic contexts during the 1897 British sack of Benin City, do not meaningfully “survive” when locked in a London basement, separated entirely from the Edo communities for whom they were made, even if their metal surfaces remain perfectly polished.
[P1.3] These claims of superior stewardship also collapse under basic scrutiny: many of the same institutions that position themselves as irreplaceable conservators hold thousands of looted works in un-cataloged backlogs, have documented accidental damage to iconic pieces, and sell deaccessioned works to private collectors who bar all public access. When countries of origin request technical or financial support to care for returned works, that collaborative aid is a choice they have every sovereign right to accept or reject on their own terms.
[P1.4] Holding artifacts hostage to claims of superior care locks in an endless cycle of colonial paternalism, where theft itself is treated as permanent proof the victims are not responsible enough to get their property back.

[C1.1] Colonial looting warrants restitution, but restitution does not mean moving every object immediately into a national institution. The proposition’s “even when” makes preservation irrelevant whenever museums invoke colonialism. That abandons the interest restitution should protect: preventing irreversible loss of cultural heritage.
[C1.2] PRO creates a false choice between foreign trophy vaults and dignified return. Better stewardship is a reason to design restitution, not deny it. A museum can provide conservation training, climate-controlled storage, digital records, funding, and long-term loans while ownership and interpretive authority pass to the source community. Countries then regain control without sacrificing preservation.
[C1.3] The proposition excludes these arrangements and confuses justice with operational incapacity.
[C1.4] The Benin Bronzes show why cultural meaning alone cannot settle the issue. Reconnecting works with Edo communities is compelling, but removing an object from a controlled environment without a credible care plan can sever scholarship, conservation capacity, and public access at once. PRO says exclusion from London means an artifact has not survived, but that establishes the wrong of exclusion, not that every receiving institution can preserve it. That concession defeats a categorical rule.
[C1.5] PRO also treats superior preservation as a circular colonial insult. The question is not whether Western institutions are historically entitled to custody—they are not—but whether immediate transfer without safeguards would preserve an object or expose it to decay. If return would endanger a vulnerable wooden object, fragile manuscript, or sacred object, restitution requires a binding conservation agreement, shared custody, and conditions for safe transfer. Otherwise, advocates transform a demand for justice into another form of neglect.
[C1.6] Justice requires ending the hold, and justice requires not destroying heritage in the process. Restitution with enforceable stewardship obligations is defensible; unconditional surrender regardless of preservation consequences is not.

The burden should rest on the custodian to identify a specific, evidence-based risk through independent assessment. Where the receiving institution lacks the capacity to manage a fragile object, phased transfer, temporary treatment abroad, shared custody, and long-term loans can protect the object without giving the holder permanent possession.

[C2.4] The museum’s historical misconduct is morally relevant, but it does not automatically erase technical competence. A museum with poor ethical standards may still possess superior facilities, documentation, or conservators for particular objects. [C2.5] Most importantly, PRO concedes that preservation concerns may be “speculative” while demanding categorical transfer anyway [P2.3]. That reverses the burden of proof. If transfer would materially endanger the object, justice requires transfer without preventable destruction. The proposition should permit conditional restitution wherever preservation genuinely conflicts with immediate return, not empower either side to turn that conflict into an excuse for indefinite retention.
