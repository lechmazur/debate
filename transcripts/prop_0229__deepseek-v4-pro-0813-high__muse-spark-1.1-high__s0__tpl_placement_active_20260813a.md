[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are regressive welfare disguised as social protection. In Gulf and North African economies, the largest share of cheap fuel is captured by high-income households with multiple vehicles, generators, and energy-heavy businesses. A low-income household in Cairo or Algiers without a car gets almost nothing from cheap gasoline; a wealthy household with several SUVs gets many times more. Every dinar, riyal, or pound spent holding fuel prices down therefore transfers public money upward.
[P1.2] That same subsidy bill is enormous and volatile. It forces governments to cut schools, clinics, and infrastructure when energy prices rise, and it creates a permanent budget risk—especially for importers facing hard-currency pressure. Cheap domestic fuel also invites smuggling across borders, wastes scarce energy, worsens congestion and pollution, and subsidizes those who consume most. None of this targets poverty.
[P1.3] The alternative is not removal without protection. It is a sequenced shift: phase out universal price suppression within five years and replace it with cash transfers aimed at households that actually need help. Five years is enough time to expand existing social registries, ration-card systems, national ID-linked payments, and mobile-money delivery without waiting so long that the subsidy becomes even more politically embedded. Transfers can be indexed to fuel price changes, so the poor are compensated before or as prices adjust, and the fiscal savings can be redirected into larger, more visible support for low-income families.
[P1.4] Political risk cuts the other way. The dangerous path is slow, uncompensated, crisis-driven reform. Sudden price hikes without compensation produce anger; transparent cash transfers before the change give households a stake in the adjustment. Gulf and North African states should choose the path that stops financing the rich through cheap fuel and starts financing the poor directly.
