[P1.1] The death penalty, even for the most heinous acts, is a tool that no just state should retain. First, the irreversible nature of execution makes it incompatible with a system that must learn from its mistakes. In terrorism and mass‑murder investigations, intelligence is often incomplete, pressured, or politically tainted; the record shows numerous cases where convictions later proved false after years on death row. When a state kills, it cannot later correct that error, and the very possibility of an innocent’s execution shatters the moral foundation of the legal system.
[P1.2] Second, deterrence arguments collapse under scrutiny. Empirical studies across jurisdictions that have abolished capital punishment consistently find no measurable increase in homicide or terrorist attacks after abolition. Terrorist groups, driven by ideology rather than rational cost‑benefit calculations, are not deterred by the threat of death; they exploit state cruelty for propaganda, turning executions into recruitment tools.
[P1.3] Third, retaining the death penalty erodes the very authority that allows a society to condemn extreme violence. A state that meets murder with murder signals that killing is an acceptable solution, normalising the very conduct it seeks to punish. This moral inconsistency weakens the rule of law and provides authoritarian regimes with a convenient pretext for political executions disguised as anti‑terrorism measures.
[P1.4] Finally, viable alternatives—life imprisonment without parole, restorative‑justice programs for victims, and targeted security measures—achieve public safety without sacrificing human rights. The global trend toward abolition, now embraced by the majority of nations, demonstrates that societies can protect citizens while upholding dignity.
[P1.5] Abolishing the death penalty, even for terrorism and mass murder, preserves justice, prevents irreversible error, denies extremists a recruitment narrative, and upholds the moral standards that define a civilized state.
