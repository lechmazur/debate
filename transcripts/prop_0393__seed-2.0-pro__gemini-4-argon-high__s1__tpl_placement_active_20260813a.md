Universal design is a matter of geometry: wider corridors, wide turning circles in bathrooms and kitchens, and elevators in smaller buildings. Inside a fixed urban lot, every square foot devoted to wider hallways and oversized bathrooms comes directly out of bedrooms and total unit counts.
[C1.2] You do not just pay a modest upfront fee; you permanently shrink the number of homes a building provides for all seventy years it stands [P1.1].
[C1.3] That tradeoff is devastating on the margin, especially for "missing middle" housing—the three-to-eight-unit walk-ups that provide naturally affordable urban apartments. Large luxury towers can absorb new mandates and pass the cost into higher rents. Small infill projects cannot. When stronger mandates require an elevator shaft or wider unit footprints on a narrow lot, the project loses the rentable square footage needed to secure a construction loan.
[C1.4] The result of [P1.3]’s mandate is not a step-free fourplex for a parent with a stroller; it is an unbuilt fourplex, replaced by a single luxury home or an empty lot.
[C1.5] Furthermore, [P1.4] creates a false choice between blanket upfront universal design and destructive, expensive retrofits. Current codes already use adaptability—like requiring reinforced blocking inside bathroom walls—so grab bars or accessible fixtures can be added cheaply on demand. Forcing full universal-design geometry into every new unit makes every renter pay for spatial buffers most households do not concurrently need. Because seniors on fixed incomes and people with disabilities are disproportionately low-income, raising baseline rents and constricting housing supply locks them out financially long before physical steps ever could.
