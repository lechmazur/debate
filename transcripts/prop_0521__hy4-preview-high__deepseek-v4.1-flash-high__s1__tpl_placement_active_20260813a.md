[P1.1] Training accounts are not company property; they are stored-up compensation for work already done. When an employer funds a certification or a training account, it is compensating the worker and building skills that travel across firms in that sector. The moment those credits are earned, they belong to the career, not the payroll. Treating them as forfeitable on resignation turns earned benefits into golden handcuffs: the worker keeps the knowledge, but the employer keeps the wallet.
[P1.2] The same-sector limit already answers the main fairness worry. A welder who moves from one fabrication shop to another is not taking the skill to a rival industry; she is keeping it where the investment was meant to pay off. Picture a nurse who uses an employer-funded tuition account for an advanced cardiac credential, then transfers to another hospital in the same health sector. Under the current rule she may lose the remaining balance and its portability.
[P1.3] Under the proposed rule she keeps both. The first hospital still received her labor while she trained; it has no right to own her next job.
[P1.4] Portability is also reciprocal, which blunts the underinvestment objection. If every firm’s accounts move within the sector, then any employer that loses an employee can hire a replacement who arrives already trained by someone else. Locking benefits to one firm does not stop rivals from hiring; it only fines the worker for changing jobs. Worse, that risk makes workers wary of training.
[P1.5] Portability puts the upside where it belongs: with the person who must live with the skill.
[P1.6] The test is simple. Firm-specific secrets can stay with the firm, but sector credentials and earned training accounts should follow the worker. The sector keeps the productivity, the worker keeps the asset, and employers still gain a more mobile, better-skilled labor pool. That is why workers should keep employer-funded training accounts and benefits when changing jobs within the same sector.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like fairness: let workers keep what they earned. But employer-funded training accounts are not wages withheld; they are conditional investments funded in exchange for a return—a trained employee who stays long enough for the employer to recover the cost. Make them portable within the same sector, and you sever the return from the investment. The predictable result is not more training; it is less.
[C1.2] Consider a mid-sized hospital that funds cardiac certifications. If every credential and remaining balance can walk to a competitor down the street, the hospital becomes a training subsidizer for its rivals. The same-sector limit is not a safeguard; it is the problem. Rivals in the same sector are exactly the firms that can bid away newly trained workers.
[C1.3] PRO's reciprocity claim assumes every employer trains and every employer can hire someone else's graduate. In reality, training is asymmetric: some firms invest, others free-ride. When free-riding is easier, the free-riders do not suddenly start training; the trainers stop.
[C1.4] Employers will adapt. They will shift to shorter, firm-specific modules, reimburse only after long tenure, reduce cash wages to offset the risk, or abandon accounts altogether. Workers who stay, and workers who need entry-level training most, bear the cost. That is not a transfer from employer to worker; it is a reduction in the total training pie.
[C1.5] The motion also includes "benefits," which is far broader than credentials. If employer-funded benefits follow workers across the same sector, firms face double coverage, coordination costs, and incentives to classify benefits as portable to avoid commitments. The worker may keep a nominal account while employers quietly cut what they contribute.
[C1.6] A clawback or vesting schedule can protect workers who leave early for good reason. Forced portability within the sector cannot. It converts training from an investment into a subsidy for competitors, and the first thing to disappear will be the funding. Vote CON.

Within the same sector, they are reciprocal: a hospital that loses a nurse can hire a nurse trained elsewhere. A chronic non-trainer does not get free labor; to attract workers who carry a portable asset, it must offer higher wages or better conditions.
[P2.3] Portability moves the value of sector skills into the worker's hands, where the market prices it. It stops one firm from confiscating that return when a worker leaves.
[P2.4] [C1.4] predicts employers will retreat to "shorter, firm-specific modules" and reimburse only after long tenure. That threat concedes the central point: if a credential is useful only inside one firm, it is the firm-specific training the motion already excludes. Sector credentials should travel. The motion does not force any employer to fund portable training; it says that once an employer funds an earned account, it cannot use tenure to seize it.
[P2.5] If firms instead cut cash wages, that tradeoff is visible in total compensation, and workers can choose employers that do not.
[P2.6] CON's fallback in [C1.6]—vesting schedules and clawbacks—is not protection for mobile workers; it is the harm. A vesting schedule still forfeits unvested balances when a worker changes jobs. The motion replaces forfeiture with portability: the worker keeps the account, the sector keeps the skill, and employers still compete for talent. That is why the proposition carries.
