[P1.1] A workplace should measure what it actually buys: useful work. Keyboard strokes, webcam visibility, online status, and hours at a desk are merely proxies—and often bad ones. It does not mean counting widgets, abandoning check-ins, or ignoring conduct.
[P1.2] Monitoring may be justified for security, compliance, or a specific performance concern; it should not be the default scorecard.
[P1.3] Output evaluation first aligns incentives. Reward visible activity and employees rationally produce visible activity: unnecessary meetings, instant replies, padded hours, and performative busyness. Reward a sound report, resolved customer problem, reliable system, or completed design, and effort moves toward the organization’s actual goals. Deep concentration often looks inactive, while frantic clicking can produce nothing.
[P1.4] Managers should care which creates value.
[P1.5] It is also more accurate and fair in remote settings. A developer considering architecture may appear idle; an employee with a permanently green status may still neglect the project. A common output standard asks the relevant question of everyone: did they fulfill their commitments?
[P1.6] Autonomy does not weaken accountability; it makes accountability substantive. Clear goals, milestones, ownership, and review expose missed deadlines, defects, poor judgment, and dissatisfied stakeholders. [P1.7] Some work is difficult to quantify, but difficulty does not validate an inferior proxy.
