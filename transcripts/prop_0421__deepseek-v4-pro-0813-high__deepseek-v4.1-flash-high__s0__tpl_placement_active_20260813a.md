[P1.1] A mandatory service charge is not a tip, a surprise, or an optional add-on. It is part of the price. If a restaurant adds 18 percent to every bill, an $18 entree is really $21.24. A customer comparing two restaurants, two ticket platforms, or two delivery apps is deciding on the basis of numbers the seller knows are incomplete.
[P1.2] Allowing that gap to remain until the final payment screen is not disclosure; it is misdirection.
[P1.3] The harm happens before the customer reaches the pay button. A buyer compares prices, selects seats, or builds a delivery order. Only after entering payment information does the mandatory charge appear. By then, the customer has sunk time and effort into the transaction.
[P1.4] Many finish the purchase anyway, not because they accept the fee, but because abandoning feels like losing the seats, the meal, or the time already spent. Early disclosure would let consumers reject the charge while alternatives are still open.
[P1.5] The burden is also unequal. Someone on a tight budget may open a delivery app after checking that the listed prices fit their limit, only to meet a mandatory service fee, delivery fee, and small-order fee at checkout. Wealthier shoppers can absorb the surprise; others pay because they have already committed to the order. A disclosure that appears at the payment screen is too late to protect them.
[P1.6] This requirement is deliberately narrow. It does not force businesses to fold voluntary tips, taxes, or variable shipping into the headline price. It only requires mandatory charges, already known to the seller, to be shown before the final checkout stage: on the menu, the ticket listing, the item page, or in the cart before payment details are requested. That is a small operational change.
[P1.7] Secrecy until payment exists only to make the real price visible after comparison has been foreclosed.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes early disclosure for accurate disclosure. Mandatory charges are not always fixed numbers sitting in a drawer. They are often contingent on choices the customer has not made yet. A delivery service fee can depend on distance, demand, basket size, membership, and time.
[C1.2] A ticketing facility fee can vary by venue, seat, delivery method, and payment type. A restaurant service charge may apply only to large parties or private events, or be a percentage of a bill that is not final until the meal is over. Requiring “all mandatory service charges” before the final checkout screen would force sellers to post estimates, ranges, or generic warnings: “fees may apply.” That is not transparency; it is noise. It makes comparison harder because customers will anchor on numbers that are not the real price.
[C1.3] The real consumer protection is an exact, itemized total before the customer commits to pay. That is what the final checkout screen is for. If a seller hides a mandatory fee until after payment, existing fraud and consumer-protection rules can address it. But the proposition goes further: it demands charges earlier, when the seller may not know them.
[C1.4] That creates bait-and-switch in reverse—customers see one number, then a different one later—and invites litigation over what “mandatory” means and when “before final checkout” begins.
[C1.5] PRO’s sunk-cost story is real but misdirected. A shopper who has spent ten minutes building a cart has not lost money. They can still abandon. The pay button is the commitment point, not the first click.
[C1.6] If we want to protect tight budgets, the answer is a clear total before payment, not speculative fees on every menu, listing, and item page.
[C1.7] Small restaurants and app developers would bear the cost of constant updates and real-time fee engines, and those costs come back to consumers. Vote CON: accurate price at commitment protects buyers; premature price guesses do not.
