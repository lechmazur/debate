[P1.1] A city that sells the shoreline has already decided who the city is for. Parks, beaches, and major waterfronts should remain broadly free because they belong to people as citizens, not customers. Enclosing them behind paid private experiences does not upgrade public space. It abolishes it.
[P1.2] These places are publicly owned and financed. The public has already paid to keep a park unbuilt, a beach open, a riverfront walkable. Ticketed decks, branded beach clubs, and leased activations charge again for the best of what people already own. That is a second rent on the commons.
[P1.3] Those least able to pay lose the view, the sand, and the shade first.
[P1.4] Free access does civic work no venue can. It lets strangers share ground without a purchase in between. That unscripted mixing is how a city remains one city rather than a stack of guest lists. A nurse after night shift, a teenager with nowhere else to go, a family that cannot afford the pier brunch—all still have a place.
[P1.5] Paid experiences sort by spending power. The city stops mixing and starts layering: premium water, leftover park, fenced lawn. Trust thins when the nicest public land becomes product.
[P1.6] Enclosure sticks. A festival fence becomes a seasonal club, then a multi-year lease, then the default way to “activate” public land. Once the best water sits behind a rope, ordinary swimming and sitting are treated as leftovers, not the point.
[P1.7] Funding and crowds do not require this bargain. Cities can tax, staff, and maintain public space, and keep cafes at the edge rather than selling the center. Private clubs can exist beside a beach; they should not become the beach. Quality is not an argument for turning the shoreline into a checkout line.
[P1.8] The question is whether we keep a shared shoreline or rent it back to ourselves.

[C1.1] A free park that no one can safely enter is the most exclusive space in the city. That is the real choice here: not free access versus private ropes, but well-funded, well-kept public space versus the free-and-failing kind.
[C1.2] Public land is expensive to hold. Lawns need crews, beaches need lifeguards, piers need engineers, and tax revenue is the first thing cut and the last thing restored. When the money fails, access fails with it — restrooms locked, hours shortened, whole parks effectively abandoned in struggling cities. PRO's nurse and teenager lose their park not to a ticket booth but to a budget line.
[C1.3] Bounded commercialization is how that gets prevented. A leased boathouse, a seasonal ice rink, a ticketed observation deck, a beach concession at the entrance — these convert a small fraction of land into the revenue that keeps the other ninety-five percent open, clean, and patrolled. New York's parks, Chicago's lakefront, and London's royal parks all run versions of this mixed model: concessions and events underwriting free lawns and open shoreline. The family that cannot afford the pier brunch still gets the pier, the sand, and the lifeguards — funded partly by those who can.
[C1.4] This is also the progressive funding mechanism PRO misses. Ticketed experiences extract money from tourists and the affluent and spend it on universal access. Refusing that revenue on principle does not defend the poor; it defunds the spaces they depend on most, because wealthy users have private alternatives and everyone else does not.
[C1.5] Nor is enclosure creep a law of nature. Lease terms are written down: caps on square footage, sunset clauses, guaranteed public hours. Slippage is a drafting problem, and a city capable of administering PRO's tax-funded model is capable of administering a contract.
[C1.6] The proposition demands purity and delivers neglect. The mixed model keeps the commons genuinely open: funded, safe, and free where it matters.
