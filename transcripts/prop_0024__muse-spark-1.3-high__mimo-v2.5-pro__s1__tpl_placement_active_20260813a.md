[P1.1] Housing unaffordability in advanced economies is not a mystery. In productive cities, jobs and people arrived faster than homes were allowed to be built. Single-family-only zoning, height limits, parking mandates, and years-long discretionary permits make legal building slow, risky, and expensive. When demand rises and supply cannot respond, prices rise for almost everyone.
[P1.2] That is the disease.
[P1.3] Streamlining permitting and zoning treats the disease directly. Faster, predictable approvals cut financing costs, uncertainty, and delay. Allowing apartments near jobs and transit, small multifamily in residential neighborhoods, and accessory units turns one lot into several homes. More builders can compete, more types get built, and vacancy rises.
[P1.4] Even when new units are expensive, they relieve pressure through filtering: a higher-income household moves into new construction instead of bidding up an older apartment, freeing that older unit for someone with less income. Benefits spread to movers, newcomers, and young families.
[P1.5] Rent control and expanded tenant protections do the opposite. A cap can help an incumbent tenant this year while shrinking opportunity for everyone else. Because the controlled rent sits below market, tenants stay put even when the unit no longer fits, mobility falls, and outsiders face fewer openings. Landlords earn less from capped units, so maintenance slips, conversions to owner-occupancy become attractive, and investors shift capital elsewhere.
[P1.6] Future rental supply shrinks where it is most needed. The result is a lottery: protected insiders, queuing outsiders, and shadow prices paid in fees, overcrowding, or endless search.
[P1.7] The comparison is stark. Places that allowed more building — from Japan's flexible national zoning to Auckland after upzoning to Sunbelt metros that permit easily — have kept rents far more moderate than costly coastal markets that layered stronger controls on top of shortage. Controls ration scarcity; supply reduces it. If affordability means ordinary people can find a decent home for a manageable share of income, building more homes faster wins.

[C1.2] Consider what happens at street level. A family in a rapidly appreciating neighborhood faces a rent hike or lease non-renewal. Zoning reform, even if enacted today, produces its first units years from now—after financing, design review, environmental clearance, and construction. That family needs protection now.
[C1.3] Rent stabilization and just-cause eviction provisions deliver immediate, measurable relief to the households actually experiencing housing stress. Supply reform offers them nothing on the relevant timescale.
[C1.4] My opponent's filtering argument—that wealthy tenants moving into new construction free up older units downstream—sounds elegant but is empirically fragile. Filtering operates over many years, depends on income growth and migration patterns, and delivers benefits that are diffuse and untargeted. A household facing displacement cannot wait for a chain of moves to eventually reach them. Moreover, in cities with strong demand, new supply is often absorbed by population growth, second homes, or short-term rentals rather than generating the downward price pressure the theory predicts.
[C1.5] Tenant protections also address a structural power asymmetry that supply alone cannot fix. Landlords control lease terms, renewal decisions, and maintenance quality. Without baseline protections—limits on sudden rent spikes, rights to lease renewal, habitability enforcement—tenants remain vulnerable even when vacancy rates are healthy. Many cities with adequate aggregate supply still see individual tenants displaced because they lack bargaining power.
[C1.6] The proposition frames this as either/or, but even accepting that framing, effectiveness must account for who is helped and how quickly. A policy that theoretically reduces average rents in a decade but leaves current renters exposed to immediate displacement is less effective than one that stabilizes the people already here while longer-run supply efforts proceed.
[C1.7] Tenant protections are the indispensable front line. They buy time, prevent displacement, and ensure that the people who need affordability most actually experience it—not someday, but now.
