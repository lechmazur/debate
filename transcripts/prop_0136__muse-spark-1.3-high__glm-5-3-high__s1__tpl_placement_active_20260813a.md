[P1.1] In Seoul, Tokyo, Taipei young couples are not asking whether a baby is worth a single check. They are asking where a family of four could live and who would look after a child every day for the next five years.
[P1.2] That is why housing affordability and childcare will move birth rates more than one-off baby bonuses. Having a child in East Asia is a twenty-year commitment in some of the world's most expensive housing markets, combined with intense pressure on mothers to quit or scale back work. A lump sum at birth does not change that arithmetic. It helps with hospital bills and early costs, then disappears while rent, mortgage payments, private daycare fees and lost income continue for years.
[P1.3] Housing and childcare do change the calculation. An affordable two- or three-bedroom flat near jobs makes marriage earlier and makes a second child imaginable instead of impossible. Reliable, affordable daycare from infancy, with hours that match real jobs, lowers the career penalty that now forces many women to choose between promotion and parenthood. These are recurring, predictable supports that turn a vague wish for children into a workable plan.
[P1.4] Bonuses also fail on psychology. Couples facing deep uncertainty about future costs discount a one-time payment heavily. Secure housing and a guaranteed childcare place build confidence to commit. That is why generous birth payments have produced headlines but little sustained rebound: cash can shift the month a baby already planned is born, it rarely creates a baby otherwise ruled out.
[P1.5] Governments with limited budgets should buy lasting feasibility, not brief applause. Fix the home and the daily care, and more couples will say yes — and crucially yes to a second child, which is what actually lifts the birth rate.
