[P1.1] When food-delivery and reservation apps let sponsored placements dominate top results so heavily that users have to scroll past screens of paid listings to find cheaper, nearby independent restaurants, they betray the core reason people open those apps in the first place. A user pulling up an app on a weeknight looking for a $12 sandwich three blocks from their apartment does not want to wade through 10 sponsored slots for 25-minute-delivery chain sandwich shops charging $20 per entree before seeing a local option that matches their search for proximity and price.
[P1.2] Worse, many apps pin those sponsored slots to the top even when users set filters for lower price points or shorter delivery distances, forcing people to sift through results that do not meet their criteria. That is not a helpful directory—it is a paid ad wall dressed up as a recommendation tool. This over-privileging of sponsors creates a vicious cycle for both diners and local small businesses. Independent restaurants operate on razor-thin, single-digit margins, so they cannot consistently outbid national chains and restaurant groups for top placement.
[P1.3] As paid slots crowd out organic results, those independents lose order and reservation volume, leaving them less able to afford placement, until the app’s catalog tilts almost entirely to deep-pocketed brands that pass their advertising costs to customers in higher menu prices and hidden fees. This is not an argument to eliminate sponsored listings entirely. It is a simple demand that apps not let ad buys override basic, user-centric ranking signals of proximity, price, and relevance. When paid placement buries the affordable local options users are actually looking for, the app erodes user trust, locks out small businesses, and pushes diners to pay more for worse, less convenient options that serve advertisers before the people the app was built to serve.

[C1.1] The proposition asks food apps to act like neutral public directories, but they are private two-sided marketplaces that must attract both diners and restaurants. Sponsored placement is not an accidental defect; it is the pricing mechanism that lets consumers search, compare menus, pay, track deliveries, and book tables without paying cash for the app. If apps are pressured to bury paid placements, platforms must recover revenue somewhere: higher per-order fees, subscription paywalls, or reduced service areas. Those costs hit low-budget diners and small independents hardest, because chains can absorb fees while a neighborhood taqueria cannot.
[C1.2] Nor is heavily weighted sponsored placement the main reason users miss cheaper nearby independents. The same apps offer price sorting, distance filters, map search, cuisine tags, and saved favorites. A diner seeking a $12 lunch can search the neighborhood or cuisine and compare directly. The struggle is ordinary search cost, not a wall: sponsored results are usually labeled, skippable, and finite.
[C1.3] The proposition would privilege only one set of preferences, cheapest and nearest independent, over users who also value speed, reliability, dietary accommodation, or consistency.
[C1.4] Small independents also buy sponsored slots. For a new biryani shop with no reviews, a short promoted placement may be the cheapest way to reach first customers and survive the platform's cold-start problem. Removing that tool would not level the field; it would lock in incumbents whose volume, photos, and reviews dominate organic rankings. Organic rankings are not neutral, just less transparent.
[C1.5] Finally, the proposed line, so heavily that users struggle, is unworkable and unlikely to stop. Platforms already face competitive pressure: if search becomes useless, users switch apps and restaurants abandon the platform. The better principle is clear labeling and genuine consumer control, not a fuzzy mandate to equalize visibility for independent restaurants.

[P2.1] The claim that heavy sponsored prioritization protects diners from fees collapses under basic math. National chains and large restaurant groups do not eat six- and seven-figure ad budgets as charity; they build those costs directly into the marked-up menu prices on every app order, so diners already pay a hidden ad tax even before platform service fees apply. The supposed tradeoff between paid placements and visible user fees or paywalls is false: CON frames paid placements as a free lunch, but that lunch is paid for twice—once in ad costs baked into prices and again when users waste time sifting past listings that do not match their search.
[P2.2] Next, the promise that filters, map search, and clear labeling solve the problem ignores the exact design flaw the proposition targets. If a user sets a filter for low-price options under one mile, those parameters should bind first; instead, sponsored slots from higher-priced chains three miles away regularly pin themselves above matching results, turning a 30-second weeknight search into a scroll past irrelevant paid posts. A tiny “sponsored” tag does not eliminate that friction when paid slots take up the first full screen of results.
[P2.3] The argument that sponsored slots help new independents misstates how ad auctions work. A new family-owned spot cannot sustain ongoing bids against a national chain spending millions per market on app placement; the temporary launch-week slot a small shop can afford is nothing next to the permanent paid real estate major brands hold. The competitive pressure CON cites is toothless in most markets, where one or two apps hold enough diner and restaurant share that users have nowhere meaningful to switch, even when search results are stacked to serve advertisers before their own needs.
