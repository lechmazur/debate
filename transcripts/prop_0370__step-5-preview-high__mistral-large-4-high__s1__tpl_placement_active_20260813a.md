[P1.1] When a large employer makes work messaging or tracking apps a routine condition of employment, it is not offering a tool; it is claiming dominion over private property. The phone in a worker's pocket holds family photographs, private medical searches, banking credentials, and intimate conversations. Treating installation on that device as a non-negotiable prerequisite turns the job offer into a demand for access to a worker's most personal digital space. That is the coercion at the heart of this proposition: a "routine condition" means the worker cannot meaningfully decline.
[P1.2] The harm is not hypothetical. A tracking app does not merely facilitate communication; it transforms a personal device into a monitoring node. Even if location data is collected only during shifts, the infrastructure for continuous surveillance now sits on hardware the employer neither owns nor fully secures. Workers are placed in the position of explaining why their personal battery died or why a permission was denied, blurring the line between professional obligation and private autonomy.
[P1.3] CON may reply that these tools are convenient or that workers can simply carry a separate phone. That misses the power imbalance. A large employer sets terms for thousands of people who need the income. When the default expectation is installation on personal devices, the burden of refusal falls on the individual, not the institution.
[P1.4] The employer already possesses the capital to issue dedicated devices or to use web-based tools that leave no permanent footprint on private hardware. The cheaper path for the company should not be the standard path for the worker.
[P1.5] Large employers may support personal-device use as an opt-in with informed, revocable consent. But as a routine condition of employment, mandatory installation on personal phones is an unjustified extraction of privacy and autonomy. The default must remain that work belongs to the workplace, not to the device that sits beside a worker's bed at night.

[C1.1] Modern work does not stop at the office door, and the proposition's blanket prohibition assumes a separation between professional and personal life that neither workers nor employers actually want. When a large employer hires for a role requiring real-time coordination—delivering packages, responding to emergencies, covering shifts across sites—expecting a messaging or safety app on the worker's own phone is a reasonable, disclosed condition of a voluntary contract, not a demand for dominion over private property.
[C1.2] Accepting the job is itself consent. Listings that specify app-based communication are transparent upfront. Workers who prioritize strict boundaries can choose roles without such requirements; the existence of alternatives in the labor market refutes the coercion narrative. Meanwhile, millions of workers prefer single-device workflows—one gadget to carry, charge, and secure—with work notifications silenced outside shifts.
[C1.3] The privacy objection collapses under modern mobile device management. Containerization creates a sealed work partition on the personal phone; the employer administers only that container, not the device's photos, messages, or browsing history. In practice, a managed personal phone is often more secure than a budget company handset that lags on security patches. The employer is not seizing the hardware; it is requesting a secure workspace on it.
[C1.4] For tracking apps specifically, the tools frequently protect workers rather than surveil them: lone-worker safety checks, automatic mileage logs for reimbursement, and tamper-proof records that prevent wage theft in field service. A blanket ban would strip away safeguards that help workers verify overtime and stay safe while working alone at night.
[C1.5] The proposed alternative—mandating separate company phones—is often more intrusive, not less. Company-issued hardware gives IT control over the entire device, whereas containerized personal phones keep private data untouched. Separate devices also double the hardware workers must carry and raise employer costs, which are passed back to workers through lower wages or fewer openings.
[C1.6] The proposition would impose a rigid, one-size-fits-all rule that ignores industry realities, worker preferences, and existing technical safeguards. Large employers should retain the ability to require connectivity tools for roles that genuinely demand them.
