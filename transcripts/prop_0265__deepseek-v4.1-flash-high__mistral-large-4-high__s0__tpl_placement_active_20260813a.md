[P1.1] The price on the menu is a promise. When a restaurant lists a burger at $16, it is telling every customer: order this, and this is what it costs. A mandatory service charge that appears only on the bill breaks that promise. It is not a tip, not a suggestion, not a voluntary act of generosity.
[P1.2] It is a required payment. If it is required, it is part of the price. And if it is part of the price, it belongs on the menu.
[P1.3] That is the case. Not aesthetics. Not hostility to tipping. Simple honesty and comparison.
[P1.4] Imagine two restaurants. One shows a $15 entrée, then adds 20% at the end. The other shows $18 all-in. At a glance, the first looks cheaper.
[P1.5] It is not. The diner who chooses it has been manipulated by arithmetic. Multiply that across a vacation, a family dinner, a business lunch, and the hidden charge becomes a real cost imposed after the decision is made.
[P1.6] Defenders of the status quo will say service charges support staff or give restaurants flexibility. Neither requires concealment. A restaurant can charge an all-in price, still distribute that money however it wants, and show the breakdown on the receipt. It can print "prices include service."
[P1.7] What it cannot do, if it respects customers, is advertise one number and demand another.
[P1.8] And notice who gets hurt most: tourists unfamiliar with local custom, people on tight budgets, anyone ordering online who only sees the fee on the final screen. They cannot consent to a price they never saw. A mandatory charge is not a surprise gift. It is the bill.
[P1.9] So the question is not whether restaurants may impose service charges. They may. The question is whether they may hide them. If the charge is mandatory, display it.
[P1.10] The menu price should be the price you pay. Anything less is bait-and-switch with a linen napkin.

A mandatory service charge—whether an automatic gratuity for a large party or a service fee to stabilize staff wages—is a distinct transaction. It compensates labor for the act of serving, not the restaurant for the act of cooking.
[C1.2] Folding these together does not create honesty; it obscures what the customer is actually purchasing and conflates two economically different things.
[C1.3] Mandatory all-in pricing would impose severe operational burdens without improving consumer welfare. Restaurants operate on razor-thin margins and face relentless pressure from supply costs, rent, and shifting local regulations. Forcing every establishment to bake service charges into printed menu prices means reprinting menus whenever labor costs fluctuate, seasonal staffing changes, or jurisdictions adjust automatic gratuity thresholds. Worse, automatic gratuities typically apply only to large parties.
[C1.4] A single menu price cannot accurately represent a charge that applies to six-person tables but not two-person tables without burying the real cost in asterisks and footnotes. It also erases crucial distinctions between business models: a $20 fast-casual bowl and a $20 fine-dining appetizer that carries a service charge would look identical on a delivery app, misleading consumers about the underlying economics.
[C1.5] Furthermore, the claim that customers are "manipulated" by seeing the charge at checkout assumes diners cannot read a bill or understand that restaurant prices, like retail prices, exclude taxes and optional add-ons. The charge is disclosed before payment is finalized, itemized clearly, and explained by staff upon request. Tourists and budget-conscious diners are not victims of hidden arithmetic; they are adults capable of adding a percentage to a subtotal. If anything, itemizing the service charge separately preserves transparency about how much of their money supports the server versus the restaurant—a distinction many diners specifically value and that all-in pricing would bury in a single opaque number.
