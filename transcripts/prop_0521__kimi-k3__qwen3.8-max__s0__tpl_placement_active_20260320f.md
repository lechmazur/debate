[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a credential for a subsidy. In most skilled sectors, the license, certificate, or experience already travels with the worker. What does not travel is the employer’s cash: tuition paid, wages during classroom time, instructor costs, and the productivity gap while a novice learns. The motion would turn those employer investments into an immediate, portable entitlement, forcing the firm that paid to underwrite its competitors.
[C1.2] That is not protecting mobility; it is destroying the incentive to fund training at all.
[C1.3] Employer training is not deferred wages. It is a conditional bargain: the firm advances costly human-capital investment now, and the worker provides a period of service in return. When a worker leaves early, the employer has not received the expected return. Reasonable vesting schedules and pro-rata repayment are not indenture; they are ordinary contract allocation of risk.
[C1.4] If portability becomes mandatory, rational firms will not absorb the loss. They will cut training budgets, prefer experienced hires, or shift to lower-skill roles that need less investment. The casualties will be exactly the workers PRO claims to help: new entrants, career changers, and employees without prior credentials.
[C1.5] The sector-wide pooling argument sounds tidy but creates a free-race. If every firm can poach a trained worker and inherit the training account, the cheapest strategy is not to train anyone. Large firms with recruiting machines can strip-mine smaller competitors, while hospitals, trucking companies, and manufacturers that actually invest retreat. “Same sector” also becomes unadministrable: is a hospital billing office, staffing agency, or medical-device supplier the same sector as a bedside nurse? Vague boundaries invite litigation and gaming.
[C1.6] Protect mobility by keeping earned credentials portable and enforcing fair, transparent repayment terms. But do not mandate that employer-funded accounts become lootable benefits. That path yields less training, more poaching, and weaker opportunity.

A prorated repayment can return some tuition, but it cannot replace the season of service, the covered shift, or the experienced worker who was supposed to remain. That is why the predictable response is not “train and get reimbursed,” but “train fewer people and poach more.”
[C2.6] PRO’s best point is that abusive repayment clauses can harm workers. Concede that. [C2.7] The clash is simple. PRO protects a portable claim on someone else’s budget. CON protects the budget itself. If the budget disappears, portability becomes a right to an empty account.
