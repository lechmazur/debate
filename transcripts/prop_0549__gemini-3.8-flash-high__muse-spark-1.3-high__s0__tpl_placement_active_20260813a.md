[P1.1] The fundamental flaw of relying on interdiction and trafficking penalties is that synthetic opioids render supply-side drug wars obsolete. Unlike heroin or cocaine, which depend on vulnerable agricultural crops, illicit fentanyl is synthesized in labs from ubiquitous precursor chemicals. It is so potent and compact that a courier can carry hundreds of thousands of lethal doses in a single backpack. Interdiction cannot meaningfully dent a supply that is infinitely cheap to manufacture and trivially easy to conceal within regular commerce.
[P1.2] Furthermore, escalating penalties simply incentivizes cartels to traffic even more concentrated, unpredictable analogs to maximize profit margins per ounce.
[P1.3] By contrast, expanding medication-assisted treatment and harm reduction addresses the crisis where it actually occurs: at the point of ingestion. Overdose deaths happen in bedrooms and alleyways, not at shipping ports.
[P1.4] Expanding access to medications like buprenorphine and methadone directly treats the underlying neurobiology of opioid use disorder. These proven therapies stabilize brain chemistry, eliminate severe withdrawal, and cut individual overdose mortality roughly in half. They transition individuals away from chaotic illicit markets and anchor them in long-term medical stability.
[P1.5] Harm reduction provides the immediate, life-saving bridge to that stability. Flooding communities with naloxone reverses fatal respiratory depression within minutes, directly pulling people back from the brink of death. Tools like fentanyl test strips and syringe service programs prevent fatal poisonings and establish trusted touchpoints with health workers.
[P1.6] Over the next five years, criminal penalties cannot outpace synthetic chemistry, and border enforcement cannot seal an entire nation against micro-volume shipments. Expanding treatment and harm reduction, however, immediately alters survival rates for people at risk right now. We will not reduce overdose deaths by chasing an endlessly replaceable poison; we will reduce them by protecting the human beings exposed to it.
