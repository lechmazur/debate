[P1.2] Over twenty years, methane warms the climate on the order of eighty times as strongly as carbon dioxide, then largely clears within about a decade. A leak fixed this year therefore removes heat that would otherwise arrive while ice sheets, crops, and cities are already under stress. Capture plants remove a weaker gas, and only after years of design, permitting, and construction. Near-term benefit is a race against that clock, not a contest of lifetime tonnage decades out.
[P1.3] Oil and gas already concentrates the cheapest cuts. A stuck valve, a leaking tank hatch, or an unlit flare can match the warming of thousands of cars. Infrared cameras, aerial surveys, and continuous monitors find those super-emitters; the fix is often a seal, a replacement part, or a change in operating practice. Tight detection-and-repair rules simply require operators to hunt and close those sources on a set schedule.
[P1.4] The technology is commercial. The abatement starts as soon as the rule bites.
[P1.5] The same money in capture buys steel, power penalties, and partial removal at a handful of point sources. Dollars that might have sealed leaks this season instead sit in capital budgets for plants that may not operate until the window for near-term relief has narrowed.
[P1.6] Picture the choice. One path sends crews to known basins, finds the worst emitters, and stops high-potency gas within months. The other path funds a capture unit that, if it is built on time, addresses a fraction of carbon dioxide at one facility while methane continues to vent elsewhere. For climate benefits inside the next two decades, speed and potency decide the comparison.
[P1.7] Leak detection and repair wins both.

[C1.1] Tightening leak rules sounds fast because methane is potent. Potency is not delivery. Per dollar, near-term benefit depends on how much emission actually stops, how soon, how reliably, and how long it stays stopped. On those tests, expanded leak detection and repair loses to targeted carbon capture and storage.
[C1.2] Start with the shrinking prize. The dramatic super-emitter is real but concentrated in a minority of sites, and operators, satellites, and existing rules are already hunting the largest because the gas has value. Tightening therefore spends the marginal dollar on mandated repeat surveys across tens of thousands of ordinary wells, tanks, and compressors, most finding little to repair. Most money buys looking, not fixing.
[C1.3] Many leaks are also intermittent: a quarterly camera pass can miss a release starting next week, and a seal fixed today can fail next season, so claimed reductions run ahead of verified, sustained reductions.
[C1.4] Carbon capture inverts that arithmetic at concentrated streams. At a gas-processing plant, refinery, or hydrogen facility, one project can capture carbon dioxide at million-tonne-per-year scale, metered continuously as it is injected underground. Equivalent funding can finish projects already engineered, expand capture units, and build shared pipelines and storage hubs that bring several sources online together. Once running, it captures every operating day, rather than certifying a site was tight on inspection day.
[C1.5] That durability matters even near term. Carbon dioxide avoided this decade lowers cumulative warming immediately and permanently, while a methane repair lasts only so long as endless re-inspection keeps recreating it. Leak rules are recurring rent on a recurring problem, vulnerable to weak enforcement and production shifting to less-regulated basins. Capture spending creates durable capacity that keeps delivering within the same ten-to-fifteen-year window.
[C1.6] Fast chemistry cannot rescue slow, diffuse, hard-to-verify delivery. Concentrated capture removes more real tonnes, sooner in aggregate, and keeps them removed.
