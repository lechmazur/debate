[P1.1] Merger review exists to stop deals that destroy real alternatives and let a firm extract more from the people on the other side of the transaction. Sometimes the victim is a shopper paying more. Sometimes it is a nurse, technician, or warehouse worker paid less because her outside options vanished. Those are not different evils; they are the same evil in a different costume.
[P1.2] A merger that reduces competition for labor exercises market power just as surely as one that raises prices, and policy should weigh it equally.
[P1.3] Labor markets are often the thinnest markets we have. A nurse cannot commute across a continent for a shift; a specialized engineer cannot instantly retool for another industry. When two hospital systems in one region merge, or two rural meatpacking plants become one employer, the combined firm can push wages below marginal productivity, freeze benefits, and impose harsher schedules—not because productivity fell, but because the alternative employer disappeared. That is monopsony, and it is antitrust's home turf: preserving competitive options.
[P1.4] A price-only lens hides this. Lower labor costs can even make consumer prices look stable while wealth moves from workers to shareholders and employment falls. The harmed workers usually have the least bargaining power and the fewest savings; the harm is regressive. We already condemn buyer power over suppliers; labor should not be the one input where monopsony gets a free pass.
[P1.5] Taking monopsony seriously does not mean ignoring prices. It means refusing to subordinate worker harm to consumer surplus. Agencies can define labor markets by occupation and commute zone, measure employer concentration, study wage effects and labor-supply elasticity, and use divestitures or conduct remedies to keep rival employers alive. A deal that would substantially lessen competition for labor should face the same presumption, scrutiny, and willingness to block as a price-raising merger.
[P1.6] Competition policy must see the whole exercise of market power, not just the half on a receipt.

[C1.1] Competition policy's job in merger review is to stop deals that raise prices, restrict output, or degrade quality for customers. Labor monopsony is a real economic phenomenon, but it is not a co-equal harm that should trigger the same presumption, the same scrutiny, and the same willingness to block. Treating it that way converts antitrust into a wage regulator and forces agencies to trade consumer benefits against noisy local labor claims.
[C1.2] The "same evil" slogan collapses on contact with how markets work. Buyer power over workers is not automatically equivalent to seller power over shoppers. Mergers can raise productivity, reallocate tasks, and lower unit costs even when some wages flatten. Those efficiencies show up in prices and availability.
[C1.3] A price-only lens is not a hiding place; it is the measurable, administrable core of the statute. If a hospital merger worsens care or raises billed rates, block it. If the complaint is only that two employers became one while patients still have options, that is a labor-policy dispute, not a consumer-protection case.
[C1.4] Labor markets are also harder to police at equal intensity. Occupations blur, commute zones leak, remote work expands options, and wages move for dozens of reasons unrelated to the deal. Employer concentration is a crude proxy. Agencies will over-block regional combinations that keep factories open, expand hours, or cut grocery prices.
[C1.5] Equal weight creates a direct conflict: a merger that slightly softens wage bidding but substantially lowers consumer prices would fail the proposed test. That is a worse outcome for the public, including workers as consumers. Labor law already addresses bargaining power. Merger review should consider labor only insofar as it maps onto output and prices—not as a second, equally serious independent axis.
[C1.6] The proposition asks for symmetry. Competition policy should refuse it.
