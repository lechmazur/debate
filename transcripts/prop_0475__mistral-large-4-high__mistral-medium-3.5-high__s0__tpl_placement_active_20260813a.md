[P1.1] Governments currently pour hundreds of billions annually into an agricultural model that is failing the climate, squandering land and water, and undermining food security. Livestock farming occupies roughly three-quarters of global agricultural land yet delivers only a minority of the world’s calories and protein, while generating substantial greenhouse gas emissions. This is not a neutral market outcome; it is the product of subsidy regimes that prop up conventional animal agriculture despite its massive environmental and public health externalities.
[P1.2] Precision fermentation offers a fundamentally more efficient path. By programming microorganisms to produce specific proteins—milk proteins, egg whites, meat tissues—in fermentation tanks rather than through animals, we can generate equivalent nutritional value with a fraction of the land, water, and emissions. The technology is no longer speculative. It already produces insulin, enzymes, and vitamins at industrial scale; companies are now brewing dairy proteins and fats molecularly identical to those from cows, without the methane, the manure runoff, or the feed-crop deforestation.
[P1.3] The policy case is straightforward. Subsidies exist to correct market failures and deliver public goods. When they instead entrench an emissions-intensive industry that degrades soil, pollutes waterways, and elevates zoonotic disease risk, they actively harm the public interest. Shifting support toward precision fermentation and low-emissions protein technologies aligns fiscal policy with climate commitments, land restoration, and resilient food systems.
[P1.4] This does not ban meat; it simply stops forcing taxpayers to fund the most environmentally destructive protein source while starving cleaner alternatives of investment capital.
[P1.5] The transition is urgent. Every year of delay locks in infrastructure and supply chains that will be stranded as climate constraints tighten. Governments should redirect these subsidies now, funding the research, scaling, and manufacturing capacity that will make low-emissions proteins affordable and ubiquitous. The alternative is to keep subsidizing the sector most responsible for pushing planetary boundaries past their limits.
