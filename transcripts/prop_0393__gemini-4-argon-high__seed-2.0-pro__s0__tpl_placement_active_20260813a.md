[P1.1] Multi-unit buildings stand for fifty to a hundred years, yet we still permit them as if no resident will ever grow old, break a leg, push a stroller, or use a wheelchair. Designing decades-long infrastructure around temporary peak physical ability is bad planning and worse economics.
[P1.2] Consider the math of blueprints versus sledgehammers. Moving a line on a drawing to widen a bathroom doorway by a few inches, or placing wood blocking behind drywall for future grab bars, adds a tiny sum during initial framing. Tearing out that doorframe, rerouting wiring, and ripping up tile after a resident suffers a stroke costs thousands. Developers want to shave every dollar off upfront construction because they never pay the retrofit bill later.
[P1.3] Tenants, families, and public healthcare budgets pay it—through costly remodels, delayed hospital discharges, or premature moves into institutional care simply because a person cannot navigate their own bathroom.
[P1.4] Second, minimal standards create artificial scarcity and isolation. When only a small fraction of units must be accessible, people with mobility impairments are forced to compete for a tiny sliver of the housing market, and cannot even visit friends or family living in the rest. Universal design—step-free entries, lever handles, reachable controls, and adaptable layouts—does not create clinical, specialized housing. It creates seamless homes that work as effortlessly for a parent with a stroller or a delivery worker with a hand truck as for a senior using a walker.
[P1.5] The opposition will claim that any added cost threatens housing supply. Keep the scale in perspective. Multi-unit budgets are driven by land prices, financing, and permitting delays. Requiring zero-step thresholds and wider interiors at the design stage is a modest adjustment that prevents decades of functional obsolescence.
[P1.6] It is far smarter to pay pennies on the blueprint than fortunes on the demolition.
