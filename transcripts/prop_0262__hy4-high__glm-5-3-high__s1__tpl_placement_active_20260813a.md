[P1.1] Third places are not a lifestyle accessory. They are where people become recognizable without an appointment, purchase, or invitation: a library table, a church basement, a shaded plaza, a laundromat, a cheap café. When those places thin out, society does not lose ambience; it loses the low-cost machinery of trust: elders checked on, teenagers known by adults, newcomers learning norms, parents swapping help, neighbors organizing after a storm.
[P1.2] That loss is large enough for public action because its causes are structural. In many towns, zoning separated housing from shops, banned corner stores, required parking where a porch or bench could have been, and made small gathering rooms hard to open. Rising rents favor businesses that turn tables quickly or sell to higher-income customers. Delivery, streaming, remote work, and irregular schedules pull more life into home and work.
[P1.3] The result is not that nobody has anywhere to go; it is that remaining options filter by income, age, car access, and willingness to spend. A mall is supervised consumption; an online group is not a room; a coffee shop is a third place only if the price is trivial.
[P1.4] Governments should actively fund and zone for their revival because these benefits are public goods that markets underproduce. A landlord cannot charge for reduced loneliness, easier caregiving, safer streets, or a teenager with somewhere to be after school; yet everyone gains. Funding can keep libraries, community centers, pools, public markets, and small meeting rooms open evenings and weekends. Zoning can permit mixed-use blocks, small shops in residential neighborhoods, shared courtyards, civic uses by right, and less parking.
[P1.5] These are not subsidies for nostalgia; they make casual association physically possible again.
[P1.6] Leaving this to markets or charities preserves the filter that produced the shortage: whoever can pay, drive, or belong gets a place. A government that helped write the rules cannot call neutrality a solution when those rules make cheap rooms hard to build.

[C1.4] Second, the mechanism. Third places live or die on demand, not square footage.
