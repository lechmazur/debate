[P1.1] Professional sport is not merely another subscription service whose content happens to feature athletes. Its value depends on a large public expecting to encounter the competition, follow its personalities, and discuss the results. Free-to-air marquee games preserve that expectation. They are not charity: they create audience, cultural relevance, and future demand, ultimately supporting media rights, sponsorships, and local franchises.
[P1.2] The central question is not whether streaming is convenient for committed fans; it can be. The question is who bears the cost of entry. When marquee contests migrate behind recurring paywalls, leagues monetize fans twice: through subscriptions, and then through sponsors seeking access to those same fans. A free broadcast window can convert occasional viewers into followers and customers, making the paywall more sustainable rather than less.
[P1.3] This matters most for marquee events—the championship-level contests and high-profile rivalries capable of attracting people who do not yet subscribe. If those events are withheld, discovery becomes a privilege of existing customers. A young or occasional fan cannot become invested without first paying, and a local business cannot join the audience that drives sponsorships. Restricting the gateway narrows the funnel on which the whole sports economy depends.
[P1.4] Opponents will say younger fans prefer on-demand viewing. But delivery method and access are separate: on-demand streaming can coexist with free access to selected tentpoles. The comparison is not free television versus perfect streaming; it is an open route into the sport versus a closed one. “Meaningful” need not mean every game, team, or minute. Leagues should reserve a substantial, predictable share of marquee contests for free-to-air television.
[P1.5] This protects accessibility, broadcaster competition, and the long-term audience. Subscription platforms should serve committed fans; they should not decide whether the public may watch the sport when it matters most.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a distribution tactic for a public duty. A marquee game is valuable because leagues have invested in competition, production, and stars; the real question is whether regulators or sports should decide how that value is packaged. A mandatory “meaningful share” on free-to-air television is not a free lunch. It forces leagues to reserve their most valuable inventory for broadcasters, depressing competitive bids and the rights revenue that pays athletes, funds youth programs, women’s leagues, and stadium investment.
[C1.2] If a streaming platform will pay a premium for a championship or a rivalry, that money can subsidize the less glamorous fixtures PRO claims to protect.
[C1.3] Nor does a paywall close the sport. Leagues already use free clips, highlights, selected free games, and low-cost ad-supported tiers to recruit fans. That is targeted sampling; PRO’s funnel argument actually supports voluntary free windows, where leagues test which games convert casual viewers and adjust. A statutory share instead locks in a broadcast-era model while audiences, especially younger ones, move to mobile and connected TV.
[C1.4] A free-to-air mandate can even be less accessible: many younger and lower-income households increasingly watch through phones and connected devices, not an aerial. The public already pays through advertising, so “free” is a financing label, not an absence of cost.
[C1.5] “Meaningful” is the giveaway. It is vague enough to mean anything and invites years of lobbying. It also invites regulatory capture: incumbent broadcasters, not fans, will define the quota. Broadcasters get a protected discount, streaming entrants face a tilted playing field, and fans get schedules designed around old distribution rather than demand.
[C1.6] Sports leagues compete globally for attention and capital. Let them negotiate access, including free tentpoles where those build the audience. A commercial choice is not a public entitlement.
