[P1.1] Water-stressed cities face a choice between two kinds of risk. Distant basins promise water, but that promise depends on aqueducts, pumps, land rights, and someone else’s willingness to keep exporting. Direct potable reuse offers something rarer: a supply the city can monitor, meter, and control within its own boundaries. That is why it should be the priority.
[P1.2] Imported water is a long chain of dependencies. A city may pump water hundreds of miles uphill, lose some to evaporation and leakage, and finance enormous infrastructure before receiving a drop. The source basin can be drained or degraded, and the politics can shift when upstream farmers, another city, or a regulator decides the export is no longer acceptable. In a drought, the distant source is often stressed at the same moment as the importing city—so the city has paid for vulnerability, not resilience.
[P1.3] Direct potable reuse closes the loop. The water a city already uses becomes a feedstock rather than a waste stream. Advanced treatment with multiple barriers—filtration, reverse osmosis where needed, oxidation, and rigorous monitoring—can produce water that meets drinking standards. Because the treatment train sits inside the city, problems are detectable and fixable locally.
[P1.4] Windhoek has relied on highly treated wastewater in its drinking supply for decades in a dry region; newer projects in drought-prone places show the regulatory path is real, not hypothetical. The designs differ, but the logic is constant: keep the resource where the demand is.
[P1.5] The objection will be cost or public unease. But the comparison is not free water versus expensive recycling. It is a local plant with known operating costs versus an aqueduct, pumping energy, land acquisition, and decades of basin politics. Public trust is built through transparency and demonstrated performance, not by pretending distant water is untouched.
[P1.6] Prioritizing reuse does not forbid imports as a backup. It asks which supply should anchor the city’s future. The answer is the one the city can reuse, inspect, and control: direct potable reuse.
