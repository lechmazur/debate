[P1.2] Consider the run-up to 2008. The Federal Reserve held policy accommodative through the mid-2000s as national home prices roughly doubled. CPI sat near target. By the metric central banks tracked, nothing was wrong.

CPI underweights housing by design: owner-equivalent rent lags, is smoothed, and ignores capital gains.

Explicit housing targets, paired with macroprudential tools, replace plausible deniability with accountability.
[P1.7] And finally, doing nothing while asset prices surge is a policy choice — it is de facto easy money. The motion asks only that this choice stop being implicit.

[C1.1] Explicit asset-price targeting would corrupt the core mission of central banking. The current mandate of stable consumer prices and maximum employment works because these are aggregate, measurable objectives that monetary policy can actually influence. Housing prices are fundamentally different: a 20% surge in San Francisco likely reflects zoning restrictions, not loose money. Giving central banks responsibility for house price stability would politicize every rate decision, turning monetary policy into a wealth management tool rather than an economic anchor.
[C1.2] Implementation would be a practical nightmare. Defining "stable" housing prices across diverse markets is impossible. Should policy tighten when Toronto rises 15% while rural areas stagnate? By the time national data confirm a bubble, local damage is done.
[C1.3] The dot-com era proves central banks cannot identify mispricing in real time. Worse, explicit targeting creates moral hazard—if markets expect bailouts, speculation worsens, guaranteeing bigger crashes.
[C1.4] The policy conflicts would be paralyzing. Rising CPI but falling house prices: hike to fight inflation and deepen the slump, or cut to prop up housing and let inflation rage? Flexible inflation targeting already allows leaning against financial excesses without this impossible choice. Macroprudential tools like loan-to-value limits and capital requirements can address housing risks directly, without compromising the inflation mandate.
[C1.5] The 2008 crisis was not caused by the Fed ignoring housing prices but by regulatory failures that allowed reckless lending. Adding explicit asset-price targeting would not have prevented the subprime collapse but would have saddled the Fed with an unworkable, politically charged task. Central banks must stay focused on their achievable mandate: maintaining broad price stability. Expanding the mandate would dilute this focus, invite political pressure, and reduce accountability.

Switzerland, Canada, Sweden, and Australia layered on macroprudential tools in the 2010s, and watched house prices accelerate anyway as monetary policy diverged from financial-stability needs.

[P2.6] The 2008 defense is the weakest leg: regulatory failure and the Fed's accommodative stance were not alternative explanations but complementary ones.
