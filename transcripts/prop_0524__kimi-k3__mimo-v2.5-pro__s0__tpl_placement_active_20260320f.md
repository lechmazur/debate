[P1.3] The proposal has three moving parts, and each repairs a distinct failure.
[P1.4] First, the guarantee. Universities and employers invest on multi-year horizons: degree programs, care homes, construction pipelines. Today's alternative is a lottery: sectors like elder care lose workers mid-contract because a minister needed a tough headline.
[P1.5] Second, the cap. Uncapped systems don't stay uncapped; they stay uncapped until they provoke a panic, and panics cut deeper than any planned ceiling. [P1.6] Third, the linkage to housing and local services — the heart of the motion. Migration's real strain is not an abstract number; it is rents and GP waiting lists in specific postcodes. A university wanting a larger allocation can earn one — by funding dormitories or local clinics.
[P1.7] The constraint becomes an incentive to expand capacity itself.
[P1.8] The other side will defend a "responsive" system where demand decides. But demand-led intake is exactly what produced Canada's rental spiral and its panicked cap. The choice is not cap versus no cap; it is planned, capacity-matched migration versus a cycle of surges and crackdowns that serves no one — least of all migrants themselves.
