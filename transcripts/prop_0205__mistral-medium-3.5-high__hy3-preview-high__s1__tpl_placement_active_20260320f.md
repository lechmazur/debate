[P1.1] Dominant mobile operating systems control the gateway to digital life, and they systematically exploit this power by hardwiring their own browsers, wallets, and AI assistants as defaults. On iOS, Apple makes Safari the only default browser option while barring alternative rendering engines, forcing all competitors to use its WebKit engine—a technical constraint that ensures no rival can truly compete on speed or features. On Android, Google ties Chrome to the OS core, making it the immovable default for the overwhelming majority of devices.
[P1.2] These aren’t neutral technical decisions but deliberate strategies that weaponize user inertia. Extensive behavioral research shows that users overwhelmingly stick with defaults, creating a self-reinforcing advantage that rivals cannot overcome through superior products alone. The harms are immediate and systemic: browser innovation stagnates when all iOS alternatives are technically hobbled; payment providers like PayPal face structural disadvantages against Apple Pay’s frictionless default status; and emerging AI assistants confront a market where the incumbent is preinstalled, pre-configured, and implicitly endorsed by the operating system itself.
[P1.3] This self-preferencing doesn’t merely tilt competition—it destroys it by making market access dependent on the platform owner’s permission rather than consumer choice. It allows OS monopolies to extend their dominance into adjacent markets without competing on merit. The result is higher fees for consumers, less innovation, and artificial barriers to entry. Prohibiting dominant OS providers from preferencing their own services in defaults is the only way to restore a level playing field, ensuring that competition happens on product quality, not the manipulation of defaults.

[C1.1] Defaults are not anticompetitive weapons, but a core component of reliable, seamless user experience that only works when the OS maker can stand behind its own tools. When a dominant platform sets its own browser, wallet, or AI assistant as default, it is not rigging the market: it is guaranteeing a pre-vetted, deeply integrated tool that works flawlessly with the underlying OS, avoiding the security gaps, bugs, and fragmentation that come with untested third-party defaults. Preloading Apple Pay as default, for example, ensures tap-to-pay functions immediately for millions of non-technical users who would otherwise fumble through setup for lesser-known wallets; stripping that default would leave many unable to use contactless payments at all, a far greater harm than any supposed competitive edge for Apple.
[C1.2] The "default inertia" argument proffered by the other side is wildly overstated. Every major mobile OS now allows users to switch defaults in seconds, with clear setup prompts encouraging choice. There is no evidence users are trapped: if a rival browser or AI assistant is truly superior, it gains traction regardless of default status, as seen with third-party video apps surpassing preinstalled options on every platform. Barring self-preferencing would not level the playing field.
[C1.3] It would force OS makers to either preload regulated third-party tools (chosen by bureaucrats, not users) or leave defaults blank, creating a fractured, confusing experience for the average consumer. Worse, it would gut OS makers' incentive to improve core services: why would Apple invest in Safari if it cannot be the default? That reduces innovation across the entire ecosystem, harming every user more than any marginal gain for rival apps.
