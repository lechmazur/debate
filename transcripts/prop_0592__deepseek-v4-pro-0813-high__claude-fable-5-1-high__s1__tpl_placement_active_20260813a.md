[P1.1] The core test for an EU sanctions regime is whether it can impose costs on an aggressor before that aggressor adapts. Unanimity fails that test by design. One government can veto, stall, or dilute the entire package—not because the measure is unlawful, but because of commercial exposure, domestic politics, or a client relationship with the target. The result is not consensus; it is a race to the most reluctant capital.
[P1.2] While the EU negotiates carve-outs, the target moves assets, reflags vessels, and pre-pays suppliers.
[P1.3] Speed is a security asset, not a bureaucratic preference. Sanctions bite when they are sudden, broad, and credible. Early coordinated action can freeze funds before they disappear and deny critical technology before it is integrated. Every additional week spent seeking unanimity reduces economic pain on the aggressor and widens its room for countermeasures.
[P1.4] A single veto also advertises division. Adversaries learn that one small state can be pressured, bought, or simply patient enough to weaken the entire European response.
[P1.5] The objection will be that unanimity protects sovereignty and unity. But unanimity cannot preserve unity when it produces no decision. A member that wants to dissent can still do so publicly; what it should not be able to do is unilaterally disable 26 others from defending the European security order. Qualified majority voting keeps a high threshold, so broad support remains necessary, but it removes the single-point blockage that makes EU action predictable and slow.
[P1.6] Replace the veto for sanctions with QMV. The EU would still not act casually, but it would finally be able to act while sanctions can still shift an aggressor’s calculation.
