[P1.1] Farmland is not just another asset class. It is the biological base of the food system and the economic foundation of rural communities. When non-farming institutional investors buy large shares of it, they convert a place-bound public good into a portfolio holding. A cap on that share is a targeted safeguard, not a ban on investment.
[P1.2] The first reason is food-system resilience. Institutional owners owe their returns to funds, not to local soil or watersheds. They consolidate parcels, standardize crops, and push output toward the most liquid commodity markets. That produces efficiency on a spreadsheet, but it makes food landscapes less diverse and less able to absorb shocks.
[P1.3] A landscape of many owner-operators can shift planting, save seed, keep local processing alive, and respond to local shortages. Concentrated absentee ownership replaces that redundancy with a single optimizing logic.
[P1.4] The second reason is rural communities. Institutional purchases drive land prices beyond what working farmers can pay, because the buyer is pricing inflation protection, not farm income. The result is that young producers become tenants or exit. When decisions move to a distant asset manager, the farm family, local suppliers, schools, and main-street businesses lose the spending and stewardship that keep communities alive.
[P1.5] Rural decline then becomes self-reinforcing.
[P1.6] Opponents will say capital is needed. That confuses investment with ownership. A cap still allows institutional funds through lending, equipment finance, crop contracts, or minority stakes. What it limits is control.
[P1.7] That control, not the money, is the threat. Capital can serve farming; ownership should remain answerable to people who live with the consequences.
[P1.8] Because land is finite, ownership determines who decides how food is produced and who benefits. A cap on non-farming institutional owners protects dispersed decision-making, local accountability, and the resilience that concentrated finance cannot provide.
