[P1.1] Shrinkflation works because it hides. The price tag stays the same; the cereal box quietly drops from 16 ounces to 14.5; the peanut butter jar gains a dimple in the bottom. The consumer sees nothing change at the shelf and pays more per ounce. That asymmetry — sellers know exactly what happened, buyers don't — is a textbook information failure, and it is precisely the kind markets cannot fix on their own.
[P1.2] Requiring clear disclosure of package-size reductions and prominent unit-price display corrects the failure without touching anyone's prices.
[P1.3] Three points establish the case.
[P1.4] First, honest pricing requires comparable information. Unit pricing exists because a sticker price alone is meaningless across different sizes. But unit price is usually printed in small type, inconsistently placed, and often absent online entirely — where a growing share of grocery shopping happens. When a package shrinks, the sticker price can stay flat or even "fall," while the real cost per unit rises.
[P1.5] A shopper comparing two boxes on sticker price is comparing apples to holes. Disclosure of shrinkage — a flag when a familiar item's size has fallen — restores the comparison the price system pretends to offer.
[P1.6] Second, shrinkflation is not rare or accidental. It's a pricing strategy used when sellers anticipate customer resistance to headline increases. If it were trivial, companies wouldn't do it. They do it because it works — because consumers anchor on the sticker and miss the denominator.
[P1.7] A practice that depends for its profitability on customer ignorance has no principled claim to protection from transparency.
[P1.8] Third, the burden of this rule is trivially small. Supermarkets already track every SKU's size and price; apps already store version histories of products. Displaying "was 500g, now 450g" costs a line of code and a shelf label. Compare that to the aggregate effect of millions of shoppers systematically overpaying without knowing it.

[C1.2] It's about mandating a second, subjective layer: a government-defined "shrinkflation flag" that retailers must generate and defend. That layer is where the case falls apart.
[C1.3] Start with definition. A flag needs a baseline. Shrunk compared to what — last month, last year, the largest size ever sold? Compared to the multipack, the promotional "bonus" size, the reformulated recipe with a new barcode?
[C1.4] Every one of those questions becomes a compliance dispute, and every answer has an obvious workaround: relaunch the SKU. Change the name, the recipe, the code, and the product has no history to disclose. Where France tried in-store shrinkflation signage, the predictable response was exactly this kind of relabelling and a mountain of paperwork for grocers, not a wave of enlightened shopping. The mandate targets the honest manufacturer who keeps the same product and shrinks it, and spares the one who games the rule.
[C1.5] Then consider what actually reaches the shelf. Thousands of SKUs change size every year; many grow. Flag only the shrinks and you've legislated a biased picture. Flag everything and you've buried the shelf in orange stickers nobody reads.
[C1.6] Warning fatigue is real: the more mandatory labels compete for attention, the less each one moves behaviour.
[C1.7] Finally, the market is already doing the useful version of this. Retailers have run shrinkflation shelf tags voluntarily as a weapon against brands; price-tracking apps exist precisely because consumers want them; journalists publish lists. Competition supplies transparency where it's valued, without freezing one format into law.
[C1.8] Sellers respond to cost shocks by raising prices or trimming sizes. The motion penalises one path, encourages relaunch gimmicks, and adds cost to every grocer. Enforce existing unit-price rules instead. That is the fix — not another sticker.
