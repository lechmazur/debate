[P1.1] Dependency is not a slur; it is a pattern: long spells on benefits, weak labor-force attachment, and benefits that become a substitute for the first step back to work. Work requirements reduce that pattern because they change the default from passive receipt to active engagement. They ask work-capable adults to work, train, search, or volunteer, and they connect benefits to the same routines that lead to stable employment. That is not punishment; it is structure that helps people move from a program to a paycheck.
[P1.2] The harm side of the motion is real only if “genuinely cannot comply” is ignored. The policy can and should exempt people with disabilities, serious illness, elderly recipients, and caregivers, and it should provide accommodations and good-cause exceptions. When those safeguards exist, the person who truly cannot comply is not the target; the target is the adult with no dependents and no barrier who is parked indefinitely in a program. A requirement waived for the medically frail does not harm them.
[P1.3] It screens them out of the obligation.
[P1.4] The dependency reduction is the point. A work requirement creates a nudge toward job search and training, and it makes long-term benefit receipt less automatic. In SNAP, work requirements for able-bodied adults without dependents reinforce existing time limits and encourage attachment; in Medicaid, community-engagement rules can push work-capable enrollees toward employment or training while preserving coverage for those exempt. The comparison is not “requirement versus perfect compassion.” It is “requirement plus exemptions and supports” versus “no expectation at all.”
[P1.5] Yes, paperwork can misfire. But that is an argument for simpler reporting, automatic exemptions, and caseworker help—not for abandoning the expectation. The motion asks whether dependency reduction outweighs harm to the genuinely unable. With real exemptions, the harmed group is small and preventable; the dependency reduction is broad and cumulative.
[P1.6] A policy that helps many move toward work while protecting the few who cannot is a net gain, not a moral failure.

[C1.3] Removing a benefit is not creating a job. Health coverage is a precondition for work, not a substitute for it.
[C1.4] Now the harm side. PRO's case depends on exemptions functioning as designed, but proving you cannot comply is itself a compliance task. The man with untreated schizophrenia must find a doctor, obtain certification, upload forms, and meet a monthly deadline—exercising the very executive function his illness destroys. [C1.5] Paper exemptions are a sieve in practice, and those who fall through are disproportionately the genuinely unable: the mentally ill, the homeless, the low-literate. [C1.6] Meanwhile, the target PRO imagines—the able-bodied adult with no barriers parked indefinitely in the program—barely exists. The requirement operates as a tripwire over a mostly compliant population, harvesting the confused, the unlucky, and the sick.
[C1.7] So the weighing is simple. Dependency reduction: negligible, because the policy removes coverage, not barriers. Harm: concentrated on exactly the people the motion concedes deserve protection. A policy that fails its stated purpose while wounding its own exceptions loses on its own terms.
