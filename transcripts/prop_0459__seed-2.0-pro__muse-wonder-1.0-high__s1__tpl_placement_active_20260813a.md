Every extra dollar spent duplicating a factory is a dollar not spent installing panels, batteries, heat pumps and transmission now. [C1.2] That is the certain cost localisation imposes. Higher prices mean utilities procure less, households postpone electric vehicles and solar, and poorer countries — where most future emissions growth will occur — are priced out. A policy that makes the energy transition more expensive everywhere does not buy climate safety. It trades away the fastest emissions cuts we have for factories that take years to build.
[C1.3] Localisation does not even deliver the resilience it promises. Concentrating production inside one border replaces a foreign chokepoint with a domestic one: a flood, fire, strike, grid failure or change of government can halt the line. Worse, a solar plant or battery gigafactory still depends on imported lithium, graphite, rare earths and equipment. Subsidising final assembly while importing every critical input is theatre, not independence.
[C1.4] Real resilience is cheaper and faster: diversify suppliers across allied countries, keep strategic stockpiles of critical components, design for substitution and recycling, and sign supply agreements that survive a shock. That spreads risk across geographies instead of betting a nation's entire transition on a handful of subsidised plants.
[C1.5] Industrial policy compounds the damage. Governments lock procurement into today's technology, reward firms best at lobbying rather than innovating, and trigger subsidy races and retaliatory tariffs. Retaliation raises the price of the minerals and machinery localisation still needs, fragments the global scale that made solar cheap, and splinters the cooperation climate action requires.
[C1.6] The proposition asks us to accept guaranteed delay, higher emissions now and trade conflict now, in exchange for an incomplete shield against a possible cutoff later. That is not insurance. It is self-harm dressed as security.
