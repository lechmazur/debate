[P1.1] American copyright is a monopoly justified by human authorship, not by output alone. An AI image, poem, or song is generated by a system; the person who asked for it supplied an occasion, not the final expressive choices. If a bare prompt earned copyright, authorship would collapse into button-pressing, and the first requester could fence off machine-produced expression that no person composed. That is a windfall, not an incentive for creativity.
[P1.2] The proposition draws the right line: no protection unless a human can document substantial creative control over the final expression. Copyright concerns that final expression, not ideas, effort, compute costs, or a prompt like “make a noir poster about grief.” A novelist who uses AI for rough sentences but rewrites scenes, cadence, imagery, dialogue, and ending has protectable human authorship; drafts and version history show it. A designer who generates a texture but draws, masks, composites, recolors, and revises the composition can show layer files and edit logs.
[P1.3] Someone who accepts output or picks one from a batch has not made those choices.
[P1.4] Documentation is what makes the line honest. A declaration, process file, drafts, or similar record tells courts, registries, licensees, and downstream users what human authorship is being claimed. Without proof, “human authorship” is a label anyone can paste on mass-produced output, enabling rights laundering: generate thousands of images, assert creative prompting, then threaten competitors over accidental similarities. The burden should sit on the party asking the public to stay off the work.
[P1.5] The contrary position has no stable stopping point. If investment is enough, server time becomes authorship. If mere prompting is enough, every request for a logo, sonnet, or screenplay becomes a property deed. That would fence off the public domain with thin monopolies and chill remix, adaptation, and follow-on creation.
[P1.6] Human creators win under this rule: proven human expression is protected; raw machine output remains free for everyone.

[C1.1] Copyright arises when a human fixes original expression, not when that human files a creative-control dossier. The proposition would withhold protection from AI-assisted output unless the person can document substantial creative control over the final expression. That standard is both too high and aimed at the wrong thing.
[C1.2] It is too high because copyright has never required the author to render, or fully control, every element of the finished artifact. A photographer claims the image even though the camera resolves light she did not paint. Selection, arrangement, rejection, and revision have long counted as authorship. Iterative prompting belongs in that family: specify the scene, discard the failures, recombine what works, and alter mood, pacing, and emphasis until the result expresses a human conception.
[C1.3] Calling that a mere occasion erases the creative act because a model executed the pixels or the syntax.
[C1.4] The documentation clause makes the error worse. It converts authorship into an archive requirement. Protection would turn less on what the human did than on whether chat logs, drafts, or layer files survived. Studios will manufacture those files.
[C1.5] Working artists often will not: sessions expire, direction is spoken, and revision outruns recordkeeping. That is not an honest line. It is a trap that rewards compliance theater, punishes incomplete records, and pushes people to hide generative tools rather than claim them honestly. A work becomes neither clearly owned nor clearly free, only a lawsuit about adjectives.
[C1.6] There is a stable stopping point without this rule. Output with no human original expression stays unprotected under ordinary authorship doctrine. Output that embodies human selection and revision should be protected whether or not a process file exists. The proposed unless-clause does not police windfalls.
[C1.7] It conditions the creative incentive on paperwork and on a degree of final control that generative tools, like cameras before them, were built not to require.
