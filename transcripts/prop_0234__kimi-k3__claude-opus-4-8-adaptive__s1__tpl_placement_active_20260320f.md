[P1.1] A labor market only works when both sides can see the price. Large employers know every salary they pay; workers know almost none. That asymmetry is not a fact of nature — it is a policy choice, and this motion corrects it.
[P1.2] First, bargaining fairness. Workers negotiate every offer against a counterparty holding all the cards. Secrecy converts differences in negotiating aggression into differences in pay for identical work — one reason gaps along gender and racial lines persist even among equally qualified workers. Any other market calls hidden prices a defect: imagine buying a house where the seller knows every comparable sale and you know none.
[P1.3] Second, accountability needs both numbers. Pay bands alone can conceal a glass ceiling — a firm can pay women fairly within each role while promoting men twice as fast into the better-paid ones. Promotion rates by role close that loophole. Sunlight works: where pay is already public, as across much of the public sector, unexplained gaps run narrower, because a disparity that must be written down must also be defended.
[P1.4] Third, the flexibility this motion concedes is mostly flexibility not worth having. Disclosure does not stop a manager from rewarding excellence — bands have ranges, and genuine outliers can be paid above them when justified. What disappears is the ability to pay two people differently for the same work without ever explaining why, and to lowball candidates who don't know better. That is not managerial prudence; it is an information rent.
[P1.5] The costs on the other side — awkward conversations, negotiation friction — are the sound of deferred accountability arriving. A worker who learns from a posting that she is underpaid asks for a raise; a worker who learns it by accident five years later quietly quits, and tells everyone why.
[P1.6] The motion asks the party with all the information and power to publish two figures it already tracks internally — a small, proportionate price for a market that clears fairly.

[C1.1] A labor market doesn't clear more fairly just because you post a number. It clears differently — and often worse — because everyone who reads that number changes their behavior. That is the flaw running through the entire PRO case: it treats disclosure as a window when it is actually a lever, and pulling it produces consequences PRO never priced in.
[C1.2] Start with what published bands actually do. Once a range is public, it stops describing pay and starts governing it. The midpoint becomes an anchor, the top becomes a ceiling no manager will exceed because every exception must now be defended to everyone below it, and the bottom becomes a floor that firms quietly stop hiring beneath — by hiring fewer people. This is wage compression, and it doesn't hurt the powerful.
[C1.3] It hurts the rising star who can no longer be paid above her peers without triggering a dozen grievances. PRO says outliers "can be paid above the band when justified." In practice, mandatory transparency makes that justification so costly that managers stop bothering. The reward for excellence is the first casualty.
[C1.4] Second, promotion rates by role are a statistic begging to be misread and gamed. Raw rates don't adjust for who applied, who's ready, or who declined. A firm facing a bad-looking number won't fix its pipeline overnight — it will reclassify roles, retitle jobs, and promote defensively to move the ratio. You get the appearance of equity and the reality of box-ticking, plus litigation built on numbers stripped of context.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's own goals have better tools. Want to kill negotiation-driven gender gaps? Ban salary-history questions and protect employees' right to discuss pay — targeted fixes without the compression. Want accountability?
[C1.6] Require confidential equal-pay audits regulators can inspect.
[C1.7] So the choice isn't secrecy versus sunlight. It's a blunt public mandate that flattens pay and invites gaming, versus sharper tools that fix the actual harm. Prefer the scalpel.
