[P1.1] The central question is who bears the cost of donor conception. For decades, the answer has been the donor-conceived person: their genetic history is sealed so that clinics, recipients, and donors can proceed smoothly. Once a donor-conceived child becomes an adult, that bargain should no longer be enforceable. The state should give them a legal right to identifying information about the genetic parent.
[P1.2] This is first a right of personal autonomy. An adult cannot understand her own body, ancestry, or family story without access to the person who contributed half her genome. That is not sentimentality; it is the kind of knowledge the law ordinarily treats as basic. Birth certificates, adoption access, and medical consent all assume that knowing one’s origins is not a luxury but a condition of making informed choices.
[P1.3] A donor-conceived adult may need to assess hereditary illness, make reproductive decisions, or simply know whether she has genetic relatives who share her traits and history. Denying that information creates a permanent asymmetry: the clinic, the recipient parents, and the donor may all know, but the one person most affected does not.
[P1.4] The opposition will say access reduces donations. Even if true, that is not a reason to deny the right. We do not generally say a person’s fundamental interest in identity or medical self-knowledge can be extinguished because respecting it would make a service less convenient or more expensive. If donation systems depend on secrecy, they should be redesigned through better recruitment, honest counseling, and fair treatment of donors, not by creating a class of adults barred from knowing their origins.
[P1.5] Jurisdictions that have moved toward identifiable donation have continued to operate donation systems, often with donors who accept openness.
[P1.6] A legal right to identifying information does not compel contact or parenthood. It only ends state-enforced ignorance. That is what adulthood should mean.

Clinics respond by importing donors from anonymous jurisdictions or by narrowing access for single parents, LGBT couples, and lower-income patients who cannot afford the resulting scarcity pricing.

Retroactively converting that anonymous transaction into an identifying one after decades doesn't vindicate a pre-existing right — it rewrites a bargain donors relied on, for people who don't yet exist and whose interests are better served by existing at all.
[C1.6] The better system preserves medical history access and voluntary, mutual-consent contact registries — real autonomy gains without the guillotine of a hard legal mandate.
