[P1.1] The relevant comparison is not apps versus isolation, but the dominant dating-app model versus the relationship pathways it has displaced. That model—photo-first browsing, gamified matching, monetized visibility—is an engagement engine, not a matchmaker. It is optimized to keep users searching, not to move them reliably into committed relationships.
[P1.2] In the core experience, users make hundreds of quick, low-information judgments; people become content, not potential partners. Abundance is the problem. When the next profile is one swipe away, the rational move is to keep options open rather than invest in one imperfect person. That optionality is what commitment cannot tolerate.
[P1.3] The app’s "choice" is a commitment tax.
[P1.4] The business model reinforces this. Subscriptions, boosts, and paid visibility profit from insecurity and unfinished search; a satisfied user stops paying. Thin profiles, curated photos, and no community accountability keep interactions shallow. Ghosting carries no social cost because the pool is anonymous and inexhaustible.
[P1.5] Trust and gradual escalation are harder when everyone feels replaceable.
[P1.6] Compare the alternative. Two people who meet through a running club, a faith community, or a workplace benefit from repeated contact, reputation, and mutual accountability. Those frictions are not inefficiencies; they are how trust forms and intentions get tested. The app strips them away and substitutes notification-driven chat.
[P1.7] A friend can spend weeks trading witty messages that never become a date, because another match always promises a better story. That is why months of matches often produce no second dates, and users describe burnout more than belonging. The modal outcome is churn, not commitment.
[P1.8] Some people do meet partners. The question is whether the model makes relationship formation better for most users than worse. By monetizing indefinite search, removing accountability, and flooding users with disposable options, it does not. The app’s real success is not pairing people off; it is keeping people looking.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes friction for virtue. For most adults, the real alternative to an app is not a vibrant running club full of eligible, interested peers; it is a shrinking circle of coworkers, exhausted friend-of-friends introductions, and hoping a stranger in a bar is single, interested, and safe. Against that baseline, expanding who can meet whom already improves relationship formation.
[C1.2] That expansion matters most for people offline geography punishes. A night-shift nurse, a divorced parent with limited evenings, an LGBTQ person in a small town, or an introvert with a niche faith or hobby cannot rely on repeated contact if the right people are never repeatedly present. An app creates presence first. It turns an otherwise near-zero chance into an actual conversation.
[C1.3] It also makes early vetting more intentional, not less. Profiles and brief chats let people screen for distance, children, smoking, religion, and stated intentions before investing months. Offline chemistry often reveals those mismatches late, after attachment has formed. Messaging is not a replacement for meeting; it is a low-cost, safer way to decide whether to meet, and to exit without social fallout when something feels wrong.
[C1.4] Endless choice sounds corrosive, but dating was always mostly non-matches — offline first dates also usually led nowhere. Users adapt by limiting conversations and moving quickly to coffee, and apps themselves now use prompts, intention labels, and match limits to push depth. Crucially, a larger pool does not just tempt people away from good partners; it lets them avoid settling for the only available partner at work or in their postcode.
[C1.5] Even the profit motive cuts both ways. Successful couples are an app’s best advertising, and a platform known only for dead ends loses trust and new users. Paid boosts sit on top of free matching; they do not erase it.
[C1.6] Frustration is real, but formation begins with access, information, and initiation. For most users, apps provide more of all three than the pathways supposedly lost.
