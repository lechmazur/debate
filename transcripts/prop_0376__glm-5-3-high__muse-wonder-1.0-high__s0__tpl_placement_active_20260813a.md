[C1.1] A cap sounds like cutting the price of debt. In practice it cuts the borrower.
[C1.2] Credit cards are among the riskiest mainstream lending: unsecured, open-ended, available instantly, with no collateral when income disappears. The rate is not just the cost of money. It must cover borrowers in the pool who never repay, fraud, servicing, and the right to borrow again without reapplying. Set the legal price below that combined cost for higher-risk customers, and a lender has one answer: no card, a token limit, or a fee elsewhere.
[C1.3] That rationing falls upside down. A prime borrower with savings, a high score, and competing offers keeps credit — now artificially cheaper. A warehouse worker with a thin file, irregular hours, and one past medical collection loses it. She does not get a capped card; she gets a declined application.
[C1.4] The policy helps people who could already negotiate a lower rate or personal loan, and takes the only flexible line from people who cannot.
[C1.5] Losing that line does not make the emergency cheaper. The transmission still fails, rent still comes due before payday, a child still needs medicine. Demand for liquidity does not vanish because Washington dislikes the price. It moves to overdrafts, pawnshops, payday loans, rent-to-own, late fees, or disconnected utilities — options often more expensive, less regulated, and useless for building credit.
[C1.6] A shutoff or missed bill can also worsen the score that determines every future price.
[C1.7] Even for those who keep cards, the cap leaks. Issuers can add annual fees, trim grace periods, cut limits, raise other charges, and strip rewards. Low-income customers who pay in full can end up subsidizing larger revolvers who capture the capped rate.
[C1.8] Protect borrowers from spirals with ability-to-repay rules, clear payoff disclosures, hardship pauses, and limits on junk fees. Do not help the risky borrower by making her legally unlendable.
