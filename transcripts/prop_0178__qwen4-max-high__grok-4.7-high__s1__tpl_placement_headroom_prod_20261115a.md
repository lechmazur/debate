[P1.1] Population health is the sum of what people actually eat, not the sum of what they intend to eat. That is the whole clash. A label intervenes at the moment of choice, and only if a shopper notices it, understands it, can afford the alternative, and cares enough today to act. A reformulation cap intervenes in the food itself.
[P1.2] It works on the distracted parent, the night-shift worker, the child eating a school lunch, and the person who has never read a nutrition panel in their life.
[P1.3] Britain's phased salt-reduction targets are the clearest demonstration: manufacturers shaved sodium out of bread, cereals, and soups over years, and population intake fell without anyone consciously choosing differently. Salt preference recalibrates; reduce it slowly across an entire category and people stop noticing, because no brand tastes saltier than its rivals. The same logic ended industrial trans fat. Decades of warnings changed little; removing partially hydrogenated oils from the supply removed the exposure.
[P1.4] Now consider what the opposition must assume: that information converts into behaviour at scale, and that a better product is already sitting on the shelf. Both assumptions fail hardest for the people carrying the most diet-related disease. Education rewards the literate, the time-rich, and the affluent, which is why knowledge campaigns so often widen the health gradient they were meant to close. A warning on the only affordable loaf is not a health policy; it is an apology.
[P1.5] Judge the two by reach multiplied by effect multiplied by durability. Mandates reach everyone who eats, including those who tune out health messaging entirely, and they do not decay when the campaign budget ends. One regulatory decision quietly improves millions of meals every day, indefinitely. Bread with less salt helps the man who never read the label.
