[P1.1] American cities are expensive partly because they made adding ordinary homes illegal. For two decades, much residential zoning made the detached house on a large lot the default, while duplexes, fourplexes, small apartment buildings, and flats over shops faced discretionary approvals, parking mandates, hearings, or outright bans. Had those rules changed around 2005, the effect would compound: housing is durable stock, and each year's blocked homes are still missing when families bid for space today.
[P1.2] Legalizing density attacks the scarcity premium. When a city caps how many households can live near jobs, transit, and schools, competition for access gets capitalized into land and existing houses. Allowing more homes lets the same land and infrastructure serve more families; a duplex or six-unit building spreads the land cost that one detached house had to carry alone. The result is lower rents and purchase prices than the exclusionary baseline, not a promise that Manhattan becomes cheap.
[P1.3] Twenty years also changes behavior. Lenders, small builders, and homeowners plan around by-right rules; accessory units, infill, and corridor apartments become routine instead of rare survivors of veto points. New housing filters down: higher-income households move into new units, pressure eases on older apartments, and the existing stock stops functioning like an auction. Refusing that chain does not protect poorer renters; it sends affluent buyers into the same older stock and then cites displacement as a reason to keep blocking supply.
[P1.4] The decisive comparison is not density against zero construction costs, low interest rates, or weak demand; those forces hit either timeline. It is the America actually built—twenty years of artificial scarcity in high-opportunity places—against one where the same demand met elastic supply. Same shock, more homes, smaller price spikes. Cities that kept adding housing generally stayed less punishing than coastal job centers with scarce approvals.
[P1.5] Opponents must defend the strange counterfactual that forbidding two decades of homes made those cities cheaper than allowing them.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a permission slip for an affordability policy. Legalizing denser housing can change what gets built; it does not by itself make a city cheaper. Affordability is the ratio between what households earn and what they must pay. Zoning is one input into supply—and a distant one once land, labor, materials, financing, and demand are in play.
[C1.2] The supply story has a leak: legalization reprices land before it produces a bargain. Give a desirable lot a by-right fourplex or six-unit entitlement, and its owner does not sell at the old detached-house land value. The new capacity is capitalized into the asking price; the builder pays for the entitlement, and the renter pays the builder. Some new units appear, but they are expensive new units in the places people most want.
[C1.3] Filtering is slow and contingent—it needs sustained construction, vacancy chains, and incomes that let people move—not a two-decade switch.
[C1.4] There is also a demand feedback the proposition ignores. A city that adds capacity near jobs and transit becomes more attractive and can absorb more people. More homes can mean more households competing for the same amenities, not a lower citywide price level. The affordable places in America are generally not the ones with the most construction; they are places with weaker demand, lower land costs, or different income mixes.
[C1.5] Permissive rules do not repeal that geography.
[C1.6] The decisive issue is not whether density is good. It is whether the counterfactual city would be more affordable today. The motion needs a price-level effect, not just a unit count. It must show that two decades of by-right densification would have outrun demand, land capitalization, construction bottlenecks, and macroeconomic shocks.
[C1.7] Permission alone does not. We would likely have a denser skyline and a larger population—not reliably lower rents.

[C2.6] The decisive comparison is not blocked homes versus a frictionless market. It is legalization versus legalization filtered through land costs, construction bottlenecks, and redevelopment.
