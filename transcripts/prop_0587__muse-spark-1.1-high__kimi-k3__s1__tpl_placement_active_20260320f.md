[P1.1] Subsidies and domestic regulations can lower emissions inside U.S. borders. They cannot lower emissions outside them, and over five years that is the test that matters. A carbon border adjustment does both.
[P1.2] First, it closes the leakage hole that subsidies leave wide open. American steel, aluminum and cement are already far less carbon-intensive than the dirtiest global alternatives because our grid and scrap-based production are cleaner. When we put costly EPA rules on our own plants and then subsidize their replacements, we raise domestic costs while leaving high-carbon imports cheap. The rational response for global markets is to buy the cheap, dirty ton from abroad and leave the subsidized clean ton stranded.
[P1.3] Every time that happens global emissions go up even as U.S. territorial emissions look better. A border fee that charges imports for their embedded carbon removes that perverse incentive and keeps the emissions cut global, not just local.
[P1.4] Second, it exports a price signal instead of exporting emissions. The United States is still the world's most valuable consumer market. If an Indian steelmaker, a Chinese aluminum smelter, or a Russian fertilizer plant wants to sell here, a border adjustment tells them they will pay unless they clean up. That incentivizes decarbonization abroad without us having to negotiate a treaty.
[P1.5] The EU is already doing this; if the U.S. does it too, the majority of high-income demand for carbon-intensive basics will face a carbon price. No domestic tax credit can do that.
[P1.6] Third, speed. Subsidies take years to permit, build and scale. A border adjustment takes effect at the port next quarter. Within five years the fastest ton avoided is the dirty import that never gets bought.
[P1.7] That is why on a global ledger adding a border adjustment to our current tools beats just doing more of the same subsidies and regulations alone — it makes reductions real, foreign, and immediate.
