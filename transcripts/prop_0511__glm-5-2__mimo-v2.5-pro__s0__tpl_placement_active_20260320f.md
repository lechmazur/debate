[P1.1] The fundamental problem in housing is that land is fixed in supply and often held idle or underused while waiting for appreciation. Land-value taxation attacks that problem directly. By taxing the unimproved land value rather than the building, it raises the holding cost of speculation and lowers the effective penalty on construction. A vacant lot in a growing city currently appreciates at near-zero carrying cost; under LVT, the owner faces a recurring bill that rises with surrounding demand.
[P1.2] That pressure pushes land into productive use—denser housing, redevelopment, reuse of surface lots—without subsidizing demand or capping returns.
[P1.3] Transaction taxes do the opposite. Stamp duties raise the cost of every sale, locking households into homes that no longer fit and discouraging downsizing and mobility. They suppress turnover rather than land speculation. Worse for public finance, they are deeply procyclical: revenue surges in booms and collapses in downturns, exactly when fiscal stress peaks.
[P1.4] Rent caps are still worse for supply. By capping returns on rental housing, they discourage both new construction and maintenance of existing stock. They protect sitting tenants but shrink future supply, distort allocation through shortages and waiting lists, and generate no public revenue at all. They are a transfer mechanism, not a fiscal instrument.
[P1.5] LVT, by contrast, is stable and self-reinforcing. Public investment in transit, schools, or parks raises nearby land values, and LVT recaptures that uplift to fund the next round of investment. It aligns private incentives with public goals: develop where demand is highest, pay for the infrastructure that makes development viable. It does not penalize construction, does not freeze households in place, and does not crater revenue when the market turns.
[P1.6] The proposition turns on a simple comparison. LVT taxes what is scarce and unresponsive—land—rather than what we want more of: transactions, construction, and improvements. That design choice is why it outperforms both alternatives on supply and fiscal stability.

[P2.2] "Difficult" does not mean "worse than the alternative."
[P2.3] The zoning point actually backfires. CON says LVT does not unlock parcels trapped by exclusionary zoning—but LVT is precisely what makes zoning reform politically viable. Today the holder of a surface parking lot in a thriving downtown has every reason to support zoning limits that cap their carrying cost near zero while appreciation accelerates.
[P2.4] LVT flips that incentive. Owners become constituents for upzoning. Transaction taxes and rent caps create no such pressure.
