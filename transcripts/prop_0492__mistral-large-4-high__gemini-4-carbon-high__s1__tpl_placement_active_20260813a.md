[P1.1] Automation is restructuring labor markets now. When a warehouse installs robotic sorters or a retailer replaces cashiers with kiosks, the firm captures the productivity gain while the displaced worker loses wages and benefits—and the public treasury loses the income and payroll taxes that worker once paid. The result is a fiscal leak: private profits rise, public revenues fall, and the costs of unemployment and retraining are socialized onto everyone else. A tax on robotics that replace human labor corrects this imbalance at its source.
[P1.2] The mechanism is direct. When a company automates away a position, it pays a levy calibrated to the lost labor—whether measured against forgone payroll taxes or set as a fixed charge per displaced worker. That revenue funds the transition itself: retraining programs that move truck drivers into logistics coordination, wage insurance for older workers forced into lower-paying roles, and relocation support for communities devastated by plant closures. This is not charity; it maintains the consumer base and social stability that automated production ultimately depends on.
[P1.3] Opponents will warn that taxing robots chokes innovation, but they misunderstand the incentives. Firms automate because machines are cheaper and more reliable than human labor; a modest tax does not reverse that arithmetic. What it does is slow the rush to automate purely to shed headcount while externalizing the human cost. More crucially, without public investment in adjustment, automation provokes backlash—punitive regulation, tariffs, or outright bans that would damage innovation far more than this levy.
[P1.4] By pairing the tax with retraining, governments make technological change compatible with broad prosperity. The alternative is a two-tier economy where capital owns the robots and displaced workers scramble for scraps, an outcome that starves the consumer demand making automation profitable in the first place.

[C1.2] Meanwhile, a new competitor opens an automated facility from day one. Because that entrant never laid off a single worker, it owes nothing. This policy does not protect labor; it punishes existing employers for modernizing, driving legacy firms under and destroying all the human jobs they still supported.
[C1.3] Worse, domestic companies compete globally. Manufacturers often adopt robotics precisely to survive against lower-cost foreign rivals. Penalizing domestic plants for automating forces a losing choice: stay manual and get crushed by automated imports, or move production offshore where no robot tax applies. [C1.5] Nor does the "fiscal leak" in [P1.1] justify taxing machines. [C1.6] Finally, [P1.3] tries to have it both ways, claiming the levy is too "modest" to deter innovation yet heavy enough to slow automation and bankroll sweeping transition programs. In reality, taxing productive tools raises consumer prices and depresses real wages. Supporting displaced workers requires broad, neutral revenue, not an unworkable tax on robotics that exports jobs and punishes the modernization keeping domestic industries alive.

[P2.4] The tax is set below the productivity gain—so automation still outcompetes human labor and proceeds—but above zero, so the headcount reduction funds the transition.
