[P1.1] Political content can be valuable; an unsolicited political recommendation is not a user’s choice. The motion changes one design decision: platforms should not inject politics into feeds before anyone asks. It does not ban posting, subscribing, searching, sharing, or deliberate news use. It requires a clear yes before a platform turns someone’s attention stream into a channel for political persuasion and conflict.
[P1.2] Defaults matter because recommendation systems do not merely display what exists; they select what becomes hard to ignore. One angry clip or inflammatory thread can swallow ten spare minutes and pull a user into rebuttals and dread. Politics is unusually prone to this because moral outrage, fear, and identity often generate engagement. The platform then reads the clicks produced by that impulse as “demand” and sends more. “Users can scroll past” misses the point: these systems are designed to find what people will not scroll past.
[P1.3] Opt-in solves that consent problem without censorship. Anyone who wants politics can follow parties, journalists, activists, local officials, or issue communities; a simple switch can unlock recommendations too. What disappears is only the decision to make every user a target for political activation. A citizen choosing to read competing arguments exercises judgment.
[P1.4] A citizen having outrage inserted between family photos and a recipe is being managed.
[P1.5] CON will call political exposure civic necessity. But necessity cannot be smuggled through an engagement-optimized feed. What default recommendations tend to amplify is not a balanced civics seminar; it is gripping, divisive, shareable material. If political content is valuable, platforms can ask users to choose it.
[P1.6] If they insist an informed public requires unsolicited targeting, they defend bypassing reflective choice.
[P1.7] Political attention should be invited, not conscripted. Opt-in leaves organizing, campaigning, and public argument fully available; it removes only the default power to draft everyone into profitable conflict. Consent before algorithmic political exposure, not silence after it.

[C1.1] PRO treats a recommendation as an act of “conscription.” It is not. A post appearing in a feed is an opportunity to read, ignore, mute, or leave; conscription eliminates choice, while recommendation preserves it. The motion answers a real problem—engagement systems rewarding outrage—with an indiscriminate information barrier.
[C1.2] That barrier falls hardest on people least likely to seek politics deliberately. Committed partisans will opt in and keep receiving arguments. The busy worker who rarely follows council elections, the tenant unaware of a zoning hearing, or the young user who has not yet developed political interests will simply encounter less. Opt-in makes existing motivation the gateway to political discovery.
[C1.3] Platforms would remain free to recommend celebrities, games, and consumer products by default while treating democratic life as uniquely dangerous.
[C1.4] “Political content” is also far broader than inflammatory campaigning. It can encompass public health disputes, court decisions, union activity, war, policing, climate policy, and claims about discrimination or identity. Enforcement therefore pushes platforms toward overclassification: when uncertain, suppress recommendation. Emerging movements and locally important voices, which often depend on discovery beyond their existing followers, stand to lose more than established parties and famous commentators.
[C1.5] The motion even fails against PRO’s central harm. Anyone who opts in still receives the same engagement-driven recommendations. Those most eager for political combat are especially likely to opt in, while casual users depart. That produces a smaller, more self-selected political audience, not necessarily a healthier one.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, useful civic information disappears by default alongside propaganda.
[C1.7] The proportionate remedy is better ranking, chronological-feed options, meaningful topic controls, easy opt-outs, and recommendations based on civic quality rather than compulsive engagement. PRO emphasizes that defaults shape attention, but that cuts both ways: visibility can inform as well as inflame. Political attention should be governable, not gated behind a switch many citizens will never think to touch.
