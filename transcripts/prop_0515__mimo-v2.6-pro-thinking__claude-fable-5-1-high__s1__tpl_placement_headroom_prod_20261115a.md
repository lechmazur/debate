[P1.1] Learning to read is not a natural discovery process like learning to talk. Written language is a code, and children must be taught, explicitly and in sequence, how the sounds of spoken language map onto letters and letter patterns. That single fact should settle the priority question: early literacy policy should put structured reading instruction first, and treat classroom autonomy as a secondary flexibility, not the design principle.
[P1.2] The case for structured instruction rests on three mechanisms. First, reading depends on a stack of component skills — phonemic awareness, decoding, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension — that build on one another. A teacher improvising a sequence from week to week risks skipping a load-bearing element, and the child who needed it doesn't get a second chance in second grade.
[P1.3] Second, sequencing matters enormously. Structured programs front-load the highest-utility patterns, revisit them with distributed practice, and check for mastery before moving on. Random access to texts and "choice time" cannot substitute for that progression. Third, complexity is deceptive: the English code is irregular, but its irregularities are learnable if presented in a planned order.
[P1.4] What looks like teacher craft in designing phonics scope and sequence is actually specialist work most teachers were never trained to do.
[P1.5] The equity argument is decisive. A child in a well-resourced home gets read to, gets vocabulary, gets someone to notice and patch decoding gaps. A child who doesn't gets whatever their school happened to choose. Under autonomy, that lottery becomes the literacy curriculum.
[P1.6] Structure is the mechanism by which a school guarantees every child the same evidence-aligned instruction regardless of which classroom door they walk through.
[P1.7] Notice what "prioritize" does not mean. It does not ban teacher judgment, read-alouds, or joyful choice reading. It means the spine of the curriculum is shared, sequenced, and systematic, and teachers exercise expertise inside that frame. Autonomy is a good servant of literacy and a poor master of it.

[C1.2] Start with PRO's own history. The reading failures they describe were not produced by autonomous teachers improvising. The lottery PRO fears, where a child gets "whatever their school happened to choose," is a description of centralized adoption, not classroom freedom.
[C1.3] Prioritizing structure over autonomy doesn't end the lottery; it just changes which vendor wins it.
[C1.4] Second, a scope-and-sequence is built for a modal child who does not exist. In a real first grade, one kid arrives reading chapter books, three are still shaky on letter sounds, two are acquiring English, and one has an undiagnosed processing difficulty. A pacing guide that says "short vowels, week six" is wrong for most of them simultaneously. Decoding gaps get closed by a teacher who notices the specific error a specific child made on Tuesday and adjusts Wednesday.
[C1.5] That responsiveness is autonomy. Policy that subordinates it to the sequence produces teachers who finish the lesson instead of teaching the student.
[C1.6] Third, PRO's point that most teachers were never trained to design instruction [P1.4] is an argument for training them, not for permanently routing around them. Script-first policy makes deskilling self-fulfilling: why learn linguistics when the box tells you page forty-seven? Jurisdictions that invested in teacher knowledge of the code have gotten durable gains; jurisdictions that bought programs got compliance audits.
[C1.7] So the real comparison: structure the teachers, not the classroom. Give them the science, diagnostic tools, and the authority to use them. Prioritize that, and structured instruction follows. Prioritize the structure, and you get fidelity without reading.
