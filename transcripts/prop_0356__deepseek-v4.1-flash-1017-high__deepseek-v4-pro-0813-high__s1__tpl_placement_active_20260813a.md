[P1.1] Car insurance is not a luxury app. In most places drivers must buy it to legally drive, and a bad price can determine whether they keep a job, a car, or both. That is why continuous in-car surveillance pricing is different from a fitness tracker: the insurer is not observing a hobby; it is setting the price of a compulsory product by watching where, when, and how someone lives.
[P1.2] The proposition does not ban telematics. It says surveillance pricing is permissible only when drivers opt in on genuinely fair terms. The decisive question is whether refusal is real. If a driver who declines surveillance pays substantially more, is denied a competitive policy, or must accept a stripped-down product, the “choice” is coerced.
[P1.3] Consent extracted under threat of unaffordable compulsory insurance is not consent; it is a pricing ultimatum. Insurers can design discounts funded by surcharges on holdouts, then call the result voluntary. A checkbox cannot cure that imbalance; fair terms must be judged by the driver’s realistic alternatives, not the insurer’s disclosure page.
[P1.4] Continuous monitoring also changes the unit of underwriting from driving record to personal life. A night-shift nurse driving home at 2 a.m., a parent lending a car to a caregiver, or a worker in a high-traffic neighborhood may be priced as risky without any crash history. Location data can proxy for income or ethnicity; braking sensors can punish potholes and passengers. The driver cannot audit the model, correct the inference, or know which trips raised the price.
[P1.5] Genuinely fair terms therefore require default-off consent, no penalty or coverage loss for refusal, a comparable non-surveillance option, granular and revocable permissions, and independent transparency. Without those safeguards, continuous surveillance pricing is not a bargain; it is a condition of access dressed as choice. The side defending that is defending coercion with extra steps.

[C1.2] A mandatory product does not prohibit the seller from asking information relevant to price. Refusal can mean a higher price without duress, just as a lender may require income documents or a life insurer may ask about smoking.
[C1.3] The central problem is “genuinely fair terms.” The proposition’s standard—no surcharge, no coverage loss, comparable non-surveillance option—requires equal prices for unequal information. That destroys the pricing signal. Insurers will not build telematics programs if opt-outs must get the same rate. Drivers will not share data if safe behavior produces no discount.
[C1.4] The fallback is the current system the proposition says it opposes: age, ZIP code, credit proxies, and broad claims history doing the underwriting.
[C1.5] Telematics can be the better alternative. A low-mileage older driver, a careful night-shift nurse, or an immigrant with little credit history but good observed driving can prove low risk. That proof only matters when monitored and unmonitored drivers may be priced differently. The proposition makes that differentiation suspect and leaves those drivers stuck with stereotype-based pricing.
[C1.6] That does not mean insurers get blank checks. Consent should be clear, collection limited, data revocable, and models explainable. Strong disclosure and competition can provide real protection. But the proposition goes further: it treats every price difference as punishment and imposes an undefined fairness test.
[C1.7] The better rule is not a conditional ban on the most individualized underwriting tool available.
