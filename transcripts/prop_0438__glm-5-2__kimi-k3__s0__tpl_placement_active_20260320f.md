[P1.1] Young consumers cannot distinguish what a product *does* from what a celebrity *implies* it does. That gap is where harm lives, and that gap is why clear labeling of medical, cosmetic, and wellness claims is not optional.
[P1.2] Three categories exist for a reason. Medical claims assert that a product treats, diagnoses, or alters a condition—they demand evidence and carry risk. Cosmetic claims assert appearance effects—temporary, surface-level, lower stakes. Wellness claims occupy a deliberately fuzzy middle: "supports," "promotes," "nurtures."
[P1.3] When influencer brands blur these boundaries, they borrow the authority of the first category, the leniency of the second, and the unaccountable vagueness of the third—all in a single product page.
[P1.4] The influencer marketing model makes this worse in a way traditional advertising is not. A celebrity founder's parasocial relationship with a teenage audience converts marketing into trusted advice. When a founder with millions of followers says a serum "transforms" skin or a supplement "balances" it, the young consumer hears medical efficacy from a trusted voice, not a wellness slogan from a brand. The existing regulatory architecture was built for labels and packaging—not for Stories, TikToks, and haul videos where claims appear and disappear in seconds.
[P1.5] Young audiences are the right place to draw this line because their skin is still developing, their sensitivity to appearance pressure is acute, and their capacity to interrogate marketing language is immature. A fourteen-year-old buying a "barrier-repair" cream does not know whether "barrier" is a dermatological term or a marketing metaphor—and the brand has every incentive to keep that ambiguous.
[P1.6] A clear-labeling requirement is proportionate. It does not ban any claim. It forces brands to say which category they are making the claim in, so young consumers—and the parents guiding them—can judge accordingly. The burden is a label.
[P1.7] The benefit is honesty at the moment of decision.
