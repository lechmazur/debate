[P1.1] A parent who wins tonight's fight over TikTok wakes up tomorrow to a new app, a new game, a settings update, a workaround learned from YouTube. The app-by-app model asks families to fight a war of attrition against an industry that ships faster than any household can keep up. That is the core failure of the status quo, and it is why schools and pediatricians should anchor digital wellbeing in sleep, boredom, and free play instead.
[P1.2] First, the mechanism. Most of the harm we worry about works through displacement: screens crowd out sleep, unstructured play, and the idle time in which kids learn to generate their own fun. Protect those positives and the harm shrinks no matter which app is trending. A child who sleeps well, reads, and roams outside has a hard ceiling on damage; a child who is sleep-deprived and never bored is in trouble even on parent-approved apps.
[P1.3] Second, enforceability. A pediatrician has fifteen minutes. She cannot audit a family's TikTok settings; she can ask two questions—how much sleep, and is there screen-free play—and actually change behavior. Goals stay stable across platforms and years; app instructions expire with the next update.
[P1.4] Third, the relationship. App-by-app policing turns parents into full-time customs officers, breeds covert use and forbidden-fruit appeal, and burns the trust that is itself a child's best protection. Compare one rule in service of a goal—devices charge in the kitchen overnight. That covers every current and future app through a single calm conversation instead of nightly combat.
[P1.5] This is not permissiveness. Limits remain—but as means to ends, not as the ends themselves. And goals travel: kids eventually leave the house, and a teenager who values sleep and can tolerate boredom carries self-regulation everywhere. A teenager who only ever experienced enforcement carries nothing.
[P1.6] The test of any strategy is whether it still works when the parent isn't in the room. Ours does.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a ceiling for a foundation. Sleep, boredom, and free play are outcomes, not interventions, and framing them as the *replacement* for app-specific guidance leaves families blind to the actual architecture of harm.
[C1.2] Not all screen time displaces sleep equally. A child watching a downloaded film knows an endpoint; a child on a platform engineered for variable rewards and infinite scroll faces a qualitatively different neurological trap. To treat both as generic "screen time" that merely crowds out play is to ignore that some apps are designed to undermine precisely the self-regulation the proposition claims to promote. Pediatricians who refuse to name current mechanisms—autoplay, push notifications, algorithmic rabbit holes—send parents to fight a fire without knowing where the heat is coming from.
[C1.3] Moreover, the app-by-app approach is not the brittle whack-a-mole the proposition describes. It is cumulative education. When schools warn parents about specific dark patterns—whether in a social game exploiting FOMO or a livestream platform enabling predatory contact—they build media literacy that transfers across services. A teenager who learns to recognize infinite scroll on one app carries that skepticism to the next.
[C1.4] Generic wellness goals carry no such transferable knowledge; "get more sleep" offers zero insight into why the child is not sleeping.
[C1.5] The proposition also overstates the practicality of its alternative. Pediatricians already ask about sleep in every visit, and parents already know the answer. What changes behavior is the specific causal link: this app, this feature, this pattern we are seeing in clinic right now. Abandoning that specificity for calm goal-setting does not reduce parental anxiety; it replaces informed vigilance with vague guilt.
[C1.6] The child who needs protection is not fighting abstract displacement. She is fighting specific systems designed to capture her attention. Naming those systems is not combative micromanagement; it is the honest, actionable guidance families deserve.
