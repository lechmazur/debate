[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept a stark trade-off: knowingly making a tax regressive because the target is behavior change. That framing should trouble any judge before a single statistic is cited. When a state deliberately designs a policy so that its heaviest costs land on those least able to pay, it needs overwhelming proof that the payoff is guaranteed and universal. That proof does not exist here.
[C1.2] Consider who actually pays a "meaningfully high" SSB tax. It is not primarily the price-sensitive occasional drinker who quits at the margin—it is the habitual, often stress- or convenience-driven consumer, disproportionately poor, whose demand is the least elastic precisely because soda is cheap, calorie-dense, and psychologically embedded in daily routine. For every household that switches to water, others simply keep buying the same drink at a higher price, absorbing a real income hit with zero health benefit. That is not correction of a market failure; it is a regressive transfer from struggling households to the treasury, dressed up as public health.
[C1.3] Nor does the substitution story favor PRO. Raise the price of soda sharply and many consumers shift to fruit juice, sweetened coffee drinks, or sugary snacks that are often untaxed and nutritionally similar or worse. The tax can hit the wallet without hitting the sugar intake.
[C1.4] The revenue-recycling promise PRO leans on is not part of the resolution at all—the motion is about taxing at high rates, not about a guaranteed, permanent reinvestment scheme. Earmarked "sin tax" revenue is routinely absorbed into general budgets once the initial political spotlight fades; treating a speculative future subsidy as if it were baked into the tax itself is a bait-and-switch.
[C1.5] My case is simple: a policy that knowingly extracts the most money from those who can least afford it, without a reliable mechanism ensuring proportional health return, fails on its own terms—especially when non-regressive alternatives like reformulation mandates or targeted subsidies for healthy foods can pursue the same public-health goal without that cost.
