[P1.1] When most public pension systems were designed, a worker retired at 65 and lived perhaps 12 more years. Today that same worker routinely lives 20 to 25. The pension promise did not change; the math underneath it broke. Linking retirement ages to life expectancy is not a cut — it restores the original deal: a similar ratio of working years to retirement years.
[P1.2] It is the only reform that addresses the actual disease, which is longer retirements, rather than endlessly medicating the symptom.
[P1.3] Higher payroll taxes, by contrast, attack the very engine that funds pensions. Payroll taxes are a tax on work. Every increase makes hiring more expensive and take-home pay smaller, discouraging employment precisely among the young workers whose contributions pay current retirees. Fewer workers and slower wage growth mean a shrinking contribution base — which then demands yet another tax hike.
[P1.4] It is a treadmill that accelerates as the population ages. Europe has run this experiment: countries with the heaviest labor-tax burdens chronically struggle with low employment among both the young and older workers.
[P1.5] The generational arithmetic is just as damning. Longer life is a gift, and indexing retirement age to longevity simply splits that dividend between more leisure and more work. Relying on payroll taxes instead hands the entire dividend to today's retirees and sends the entire bill to smaller cohorts of the young — people facing housing costs and wage stagnation who had no say in the bargain.
[P1.6] Indexing is also the superior *governance* tool: automatic, predictable, and removed from the political cycle. Countries like Denmark, the Netherlands, and Italy have already tied pension ages to life expectancy, so this is tested, mainstream policy — not a thought experiment.
[P1.7] The strongest objection — the construction worker whose body wears out — deserves an answer, and the proposition allows one: disability provisions and carve-outs for arduous occupations. But a real problem for a minority of occupations cannot justify taxing an entire generation's labor to fund retirements that grow longer every decade.

Longevity gains have not been shared equally. Higher-income workers captured most of the extra healthy years.
[C1.2] A construction worker or caregiver worn out by 62 receives none of that dividend. Indexing to the average makes that worker finance a retirement he may never collect, or take a cut after years he could not work.
[C1.3] Disability rules do not close the gap. They reach formal diagnoses and miss the larger group too worn to hold a job but not disabled on paper. Age discrimination and health limits already push many out in their late fifties. A higher pension age creates no demand for their labor.
[C1.4] It opens a stretch with no wages and no full pension. Poverty fills it.
[C1.5] Payroll taxes handle the same math more fairly. A phased rise spreads the cost of longer lives across people still earning, including those whose work remains feasible later. Treating every increase as a job-killing treadmill overstates what modest, predictable hikes do. High-contribution systems have kept employment strong where training and flexible hours hold younger and older workers in jobs.
[C1.6] Stranding the worn-out does more to shrink the base, because early exit into disability or unstable work takes them off payroll.
[C1.7] The old ratio of work years to retirement years is the wrong anchor. Output per worker is far higher than when these systems were built. That productivity is the real dividend. Using some of it for retirement does not require extra years from the people least able to give them.
[C1.8] Automatic indexing loads the cost onto the short-lived and the physically spent. Contribution rates charge those who can still pay. A formula that ignores who can actually work is not better governance. It is harder to correct when it hits the wrong people.
