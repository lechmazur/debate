[P1.1] Sectoral bargaining and wage boards would raise US low-wage pay more precisely and with fewer employment losses than broad minimum wage hikes. A universal $15 floor treats a rural gas station and a Wall Street deli identically, forcing vulnerable employers to cut jobs or hours. Sectoral systems set wages by industry capacity, letting increases flow where productivity and profits support them. Historical precedent is strong: New Deal wage boards delivered targeted pay raises in textiles and garments without the broad displacement seen after large federal minimum wage jumps.
[P1.2] Modern evidence reinforces this. New York’s fast-food wage board implemented substantial raises through gradual, industry-specific steps, with research finding negligible employment effects because the policy accounted for sector health. Sectoral bargaining also enables package deals—wage hikes paired with training funds or productivity agreements—that make higher pay sustainable without layoffs. Unlike blanket mandates, these systems can phase changes, adjust for regional costs, and accommodate firm size differences.
[P1.3] When Seattle hiked its minimum wage, employment in low-margin restaurants fell; sectoral approaches avoid this by letting each industry find its equilibrium. The flexibility reduces automation pressures and offshoring incentives that plague one-size-fits-all policies. Moreover, sectoral systems often include worker and employer representatives, leading to more realistic, enforceable standards. They can also incorporate regional variations within sectors, preventing job losses in areas with lower costs.
[P1.4] The outcome is higher pay for low-wage workers, with employment impacts limited to sectors that can genuinely absorb the change. Meanwhile, across-the-board hikes guarantee job losses in industries that cannot sustain the shock, making sectoral approaches both more effective and more targeted. Critically, sectoral approaches allow for more nuanced adjustments, like different wage floors for urban and rural areas within the same industry, further reducing unnecessary job losses.

[C1.1] The entire appeal of a wage board is that it raises pay in one industry above what everyone else must pay. That is also exactly why it destroys more jobs per dollar of raise, not fewer. Think about a fast-food cook whose employer is suddenly required to pay $20 while the grocery store deli counter, the gas station, and the ghost kitchen across the street still pay $16. Customers shift to the untouched substitutes, the covered employer substitutes kiosks and smaller crews, and the job loss is concentrated precisely among the workers the board was meant to help.
[C1.2] A broad minimum wage does the opposite: every competitor's costs rise together, so no firm loses relative position and the increase passes mostly into slightly higher prices rather than fewer hours.
[C1.3] This is not a novel claim; it follows from the oldest rule in labor economics. Demand for labor is more elastic the narrower the slice of the market you tax. Narrow coverage means easy substitution. That is why the most credible modern research on broad minimum wage increases, including studies tracking jobs near the new floor across dozens of state increases, finds little net employment loss, while the Seattle findings PRO leans on were contested and partly reversed by later work.
[C1.4] Sectoral systems also invite capture. California's fast-food law ended up with carve-outs negotiated by connected chains, and large incumbents routinely welcome rules that raise rivals' costs. The New York fast-food board overlapped with a statewide increase, so attributing its calm outcome to sectoral design is guesswork.
[C1.5] Finally, wage boards cover far fewer workers. A policy that helps a few hundred thousand while displacing a larger share of them cannot beat one that raises pay for millions with diffuse, modest effects. Targeting sounds precise; in practice it aims the damage.
