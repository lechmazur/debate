[P1.1] Severe treatment-resistant conditions are exactly where medicine should be allowed to be bold, not paralysed. The proposition does not legalize psychedelics for recreation; it asks governments to permit a tightly bounded medical pathway—screening, licensed clinicians, supervised dosing, psychological preparation and follow-up, adverse-event reporting, registries, no take-home supply—for people who have already failed standard treatment. That is regulation, not deregulation.
[P1.2] The core injustice is simple. A patient with severe, enduring PTSD or treatment-resistant depression may be too complex, too medicated, or too geographically distant to enter a narrow trial. Trials are designed to produce clean evidence, not to serve everyone. For a veteran who has failed trauma-focused therapy and medication, the choice may be another year of flashbacks or a supervised course of treatment.
[P1.3] Once a treatment has promising controlled evidence and a plausible safety protocol, “wait for another trial” becomes a rationing decision with real costs: continued suffering, hospitalization, lost work, suicide risk. We do not usually tell cancer patients that a promising last-line therapy must remain inside a trial until every long-term question is settled.
[P1.4] The opponent must defend a hard line: even with screening, trained therapists, supervision, and follow-up, the state should forbid access outside trials. But that position does not eliminate risk; it relocates it. It pushes desperate people toward underground use with no screening, no dose control, no therapist, no reporting. A regulated clinic is safer than a black market, and a registry detects harms faster than anecdote.
[P1.5] “Carefully regulated” is the safeguard. If a clinic fails screening or supervision, shut it down. If evidence weakens, restrict it. But the choice is not perfection versus danger.
[P1.6] It is accountable, monitored access versus preventable suffering and unaccountable use. Governments should permit the former. The state should not make a black market the only clinic open after midnight.

[C1.1] The proposition invites governments to bypass the very trials designed to protect patients. Psychedelics can trigger lasting psychosis, hallucinogen persisting perception disorder, and severe anxiety. Early studies are small, short, and often lack blinding—hardly a basis for broad access. The state’s duty is to demand robust evidence, not to yield to urgency.
[C1.2] Desperation does not justify lowering the bar.
[C1.3] The evidence for psychedelics is not yet strong enough. Many trials lack rigorous controls, and the placebo effect is high. For example, in psilocybin trials for depression, patients often know they are receiving the active drug because of its obvious effects, making it hard to isolate the true therapeutic benefit. Also, the long-term effects of repeated use are unknown.
[C1.4] Regulation is not a cure-all. Even with screening, a patient’s complex history may interact with psychedelics in unpredictable ways. The ‘carefully regulated’ label assumes perfect compliance, but real-world implementation will see corners cut, unqualified providers, and off-label use. History warns us: premature adoption of medical innovations has caused harm.
[C1.5] Moreover, the cost and complexity of such regulation might be prohibitive, leading to a system that is either ineffective or so expensive that it excludes the very patients it aims to help. Additionally, therapist training for this work is not standardized, risking exposure to unqualified practitioners.
[C1.6] The black market argument is a false choice. Legal medical access for opioids did not prevent an epidemic; it contributed to one. We cannot assume regulation will contain use. Moreover, the existing black market already supplies these substances.
[C1.7] Creating a medical pathway may simply add a veneer of legitimacy to risky use without reducing overall harm. In fact, it might increase use by making psychedelics seem safer than they are.
[C1.8] Most critically, this move undercuts the clinical trial system. If patients can access the therapy outside trials, recruitment for long-term studies will collapse. Without those studies, we may never know the true risks of repeated use, or the outcomes for patients with multiple co-morbidities. The state must not abandon science for expediency.

[C2.1] The proposition’s narrow pathway still guts the trial system. If regulated clinics exist, patients eligible for trials will bypass them to avoid placebo risk, collapsing recruitment for the long-term studies needed to detect rare, delayed harms like late-onset psychosis or treatment-emergent mania. Conditioning access on trial enrollment [P2.2] fails for exactly the patients PRO claims to help: those too complex, too comorbid, or too unstable to meet typical trial inclusion criteria, creating a parallel system that generates weaker, confounded data with no control group and significant attrition.
[C2.2] Real-world registries cannot replace randomized controls; they are riddled with selection bias, lack blinding, and cannot disentangle treatment effects from powerful expectation effects, especially when the drug’s subjective effects are inherently unblindable.
[C2.3] Supervision does not eliminate core neurological risks. Psychedelics can trigger psychosis, hallucinogen persisting perception disorder, or severe anxiety during the session itself; no pre-screening protocol can guarantee safety given unpredictable interactions with individual brain chemistry, psychiatric history, and concurrent medications. The opioid analogy [P2.4] collapses: while use is clinic-bound, medical legitimization drives normalization, off-label pressure, and diversion—exactly as occurred with medical cannabis, which expanded far beyond initial narrow indications. Even with strict rules, the cultural message is that these are “medicines,” creating demand that strains containment.
[C2.4] Most fundamentally, PRO’s framing is a false dichotomy. We need not choose between regulated clinics and the black market; trials already provide a pathway generating robust evidence while protecting patients. Premature approval risks undermining this system, leaving us with neither reliable data nor the ability to detect long-term harms, while fostering mission creep toward broader, less controlled use.

[C5.1] Governments must not sacrifice the evidence base on the altar of expediency. The patients most in need of psychedelic therapy—those with severe, treatment-resistant, and often comorbid conditions—are precisely the individuals whose long-term outcomes in controlled trials we most urgently need to study. Offer them a clinic pathway, and trial recruitment evaporates. Without those patients, we cannot detect rare, delayed harms like late-onset psychosis or treatment-emergent mania.
[C5.2] Registries cannot substitute for randomized trials: they cannot blind participants to a drug with inherently obvious effects, cannot control for confounding variables, and are overwhelmed by expectation bias. The state’s duty is to demand robust, causal evidence before exposing patients to interventions with known, serious, and potentially irreversible neurological risks. Permitting access now does not merely risk individual patients—it ensures we will never have the data to know if the risk was justified. For severe, treatment-resistant conditions, where the margin for error is smallest, this is the one reason the state must say no: without trials, we fly blind, and the most vulnerable will pay the price.
