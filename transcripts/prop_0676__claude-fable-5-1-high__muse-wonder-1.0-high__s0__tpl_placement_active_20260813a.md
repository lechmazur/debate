[P1.1] Picture a qualified applicant with a facial tic, or an autistic candidate who doesn't make eye contact, or someone whose accent slurs the vowels an algorithm was trained to reward. They record a one-way video interview, and before any human sees them, a model scores their "enthusiasm," "conscientiousness," and "cultural fit" from blink rate, vocal pitch, and word choice. They get a rejection email, or nothing. They will never know why, and neither, often, does the employer.
[P1.2] That is the product. And the case for banning or heavily restricting it rests on three things.
[P1.3] First, the science isn't there. Inferring personality or job performance from facial micro-expressions and voice tone is phrenology with a webcam. Even major vendors in this space quietly dropped facial analysis after researchers and regulators questioned it, which tells you the claims never survived scrutiny. Traditional personality questionnaires fare only modestly better; they measure how you want to present yourself, not how you'll perform.
[P1.4] Second, the harm is discriminatory and systematic. A biased human interviewer harms the candidates in front of them. A biased model harms every candidate, identically, at scale, while wearing the costume of objectivity. Disabled people, older workers, non-native speakers, and racial minorities are precisely the groups whose faces, voices, and self-presentation diverge from the training data's idea of "normal."
[P1.5] Disparate impact law exists for exactly this, but it only works if someone can see the mechanism. Here, nobody can.
[P1.6] Third, the market will not fix this. The rejected applicant bears the cost; the employer gets cheap screening and a vendor's liability shield. Nobody inside the transaction has an incentive to stop. That is the textbook condition for regulation.
[P1.7] "Heavily restrict" is not Luddism. It means: no emotion or facial inference, mandatory independent validation showing the tool actually predicts job performance, disclosure, a human able to override, and audit rights. Tools that can meet that bar can operate. Tools that can't are not assessing people; they're sorting them by guesswork and calling it data.

[C1.1] Banning or heavily restricting these tools does not remove judgment from hiring. It returns judgment to the most biased, least accountable assessor we have: the hurried human with a resume and a gut feeling.
[C1.2] Unstructured interviews reward confidence, similarity, small talk, and where someone went to school. They vary by mood, time of day, and who interviewed before you. An employer cannot audit a manager's intuition, replay it for ten thousand candidates, or prove it treated a disabled applicant fairly. A model, whatever its flaws, can be tested at that scale.
[C1.3] Its scores can be compared across race, age, sex, and disability, its weak features removed, and its decisions reproduced. Standardization is not the enemy of fairness; it is the first condition for detecting unfairness.
[C1.4] That is why the proposal lumps together too much and bans too bluntly. Reading personality from blinks is not the same as a validated personality questionnaire, a structured language assessment for a customer-facing role, or an asynchronous video answer scored against a job-specific rubric. Some tools are speculative. Others give employers something more predictive than pedigree and polish, and give applicants who work shifts, care for family, or live far away a chance to be seen without taking unpaid time to travel for a fifteen-minute screen.
[C1.5] Heavy restrictions would not eliminate screening. They would make it more expensive and more human. Large firms would hire more recruiters and rely more on referrals and elite credentials — channels famous for reproducing privilege. Small firms facing hundreds of applications would fall back on keyword filters, university names, and quick resume scans.
[C1.6] The candidates meant to be protected would face the old gatekeepers, only now with no data trail to challenge.
[C1.7] Discrimination in hiring is already illegal, whether done by a person or software, and employers should remain liable for unjustified disparate outcomes. Enforce that law, require records, and punish false claims. Do not impose an innovation-freezing barrier that treats automation as uniquely suspect when the human status quo is demonstrably worse.
