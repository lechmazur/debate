[P1.1] An employer can now photograph your face, record your voice, and score your personality, then reject you before any human being learns your name. That decision may determine whether you can pay rent, yet you will never be told the score, the criteria, or even the vendor's name. The state should not permit that kind of consequential opacity. Ban it.
[P1.2] The case is not that software is always worse than people. It is that these specific tools are unvalidated, unauditable, and discriminatory in predictable ways. Personality tests in hiring have a long track record of measuring nothing stable; when vendors add face and voice analysis to the mix, the inference chain becomes speculative. Eye contact is read as confidence.
[P1.3] A flat vocal affect is read as disinterest. Someone with a disability, an unusual accent, or a different cultural style of self-presentation is quietly down-scored — and the candidate never knows.
[P1.4] Bias here is no hypothetical glitch. These systems are trained on historical data about who succeeded. If a firm's past hires skewed young, male, local, and conventionally extroverted, the model learns that profile and applies it at scale. A biased human screener is bad; a biased algorithm applied to every applicant, every day, with no opportunity to appeal, is worse by orders of magnitude.
[P1.5] The infrastructure inequality is just as damning. Automated video interviews reward a quiet room, a good camera, and stable bandwidth. The caregiver interviewing from a car, the applicant with an old laptop, the candidate whose camera freezes for two seconds — each is penalized for circumstance, not capability.
[P1.6] Nothing in the motion prevents hiring on merit. Structured interviews, work samples, and skills tests remain. What we remove is the use of secret, pseudoscientific scores as gatekeepers to a livelihood. Where rights are at stake, the burden belongs on the tool to prove validity — and these tools cannot meet it.

[C1.1] The motion treats any automated screening as a dangerous black box that must be stopped. But the harms identified are reasons to regulate, not to ban or heavily restrict. Opaque scores can be disclosed; models can be audited; adverse impact can be tested and corrected. A blanket prohibition would discard tools that standardize evaluation and reduce reliance on who you know, how well you perform in an unstructured chat, or whether a manager simply likes you.
[C1.2] Automated video interviews ask every candidate the same questions and keep a record. That is an accountability advantage over a human interview, where reasons are seldom documented. If scoring disadvantages an accent or a disability, that pattern can be measured across thousands of cases and fixed. The human alternative—one interviewer’s gut—is harder to inspect, yet remains lawful.
[C1.3] Banning the automated option leaves the less transparent system in place.
[C1.4] Personality assessments are not all horoscopes. Some measure job-relevant traits through standardized items; no one serious says they should be the sole criterion, but as one input they can reduce halo effects and unstructured likeability judgments. If employers cannot use them, they will lean harder on resumes, referrals, and informal charm, which carry their own well-known biases.
[C1.5] The access argument cuts both ways. Asynchronous video lets a parent, student, or distant candidate interview without missing a shift or paying for travel. A poor connection is a real problem, but the answer is to require reasonable alternatives and accessibility, not to make the tool illegal for everyone. Heavy restrictions would impose compliance costs that small employers cannot absorb, pushing hiring back to networks and large incumbents.
[C1.6] The strongest form of the concern is already addressed by anti-discrimination law and can be improved with narrow rules: tell candidates when automation is used, permit human review, prohibit reliance on protected traits, and audit for disparate impact. That is oversight. A ban is overkill—and it would preserve the very status quo the proposal claims to fix.

[C2.1] The rebuttal works only by treating every regulation as ornamental and every alternative as the status quo. A validity rule is precise: before an automated personality or video-derived score may be used to reject, the employer must identify the trait measured and show evidence it predicts job performance. That directly answers the equal-invalidity objection [P2.2]: a score that is invalid for everyone can be barred whether or not it tracks a protected group. No ban is needed; enforce the same job-relatedness standard that already governs selection tests.
[C2.2] Disclosure is not empty. It tells the applicant what was scored and triggers review and appeal. Audits need not depend on employer goodwill; regulators can require vendors to file validation reports as a condition of lawful sale. Trade secrecy protects public disclosure, not confidential review by a regulator.
[C2.3] If enforcement today is weak, the remedy is stronger enforcement, not prohibition.
[C2.4] PRO then undercuts itself. It agrees structured questions and notes can come from humans—but they usually do not, especially at volume. Automated video makes consistency cheap enough to replace the informal gut check. Banning it removes that affordable consistency and leaves referral, fit, and charm networks untouched.
[C2.5] The camera-freeze point proves too much. A freeze penalty is a scoring design flaw. Regulators can prohibit environmental or affect-derived variables, require human review before rejection, and let candidates retake or submit written responses when the connection fails. The motion instead removes asynchronous video entirely, hurting the parent in a car more than the curable flaw does.
[C2.6] PRO says a documented wrong score is still wrong. Precisely—so require validation. And a recorded score is easier to contest than an undocumented hunch. If scores become a filter before humans appear, mandate that no automated result alone can reject.
[C2.7] These are targeted conditions, not bans. The appeal to current noncompliance cuts the other way: enforce the rules rather than ban the practice because enforcement failed.
