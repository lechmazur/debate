[P1.1] Isolation and burnout are not individual deficits; they are products of how work is structured. Apps are solitary; recovery is relational. Corporate wellness apps ask employees to meditate their way out of unsustainable schedules and screen-mediated loneliness. They treat the symptom while leaving the disease intact.
[P1.2] Predictable time off and funded, in-person team gathering treat the disease.
[P1.3] Predictable time off is the most credible anti-burnout lever an employer controls. When workers know that vacation is real—that no Slack message will be waiting, that coverage is guaranteed, that using it carries no penalty—they can actually recover. A meditation app cannot simulate a nervous system that has genuinely downshifted. Worse, the "another thing to optimize" framing of wellness apps often adds to the very cognitive load that drives exhaustion.
[P1.4] Employers that buy apps but preserve always-on expectations have simply outsourced the solution to the person who is suffering.
[P1.5] Isolation is a problem of contact, not content. Remote and hybrid work have made teams into sets of usernames. Apps deepen that abstraction: another interface, another task performed alone in front of a screen. A well-designed in-person gathering rebuilds the messy, human stuff of trust—the hallway conversation, the shared meal, the glance that says "we are in this together."
[P1.6] Belonging is not a feature that can be downloaded; it is built through bodies in rooms.
[P1.7] The fiscal comparison is just as stark. Per-employee app licenses buy little durable change and often go unused. The opportunity cost is enormous: they consume attention without changing the calendar or the culture. The same dollars redirected into protected time off or a substantive annual gathering buy actual rest and actual relationship.
[P1.8] If the goal is reducing isolation and burnout, spend on the conditions of work, not on coping tools that ask workers to endure those conditions more gracefully.
