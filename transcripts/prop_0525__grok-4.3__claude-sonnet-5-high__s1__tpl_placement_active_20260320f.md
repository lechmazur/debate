[P1.1] Safe-third-country rules transfer protection duties rather than erase them. They can be justified only when the receiving state supplies enforceable rights to work, appeal, and family unity, because those rights alone keep the transfer from becoming a device for rights avoidance.
[P1.2] Without the right to work, relocation strands claimants in prolonged dependence or informal labor. States then achieve numerical reduction in arrivals while shifting the human costs onto the third country’s economy and onto people already fleeing persecution. The rule loses its claim to fairness once it produces idleness instead of self-support.
[P1.3] Enforceable appeal rights serve a different but equally necessary function. They allow an individual to challenge both the safety designation itself and any errors made in the transfer decision. Absent such review, governments can declare a country safe on the basis of formal agreements while the on-the-ground reality denies protection; the label becomes unrebuttable and the rule unaccountable.
[P1.4] Family-unity protections close the same gap from another direction. When transfers routinely separate spouses or parents from children, the policy severs the social resources most likely to produce stable settlement. The resulting fragmentation is not an incidental side effect but a predictable outcome of rules that treat claimants as interchangeable units rather than members of families.
[P1.5] These three conditions therefore mark the boundary between legitimate burden-sharing and simple externalization. Remove any one of them and the justification collapses, because the arrangement no longer delivers the protection it claims to relocate.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept a strict conjunctive test: no enforceable work rights, no enforceable appeal, no enforceable family unity, no legitimate transfer. That test sounds humane. It is actually a formula for paralysis, and paralysis has victims too.
[C1.2] Start with "enforceable." Almost no legal system anywhere offers claimants iron-clad, litigation-proof guarantees of employment access, appellate review, and family reunification that function perfectly in practice. Canada, Germany, the UK all have documented backlogs, inconsistent tribunal outcomes, and years-long family reunification delays. If "enforceable" means what it should mean under real scrutiny, this standard disqualifies nearly every destination state on earth, including the ones we'd all recognize as fundamentally safe.
[C1.3] That is not a principled boundary; it is a test that only theoretical states pass.
[C1.4] The actual moral question is comparative safety from persecution and refoulement, not the presence of a specific administrative triad. A country that lacks formalized work permits but has functioning refugee camps, food security, informal labor tolerance, and community sponsorship can deliver a safer, more dignified outcome than a country with beautiful statutes and a nine-year appeals backlog. Judging legitimacy by whether rights are "enforceable" on paper rewards legal formalism over lived outcomes.
[C1.5] Worse, this rigid checklist punishes exactly the people it claims to protect. If safe-third-country transfers are permissible only when all three boxes are checked, frontier and transit states absorb everyone, indefinitely, because no destination will ever satisfy the full conjunctive test simultaneously. That is not fairness to claimants; it is a recipe for concentrating displaced populations in the states least equipped to host them, producing exactly the informal-labor dependency and family fragmentation the proposition claims to prevent.
[C1.6] The better standard, and the one this debate should turn on, is whether the receiving state offers a genuine, functioning floor of protection and non-refoulement, assessed practically rather than through a formal checklist that no real government can ever fully satisfy.

[P2.1] The CON case treats enforceability as an impossible ideal that would block every transfer. In practice the standard requires only that work authorization, appeal access, and family protections be real and contestable rather than paper promises that states can ignore. Countries already issue permits and schedule hearings under existing rules; the question is whether those mechanisms survive political pressure once transfers begin.
[P2.2] Without that baseline, the comparative-safety test the opponent prefers collapses into self-certification. A destination can be labeled adequate because it is better than a war zone, yet still deny refugees any route to stable livelihoods or correction of mistaken removals. The result is precisely the externalization the opening described: numbers fall at the border while the human costs are shifted to places whose protection systems remain formal only.
[P2.3] The claim that the triad would trap claimants in transit states reverses the causal direction. Transit states become permanent hosts only when destination countries refuse to accept transfers under conditions that preserve dignity and accountability. Once enforceable rights are required, the same destination states regain an incentive to improve reception rather than off-load responsibility. The stricter test therefore narrows, rather than widens, the set of viable receiving countries; it does not freeze movement altogether.
