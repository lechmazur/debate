[P1.1] Automated immigration and asylum screening decisions carry life-or-death stakes. A denied asylum claim can force return to persecution; an erroneous admissibility rejection can separate families for years; a flawed risk assessment can trigger unwarranted detention or deportation. Yet most jurisdictions allow these decisions to be made by opaque algorithmic systems that never disclose which factors drive outcomes, how those factors are weighted, or what recourse exists for errors. These systems often rely on proxies like social media activity, past travel patterns, or neighborhood-level data tied to historical migrant populations, none of which are disclosed to the people they affect.
[P1.2] Migrants and asylum seekers are frequently left with no way to challenge a decision they cannot even understand, turning automated tools into unaccountable gatekeepers to basic human rights.
[P1.3] A global minimum transparency standard would end this. It does not ban automation, nor does it force states to disclose proprietary code. It simply requires that any automated system used for immigration or asylum screening publicly document its core input categories, the relative weight assigned to each factor, and clear appeal pathways for individuals affected by its decisions. Without a global standard, countries with weak oversight can use secret algorithms to bypass international human rights obligations, while states with stronger rules face pressure to lower standards to match competitors.
[P1.4] This global rule closes the current loophole where governments hide behind "trade secret" or "national security" claims to avoid scrutiny of biased, error-prone tools. This standard aligns with existing international human rights frameworks that require due process for all migration decisions.
[P1.5] Critics will claim this infringes on state sovereignty or exposes security vulnerabilities. But transparency only requires disclosing the general logic and input categories used to make decisions, not the raw data or specific threat indicators used in individual cases. States retain full control over their screening criteria and can still protect sensitive operational details; the standard only ensures those criteria are applied through auditable, not secret, systems. When a person's right to protection hangs in the balance, secret algorithms are never acceptable.
