[P1.1] Employers hire workers to solve problems, serve clients, and deliver finished work, not to jiggle a mouse or keep a status bubble green. When remote and hybrid workers are evaluated through constant monitoring of activity or presence signals, management confuses the theater of work with work itself. If evaluation hinges on keystrokes, screen-time, or instant chat replies, rational employees quickly learn to prioritize visible busyness over actual productivity.
[P1.2] They stretch a two-hour task into eight, send unnecessary messages to prove they are at their desks, and avoid the quiet, unmonitored reflection that complex tasks require.
[P1.3] This distortion is especially destructive in remote and hybrid roles, which are overwhelmingly forms of knowledge work. Consider a software engineer diagnosing a critical security flaw, or a financial analyst structuring a complex model. Their highest-value moments often occur while sketching on paper, reading documentation, or simply thinking away from the keyboard. To surveillance software, that deep focus looks identical to slacking off.
[P1.4] Worse, activity monitoring actively penalizes efficiency. A talented designer who finishes a campaign in four focused hours appears less "active" than a struggling peer who clicks aimlessly for nine. [P1.5] Finally, constant monitoring corrodes workplace culture. Invasive tracking signals fundamental distrust, fueling burnout and driving top performers toward employers who respect their autonomy. Crucially, evaluating workers mainly on outputs does not forbid core collaboration hours or excuse a colleague who vanishes during a team crisis. [C1.2] Those are activity and presence signals, but they are not theatrical busywork; they are the daily fabric of functional team coordination. Worse, centering evaluation mainly on end outputs creates perverse incentives far more damaging than occasional mouse-jiggling or performative check-in messages. A customer support rep can rush through tickets to hit a resolution count, leaving angry customers and messy follow-ups for teammates who have no recourse in a purely output-based system. If a critical server goes down, or a client needs an immediate answer during a launch, a teammate who is consistently unresponsive does real, immediate harm long before any quarterly output metric captures that failure. Consistent, clear activity and presence monitoring is not invasive surveillance; it provides guardrails against free riding, corner-cutting, and the slow collapse of shared accountability that mainly output-based evaluation inevitably enables.
