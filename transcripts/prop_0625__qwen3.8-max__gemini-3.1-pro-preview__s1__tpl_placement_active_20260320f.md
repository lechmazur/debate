[P1.1] Solar geoengineering is not a local technology. Whether by injecting reflective aerosols into the stratosphere or brightening marine clouds, it aims to alter planetary energy balances. The effects cannot be fenced off by a launch site: shifts in atmospheric circulation can change monsoon timing, storm tracks, rainfall, and ozone far from the experiment. That makes even modest field tests international acts, not ordinary science.
[P1.2] The proposition is narrow: a temporary moratorium on field experiments until a binding international governance framework exists. It does not stop computer modeling, laboratory work, or public deliberation. It says that before anyone puts reflective material into the sky over shared systems, humanity needs agreed rules: who may decide, who must be notified, who bears liability, how risks are monitored, how disputes are settled, and how vulnerable states can object.
[P1.3] Without those rules, experiments create three dangers. First, physical harm: plausible mechanisms suggest intervention large enough to measure could perturb weather patterns; a small test may be physically limited, but the political signal is not. Second, legitimacy: a successful field test becomes a proof of concept that accelerates pressure toward deployment.
[P1.4] Third, unilateralism: one country, or even a wealthy private actor, could conduct tests under the banner of research, then claim emergency justification to scale up. Once that precedent is set, every aggrieved state will respond with suspicion or imitation.
[P1.5] CON will say learning is valuable. But the critical bottleneck is not a missing pilot experiment; it is the absence of shared authority over a technology that could threaten food security, water supplies, and international peace. A moratorium is the cheapest insurance policy available: it preserves the option to proceed under law rather than under force or fear.
[P1.6] Until binding rules exist, the safest experiment is no field experiment.
