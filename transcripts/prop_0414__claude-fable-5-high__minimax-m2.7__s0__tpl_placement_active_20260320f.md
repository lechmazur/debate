[P1.1] Overcrowded cities tax tourists for a reason: visitors impose specific, measurable costs on the people who actually live there. Short-term rentals hollow out housing stock in Barcelona and Lisbon. Transit systems built for commuters buckle under cruise-day crowds in Venice and Amsterdam. Streets in historic centers generate trash at rates no residential neighborhood would.
[P1.2] The tourist tax exists precisely because tourism creates these externalities. Basic fairness says the revenue should repair the damage at its source—housing, transit, sanitation—not vanish into a general fund where it might subsidize anything from stadium debt to administrative overhead.
[P1.3] Earmarking wins on three grounds. First, legitimacy. Tourist taxes survive politically only as long as residents believe they're compensated for bearing the crowds. When the money disappears into the general budget, residents see no link between their daily burden and any benefit—and you get the backlash we've already watched erupt: anti-tourist protests, water pistols in Barcelona, ballot pressure to cap visitors entirely.
[P1.4] Earmarking is what keeps the tourism economy sustainable at all.
[P1.5] Second, accountability. A dedicated fund is auditable: residents can ask exactly how many affordable units, bus lines, or sanitation crews the tax bought. General funds are deliberately opaque; money is fungible, and fungibility is how promises evaporate. The standard CON argument—"let elected officials allocate flexibly"—is really an argument for unaccountability dressed up as prudence.
[P1.6] Third, targeting. Housing, transit, and sanitation are precisely the systems tourism strains hardest. Directing revenue there isn't arbitrary ring-fencing; it's matching the remedy to the injury, the same logic as gas taxes funding roads.
[P1.7] Note what the motion actually says: "primarily." This isn't a rigid lockbox that bankrupts a city in a crisis—it's a default presumption that the people who absorb tourism's costs get first claim on tourism's revenue. CON must defend the alternative: that overcrowded cities should keep charging visitors in residents' name while spending the money on whatever is politically convenient. That position has already failed on the streets of Europe.
