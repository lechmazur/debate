[C1.1] The proposition asks countries to "invest heavily" as a general policy - not to build tunnels case-by-case where justified, but to treat underground boring as the default heavy-spend strategy everywhere. That is the claim I am attacking, and it collapses under its own cost structure.
[C1.2] Start with what "high cost" actually means underground: not a fixed premium, but an open-ended risk. Boring runs into unmapped utilities, groundwater, unstable rock, and unexploded ordnance in older cities - problems you cannot fully price before you dig. Boston's Big Dig was sold as a manageable investment and became a decade-long overrun that swallowed billions beyond projection, with debt service the city and state are still carrying. That is not a bad execution story; it is what tunneling does to budgets almost everywhere it is tried at scale.
[C1.3] PRO's own comparison - tunnel invoice versus surface disruption cost - only works if the tunnel invoice is honest. It almost never is.
[C1.4] Meanwhile the land-constraint problem PRO leans on has cheaper solutions sitting in plain sight. Congestion pricing, bus rapid transit on existing lanes, zoning reform that lets density grow near existing surface transit, and selective undergrounding only of the highest-risk utility segments - these add capacity and resilience without a decade of tunnel-boring risk. California wildfire-prone lines are being hardened with insulated conductors and vegetation management at a fraction of full undergrounding cost; full burial was tried, priced, and largely abandoned as too slow and too expensive to scale statewide.
[C1.5] Every dollar poured into deep tunnels is a dollar not spent on transit that moves people this decade, in cities that need capacity now, not in twenty years. A blanket policy of heavy tunneling investment isn't prudence in the face of scarce land - it's committing scarce capital to the slowest, riskiest, least reversible option when faster, cheaper alternatives solve the same constraint.
