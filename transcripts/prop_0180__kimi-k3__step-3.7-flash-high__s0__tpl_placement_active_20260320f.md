[P1.1] A hectare of abandoned Galician hillside or Danube-basin pasture asks almost nothing of us: stop ploughing, pull down a fence, perhaps unblock a drain. A hectare of new plantation demands site preparation, nursery stock, planting crews, herbicide, fencing, and years of thinning — commonly thousands of dollars before canopy closes. This motion is judged per dollar, and that cost asymmetry is the spine of our case.
[P1.2] First, scale. Europe alone is projected to release tens of millions of hectares from agriculture over coming decades as marginal farms fail; North America holds vast tracts of exhausted cropland. Rewilding converts that abandonment into the cheapest carbon and habitat machine on either continent. Reforestation programs must acquire, clear, plant, and maintain; rewilding mostly steps aside and lets succession — a process with no payroll — do the work.
[P1.3] Second, carbon integrity. Planting often disturbs soil and drains ground, releasing stored carbon up front, and many plantations are monocultures harvested on short rotations, returning much of their carbon to the atmosphere. Rewilded land accumulates carbon across the whole system — soils, scrub, wetlands, self-seeded woodland — and a mixed-age, self-assembled ecosystem better resists the fire and pest regimes that have incinerated plantation carbon, as Iberia's plantation fires grimly demonstrated.
[P1.4] Third, biodiversity. A spruce or eucalyptus row-crop supports a thin slice of native life; rewilded mosaics of grassland, scrub, wetland, and woodland edge are among the richest habitats in the temperate world, and they recover large fauna for free — post-Soviet farmland abandonment brought back boar, deer, elk, and wolves without a dollar spent on planting.
[P1.5] CON will plead measurability and food security. But satellite monitoring makes passive regrowth verifiable and cheap to track, and this land is abandoned precisely because it is marginal for food. CON must explain why scarce conservation dollars should buy the most expensive, lowest-diversity way of growing trees while millions of hectares are ready to do the job nearly for free.
