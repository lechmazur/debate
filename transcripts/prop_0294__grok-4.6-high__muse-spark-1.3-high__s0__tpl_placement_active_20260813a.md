[P1.1] When legislatures and school boards treat history as a campaign asset, students inherit myths instead of methods. Public schools should put textbooks on war, colonialism, and national identity under stronger independent scholarly review rather than leave those narratives mainly to shifting political control.
[P1.2] Political capture does not produce a shared civic story. It rotates one. One majority burnishes founding glory and trims atrocity; the next recasts the same events as indictment. Children learn that the past is a loyalty test, not an evidence problem.
[P1.3] Independent scholarly review cannot manufacture a single official truth. It can require sources, competing interpretations, documented methods, and open criticism. That is how historical practice actually works. It is the opposite of a platform plank.
[P1.4] The named topics invite capture because they define belonging. War produces heroes and enemies. Colonialism still organizes claims of debt and pride. National identity is the language of mobilization.
[P1.5] Leave those mainly to political majorities and you get nationalist sanitizing in one era and presentist score-settling in the next. Both are civic malpractice. Textbook fights that swing with partisan boards illustrate the pattern: the chapter changes faster than the archive. A ministry that answers to the next election will prefer a usable past.
[P1.6] A review process that answers to other historians will prefer a defensible one.
[P1.7] Scholars have biases. That is why review must be independent, plural, and revisable when evidence changes—not a reason to let the last election rewrite the chapter on empire. Politicians are selected to win arguments, not to weigh sources. Teachers can still interpret.
[P1.8] Communities can still debate values. The baseline text should not be spoils.
[P1.9] If public schools exist to form citizens who can argue about the past, they cannot treat that past as a ballot prize. Stronger independent review is the restraint that keeps history from becoming just another campaign.

[C1.1] Public schools do not belong to historians. They belong to citizens who pay for them, vote for their boards, and entrust their children to them. Taking the most identity-forming chapters — war, colonialism, nationhood — out of democratic hands and giving them to an unaccountable scholarly panel does not depoliticize history. It disenfranchises the public on the very questions that define it.
[C1.2] Scholars are not umpires above politics. Academic history has its own fashions, orthodoxies, and moral panics, often concentrated in a narrow slice of elite universities. A review board drawn from that guild would not eliminate bias; it would entrench one bias behind a wall of credentials where voters cannot reach it. A legislature that whitewashes can be voted out.
[C1.3] A permanent review college that distorts cannot.
[C1.4] “Independence” is also a fiction. Someone must choose who counts as independent, what counts as scholarship, which interpretations count as legitimate dissent. That choice is itself political, only now made once, quietly, and then shielded from challenge as expertise.
[C1.5] Most importantly, war and colonialism are not puzzles that archives alone can settle. Whether a founding war was heroic, tragic, or criminal, whether empire left debts or institutions, are moral and civic judgments. Footnotes inform them; they cannot resolve them. Those judgments rightly belong to the community arguing through its representatives, not to peer review.
[C1.6] Shifting political control is not the disease. It is the safeguard. It lets Texas and California teach differently, lets a generation correct the exaggerations of the last, and keeps no single priesthood in permanent possession of the national story.
[C1.7] PRO warns chapters change faster than the archive. Good. When the public changes its mind about itself, textbooks should follow, not wait for permission from a committee designed to be immune to elections.
[C1.8] History taught to future citizens must be authorized by citizens.

[C2.7] Evidence must inform citizens, but it cannot authorize them. A textbook the public cannot change is not better history teaching; it is taxation without representation over the story that tells children who they are.
