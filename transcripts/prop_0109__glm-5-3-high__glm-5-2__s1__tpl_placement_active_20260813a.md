[P1.1] Housing is not just an asset class. In a tight market, a single-family home is the difference between a family building equity and a generation renting indefinitely. When institutional investors—private equity funds, Wall Street landlords, iBuyers—buy single-family homes at scale in markets where supply can't meet demand, they change what those homes are for. Cities are right to push back.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. In a market with scarce supply, the price of a home is set at the margin by whoever can pay the most. A family with a mortgage pre-approval competes against a fund that can pay cash, waive contingencies, close in a week, and simply absorb a above-market offer because it's buying two hundred homes, not one. The family loses the bidding war, then faces that same investor as its landlord—paying rent that builds someone else's balance sheet.
[P1.3] That is a structural transfer of ownership from residents to institutions, and it concentrates wealth rather than distributing it.
[P1.4] Second, institutional purchases don't add supply. They convert owner-occupied stock into rental stock, and in the tightest markets they can even reduce effective supply by holding homes vacant or algorithmically coordinating rents. Whatever the merits of investment housing where land is plentiful, in a supply-starved market every institutional purchase is a home a local buyer cannot have. Restricting that purchase doesn't destroy wealth; it reroutes it.
[P1.5] Third, ownership has civic value that renter households, however well-housed, cannot replicate: stability, community investment, school continuity, and the one lever most families have for building wealth. Localities already regulate who can buy for exactly these reasons—foreign-buyer taxes, short-term rental caps, limits on corporate ownership of condos near resorts. Restricting large-scale institutional purchases of single-family homes in defined tight markets is the same tool, aimed at the same problem.
[P1.6] This isn't a blanket ban on investment or on renting. It's a targeted guardrail: where supply is tight and local buyers are being crowded out, protect the entry-level owner-occupied market. Municipalities have both the democratic mandate and the police-power basis to do it. They should.

[C1.1] The proposition misdiagnoses the disease. [C1.2] When a fund purchases two hundred homes, those homes do not vanish. They become rental stock—serving families who lack a down payment, who are new to the region, who need flexibility, or who are saving to buy. [C1.3] That is a reshuffle, not a housing gain.
[C1.4] The PRO framing concedes this: institutional purchases "don't add supply." But neither does blocking them. The supply was already scarce. The question is whether policy addresses the cause or merely reallocates the pain.
[C1.5] Worse, the policy manufactures a scapegoat. Institutional investors own a modest fraction of single-family rental stock even in hot markets. The dominant forces are decades of underbuilding, restrictive zoning, and land costs. A city that bans investor purchases and declares victory while approving fewer new permits than last year has solved nothing—it has held a press conference.
[C1.6] The targeting itself is unworkable. "Large institutional investor," "tight market," and "crowded out" are each fuzzy categories that invite evasion and litigation. Funds restructure into smaller LLCs. Markets soften and restrictions linger.
[C1.7] Local partnerships get swept up. Enforcement consumes the very resources that should fund housing production.
[C1.8] The path forward is obvious and hard: rezone, permit, and build. Buyer restrictions let politicians look tough while avoiding the fights that actually expand supply. They don't lower rents. They don't increase homeownership at scale.
[C1.9] They just pick winners among the same pool of housing-seekers—and distract from the only fix that has ever mattered.
