[P1.1] On a smartphone, the default is not a small convenience; it is the first and often only real chance a product gets. A dominant mobile OS is not just another app store or launcher; it is the bottleneck that decides how any wallet, browser, or assistant meets the user. When one firm owns the road, letting it also drive the only taxi on that road is not neutral design, it is leverage. That is why self-preferencing in defaults deserves a targeted bar.
[P1.2] Start with mechanics. People stick with default settings. Moving to a rival means finding it, installing it, granting permissions, and changing system defaults—small frictions that most users never overcome. The incumbent does not merely compete; it begins at the finish line.
[P1.3] Then comes the data flywheel, most dangerous for AI assistants. The default assistant hears the first questions, learns the user's routines, and improves fastest. A rival that might be smarter on day one never gets the queries, while a preinstalled incumbent gets both usage and a head start that no merit-based race can undo. The same leverage spreads to wallets and browsers through tap-to-pay functionality and search entry points.
[P1.4] CON's retreat to "users can always switch" ignores who reaches viable scale first.
[P1.5] None of this bans the platform's own apps. They can ship excellent products, integrate deeply, and market them hard. What the bar removes is the automatic gift of the starting position. A neutral choice screen or non-preferenced default leaves convenience intact while restoring competition on merit, not ownership.
[P1.6] The user still gets a working setup instantly, but the initial tap becomes a choice rather than a capture. Consumers then lose twice: weaker entrants now, and later services shaped by the gatekeeper's incentives rather than user choice. The burden is CON's: explain why the owner alone must win the default without asking users to choose.
