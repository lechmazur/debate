[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether Britain can eventually prosper outside the EU. It is whether, holding global shocks and domestic policy equal, the British economy today would be stronger without the withdrawal process and the trade barriers it created. It would.
[P1.2] Brexit removed something concrete: seamless access to Britain’s largest, closest market. Exporters now face customs paperwork, rules-of-origin checks, regulatory duplication, and border delays. A car-parts maker, a food exporter, a professional-services firm posting staff to Europe—all now spend money and management time on compliance that once did not exist. That is a direct tax on trade, and trade intensity helps drive productivity.
[P1.3] Brexit also imposed an investment penalty. From the referendum onward, firms had to plan around the possibility of no deal, then adapt to a new regime. Long-horizon investment hates uncertainty. Less business investment means less automation, software, training, and capacity—exactly the supply-side improvements Britain needs.
[P1.4] So the economy is not just smaller in the short run; it is less productive over time.
[P1.5] The labor-supply shock compounds this. Ending free movement made it harder to fill shortages in care, hospitality, logistics, agriculture, and food processing. Those shortages raise costs and cap output. They are not the only cause of Britain’s problems, but they are an additional drag that would not have been imposed without Brexit.
[P1.6] The supposed gains are weaker than advertised. New trade deals cannot substitute for the EU market because geography and proximity still matter enormously. Regulatory freedom is worthless if it is not used, and there is little sign of a deregulatory boom. If anything, firms face more rules at the border, not fewer.
[P1.7] So the comparison is straightforward. Without Brexit, Britain would today have lower trade friction, more investment certainty, and a larger effective labor force. It would still face aging, weak productivity, and global shocks—but from a stronger starting point. The motion asks whether the economy would be stronger, not perfect.
