[P1.1] Rehabilitation should be the primary aim of criminal punishment because the law’s core purpose is to protect society, and only changing offenders can do that sustainably. Retribution may satisfy a desire for vengeance, but it does nothing to prevent the next crime. Empirical evidence from jurisdictions that prioritize rehabilitation shows dramatically lower recidivism. Norway invests in education, vocational training, and therapy; its re‑offending rate is roughly 20 percent, compared with over 70 percent in the United States, where sentencing leans heavily on punitive measures.
[P1.2] The cost difference is stark: every dollar spent on effective rehabilitation saves multiple dollars in future policing, court, and incarceration expenses.
[P1.3] Beyond statistics, rehabilitation respects the moral agency of the offender. It acknowledges that people are capable of growth, aligning with the principle that punishment should aim at restoration, not merely inflicting pain. Programs that treat substance abuse, mental illness, or trauma directly address the root causes of criminal behaviour; retribution ignores these drivers and leaves them untouched.
[P1.4] Consider drug courts in the United States, which divert low‑level offenders into treatment instead of prison. Participants show recidivism rates below 10 percent, far lower than comparable sentenced groups. Victims also report higher satisfaction when offenders take responsibility and make amends—a benefit retribution never delivers.
[P1.5] Retribution also carries hidden social costs. Harsh sentences create cycles of stigma, making reintegration harder and fuelling resentment that can spark further violence. By contrast, a rehabilitative model builds social cohesion: former inmates who acquire skills and emotional stability become contributors rather than burdens.
[P1.6] Thus, when we weigh the goals of criminal punishment—public safety, fiscal responsibility, and moral development—rehabilitation outperforms retribution on every metric. The debate should revolve around which aim actually fulfills the law’s core purpose, and the answer is clear: rehabilitation is the more important aim.
