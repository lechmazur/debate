[P1.1] The state has no legitimate power to keep an adult from the facts of their own birth. Adoption records are the adoptee’s records: names, dates, and the legal acts that created a civil identity. Once that person is grown, sealing those files is not protection. It is a disability imposed on someone who never agreed to live without a past.
[P1.2] Non-adopted citizens can obtain a birth certificate that names their parents. Adopted adults, uniquely, are told that parentage is a secret the government will keep from them forever if a stranger once preferred it. That stranger may have every reason to decline a relationship. They do not have a right to conscript the state into erasing another adult’s origins.
[P1.3] This is not a right to reunion. It is a right to paper. Identifying information is the difference between existing in history and knowing only that some anonymous person relinquished you. Medical risk, half-siblings, the chance of accidental incest, even the simple ability to say a parent’s name—all of that starts with a name.
[P1.4] A file that says “healthy woman, twenty-two” is a bureaucratic shrug, not disclosure.
[P1.5] Confidentiality fails on consent. The child never promised secrecy. Agencies and courts struck bargains over a minor. Adulthood is the line at which that person may claim the file about them.
[P1.6] Birth parents keep ordinary privacy: they can refuse meetings, ignore letters, seek orders against contact. What they cannot own is another person’s knowledge of who they are.
[P1.7] Sealed records also freeze an older bargain—shame around unmarried motherhood, and a preference that adoptive families pretend the child was born to them. That bargain is obsolete, and it was never the adoptee’s to keep. Give adults their records. Private life can still be private after the names are known.

[C1.1] An adoption begins with a promise. A frightened teenager, a poor mother with no options, sometimes a victim of assault or incest, surrenders a child only because the state guarantees her identity will stay sealed. Tear up that guarantee eighteen years later and you do not correct an injustice. You ambush the very person whose sacrifice made adoption possible.
[C1.2] These files are not, as claimed, simply the adoptee's own records. A name on a birth certificate is information about another living adult: who she is, where she might be found, what past she tried to leave behind. No adult has an automatic right to another adult's identity merely because knowing it would feel complete. Curiosity, even profound and sympathetic curiosity, does not extinguish someone else's privacy.
[C1.3] The distinction between paper and reunion is a fiction. In an age of search engines, DNA databases, and social media, to disclose a name is to disclose the person. Telling a birth mother she can just refuse contact after her name, town, and age have been handed over is cruel reassurance. Harassment needs only one determined searcher; peace requires permanent vigilance.
[C1.4] And the cost spreads beyond one family. If every relinquishment becomes guaranteed exposure at eighteen, fewer desperate women will choose adoption at all. Some will parent under duress, some will abort when they might have placed, some will hide pregnancies and abandon infants unsafely. The promise of confidentiality is what makes a safe, legal surrender thinkable.
[C1.5] Compassion does not require an absolute legal right. Provide thorough non-identifying medical and family history, require updated health disclosures, expand mutual-consent registries and trained intermediaries who can ask, not force. Adoptee pain is real, but it cannot justify conscripting the state to strip another person of anonymity she was promised when she was at her most vulnerable.
