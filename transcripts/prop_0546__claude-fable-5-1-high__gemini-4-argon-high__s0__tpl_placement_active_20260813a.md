[P1.2] Three things fill that chair when local journalism dies. First, national partisan media. People who lose a local paper don't stop consuming news; they shift to cable and social feeds that frame every zoning fight as a culture war. Research on news deserts consistently finds split-ticket voting declines and local races turn into referenda on Washington.
[P1.3] That is polarization manufactured by absence. Second, unchecked officials. Studies of newspaper closures find municipal borrowing costs rise afterward, because lenders know nobody is watching the books. Third, "pink slime" sites, partisan operations dressed up as hometown papers, funded by donors precisely because the real thing is gone.
[P1.4] The propaganda risk CON fears is already here, unfunded and unaccountable.
[P1.5] Against that baseline, firewalled public funding is a hedge, not a hazard. The design is known: multi-year appropriations so no single legislature can punish coverage, independent boards, and formula-based distribution, per-journalist payroll credits or per-outlet grants, so no official decides which story gets paid. Canada's local journalism initiative, the UK's local democracy reporting scheme, New Jersey's civic information consortium, and decades of American public broadcasting have operated under hostile governments without becoming house organs. Every real capture story, from Hungary to state broadcasters in weak democracies, involves exactly the thing this motion stipulates away: no firewall.
[P1.6] So the comparison is simple. Funding restores a reporter at the budget hearing; the status quo leaves the seat to the partisan feed and the self-dealing manager. The question is whether a firewalled grant is more dangerous than that emptiness. It isn't close.

[C1.2] The moment a state-backed panel defines acceptable journalism, you have built an engine for ideological gatekeeping. Moreover, statutory firewalls only last until a determined majority rewrites them or slowly packs the board—which is precisely how media capture actually unfolds.

When a fragile newsroom knows its survival depends on the next legislative renewal or tax-credit extension, editors face a quiet chilling effect. [C1.4] Finally, PRO gets the causality of polarization backward [P1.2]. [C1.5] PRO points to American public broadcasting [P1.5], yet its funding is a perpetual partisan football, and public trust in it is sharply divided along party lines. Public funding fails to cure demand-driven polarization while creating a press that is softer on power and less trusted by the public.
