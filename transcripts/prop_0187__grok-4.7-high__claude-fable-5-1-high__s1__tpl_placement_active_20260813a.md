[P1.1] Markets exist to price real economic claims, not to host a private arms race measured in microseconds. High-frequency trading has turned that public function into a contest of cancellations, probes, and retreats. Firms post and pull vast numbers of orders, sniff out slower traders, and vanish the moment stress arrives. When thousands of algorithms chase the same signal, a small shock becomes a cascade.
[P1.2] The liquidity that looked deep on a quiet morning evaporates in the first violent minute. A financial transaction tax on high-frequency trades taxes that behavior where it is cheapest to discourage.
[P1.3] The liquidity objection is already priced into the motion, and it should not be mistaken for a refutation. Much of what high-frequency trading advertises as liquidity is phantom: quotes that exist only until someone tries to use them. A modest levy raises the cost of strategies whose profit depends on enormous turnover and razor-thin margins. Longer-horizon investment and genuine market-making can absorb a small charge.
[P1.4] Quote stuffing, latency arbitrage, and momentum ignition cannot. The trades that disappear are disproportionately those that add noise, withdraw under stress, and force everyone else to pay for a speed race they never entered.
[P1.5] Instability is not a technical footnote. Violent spirals hit retirement savings, trigger margin calls, and make firms hesitate to issue capital because the screen price no longer looks like information. Those losses are large, asymmetric, and borne by households and businesses that gain nothing from being a microsecond late. A slight thinning of average displayed depth is a tolerable price for fewer episodes in which depth was an illusion.
[P1.6] Waiting for the next cascade and then improvising circuit breakers treats the symptom after the damage. A targeted tax changes incentives before the spiral starts. It slows the race without closing the market, and it shifts advantage from the fastest cable toward better analysis. Countries should accept a measured liquidity cost for a more resilient price.
[P1.7] That is the bargain on the table, and it is the right one.

[C1.1] Start with the mechanism the motion assumes and never demonstrates: that taxing high-frequency trades reduces instability. It does the opposite, because liquidity and stability are not separate goods to be traded against each other. Thin liquidity is what instability is made of. A flash crash is, by definition, a price moving violently through a book with nothing in it.
[C1.2] Deliberately thinning that book every ordinary day to punish a rare event is like draining the reservoir to prevent floods.
[C1.3] Second, the tax misses the behavior [P1.4] complains about. Quote stuffing, momentum ignition, and phantom depth are cancellation problems—orders posted and pulled, never executed. A transaction tax lands only on trades that actually happen. So the manipulative quote that vanishes pays nothing, while the market maker who honors a fill pays every time.
[C1.4] Regulators already have tools aimed at the real target: order-to-trade ratio penalties, minimum resting times, cancellation fees, speed bumps, and circuit breakers. Those attack cancellations directly. The tax attacks the honest fill and calls it reform.
[C1.5] Third, this has been tried. When France and Italy introduced transaction taxes roughly a decade ago, turnover fell sharply, bid-ask spreads widened, and volatility did not fall in any consistent way. Sweden's 1980s experiment mostly relocated its own market to London. Trading does not stop; it migrates offshore, into dark venues, or into untaxed derivatives, leaving the domestic exchange shallower and the remaining market makers wider and more skittish—exactly the crowd most likely to step away under stress.
[C1.6] Fourth, weigh who pays. Spread compression over the past two decades is a daily, compounding saving for every pension fund rebalancing and every household buying an index fund. Reversing part of it is a permanent tax on ordinary savers to purchase a benefit the tax cannot deliver. [P1.5] worries about retirement savings, then proposes a levy that quietly bills those same accounts on every trade.
[C1.7] The bargain in [P1.7] is a bad one: certain daily costs, no plausible stability gain, and the actual culprit left untouched.
