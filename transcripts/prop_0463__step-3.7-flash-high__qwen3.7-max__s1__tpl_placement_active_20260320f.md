[P1.1] Moral claims derive their force from their capacity to uphold the equal inherent worth of all persons, not from arbitrary, historically contingent boundaries like national borders. National loyalty is a constructed allegiance tied to the accident of one’s birth geography, while duties to universal human welfare apply equally to every human being regardless of nationality, giving the latter far stronger moral weight.
[P1.2] When these claims conflict, national loyalty routinely justifies preventable harm to outsiders. Take global climate policy: national loyalty demands that wealthy, high-emitting nations prioritize domestic fossil fuel jobs and short-term economic gain, even as their inaction accelerates warming that will kill millions of people in low-lying, low-emitting nations who contributed nothing to the crisis. The universal duty to reduce suffering for all people demands immediate, aggressive emissions cuts, making it a far stronger moral claim than the narrow, parochial demand to prioritize one’s own countrymen.
[P1.3] The same logic applies to refugee protection. National loyalty arguments are routinely deployed to justify closing borders to people fleeing violence, persecution, or starvation, even as those people face imminent, preventable death. Their universal moral claim to safety and dignity cannot be overridden by the arbitrary demand that a state prioritize its existing citizens over vulnerable outsiders. National loyalty is a social convention with no inherent moral standing, deriving whatever weight it has only when it aligns with universal human welfare.
[P1.4] When the two clash, the parochial, arbitrary demand of national loyalty must always give way to the equal, inherent worth of all people.
