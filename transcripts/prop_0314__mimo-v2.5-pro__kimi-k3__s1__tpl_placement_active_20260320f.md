[P1.1] Public subsidies for professional sports stadiums represent one of the most lopsided deals in local government finance. The proposition before us is modest and reasonable: cities should require enforceable revenue sharing or neighborhood-benefit requirements before writing checks to wealthy team owners. This isn't radical—it's basic fiscal responsibility.
[P1.2] Here is the core problem. When a city subsidizes a stadium, it transfers enormous value to franchise owners and their investors. The team captures gate revenue, naming rights, luxury suite income, concessions, and a dramatically more valuable asset. The public, which funded the infrastructure, gets almost none of that back in direct return.
[P1.3] Without enforceable conditions, the subsidy becomes a gift wrapped in civic pride.
[P1.4] The supposed justification—that stadiums generate economic growth and tax revenue—has been studied extensively, and the weight of credible economic analysis finds these benefits wildly overstated. Sports spending largely substitutes for other entertainment spending. Fans redirect their dollars rather than create new ones. The jobs generated tend to be low-wage and seasonal.
[P1.5] And yet cities routinely commit hundreds of millions in public bonds, tax abatements, and infrastructure spending with no mechanism to recapture value.
[P1.6] Revenue sharing requirements solve this directly. If the public invests in the building, the public should share in the building's revenue streams—or at minimum, teams should commit to concrete neighborhood benefits like affordable housing set-asides, local hiring targets, youth recreation facilities, or transit improvements tied to the project. These aren't burdensome demands; they're the bare minimum of accountability.
[P1.7] Without such conditions, cities face a destructive race to the bottom. Teams threaten relocation, playing municipalities against each other, extracting ever-larger subsidies while communities near the stadium see disruption without reward. The current dynamic encourages exactly this behavior because owners know there is no consequence for taking the money and delivering nothing back.
[P1.8] The burden of proof falls squarely on those who want unconditional public generosity toward billion-dollar enterprises. Our position simply says: if you want public money, accept public accountability.
