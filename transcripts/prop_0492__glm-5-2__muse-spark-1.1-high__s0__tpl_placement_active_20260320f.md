[P1.1] Automation lets companies slash labor costs while quietly offloading the human consequences onto workers, families, and public budgets. That is a textbook externality, and the fix is equally textbook: tax the activity that creates the social cost, and use the revenue to repair the damage.
[P1.2] When a factory replaces thirty welders with robotic arms, the company captures the productivity gains. The welders capture unemployment, lost health coverage, and a labor market that no longer values their skills. The surrounding community captures declining tax revenue and rising demand for social services. Right now, the entire adjustment burden falls on the people least able to bear it.
[P1.3] An automation tax flips that logic: the party that benefits from displacement helps fund the transition.
[P1.4] The revenue purpose matters. This is not a punitive levy. It is a targeted mechanism for retraining, relocation support, income bridges, and community adjustment—exactly the interventions that markets underprovide because displaced workers cannot afford them and firms have no incentive to offer them. Without dedicated funding, retraining depends on general revenue that shrinks precisely when displacement erodes the tax base.
[P1.5] The automation tax creates a self-correcting funding stream tied to the source of the problem.
[P1.6] The objection that this slows innovation misunderstands the argument. Taxes on externalities do not stop beneficial activity; they ensure it proceeds only when its gains exceed its full social costs. Automation that is genuinely productive will still pay off even after bearing its adjustment costs. Only automation that was profitable solely because it dumped costs on others gets discouraged—and that is precisely the automation a well-functioning economy should discourage.
[P1.7] Fairness and efficiency point the same direction. Those who profit from replacing workers should help those workers land on their feet. A government that refuses to make that connection is choosing to protect private windfalls at public expense.

[C1.4] Is a self-checkout kiosk a robot? Any rule will be arbitrary — tax hardware but not AI, punish manufacturing but not law firms — and firms will spend more time re-labeling upgrades as capacity expansion than actually upgrading. The safest way to avoid the tax is simply not to invest.
[C1.5] That avoidance is the harm. [P1.6] says productive automation will still pay off. That is true only for the richest firms with the fattest margins. [C1.6] You slow diffusion, you keep prices higher, you keep dangerous and tedious jobs in place, and you push investment to countries that do not tax efficiency.
[C1.7] The funding story is self-defeating. If the tax deters automation, there is no revenue for retraining precisely as jobs still change. If it raises stable revenue, you have admitted you are not deterring displacement at all, only adding a toll on productivity and calling it compassion.
