[P1.1] The question is not whether police are necessary; it is what the next public dollar buys. An additional officer buys a general-purpose, high-cost capacity: salary, benefits, equipment, supervision, training, pension obligations, and months before full productivity. Much of that capacity is absorbed by calls, reports, court time, and responding after violence. Civilian community violence intervention buys something narrower: interruption of the specific conflicts most likely to become shootings.
[P1.2] On shootings prevented per dollar, the narrower instrument is the better marginal purchase.
[P1.3] Shootings are not randomly distributed. They cluster in small, connected networks and retaliatory chains after an assault, insult, or homicide. That structure favors credible local interveners. A violence interrupter can sit beside a wounded teenager in the hospital, learn who is threatening whom, call the cousin with the gun, mediate the dispute, arrange safe transport, and keep contact through the dangerous days.
[P1.4] A patrol car can light up a block; it usually cannot learn the rumor that started the cycle, and an arrest after a shot is fired is not a prevented shooting.
[P1.5] Permitting and scaling remove the barriers that keep this work sporadic: unstable grants, unclear referral rules, limited hospital access, and no lawful channel for nonpunitive information-sharing. With training, transparent governance, and an enforcement backstop for imminent threats, cities can cover conflict networks with teams, flexible support funds, and hospital-based response. A neighborhood team can cost far less than additional officers covering the same hours, while focusing on the small set of disputes generating much of the bloodshed.
[P1.6] The case for more officers confuses visibility with prevention. More officers may raise arrests, stops, or response capacity, but those are inputs or after-the-fact outputs. Badges can deter, yet broad patrol is expensive, slow to scale, and weaker when witnesses fear cooperation. Spending the marginal dollar on another generalist buys more process after shootings rather than fewer shootings.
[P1.7] Cost-effectiveness means fewer funerals per dollar, not more badges per dollar.

And an arrest after a shooting [P1.4] is direct prevention: in tight retaliatory networks, incapacitating one serial shooter stops the next several retaliatory attacks.
[C1.5] Lower wages do not make a program cost-effective if its mechanism breaks down at scale and relies on voluntary compliance. Additional officers cost more per hire because they deliver what civilian mediators never can: lawful coercion, reliable hot-spot deterrence, and the incapacitation of gunmen who refuse to be talked down.
