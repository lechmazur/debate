[P1.1] The core question is not whether blockchain is new, but whether a platform that holds customer assets, decides which products can be traded, profits from order flow, and markets yields is doing something functionally familiar. In most cases, it is. Crypto exchanges are not passive bulletin boards. They list assets, control custody, set trading rules, market pairs, and often trade against users or route orders through affiliated entities.
[P1.2] When a retail customer deposits Bitcoin or stablecoins expecting the platform to safeguard it and facilitate returns, that relationship looks like brokerage, exchange operation, or investment solicitation—not a novel activity beyond investor-protection law.
[P1.3] Regulating most platforms as securities exchanges or broker-dealers follows the basic principle that rules should track economic function, not marketing labels. The securities framework demands segregation of customer funds, capital adequacy, conflict-of-interest controls, transparent disclosures, market surveillance, and accountability when assets are lost or misused. A lightly supervised separate category would repeat the mistake of treating novel wrappers as exempt simply because they use new technology. The harm is not hypothetical: platform collapses have shown what happens when custody, trading, market-making, and lending are combined without exchange-grade safeguards, audits, or enforceable duties to customers.
[P1.4] The opposition may say some tokens are commodities or pure software. That does not defeat “most.” Many retail-facing platforms do far more than connect buyers and sellers; they solicit investment, promise yields, commingle funds, and control access. If a small subset of truly non-security activity exists, it can be addressed with narrow exemptions, while the default regime should be the one designed for intermediaries handling public money. Light-touch categorization invites arbitrage, leaves customers unsecured, and forces regulators into after-the-fact enforcement.
[P1.5] Securities and broker-dealer regulation is not perfect, but it places the burden of safety on the firms before harm occurs.

Securities regulation is not a generic consumer-protection stamp. That is the Howey test, and most major crypto assets do not fit it.
[C1.2] Bitcoin has no issuer, no management team, no promise of returns from entrepreneurial effort. Applying securities exchange rules to platforms that primarily trade Bitcoin or similar decentralized assets is a category error, not a policy choice.
[C1.3] PRO's framing treats this as binary: full securities regulation or a lawless sandbox. That is a false choice. A purpose-built regulatory category can impose robust custody requirements, capital standards, segregation of funds, and market surveillance—without pretending that every token traded is a security. Several jurisdictions are already developing such frameworks without the distortions of shoehorning crypto into the securities model.
[C1.4] "Lightly supervised" is not the only alternative to securities classification; that framing stacks the deck by eliminating the middle ground that actually fits.
[C1.5] The practical harms of PRO's approach are severe. Securities exchange registration imposes issuer-disclosure obligations that are structurally incoherent for decentralized protocols with no identifiable issuer. It would force platforms to delist assets that regulators elsewhere classify as commodities, shrinking market access for millions of users. Compliance costs would crush smaller competitors, entrenching a handful of incumbents—precisely the concentration that makes systemic collapse more dangerous, not less.
[C1.6] And it would push activity to offshore jurisdictions with genuinely weak oversight, achieving the opposite of investor protection.
[C1.7] The platform failures PRO invokes—FTX, Celsius—were not caused by insufficient securities regulation. They were caused by fraud, commingling, and nonexistent internal controls that existing fraud statutes and commodity regulations already prohibited. Enforcement failed, not classification. Applying the wrong framework harder is not a solution.
