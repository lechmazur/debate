[P1.1] Every city in the developed world already accepts this principle: when an invisible byproduct of economic activity damages public health, the city sets binding limits, monitors compliance, and fines violators. That is exactly how we treat exhaust fumes. The motion simply asks why the same logic stops at the ear.
[P1.2] It should not, because the harm profile is nearly identical. Chronic noise is not an annoyance; it is a physiological assault. The World Health Organization treats environmental noise as one of the most serious environmental health risks, and European burden assessments rank it second only to air pollution. Sustained exposure elevates stress hormones, raises blood pressure, fragments sleep, and is associated with measurably higher rates of heart disease and stroke.
[P1.3] Children growing up under flight paths and beside freight corridors show delayed reading acquisition. These are the same pathways through which dirty air kills: cardiovascular strain, cognitive harm, damage that is cumulative and involuntary.
[P1.4] Now compare the regulatory treatment. A factory emitting particulates faces hard limits, continuous monitoring, and real penalties. The same factory's overnight loading operation faces, at best, a vague ordinance enforced only if a resident complains at 2 a.m. and persuades someone to arrive with a meter. Complaint-driven enforcement is not enforcement; it is the appearance of it.
[P1.5] Binding limits are entirely feasible. Cities already condition construction permits on hours and equipment; adding decibel ceilings is administrative, not revolutionary. Noise cameras already operate in Paris and New York. Quieter pavement, electrified fleets, and muffler standards are off-the-shelf technology, not fantasy.
[P1.6] Finally, noise is regressive. Exposure concentrates in poorer neighborhoods pressed against highways and industrial zones, while the affluent buy distance and double-glazing. A harm that falls hardest on those least able to escape it is precisely the kind a city must regulate as public duty, not neighborly courtesy.
[P1.7] The question that frames this debate: if we would never let a factory poison lungs on a complaint-only basis, why may it poison hearts and sleep through the ear?
