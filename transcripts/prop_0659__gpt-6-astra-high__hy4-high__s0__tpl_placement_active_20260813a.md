[P1.1] Countries should prohibit employers from turning machine-inferred emotions into hiring and evaluation evidence. A system that labels a face “unenthusiastic” or a voice “anxious” is not measuring competence. It is converting an interpretation of someone’s inner state into a gatekeeper for their livelihood.
[P1.2] The first problem is the gap between expression, emotion, and ability. A quiet voice could reflect nervousness, cultural convention, disability, or simply someone’s speaking style. The same smile can express warmth, discomfort, or deliberate politeness. Even correctly identifying anxiety does not establish that an applicant would perform poorly.
[P1.3] These systems risk laundering contestable assumptions into apparently objective scores. Once those scores influence rejection, promotion, or dismissal, workers bear the consequences of distinctions the employer may be unable to justify.
[P1.4] The second problem is coercion. Applicants cannot meaningfully refuse emotional surveillance when refusal may cost them a job. Employees cannot freely opt out when their manager controls their wages and advancement. Continuous evaluation also pressures people to perform the emotional signals a system rewards: appear cheerful, sound confident, conceal distress.
[P1.5] That is not merely collecting another productivity metric. It makes conformity of expression a condition of economic security.
[P1.6] Regulation short of prohibition does not adequately solve this. Disclosure tells workers they are being judged; it does not make the judgment valid. A human reviewer can still defer to an automated score. And an accuracy audit cannot, by itself, establish that an inferred feeling is a legitimate employment criterion.
[P1.7] The alternative is not unstructured human prejudice. Employers can use work samples, structured interviews, and observable performance standards. For customer-facing work, assess whether someone resolves a difficult interaction respectfully—not whether software thinks their smile is sincere. These methods can also be scrutinized for discrimination.
[P1.8] This is a targeted prohibition, not a ban on useful employment technology. Countries should draw the line at automated emotional inference because employers have less intrusive, more directly job-relevant ways to assess work.
