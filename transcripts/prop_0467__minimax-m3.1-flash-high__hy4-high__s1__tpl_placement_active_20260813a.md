[P1.1] International law should judge cyberattacks by their effects, not by the weapon used. When a cyberattack deliberately causes civilian deaths, mass injury, destruction of essential facilities, or prolonged nationwide disruption comparable to a conventional armed attack, it should be treated as an armed attack for purposes of self-defense and countermeasures. Otherwise, states gain a perverse advantage: the same physical consequences justify military action when delivered by a missile, but supposedly no comparable response when caused through a network. A cyberattack that cripples a power grid and puts hospitals and patients at risk is not legally less serious because no bomb fell from the sky.
[P1.2] This equivalence concerns legal consequences, not automatic war. A proportional response must remain necessary to stop the attack, protect civilians, and restore essential services. Intelligence operations, sanctions, law-enforcement cooperation, and narrowly targeted cyber counteroperations may suffice. If those measures cannot address an imminent or continuing armed attack, the victim state should retain the option of proportionate military force.
[P1.3] That is neither an unrestricted right to retaliate nor a lowering of the traditional military threshold; it applies the same rule consistently to a new means of harm.
[P1.4] The distinction should be effect-based. A hospital or water system knocked offline for hours is not equivalent to an armed attack. An operation that kills people, destroys critical capacity, or creates lasting consequences on a scale comparable to a conventional strike may be. This standard protects innocent civilians and peaceful uses of cyberspace while denying states refuge behind the fiction that infrastructure networks are legally invulnerable.
[P1.5] The core principle is equality of legal protection. If civilians and essential services are the target, states cannot receive less protection merely because the attacker uses code rather than explosives. Civilian critical infrastructure therefore should attract armed-attack treatment, with measured military response options where lesser measures are inadequate.

[C1.1] Effects matter, but “armed attack” is a legal gate, not a severity label. PRO’s “effects, not by the weapon used” [P1.1] jumps from harm to a license for military force against another state. That category carries killing and escalation that ordinary countermeasures do not. A catastrophic hospital blackout can demand defense, sanctions, indictments, and compensation without making hidden code evidence a basis to strike a suspected origin.
[C1.2] The missing steps are attribution, state responsibility, imminence, necessity, and why force is the proper forum.
[C1.3] Cyber operations make those steps fragile. Malware is routed through hijacked machines, contractors, criminal brokers, and false flags; a cascading outage may mix intrusion with bad patching, weak backups, or a failing generator. PRO says “not automatic war” [P1.2], yet “proportional military response options” changes the crisis default: ministers can act on classified attribution, public panic, and fear of continuing disruption. Proportionality limits reply size; it does not prove agency, intent, or that a strike will restore power rather than widen the conflict.
[C1.4] The target category compounds it. Civilian critical infrastructure is not a battlefield object. Grids, hospitals, water systems, and payment networks are shared, private, interdependent services; routine espionage, ransomware, and probing constantly touch them. If serious disruptions enter armed-attack law, states gain incentives to securitize those systems and turn ambiguity into tests of resolve. “Scale comparable to a conventional strike” [P1.4] is unstable when disruption depends on victim resilience.
[C1.5] A narrow intrusion in a fragile society should trigger hardening, not a war threshold.
[C1.6] That is not leniency. States should treat severe cyber operations as unlawful acts, crimes, or uses of force short of armed attack: defensive cyber operations, sanctions, arrests, asset freezes, compensation demands, and collective pressure. Military force should remain available for genuinely armed violence or cyber acts embedded in an ongoing military campaign. The proposition collapses that distinction, protecting civilians less by pulling more systems under war’s shadow.

Investigators can examine intrusion paths, command infrastructure, timing, targeting, and the operational consequences of an outage. [P2.3] A criminal breach, accidental cascade, or short-lived outage should not trigger armed-attack treatment.
[P2.4] CON also treats “proportional military response options” as a crisis default. It is the opposite. [P2.5] Defensive cyber operations may block an intrusion; they cannot rebuild a destroyed generating station. [P2.6] Finally, civilian infrastructure is not a reason to deny equivalence. Hospitals, grids, and water systems receive especially strong protection precisely because their destruction can kill and deprive entire populations. If an attacker deliberately produces that outcome, the target’s civilian character strengthens the case for protection; it does not downgrade the assault into a technical malfunction.
[P2.7] CON says this “collapses” the distinction. It preserves the distinction by requiring comparable consequences, attribution, necessity, and proportionality. The parity principle remains: those who deliberately inflict armed-scale harm through infrastructure networks cannot gain immunity merely by choosing a different delivery method.
