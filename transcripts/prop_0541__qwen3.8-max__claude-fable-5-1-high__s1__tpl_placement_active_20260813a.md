[P1.1] The central question is not whether exquisite platforms are impressive, but whether the United States can afford, produce, and replace enough capability to win a high-intensity conflict. The answer favors attritable systems.
[P1.2] Modern defense procurement increasingly rewards volume, resilience, and favorable cost exchange. A $100 million interceptor used against a $20,000 drone may preserve a ship, but repeated across a long war it bankrupts readiness. Recent conflicts have shown cheap drones and one-way munitions forcing expensive air defenses to burn scarce interceptors. Exquisite platforms concentrate scarce sensors, stealth, and command authority in small numbers; each loss is strategically crippling.
[P1.3] Attritable systems—small drones, loitering munitions, autonomous patrol craft, expendable sensors, cheap standoff munitions—spread capability across many nodes. The enemy must find, target, and destroy dozens or hundreds of dispersed systems instead of one carrier, one satellite, or one high-end aircraft.
[P1.4] This matters most in the Pacific, where distances are vast and bases are vulnerable. Massed attritable systems can create denial: swarms of inexpensive anti-ship munitions, distributed ISR, and layered one-way attacks can complicate invasion planning without betting everything on a handful of survivable platforms. They also allow rapid iteration. Commercial electronics and software updates can improve performance faster than a decade-long platform upgrade.
[P1.5] The CON will say attritable systems cannot replace stealth fighters, bombers, or submarines. That is a straw man. The motion says shift a significant share, not all procurement. The United States should keep a backbone of high-end capabilities while buying enough cheap systems to absorb losses, saturate defenses, and impose costs at scale.
[P1.6] If the next major conflict becomes a contest of industrial endurance, exquisite platforms alone are too few, too slow to replace, and too tempting to target first. Shifting procurement toward attritable mass gives the United States a force that can fight longer, lose pieces without losing the fight, and out-produce adversary attrition.
