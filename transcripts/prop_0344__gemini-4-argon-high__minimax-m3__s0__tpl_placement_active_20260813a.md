[P1.2] Early secondary school—roughly ages eleven to fourteen—is the worst possible window to tether every student to a personal device all day. Biologically, early adolescence is defined by a developmental mismatch: reward-seeking impulses surge while executive function and impulse control remain under construction. Handing a twelve-year-old a personal laptop for every class asks a developing brain to constantly resist a machine engineered for distraction. Even with district web filters, students inevitably pivot to unblocked games, shared chat documents, and aimless clicking the moment a lesson requires effort.
[P1.3] Teachers are forced to become screen police, competing with glowing rectangles for basic eye contact, while students lose the cognitive stamina required for deep reading and sustained thought.
[P1.4] Just as damaging is the cost to social development. [P1.5] Between classes, devices offer an easy escape from the healthy awkwardness of real conversation, replacing peer connection with solitary withdrawal.
[P1.6] Prioritizing attention and social growth does not require banning technology. [P1.7] However, the capacity for sustained attention and face-to-face social competence must be wired during early adolescence. Public schools should put developing minds and human connection ahead of ubiquitous screens.

In a one-to-one school, she opens a text-to-speech reader, adjusts the font, and stays engaged.
[C1.3] Her attention is protected precisely because the device exists. The same logic applies to English learners, students with ADHD, and any child whose attention profile doesn't match the median. [C1.5] In an era where young people form friendships through shared playlists, group chats, and creative projects, digital fluency is itself a form of social development. Stripping devices from the daily baseline strips students of the medium where their generation practices cooperation.
[C1.6] Finally, equity. When schools treat devices as optional, the homework gap widens immediately. Students without home Wi-Fi fall behind on every digital assignment while wealthier peers continue with tutors and tablets at home. "Prioritizing attention" in the abstract often means shipping kids back into a 1995 classroom and calling it focus.
[C1.7] The motion asks us to choose. I'll take the third option: keep the devices, and use them to build the attention and social skills the opponent claims to defend. That is the actual mission of a modern public school.
