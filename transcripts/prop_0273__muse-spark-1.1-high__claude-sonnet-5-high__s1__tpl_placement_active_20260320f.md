[C1.1] The problem PRO describes is real. The fix PRO proposes is the wrong one, and mandating it will make air travel more expensive and less flexible for almost everyone who is not exactly the family in their example.
[C1.2] Start with the basic flaw: there is no such thing as "the full practical cost of a family trip." Families are not uniform. A family of five needs three seats together and possibly two bags each; a family of three flying to see grandparents may need zero checked bags because grandma has the crib and the extra clothes. Whatever bundle a law forces sites to price and display by default — "one bag plus a standard seat per person" — will be wrong for most actual families, and it will still mislead the ones it's supposedly protecting, just in a different direction.
[C1.3] Now follow the money. Unbundled fares exist because millions of travelers do not want bags or seat selection and do not want to subsidize people who do. That is the entire innovation of budget carriers, and it is why a solo business traveler with a carry-on can fly for $89 instead of $220. If the law requires every comparison to lead with fare-plus-bag-plus-seat as the default price shown, airlines will simply reprice around that default, because that becomes the number customers see and compare on.
[C1.4] The base fare stops functioning as a real, purchasable price. Everyone's ticket, including the childless student who packs light, gets more expensive to make the "family bundle" look honest.
[C1.5] There's already a better fix moving through the market and regulators without this rigidity: toggles that let you input your own bag count and seat need before you compare, and disclosure rules that require ancillary fees to be shown clearly before purchase. That solves PRO's deception problem — you see your real total, for your real trip — without freezing one arbitrary definition of "family" into law and quietly re-bundling prices for everyone else.
