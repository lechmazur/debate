[P1.1] Money is not neutral. When a venture fund in Boston or Munich backs an AI startup in a rival authoritarian state, it does not simply wire dollars. It sends board seats, engineering mentors, hiring networks, access to the fund's portfolio companies, and the credibility that unlocks the next ten investors. That is why founders call it "smart money."
[P1.2] The motion asks whether democracies should at least look before sending that package into the two sectors—advanced chips and frontier AI—where a rival's military and surveillance capacity is being built. The answer is plainly yes.
[P1.3] Three reasons.
[P1.4] First, coherence. Every major democracy already runs export controls on advanced semiconductor equipment and inbound screening on foreign takeovers of sensitive firms. It is incoherent to block the sale of a lithography tool on Tuesday and let a domestic pension fund finance the fab that will house it on Wednesday. Outbound screening closes a gap the rest of the toolkit assumes is shut.
[P1.5] Second, civil-military fusion. In authoritarian systems there is no firewall between a commercial AI lab and the security services. Western investors helped seed facial-recognition companies that later ended up on sanctions lists for their role in mass surveillance of minorities. Those investors did not intend that outcome; they never had to ask.
[P1.6] Screening makes someone ask.
[P1.7] Third, proportionality. The proposition says screen, not ban, and it is limited to strategic sectors of rivals, not all investment everywhere. A notification requirement with the power to block the narrowest tranche of deals is cheaper than almost any other national-security instrument, and it leaves ordinary trade, consumer apps, and pharmaceuticals untouched.
[P1.8] Expect the reply that capital is fungible and rivals will find money elsewhere. Perhaps dollars are fungible. Expertise, network effects, and a Silicon Valley partner's signature on your term sheet are not. The question is not whether a rival can build chips without you; it is whether your own citizens' savings should be the accelerant.
[P1.9] Democracies can say no to that, and they should.

[C1.3] Money has a thousand substitutes.
[C1.4] The 'smart money' objection proves too little. A foreign minority investor rarely controls strategy or accesses sensitive military work where party committees and state customers dominate. [C1.5] Second, that seat has security value. Due diligence, board observation, and commercial ties reveal roadmaps, talent networks, and emerging firms — early warning satellites cannot provide. [C1.7] The screen becomes a de facto ban, deals migrate to non-screening hubs, and allies splinter over who is a rival.
