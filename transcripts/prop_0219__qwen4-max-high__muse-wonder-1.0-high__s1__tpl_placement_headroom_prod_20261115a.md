[P1.1] Pay gaps are built at the first number. When applicants must guess what a job pays, they guess from their last salary — and that salary already carries every discount the market gave them for being a woman, or Black, or Hispanic. Opacity does not erase that history; it imports it into the next offer. A posted range breaks the chain at its cheapest link: before anyone has invested in a search.
[P1.2] The anchor shifts from the candidate's past to the employer's own valuation of the role. That matters because the penalty for negotiating falls unevenly: identical assertiveness reads as confidence in one applicant and aggression in another. A published band removes the need to ask, so the people most punished for asking stop losing money for asking. It also compresses dispersion inside the same job level, which is where most of the unexplained gap actually lives.
[P1.3] Now the cost side. Ranges already exist — every requisition has a budget band, and recruiters already disclose it on the first screening call. The mandate moves that conversation earlier, eliminating interview rounds that end in a salary mismatch. Colorado, New York, and California did not empty their job boards; listings kept coming, and aggregators turned ranges into searchable data.
[P1.4] Efficiency lost is mostly the employer's lost ability to price-discriminate.
[P1.5] So the comparison is asymmetric. The efficiency cost is a one-time adjustment, partly a transfer rather than a loss, and tunable — absurdly wide ranges are an enforcement problem, not a refutation. The other side must show a larger, lasting efficiency loss, not discomfort. The equity gain compounds: every cohort hired under a visible band starts closer to par, and starts the next job from a higher base.
[P1.6] Friction fades; a corrected starting salary follows a person for decades.

[C1.1] Posted salary ranges sound like transparency, but they turn a flexible negotiation about total value into a rigid public sticker, and that sticker does less for equity than it costs in matches never made.
[C1.2] First, ranges do not erase the gaps they target. Employers can post wide, vague bands — $70,000 to $150,000 for a mid-level role — that satisfy the law while preserving discretion where offers are actually set. Within any band, managers still reward prior pay, referrals, scarce credentials, and negotiation at the top versus the bottom, the very channels that reproduce gender and racial disparities. The mandate may compress the posted range; it does not compress the paid range.
[C1.3] Second, hiring is not buying a commodity. Pay bundles experience, scope, location, flexibility, equity, signing bonuses, and growth potential into one match. A posted band forces employers to price a role before meeting the person who might expand it or split it. Faced with legal exposure and internal-equity complaints if they exceed the band, firms respond predictably: lower the top, narrow job levels, hire more cautiously, leave postings generic, or move roles to states without mandates.
[C1.4] Small firms without compensation teams suffer most, posting defensively low or not at all.
[C1.5] That rigid pricing destroys efficient matches on both sides. A strong candidate who would accept flexible hours for slightly less pay never applies because the floor looks wrong. A firm that would stretch for an exceptional hire cannot without triggering demands from current staff. Search lengthens, vacancies persist, and employers substitute toward referrals and closed networks — channels historically worse for underrepresented workers.
[C1.6] The result is the worst trade: symbolic leveling inside postings, real leveling down of opportunity outside them, and persistent gaps relocated to bonuses, titles, and promotions where transparency cannot follow.

Wide enough to avoid compressing pay, rigid enough to distort hiring: that is the worst slice of each.
[C2.2] A private budget is not the same as that public promise. A manager can revise a budget for an unusual candidate, a split role, or a different location. Once a range is posted, revising it looks like bait-and-switch, invites complaints from rejected applicants, and forces consistency over fit. Employers respond beforehand by leveling down the top, broadening a title to cover several levels, or keeping postings generic, which makes matching worse even when the band looks wide.
[C2.3] The outsider argument ignores substitution. A posted number can inform a stranger, but if posting raises the cost of an open search, firms do fewer open searches. They fill roles through agencies, convert them to contract work, promote internally, or tap referrals before a broad posting generates hundreds of mismatched applications. Public boards then display compliant ranges for jobs that remain, while access to faster-moving opportunities narrows for workers without networks.
[C2.4] Calling bonuses, titles, and promotions mere leaks concedes the comparison. If discretion migrates to less visible pay, the posted gap can shrink while total compensation gaps persist. And the efficiency loss is not a one-time rewrite. It recurs with every requisition, every exceptional candidate, and every negotiation anchored to a public ceiling instead of the value of the actual match.
