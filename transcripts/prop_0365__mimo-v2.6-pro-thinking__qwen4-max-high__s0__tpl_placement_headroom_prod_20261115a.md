[P1.1] A family of four deciding whether to see a blockbuster this weekend faces a simple arithmetic problem: when the standard auditorium runs the film twice a day at inconvenient hours and the premium hall runs it six times at prime times, the real ticket price of a Friday night has become the premium price. That is not consumer choice. That is scheduling as upsell.
[P1.2] The proposition is not anti-premium. It is anti-dominance. Premium screens have every right to fill every seat they can sell. What they should not do is consume so much of a film's finite opening-weekend screen time that ordinary auditoriums get thin, off-peak schedules.
[P1.3] Blockbusters are the only films most moviegoers see in a year; the opening window is when the audience is largest. Cutting standard supply then is the worst moment to restrict access.
[P1.4] The mechanisms matter. A multiplex might give its premium halls the 5, 7:30, and 10 shows and relegate standard screens to mid-afternoon. Shift workers, families with early bedtimes, and price-sensitive viewers are pushed out not by taste but by timetable. Some will pay the premium; many will skip the film or wait for streaming.
[P1.5] The theater captures margin on the few who pay more, while the studio loses the walk-in audience that sustains a film's long tail.
[P1.6] The obvious reply is that premium seats cost more to build and serve a willing segment. Granted. But willingness to pay is only measured if the schedule lets the alternative exist. When the cheaper product is deliberately made inconvenient to find, willingness is manufactured, not tested.
[P1.7] Demand should decide pricing; scheduling should not pre-decide who gets in the door. Give premium formats their fair share, not the majority of prime-time shows, and let the standard-price audience have a shot at the same weekend.
