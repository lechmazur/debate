[C1.1] Dignity is not a blank check for authority; it is the ground that makes autonomy worth respecting at all. The proposition fails because it treats dignity as a rival to choice, when in fact dignity is why choice matters.
[C1.2] First, autonomy is a derivative value. We honor medical choice because a competent patient is a bearer of intrinsic worth, capable of self-authorship. Strip out dignity and autonomy collapses into mere preference satisfaction. The patient who requests unnecessary amputation of a healthy limb, or a teenager who seeks dangerous cosmetic procedures, exercises autonomy.
[C1.3] But medicine rightly refuses because dignity marks a line that even the patient cannot cross against themselves. The limit is not paternalism for its own sake; it is the recognition that some goods cannot be traded away.
[C1.4] Second, dignity constrains distorted choice. Vulnerable patients often "choose" death or refusal not from authentic self-authorship but from shame, poverty, or the belief that they are a burden. Dignity does not override autonomy here; it protects autonomy from being extorted by bad conditions. When a disabled patient requests assisted dying because society offers them no support, celebrating their autonomy while ignoring the dignity violation that produced the request is not liberation.
[C1.5] It is abandonment dressed up as respect.
[C1.6] Third, history cuts both ways. The medical establishment has indeed imposed treatments "for patients' own good." Dignity demands that we neither bind the patient nor desert them.
[C1.7] Autonomy alone permits desertion.
[C1.8] PRO says dignity can point in opposite directions. True, but its core meaning is stable: human beings are ends, not means. [C1.9] That foundation carries more weight.
