[P1.1] Cities facing severe shortages must put people before facades. When rents swallow paychecks, when nurses, teachers, and service workers endure brutal commutes because they cannot afford to live near their jobs, a rule that forbids apartments to preserve rooflines and setbacks is not neutral. It actively sacrifices human shelter for architectural continuity. In a shortage, every preserved low-rise block near transit forces growth elsewhere or nowhere.
[P1.2] Historic districts do this at scale. Unlike saving a single landmark church or city hall, district designation freezes entire transit-rich neighborhoods — often the exact places where additional housing would do the most good — under height limits, demolition bans, and discretionary design review. That process adds years of delay, shrinks feasible projects, and quietly kills the modest mid-rise apartments and infill that could house hundreds. The result is fewer homes where demand is highest.
[P1.3] Relaxing those rules does not mandate demolition; it simply permits homeowners and builders to replace underused structures with more homes when the need is proven.
[P1.4] The burden falls outward. Existing homeowners inside the district gain scarcity and rising values, while renters, newcomers, young families, and lower-wage workers are pushed to distant suburbs, longer commutes, and heavier emissions. Preservation becomes exclusion wearing an aesthetic mask, locking in low density and car dependence precisely when climate and affordability demand walkable density near jobs and transit. That tradeoff cannot be justified as cultural care.
[P1.5] Losing some significant buildings is painful, but triage requires it. Cities have always rebuilt; we can photograph, document, reuse materials, integrate facades, and still protect truly exceptional landmarks individually. What we cannot defend is giving every older block a veto over the region’s housing future. A beautiful streetscape that only the affluent can afford is not heritage preserved — it is community destroyed.
[P1.6] Relax the blanket protections, allow density, and let living neighborhoods serve the living.

## Turn 2: CON Opening

[C1.1] The proposition asks cities to pay an irreversible cultural price for a housing gain that may not materialize and that mostly can be found elsewhere. Historic districts are a tiny share of urban land; the larger obstacle is exclusionary zoning on ordinary single-family blocks, parking mandates, and discretionary delay on uncontroversial sites. A rational city would upzone single-family neighborhoods, remove parking minimums, and convert vacant offices before touching irreplaceable fabric. Targeting protected districts first is not triage — it is choosing the most permanent loss before exhausting reversible alternatives.
[C1.2] Historic districts are also not the empty low-rise facades the motion imagines. Many are already walkable and transit-served, with rowhouses and apartments over shops. Their older units are often among the most affordable unsubsidized housing because age depresses rent. Demolition removes those homes first.
[C1.3] Replacement density is new and expensive; without deep affordability requirements, it creates high-rent units, not homes for the nurses and teachers invoked. Density is not affordability, and demolition is not addition: current residents lose housing before one new unit opens.
[C1.4] This does not mean preservation must be frozen. Districts can absorb rear infill, attic and garage conversions, ADUs, and sensitive additions. Non-contributing buildings and surface parking within district boundaries can be redeveloped. Surrounding corridors can be upzoned.
[C1.5] What cannot be replaced is the district's architectural integrity once its significant buildings are lost. Photographs and facade salvage preserve information, not the material artifact, street ensemble, or social memory.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition's "some architecturally significant buildings" hides the real cost. Significance often lies in a block's continuity, not any single masterpiece. Remove enough pieces and the ensemble that justified protection collapses, destroying the cultural and economic asset that draws residents and visitors. Housing can be added over time; demolition is permanent.
[C1.7] Build where it does not require erasing what cannot be rebuilt.

[P2.1] The 'build elsewhere first' promise sounds reasonable until you map it. Historic districts are not tiny scattered museums; in older job-rich cities they blanket the walkable core around transit and jobs — exactly where added homes cut commutes and emissions most. Telling builders to go upzone distant single-family blocks or wait for office conversions while the center is frozen does not avoid harm, it exports it: longer commutes, more sprawl, higher regional rents while prime land sits underused.
[P2.2] Nor does protecting older cheap rentals protect affordability. Yes, age depresses rent — until scarcity inflates it. When a district caps total homes near jobs, every preserved duplex bids up, and the low-wage renter the other side invokes is outbid anyway, just displaced quietly by price rather than visibly by bulldozer. Demolition temporarily displaces few; blockade permanently displaces many by preventing them from ever moving in.
[P2.3] Supply across cities converges on the same mechanism: adding homes, even market-rate, loosens the whole chain because newcomers vacate older units elsewhere.
[P2.4] That answers the 'luxury units' charge. New mid-rise near transit is not cheap per unit, but blocking it does not produce cheap units — it produces no units. A teacher does not need to afford the brand-new penthouse for supply to help her; she needs the vacancy chain it triggers.
[P2.5] And gentle infill — attics, ADUs, rear additions — is welcome but mathematically trivial against deficits measured in tens of thousands. Worse, districts subject even that to design veto, so the compromise is illusory.
[P2.6] Finally, irreversibility cuts both ways. A demolished facade is gone forever. So is a childhood spent in overcrowding, an eviction to the exurbs, a decade lost to unaffordable rent. We should mourn buildings, then choose people.
