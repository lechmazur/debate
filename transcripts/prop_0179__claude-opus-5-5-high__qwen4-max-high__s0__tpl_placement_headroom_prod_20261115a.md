[P1.1] Octopus farming does not exist at commercial scale yet. That makes this the rare animal-welfare question we can answer before an industry is built, before jobs, lobbies and sunk capital make cruelty politically untouchable. The question is simple: is there any way to confine octopuses industrially without inflicting serious, avoidable suffering? There is not.
[P1.2] Start with the animal. Octopuses solve novel problems, open jars, escape tanks, and in the wild carry coconut shells to use later as shelter, which is planning, not reflex. They distinguish individual humans, explore objects in ways that look like play, and respond to injury by guarding wounds and avoiding places where they were hurt. That pattern is why scientific reviews have increasingly treated cephalopods as sentient, and why the UK extended its animal-welfare law to cover them.
[P1.3] We need not claim certainty about their inner lives. Strong evidence of a mind capable of suffering is enough to demand we not engineer its misery.
[P1.4] Now the farm. Octopuses are solitary and often cannibalistic, yet farming economics require density. They are restless explorers, yet a production tank is barren by design, because enrichment costs money and complicates harvest. Their soft bodies are easily injured, and there is no established humane slaughter method; ice slurry, a common proposal, likely means a slow, conscious death.
[P1.5] Every feature that makes octopuses remarkable makes industrial confinement uniquely punishing. This is not a fixable implementation flaw; it is the business model colliding with the biology.
[P1.6] Finally, weigh what we gain. Octopus is a luxury food, not a food-security staple. Farming them typically requires feeding them other marine animals, so it does not even relieve pressure on wild fish.
[P1.7] On one side: plausible mass suffering of intelligent animals. On the other: cheaper calamari-adjacent dining. A global ban costs almost nothing and prevents a cruelty we can already foresee.
