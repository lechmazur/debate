[P1.1] A country that has just stopped killing itself cannot afford two official pasts. Public memorials are civic architecture, not private grief rooms. When they freeze rival ethnic stories of who suffered first, who was purely innocent, and who remains the enemy, they teach the next generation that the political community is only a ceasefire.
[P1.2] A shared civic account is not a bland peace poster and not a winner’s alibi. It names violence as a collapse of citizenship: civilians were killed, institutions failed or were captured, crimes were crimes regardless of the perpetrator’s ethnicity, and no group holds a hereditary veto over the country. Particular losses can still be named inside that frame. What it refuses is the museum of parallel nations: separate martyr calendars, separate lists of innocents, separate claims that the same massacre was either genocide or necessary defense.
[P1.3] Fully competing ethnic narratives travel because they are simple. They convert mixed villages, mixed casualty lists, and wartime alliances into a morality play. Then memorials become recruitment. Young people inherit unfinished war, not complexity.
[P1.4] Parallel commemorations in Bosnia still stage incompatible truths about the same years. Rival murals and parades in Northern Ireland keep the conflict’s grammar alive long after the guns quieted. People may remember privately. The state should not subsidize two irreconcilable founding myths.
[P1.5] After civil war, peace depends on co-citizens who can share streets, courts, and a flag without treating history as a zero-sum census of suffering. Separate public narratives make every textbook, anniversary, and monument budget a proxy fight over who won. A civic account is the minimum common ground that turns former enemies into a public rather than waiting audiences for the next mobilizer. Private memory can remain plural.
[P1.6] Official space should not.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes unity for peace. Public memorial projects after civil war are not classrooms for a tidy civics lesson; they are the state’s first moral contract with survivors. To emphasize a shared civic account rather than preserve fully separate ethnic narratives is to ask wounded communities to ratify an official story written by successor elites—or by institutions that were themselves perpetrators. That produces coerced consensus, not legitimacy.
[C1.2] A shared civic account sounds neutral—all suffered, crimes were crimes—but neutrality here erases asymmetry. In Bosnia, a generic “we all suffered” frame blunts the specific truth that Bosniaks were targeted for destruction at Srebrenica. A memorial emphasizing common citizenship over separate truths tells survivors their dead are dissolved into an abstraction, handing nationalists the winning cry: the state is erasing you. Northern Ireland’s murals and parades are not mere recruitment; they are how segregated neighborhoods make losses visible.
[C1.3] Sanitize them into one civic story and memory goes underground, nursing the grievance that the powerful stole the past. Suppressing separate memory does not create co-citizens; it creates hypocrites and resisters.
[C1.4] Preserving fully separate public narratives does not subsidize war; it refuses to make recognition scarce. When each community sees its martyrs and massacres named in official space, the zero-sum fight over victimhood loses fuel. Recognition in public space is not a zero-sum census; it is the condition for trusting institutions at all. People can share courts and streets while disagreeing about history; they cannot accept a peace that requires their suffering to be translated into someone else’s civic story before it counts.
[C1.5] The alternative to plural memory is not neutral ground—it is usually the majority’s account, or a donor-friendly template, in civic disguise. Keep the messy, agonistic memorials. That is the honest beginning of peace.
