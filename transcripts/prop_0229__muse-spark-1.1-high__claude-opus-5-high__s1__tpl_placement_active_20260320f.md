[P1.1] Cheap gasoline looks like generosity. It is not. In Gulf and North African states, universal fuel subsidies are a machine for turning public wealth into private waste.
[P1.2] They are staggeringly regressive. A subsidy per liter gives most to those who drive most — wealthy households, trucking fleets, energy-intensive factories. The poorest quintile, who often own no car, gets crumbs while paying indirectly through underfunded services. This is Robin Hood in reverse, justified as pro-poor.
[P1.3] It is also fiscal suicide. These programs routinely swallow a significant share of GDP and government budgets, exploding when oil prices fall and governments can least afford them. That money crowds out everything that actually builds futures: health, education, industrial upgrading, water infrastructure. Gulf states trying to diversify cannot simultaneously bribe their own citizens to waste energy.
[P1.4] The distortions compound. Near-zero pump prices mean some of the world's highest per-capita consumption and emissions, monstrous traffic, and industries whose only comparative advantage is cheap fuel. Across North Africa it means smuggling — tanker trucks crossing from Libya to Tunisia to chase price gaps — corruption and shortages.
[P1.5] Phase out and replace with targeted cash transfers solves both fairness and efficiency. Instead of subsidizing fuel, you support people. A monthly transfer to lower-income households preserves purchasing power, lets families choose whether to spend on transport, food or schooling, and costs a fraction of universal subsidies. It is transparent, portable, and does not reward waste.
[P1.6] Five years is precisely the right horizon to do it credibly: gradual, pre-announced price steps, with cash in place before the next hike, exactly as the UAE, Morocco and Jordan have shown is possible. Keep the help, end the waste.

[C1.1] Cheap fuel is not generosity — agreed. But the motion is not "subsidies are bad." It is a package: full phase-out of universality, on a five-year clock, replaced by targeted cash. Each of those three commitments is where this fails.
[C1.2] Start with targeting. Targeting requires knowing who is poor. In economies where half of employment is informal, with no reliable income records, means-testing produces large exclusion errors — the day-labourer, the widow without documents, the near-poor just above the line who now pays market prices for diesel and gets nothing. Universality's ugly inefficiency is also its virtue: nobody is screened out, no local official decides who qualifies.
[C1.3] Trading a leaky-but-universal benefit for a clean-on-paper list is not obviously pro-poor; it is a bet on state capacity that these particular states have not demonstrated.
[C1.4] Second, fuel is an input, not just a purchase. Remove the subsidy and you move minibus fares, irrigation pumping, fishing boats, bakery costs, water trucking. A household transfer compensates a family's petrol bill; it does not compensate economy-wide cost-push inflation. Egypt's post-2016 experience is instructive: subsidies were cut and a targeted transfer programme built — genuinely well-designed — yet devaluation and inflation outran the transfers and poverty measures worsened.
[C1.5] Cash is nominal and gets eroded; the price liberalisation is permanent. That asymmetry is the mechanism by which "compensated reform" quietly becomes uncompensated austerity.
[C1.6] Third, the clock. Five years is a calendar, not a capability. Fuel price rises are among the most reliable triggers of unrest in this exact region — Jordan, Sudan, Nigeria, Egypt's bread-and-fuel politics. A government forced into partial retreat mid-sequence lands in the worst world: subsidy gone, transfer suspended, legitimacy spent.
[C1.7] In the Gulf the subsidy is part of the rent bargain that substitutes for taxation and representation; dismantling the distribution while leaving the no-representation half in place is not fiscal hygiene, it is renegotiating a political settlement by pump price.
[C1.8] Better tools exist without this gamble: lifeline volumetric blocks, subsidised public transport, taxing vehicles and industrial users. Reform the subsidy. Don't detonate [clipped]
