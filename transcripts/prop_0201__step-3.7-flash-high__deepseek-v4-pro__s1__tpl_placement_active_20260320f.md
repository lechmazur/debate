[P1.1] Allowing courts to redirect frozen sovereign assets of aggressor states to reconstruction of the countries they harmed is a necessary, legally sound enforcement of the foundational international law principle that aggressors must pay for the damage they inflict.
[P1.2] The UN Charter prohibits wars of aggression, and customary international law has long held that states that commit this unlawful act are liable for full reparations to their victims. Freezing aggressor state assets is already a widely accepted, proportionate tool to pressure invaders and prevent those assets from funding further violence. Redirecting those assets to reconstruction is the natural next step: it transforms a temporary coercive measure into permanent redress for victims, who are currently left bearing the full cost of rebuilding while aggressors retain access to the wealth they amassed to wage war.
[P1.3] Consider the ongoing full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine: over $300 billion in Russian central bank assets are frozen in Western jurisdictions, accruing billions in interest annually, while Ukraine faces an estimated $486 billion in documented war damage. Using these assets to rebuild Ukrainian homes, hospitals, and energy infrastructure does not violate property rights or sovereign immunity, because those legal protections exist to shield lawful sovereign conduct, not to reward illegal aggression. When a state chooses to launch an unlawful war, it forfeits the right to claim immunity from the consequences of that choice, including being forced to compensate its victims.
[P1.4] This rule also strengthens global deterrence: future would-be aggressors will face tangible financial penalties for their actions, rather than being able to launch wars with no risk of material consequence. The alternative—leaving frozen assets idle while victims suffer—rewards aggression and violates the fundamental promise of justice that no harm should go uncompensated.
