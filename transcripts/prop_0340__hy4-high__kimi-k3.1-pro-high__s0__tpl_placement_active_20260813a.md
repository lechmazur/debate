[P1.1] A recommendation feed is not a neutral pipe; it is a policy about what attention is for. Rankers choose among recency, close friends, learning, novelty, viewpoint diversity, local relevance, discovery, calm, entertainment, and probable engagement. A platform optimizing one feed for everyone does not avoid value choices; it hides them in one proxy, usually the behavior easiest to measure and monetize: clicks, watch time, return visits. The clash is simple: who sets the target, the user or the platform?
[P1.2] Large platforms should give users far more control over that recommendation goal.
[P1.3] Clicks are not goals. The same video can be watched because it teaches a skill, because it provokes anger, because a friend sent it, or because the interface made refusal annoying. A ranker using those traces mistakes compulsive moments for enduring purposes. A student revising for exams, a new parent seeking local advice, someone avoiding an ex, and a small business owner finding customers do not need "relevance" in one universal sense.
[P1.4] They need different objectives. One universal objective flattens that difference.
[P1.5] Goal control is therefore better personalization, not a clumsy substitute for it. It need not mean item-by-item micromanagement. Platforms can offer presets and dials: close friends, chronological, learn a topic, broaden sources, favor local, support small creators, reduce outrage, cap rabbit holes, reset the model, explain why an item appeared. A default feed may remain, but it becomes visible, reversible, and contestable rather than sovereign.
[P1.6] Defenses of a single optimized feed invoke simplicity and safety. But simplicity is not neutral when the simple thing is a private objective imposed on millions. A universal objective creates correlated failure: if one metric rewards outrage or compulsive scrolling, that bias propagates at once. User-chosen goals distribute risk, expose tradeoffs, and make platforms accountable to purposes people endorse.
[P1.7] The issue is not whether a company can predict the next click; it is whether it should own the purpose of the feed.

[C1.2] This creates a dilemma. If goal controls are simple presets, they are too vague to capture the purposes PRO describes: “learn a topic” still requires decisions about difficulty, accuracy, novelty, and what counts as progress. Watch time may simply return wearing a user-approved badge. If controls are detailed enough to specify those tradeoffs, most users must diagnose their own needs and manage a complex system.
[C1.3] Preferences shift between exam week, illness, work, and relaxation. The sophisticated few gain an advantage; everyone else remains on a default, now blamed for choosing badly.
[C1.4] Nor does fragmentation solve hidden value judgments. “Broaden sources,” “reduce outrage,” and “support small creators” each require contested definitions and enforcement. More configurations make outcomes harder to reproduce, complaints harder to interpret, and manipulation easier to disguise. PRO warns that one objective produces correlated failure.
[C1.5] But one legible standard also permits centralized correction; fragmented objectives can spread failures that no auditor sees as a whole.
[C1.6] Most importantly, recommendation goals are not purely private. A user may prefer inflammatory material, dubious health claims, or relentless content about a vulnerable group, then redistribute it to others. Attention is individual; harms from deception, harassment, and polarization are shared. Platforms therefore need non-delegable duties for safety, integrity, and quality. “You chose that goal” is not an adequate answer to the person harmed downstream.
[C1.7] Ordinary controls can address specific frustrations without accepting this motion’s much larger transfer of objective-setting. The question is not platform power versus user power. It is whether platforms retain clear responsibility for a common baseline or hide behind thousands of nominally chosen purposes. Keep the responsibility where the design power actually sits.
