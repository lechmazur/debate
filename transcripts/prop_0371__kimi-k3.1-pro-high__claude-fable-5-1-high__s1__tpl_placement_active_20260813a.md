[P1.1] A plane ticket should cover the ordinary purpose of the purchase: getting a traveler and a reasonable amount of personal belongings to the destination. Requiring a standard carry-on in that price is not a demand for unlimited luggage or an end to ancillary fees. Checked bags, extra weight, oversized items, and premium services can still cost more. The line is narrower and fairer: airlines may sell optional extras, but not disassemble an ordinary trip into a deceptively cheap headline fare.
[P1.2] The central problem is fragmented pricing. A traveler comparing two flights cannot meaningfully compare fares when one includes a cabin bag and the other reveals a substantial charge only after several screens, or imposes it through a restrictive fare class. That does not merely inconvenience shoppers; it weakens competition. Airlines compete most visibly on the advertised fare while recovering revenue through charges that are harder to compare.
[P1.3] A baseline makes the advertised product more closely resemble the product people actually buy.
[P1.4] The familiar defense is “unbundling”: passengers who travel lightly should not subsidize those with bags. But a carry-on is often no more optional than the clothing and medication needed for a short trip. Calling it a choice does not make the trip workable without it. And the relevant comparison is not an artificially stripped fare against a complete fare; it is the total price each passenger pays.
[P1.5] If airlines genuinely offer better value, they can still compete on that total price after including the baseline.
[P1.6] A modest rule also reduces friction at exactly the worst point: the airport. Ambiguous allowances and fare-specific restrictions invite disputes at check-in and at the gate, when passengers have virtually no bargaining power. A clear included allowance, with published size and weight limits, replaces surprise enforcement with an understandable term of purchase.
