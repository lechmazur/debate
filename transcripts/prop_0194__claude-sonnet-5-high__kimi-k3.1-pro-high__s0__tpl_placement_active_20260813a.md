[P1.1] Democracies are brilliant at representing the people in the room. They are structurally incapable of representing the people who are not yet born. A future-generations commissioner with binding veto power over a narrow class of major, long-horizon decisions fixes that blind spot without dismantling democracy itself.
[P1.2] Here is the core problem: elected officials face election cycles measured in years, but decisions like nuclear waste siting, sovereign debt that mortgages future budgets, or policies that lock in irreversible biodiversity and climate damage play out over decades or centuries. The people who bear the cost cannot vote, cannot lobby, cannot run ads, cannot threaten to unseat anyone. Every other interest group in a democracy has some channel of influence; future generations have none. That is not a minor gap in representation, it is a complete structural exclusion of the affected party, and normal democratic bargaining has no mechanism to correct it because the beneficiaries of short-termism are present and organized while the victims are future and silent.
[P1.3] Democracies already accept binding, counter-majoritarian constraints when majorities cannot be trusted to protect a vital interest. Independent central banks hold veto-like power over monetary policy because legislatures have a built-in incentive to inflate away debt before elections. Constitutional courts can strike down popular legislation to protect minority rights that transient majorities would trample. These are not failures of democratic legitimacy; they are precisely what makes democracy sustainable, by preventing today's majority from permanently foreclosing tomorrow's choices.
[P1.4] A future-generations veto is the same logic applied to time instead of minority status. It would not run the government. It would not set ordinary policy. It would intervene only where decisions are irreversible, long-horizon, and structurally biased against absent stakeholders, the exact cases where ordinary accountability mechanisms cannot function because the people harmed do not yet exist to hold anyone accountable.

[C1.1] Future generations deserve serious consideration. It does not follow that an appointed commissioner should be able to overrule democratically accountable government. PRO moves too quickly from a real representation problem to an undemocratic remedy.
[C1.2] First, a commissioner cannot “represent” future people in any meaningful electoral sense. Future generations cannot instruct, remove, or even identify themselves to that official. Nor do they have one obvious interest: descendants may benefit from inherited infrastructure, energy security, scientific investment, or debt-financed disaster protection, as well as suffer from environmental damage. A veto therefore does not transmit their voice; it empowers one institution to decide what their voice ought to be.
[C1.3] That is guardianship, not representation.
[C1.4] PRO’s comparisons expose the problem. Courts enforce publicly enacted constitutional rights through legal reasoning; central banks operate within delegated, revisable mandates and comparatively defined objectives. A commissioner vetoing major policy because of predicted effects decades later is making contestable judgments about technology, risk, distribution, and priorities. The more consequential the decision, the less acceptable it is to remove that judgment from institutions answerable to voters.
[C1.5] Second, a veto is not a harmless pause. It can lock in the very dangers it claims to prevent. Blocking a nuclear-waste repository does not make existing waste disappear; it may prolong less secure temporary storage. Blocking debt or construction can leave future citizens with deteriorating infrastructure and fewer resources.
[C1.6] Long-horizon policy involves choosing among risks, not choosing between risk and safety. PRO treats governmental inaction as neutral when it can also produce irreversible harm.
[C1.7] Finally, “some major decisions” does not solve the legitimacy problem. Those are precisely where trade-offs are largest and democratic accountability matters most. Democracies can require future-impact assessments, independent commissioners with public reporting powers, enforceable environmental duties, legislative supermajorities, and judicial review under established law. These measures force future interests into decisions without giving one office an unaccountable final word.
[C1.8] The democratic answer to short-termism is better duties, information, and accountability—not government by a veto-holder whom neither present nor future citizens can remove.

[P2.5] The veto is asymmetric by design: it stops permanent foreclosure, it doesn't mandate any particular alternative path.

[C2.3] PRO’s retreat to “irreversibility” does not make the judgment thin. A railway may consume land while creating decades of mobility; rejecting it preserves land but may entrench dirtier transport. The veto is institutionally asymmetric, not substantively neutral: it systematically privileges the commissioner’s preferred baseline and the harms of action over the harms of delay.
[C2.4] Calling interim storage “reversible” also assumes away accidents, mounting costs, and continued political deadlock. Delay can itself narrow choices. And because the commissioner need only veto, never deliver a safer alternative, responsibility for the resulting default disappears.
[C2.5] Finally, [P2.6] treats every milder mechanism as failed status quo, although enforceable duties and judicial review are not mere reports. More importantly, legislative disregard for advice does not prove a veto is correct; it proves only that it cannot be overruled. A mechanism immune to the majority is also immune when its forecast is wrong. On irreversible decisions especially, that is not a safeguard.
[C2.6] It is an unanswerable gamble.

[P4.1] A1: The threshold is set the same way every counter-majoritarian mandate is set: by statute, enacted by the very legislature CON wants to protect. Parliament defines "irreversible, long-horizon, major" the way it defines a central bank's inflation target or a court's enumerated rights — narrowly, in advance, through ordinary democratic process. The commissioner then applies that standard through a published, reasoned finding, and citizens can challenge the *process* — evidence, procedure, jurisdiction — through judicial review, exactly as they challenge central bank or regulatory findings now.
[P4.2] What they cannot challenge is the substantive outcome once the irreversibility threshold is genuinely met, because that is the entire point of a precommitment device: it has to bind even when the current majority wants out.
[P4.3] A2: The correction mechanism is amendment of the mandate itself, by the same supermajority process that creates it — not case-by-case override, because case-by-case override is what let short-termism win every time under CON's "soft" alternatives. But the deeper answer is the asymmetry CON still hasn't touched: a wrongful veto delays a repository, a bridge, a bond issuance — all retryable next session with better evidence. A wrongful approval of an irreversible policy cannot be retried; the waste is sited, the species is gone, the fiscal space is spent.
[P4.4] CON keeps calling this "an unanswerable gamble" as if both errors are equally unanswerable. They aren't. One side's mistake is a setback. The other side's mistake is final.
[P4.5] That asymmetry is the whole debate. CON's alternatives — review, duties, supermajorities — all police *process*, none can stop a procedurally clean, popular, irreversible decision once it has the votes. Only a binding veto, scoped to exactly that category, closes the one gap democracies cannot otherwise close: a majority empowered to make permanent choices for people it will never have to answer to.
