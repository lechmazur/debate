[P1.1] A warehouse worker learns her productivity target has changed because a model rewrote it overnight. A nurse discovers an algorithm now decides staffing ratios on her ward. A claims adjuster finds her call transcripts scored by a system nobody can explain to her. In each case, the terms and conditions of a person's working life were rewritten — and the people doing the work had no standing to ask a single question.
[P1.2] That is the gap this proposal closes. Wages, hours, safety and discipline are already subjects employers cannot unilaterally reset in most advanced economies. Workplace AI now sets all four indirectly: it schedules, it paces, it surveils, it ranks, and increasingly it recommends who gets fired. To say AI deployment is management's sole prerogative is to say the substance of employment law can be hollowed out simply by routing decisions through software.
[P1.3] Two reasons a negotiation duty specifically, rather than vague consultation or after-the-fact litigation. First, information. Workers hold knowledge no vendor demo contains — that the "idle time" the model flags is actually a machine warming up, that the safety shortcut the system rewards is the one that breaks fingers. Firms deploy AI badly not because they are villains but because they are buying from vendors whose incentive is to promise savings, and there is no institutional voice in the room that has to live with the result.
[P1.4] Second, timing. Once a system is procured and integrated, remedies come far too late for the people already managed out by it.
[P1.5] Note what the motion does not require. Not a veto. Not co-ownership of the model. A duty to bargain in good faith over deployment: how it is used, what it monitors, what happens to those affected.
[P1.6] Germany and much of northern Europe have run works councils through automation, offshoring and digitisation without collapsing into industrial paralysis — and their manufacturing sectors are not exactly known for technological timidity.
[P1.7] The choice is not AI versus no AI. It is AI deployed with the workforce's knowledge, or deployed at them.

[C1.1] The harms described are real, but the proposed cure misfires. A legal duty to negotiate with an elected worker council turns an AI-governance problem into a labor-procedure contest. A dangerous scheduling model, biased call-scoring system, or unsafe staffing algorithm is dangerous because it is opaque, poorly tested, or unaccountable; seating a council at a negotiation table does not fix the model, retrain the data, or set safety thresholds.
[C1.2] [P1.3]’s information point actually supports consultation, not negotiation. Employers can and should run pilots, red-team deployments, and hear frontline workers about machine warm-ups or hidden shortcuts. But once input becomes a legally enforceable bargaining obligation, it becomes positional. Because good faith is vague, every deployment invites delay, lawyers, and complaints.
[C1.3] [P1.5] disclaims a veto, but a predeployment bargaining duty gives a council de facto hold-up power: slow-walk talks, add unrelated demands, and threaten legal challenge until management pays a settlement in time and money.
[C1.4] The Germany comparison does not transplant. Works councils work within a dense co-determination system of labor courts, sectoral bargaining, and established roles; copying one duty onto employers in systems without that infrastructure creates a parallel or competing body. Where unions already hold exclusive bargaining rights, a separate elected council would confuse who speaks for workers.
[C1.5] Most troubling, negotiation can legitimate bad systems. Once a council signs off, an employer will claim the workforce bargained over the deployment. Individual workers harmed later may face the answer that their representatives accepted the tool. That is worse than transparency and accountability rules: require impact assessments, audit and anti-discrimination enforcement, and give regulators power to stop unsafe systems.
[C1.6] Those tools target the harm; this motion buys process, delay, and false consent.
