Firms that can track a container to the hour and a counterfeit to the factory somehow cannot say who picked their cotton or mined their cobalt. The problem is not capacity.
[P1.2] It is that nobody is required to look, and nobody is required to show their work.
[P1.3] Mandatory, standardized, audited due-diligence reporting fixes three failures at once.
[P1.4] First, it ends the race to vagueness. Where reporting is voluntary and unaudited, as under the UK and Australian modern-slavery regimes, companies file glossy statements that promise "zero tolerance" while disclosing nothing checkable. Standardization makes reports comparable across competitors; auditing makes them costly to fake. We already accept exactly this discipline for financial statements, and no one calls audited accounts a burden on commerce.
[P1.5] Second, it stops punishing the responsible. A firm that genuinely maps its tiers and remediates abuses currently subsidizes the rival that outsources blindly and undercuts it. [P1.6] Third, it gives investors, workers, journalists, and regulators something to act on. Disclosure is not the remedy; it is the precondition for every remedy, from shareholder pressure to import bans to litigation.
[P1.7] The motion is scoped carefully: large multinationals, who have the leverage and the balance sheets. The question is simply whether their knowledge of the people making their products should be real and public, or performative and private. Hidden abuse persists because it is cheap to hide.
