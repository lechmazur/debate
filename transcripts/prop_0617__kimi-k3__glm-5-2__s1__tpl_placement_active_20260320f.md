[P1.1] A presumption is not a prohibition. The motion asks only that when a dominant platform wants to acquire another firm, the burden of proof sits where the evidence sits: with the acquirer. Three reasons.
[P1.2] First, errors in digital markets are irreversible. Network effects and data feedback loops make markets tip, and no remedy untips them. When Facebook bought Instagram, regulators waved through a small photo app; within years it anchored Facebook's social dominance, and no court could realistically unwind it. Antitrust litigation moves in years; platform markets move in months.
[P1.3] Serial acquisitions compound this—each deal looks harmless, together they foreclose competition. A rule that waits for proven harm arrives only after the harm is permanent.
[P1.4] Second, the information asymmetry is total. Whether a deal is a genuine complement or a killer acquisition—buying a nascent rival to neutralize it—turns on internal plans the enforcer never sees. The platform alone knows whether the deal yields real consumer value, so it alone should carry the proof. If integration, lower prices, or new capabilities are real, demonstrating them is easy.
[P1.5] If the firm cannot demonstrate them, that silence is the evidence.
[P1.6] Third, the status quo already ran the experiment and failed. Under today's permissive standard, the major platforms made hundreds of acquisitions over two decades with almost none blocked and most never reviewed—while challenges to their cores were bought, not beaten. This is not a hypothetical risk; it is the observed record.
[P1.7] Now weigh the errors. A false negative—a killer acquisition waved through—costs a market its competitive future, permanently. A false positive costs one exit path; the startup can still sell to any non-dominant buyer or go public. Rational enforcement presumes against the error that cannot be undone.
[P1.8] The presumption is rebuttable, narrow—only dominant platforms—and proportionate. It asks firms to prove what their own pitch decks already promise: substantial consumer benefit. If the benefit is real, the deal proceeds. If it isn't, it shouldn't.

[C1.2] Start with what a presumption actually does in practice. "Prove substantial consumer benefit" before a deal closes is not a light evidentiary nudge. Consumer benefit from an acquisition is often speculative, emergent, and impossible to quantify at the moment of filing. Integrating a mapping service into a ride-hailing platform, or folding a speech-recognition engine into a device ecosystem—these produce value through iterative integration that no pitch deck can fully prove ex ante.
[C1.3] A standard demanding demonstrated benefit before the fact will block precisely the complementary, pro-consumer deals that the proponents say they welcome. The rebuttable presumption becomes a functional veto.
