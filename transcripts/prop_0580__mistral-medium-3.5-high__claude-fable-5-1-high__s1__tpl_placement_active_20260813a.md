[P1.1] Expanding child tax credits with monthly payments is the most cost-effective way to reduce child poverty because it delivers aid directly, flexibly, and immediately to those who need it. Unlike childcare subsidies, which require expensive infrastructure, staffing, and complex administration, tax credits use existing systems to put cash straight into parents’ hands. This means more of every dollar reaches families, and it reaches them faster. The 2021 CTC expansion proved this: monthly payments substantially reduced child poverty in a matter of months, with near-universal uptake among eligible households.
[P1.2] Childcare subsidies cannot match this speed or scale. They are constrained by the availability of daycare slots, work requirements that exclude the very poorest families, and administrative overhead that eats into the budget. Even when subsidies work, they only address one need. Cash empowers parents to prioritize their most pressing expenses—rent, food, utilities, or transportation to a job.
[P1.3] Moreover, credits avoid the deadweight loss of subsidizing services some families would purchase anyway, while also reaching non-working parents who may be most in need. Administrative costs for credits are minimal, leveraging existing tax mechanisms; for subsidies, they are substantial, requiring new systems for eligibility verification and provider reimbursement. For equivalent spending, monthly tax credits lift more children out of poverty because they are simpler, more inclusive, and more responsive to diverse family circumstances. Direct cash works at scale, and it works cost-effectively.

[C1.1] The motion asks a narrow question: which dollar buys more poverty reduction? PRO's opening never actually compares the two per dollar. It describes how easy cash is to send, then assumes ease equals efficiency. Those are different things.
[C1.2] Start with where the money goes. A monthly child tax credit is close to universal by design. In 2021 the expanded credit reached families well up the income ladder; the poor were a minority of recipients. So for every dollar that reached a child in poverty, several dollars went to households that were never poor.
[C1.3] That is the very "deadweight loss" PRO warns about, just wearing a different hat. Childcare subsidies, by contrast, are targeted by construction at low- and moderate-income working families. Nearly the whole budget lands where poverty actually is.
[C1.4] Second, and more decisive: cash fills a gap dollar for dollar. Childcare works as a lever. A single mother with two young children faces care costs that can rival her potential wage; she cannot take the job, or takes fewer hours. A subsidy covering that care unlocks her earnings—often far more than the subsidy costs.
[C1.5] Quebec's low-cost childcare pushed maternal employment up sharply enough that added tax revenue offset much of the program. A monthly credit raises her income by exactly the credit. A childcare slot raises it by a paycheck.
[C1.6] Third, durability. The 2021 experience PRO celebrates is also its indictment: poverty fell while checks flowed and snapped straight back the month they stopped. Measured poverty fell because the transfer is counted as income—arithmetic, not transformation. Childcare builds earnings histories, job tenure, and, for the child, early development that pays off across a lifetime.
[C1.7] That is poverty reduction that compounds instead of expiring.
[C1.8] On administration: the childcare subsidy system already exists; expanding it is scaling, not building. And "near-universal uptake" is generous—the poorest non-filers were precisely the ones the IRS struggled to reach.
[C1.9] Cash treats the symptom monthly. Childcare changes the household's trajectory. Per dollar, the lever beats the bucket.
