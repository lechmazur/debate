[P1.1] A streaming original is not ordinary inventory that a shop can stop stocking while customers buy it elsewhere. The platform commonly controls the exclusive, sometimes only, public edition. When it withdraws that edition early—especially where no download, disc, or alternative licensed stream exists—it can make a completed work effectively inaccessible. A minimum availability rule addresses that distinctive gatekeeping power without demanding permanent carriage or free access.
[P1.2] The first principle is legitimate reliance. Subscribers are sold continuing access to a catalogue, creators accept distribution on the understanding that audiences can find their work, and the public is encouraged to treat streaming libraries as cultural infrastructure. A company should be able to change its catalogue for ordinary editorial or commercial reasons, but using an early disappearance as a tax or licensing strategy breaks the bargain on which that reliance rests. “Publicly available” can mean available on reasonable paid terms; it need not mean free, advertised, or placed on the homepage.
[P1.3] Second, exclusivity turns a private accounting decision into a public access problem. If a publisher stops printing one novel, used copies and other editions may remain. If the sole distributor removes an original series, later viewers may have no lawful route to it at all. Completed films and series embody substantial collaborative labour and cultural value.
[P1.4] A modest fixed availability period ensures that value is not sacrificed merely because deletion produces a better balance-sheet outcome than continued distribution.
[P1.5] Third, the obligation is administrable and proportionate. Platforms know release dates and can write licensing contracts around a statutory minimum. The rule need not require perpetual availability: after the minimum, ordinary removals remain lawful, with narrow exceptions for legal or safety necessities. Because these works were already produced and released, the marginal burden is chiefly forgone tax advantages or renegotiated licence terms—not an impossible duty to create new infrastructure.
[P1.6] The central question is therefore not whether firms may ever remove content. They may. It is whether a firm may acquire exclusive control over a finished work, invite public reliance on it, and then withdraw [clipped]

[C1.1] A streaming service is a commercial publisher, not a public archive. The proposition would turn ordinary catalogue management into a legal duty to keep exhibiting specific works merely because the firm once released them and later finds continued distribution costly. That is not a narrow fix for gatekeeping. It is compelled carriage of private speech and private property, aimed at a problem the state can address more cleanly.
[C1.2] The reliance claim in [P1.2] cannot bear that weight. Subscribers buy access to a changing library, not a vested right in every title present this month. Terms already say programmes come and go. Creators are paid under contracts that allocate windows, residuals and third-party clearances; delivering a finished cut does not create a public-law right to a statutory exhibition period.
[C1.3] Calling the catalogue cultural infrastructure is a metaphor, not a reason to conscript Netflix, Disney or a smaller service into the role of a national film archive.
[C1.4] Exclusivity does not make a carriage mandate harmless. Many so-called originals are bundles of music, footage, performer and territorial rights that expire or become ruinously expensive. Contrary to [P1.5], the burden is not chiefly a forgone tax advantage. It can force renewal of hold-out licences, continued residual payments triggered by availability, or breach of contracts the platform cannot rewrite.
[C1.5] Firms will adapt before release: fewer risky originals, more licensing instead of ownership, more territorial fragmentation, and higher prices to carry a long tail almost nobody watches.
[C1.6] If tax write-offs reward taking a finished work down, change the tax rule. Disallow the deduction where non-distribution is the strategy. Do not enact a motive-based carriage mandate that regulators cannot cleanly police and that also freezes licensing disputes. Where society truly wants preservation, that task belongs with archives, libraries and voluntary purchase or download markets—not a statutory minimum that mistakes a shop window for a public trust.

[P2.1] The opposition’s case depends on recasting a limited availability rule as permanent compelled carriage. It is neither. “Publicly available” can mean reasonable paid access through purchase, rental, or another authorized service; the original platform need not host the work forever, promote it, or turn itself into an archive. A viewpoint-neutral obligation applying to its own originals does not dictate an editorial message. It simply prevents exclusive control from becoming a power of strategic disappearance.
[P2.2] Nor does a changing-library disclaimer defeat reliance. Subscribers expect ordinary rotation; that does not make every disappearance legitimate. This rule does not freeze every title appearing in a catalogue. It concerns originals released under a service’s control and withdrawn early for tax or licensing reasons, potentially with no lawful alternative.
[P2.3] Terms can warn that a library changes. They cannot justify inviting audiences and creators to invest attention in the only public edition, then treating its inaccessibility as an accounting convenience.
[P2.4] The licensing objection shows why duties should shape contracts before release, not why completed works should vanish. Services choose which projects to commission, acquire, and brand as originals; they can clear rights for the minimum period, negotiate residuals, or arrange access elsewhere. That is prospective planning, not retroactively compelled breach. Claims of fewer daring originals and higher prices are speculative, while the opposing harm is concrete: production is finished, yet the public cannot lawfully see it.
[P2.5] The rule changes bargaining incentives rather than assuming licences cost nothing.
[P2.6] Finally, [C1.6]’s alternatives do not solve the problem. Denying a tax deduction might reduce one incentive, but it neither secures availability nor addresses licensing removals, and policing a write-off strategy also requires examining purpose. Archives can preserve a copy without providing ordinary public access; voluntary downloads cannot exist when the rights holder refuses to release them. CON would leave control intact, remove the work, and ask the public to hope tax rules or private generosity eventually help.
[P2.7] A bounded minimum directly protects access while preserving ordinary removals afterwards.
