[P1.1] Undersea communications cables carry nearly all intercontinental data: every cross-border financial transaction, military command link for deployed forces, allied coordination channel, and logistics feed that makes high-visibility defense hardware actually functional. A coordinated strike on just a handful of chokepoint cables in shallow, narrow straits could paralyze national economic function, cut off forward-deployed units from command, and trigger cascading market losses running to tens of billions of dollars a day—a scale of damage no individual conventional weapons procurement could ever inflict or defend against.
[P1.2] Right now, those cables are catastrophically underprotected. Patrol coverage for high-risk routes is negligible, geographically diverse redundant paths through low-risk areas are rare, and the global fleet of cable repair ships is small enough that even a handful of simultaneous cuts could leave entire regions disconnected for weeks. Adversaries already regularly deploy submersibles and uncrewed underwater vehicles to map and tamper with cable routes, knowing an attack on this infrastructure can deliver strategic effect without triggering an armed response, since cuts can easily be masked as accidental trawler damage or natural fault.
[P1.3] This is not a tradeoff between defense and non-defense priorities. It is a choice between spending marginal dollars on flashy, visible procurements that deliver incremental improvements to already robust, overmatching capabilities, and closing a gaping, unaddressed single point of failure that renders all those expensive platforms useless if cut off from the data and communications they need to operate. Cable protection and redundancy does double duty: it hardens against military attack, insulates against accidental damage and natural disaster, and blocks routine industrial and state espionage that siphons sensitive data every day.

[C1.1] The proposition demands that we hollow out visible defense budgets on the promise of preventing a catastrophic undersea strike. That is a bad trade built on an inflated threat. The overwhelming majority of undersea cable outages stem from ordinary accidents—trawler nets, anchoring gear, seismic events—not military attack. Those recurring, costly risks are best handled by the commercial operators who own the cables, insurers who price the hazard, and regulators who mandate diverse routing and maintenance standards.
[C1.2] Diverting scarce defense procurement dollars into a permanent undersea babysitting bureaucracy confuses commercial upkeep with national defense.
[C1.3] Military deterrence is not a competing luxury; it is the shield that keeps all infrastructure, including cables, beyond an adversary’s reach. If we slow submarine procurement, anti-surface capabilities, and command-and-control modernization, we invite exactly the gray-zone probing PRO warns about. A navy that cannot dominate the undersea domain cannot secure cable landing stations no matter how many redundant paths we lay. Visible platforms also serve multiple missions—deterrence, power projection, alliance reassurance—whereas cable repair is a narrow, technical function better left to those with specialized vessels and expertise.
[C1.4] Ships, aircraft, and allied presence produce strategic effects that a cable repair contract never will.
[C1.5] The network is also far more resilient than the doomsday scenario suggests. Parallel routes, satellite backhaul, and an international repair industry mean a single cut is usually a managed inconvenience rather than national collapse. Storms and accidents, not saboteurs, account for the overwhelming share of downtime. Repair vessels can be repositioned and traffic rerouted within days; the system degrades slowly and recovers predictably.
[C1.6] The sensible course is targeted coordination: intelligence sharing on adversary submersible activity, hardened landing-station security, and joint exercises with cable operators. What we must not do is treat a privately operated commercial network as a blank check for defense reallocation. Protect cables as one task within maritime security, not by dismantling the forces that make maritime security possible.
