[P1.1] When animal models cannot predict human protection, refusing controlled infection studies trades a bounded, consented risk for slower science and more preventable deaths. Regulators should permit these studies more broadly under strict criteria, not because the ethical objections are trivial, but because those objections do not erase the larger harm of waiting.
[P1.2] Species differences in receptors, immunity, and disease course routinely mislead. A candidate that protects mice or even primates can fail in people, and the reverse happens too. For malaria, influenza, cholera, and other infections with poor animal correlates, challenge studies in healthy adult volunteers have already sorted promising candidates from failures with far fewer participants than field trials. Early SARS-CoV-2 challenge work made the same bargain concrete: young adults, a carefully limited inoculum, continuous monitoring, and rapid care produced infection data that outbreak observation could not deliver on a useful timetable.
[P1.3] The ethics are serious and should stay visible. Deliberate infection is not ordinary care. It is justified only when the pathogen is manageable, exclusion criteria are tight, treatment or rapid recovery is realistic, consent is specific and uncoerced, and independent review can show a credible public benefit. Those conditions already govern first-in-human drug trials that expose healthy people to uncertain harm.
[P1.4] Controlled infection is the same moral structure applied to a different scientific gap.
[P1.5] The real alternative is not safety. It is larger, slower trials, products advanced on weak evidence, and neglected diseases left unstudied because natural exposure is too rare or too unpredictable to power a trial. Broader permission means published criteria and hard stops, not a blank check: inadequate animal models, a controllable pathogen, robust consent, and a pre-specified rescue plan. Under those limits, controlled human infection is the more ethical regulatory choice.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to broaden permission precisely where our uncertainty is greatest — and that is where regulatory caution earns its keep. My case rests on three claims: the science is weaker than advertised, consent cannot do the moral work assigned to it, and "more broadly" is the operative phrase that turns exceptional judgment into routine practice.
[C1.2] First, an inadequate animal model does not make a human challenge model adequate. Challenge studies infect healthy young adults by an artificial route at an artificial dose, then measure short-term endpoints. Real vaccines must protect the elderly, the immunocompromised, and the malnourished against natural exposure over months and years. A result from a controlled challenge can look decisive while quietly failing to predict field effectiveness — and worse, it can certify as safe a product that behaves differently when antibody levels wane or exposure routes vary.
[C1.3] The history of dengue vaccine development is a warning here: immune responses that looked protective in one context worsened outcomes in another. Broader permission to run challenge studies risks laundering weak evidence into regulatory confidence, which is exactly the failure PRO warns about in field trials.
[C1.4] Second, consent is being asked to carry too much weight. You cannot meaningfully consent to unknown long-term consequences of a deliberately induced infection — post-viral syndromes, immune complications, delayed sequelae we cannot enumerate in advance. Payments to healthy volunteers, who are disproportionately young people with limited income, add coercion that "robust consent" paperwork does not remove. And unlike a first-in-human drug trial, the intervention here is the harm itself: the volunteer is infected on purpose, not exposed to a side effect while seeking benefit.
[C1.5] Third, "more broadly" is doing unexamined work. Case-by-case review with hard stops is a fine description of what strict oversight already permits. Expanding permission creates institutional momentum, career incentives, and normalization pressure — the classic mechanism by which exceptions become pipelines. PRO must show not that some challenge study was defensible, but that loosening the default is justified despite these structural risks.

[P2.1] Caution earns its keep against overclaiming, not against the most controlled human evidence when animals fail. A challenge in healthy adults, at a defined dose, with short endpoints, is an early discriminator — not a warrant to claim protection for the elderly, the immunocompromised, or immunity that later wanes. [C1.2] and [C1.3] describe laundering partial evidence into regulatory confidence. That failure is real, and the rule against it binds animal data, field surrogates, and challenge results alike.
[P2.2] Dengue warns that a response protective in one context can harm in another — which is why a young-adult challenge must not be read as field protection, not why the controlled window should be closed. No model may certify a population or a time horizon it never measured. Withholding the most monitored measurement does not make the rest honest.
[P2.3] Consent authorizes a bounded risk: a manageable pathogen, tight exclusion, rescue on hand, and a plain account of what cannot be ruled out. Healthy-volunteer drug trials already use that standard. [C1.4] says intentional infection is a different moral kind because the harm is the intervention. Intentionality tightens the duty of care.
[P2.4] It does not reverse the arithmetic. A monitored infection with rapid treatment can be smaller, and more informative, than the uncontrolled infections a field trial requires. Payment can distort any volunteer study. Caps and independent review meet that objection; if they do not, the argument abolishes Phase I.
[P2.5] [C1.5] is right that some challenges are already defensible one by one. Defensible on paper and stalled in practice are different regimes. “More broadly” means a published default: when animal models are inadequate and the stops are met, permission is ordinary, not a political exception. Momentum is why those stops must be revocable — controllable pathogen, endpoints that cannot be over-read, uncoerced consent, pre-specified rescue — not why regulators should keep choosing unconsented population risk. Serious objections are reasons to police the instrument, not to withhold it.

A default is what converts judgment into throughput: ethics committees reviewing in series, sponsors optimizing submissions to clear fixed criteria, healthy-volunteer registries building challenge studies into how students pay rent. The safeguards PRO lists — revocable stops, hard endpoints — are only as strong as the institution applying them under that pressure.

[C2.5] Finally, the Phase I analogy fails on its own terms. In a drug trial the volunteer is exposed to risk as a side effect of a possible benefit; in a challenge study the harm is the payload and no participant can benefit. That difference does not merely "tighten the duty of care" — it changes what consent can authorize. You can consent to a risk of harm in pursuit of therapy.
[C2.6] You cannot consent away the wrong of being made sick for someone else's data when safer ways to gather most of that data still exist. That is why controlled infection must stay exceptional, case by case — and why "more broadly" loses.
