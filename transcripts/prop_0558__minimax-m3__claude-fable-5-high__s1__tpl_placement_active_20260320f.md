[P1.1] Indefinite detention is one of those policies that survives on fear, not evidence. The proposition is precise: governments should abolish that practice and replace it with presumptive release backed by case management. That is not a radical idea. It is a moderate, evidence-based reform that respects both due process and border administration.
[P1.2] The first point is moral clarity. Locking human beings in cells for months or years without a release date, often after they have committed no crime, offends basic dignity. Courts in multiple democracies have struck down such practices as disproportionate. The proposition accepts that detention can still be used — sharply, briefly, and only when justified — but it refuses to let "indefinite" remain on the menu.
[P1.3] The second point is empirical. Decades of supervised-release, ankle-monitoring, and case-management pilots show appearance rates for hearings of roughly 90 percent or higher — comparable to detention — while saving governments substantial money per person per day. The flight-risk story used to justify mass incarceration simply does not survive contact with these results. Most people summoned to court show up if they understand the process and have a stable contact point.
[P1.4] The third point is fiscal. Detention costs orders of magnitude more than community-based alternatives. Replacing it frees resources that can be reinvested in faster case processing — which actually improves compliance and reduces backlog rather than worsening it.
[P1.5] The fourth point is harm reduction. Indefinite detention inflicts documented psychological injury: depression, anxiety, PTSD, and suicidality, including among children held with their families. Those wounds do not disappear at release; they often reappear as downstream costs to health systems, social services, and re-integration.
[P1.6] The real clash is therefore narrow: are there alternatives that work? The evidence says yes, and the proposition adopts them. Detention remains available as a tool; it just cannot be open-ended. That is the policy the motion asks us to defend.

A blanket prohibition means that once a bureaucratic time-clock runs out, the state must release someone it has lawfully ordered removed and who has demonstrated he will not comply.
[C1.3] That is not humane policy; it is a self-inflicted veto on enforcement.

[C1.6] Finally, there is a better answer PRO must explain away: mandatory periodic judicial review, strict necessity tests, time-triggered burden-shifting to the government, and expanded case management for the genuinely low-risk. It is disciplined detention with oversight versus a presumption that removal orders are optional.
