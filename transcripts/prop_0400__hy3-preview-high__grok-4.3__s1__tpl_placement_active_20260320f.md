[P1.1] Life-altering, high-stakes government decisions strip away autonomy when reduced to opaque, unaccountable algorithmic or bureaucratic processes. When a family’s SNAP benefits are cut, a refugee’s deportation order is issued, or a defendant’s pretrial liberty is restricted, those outcomes shape entire futures and livelihoods, often with no path back from error. No person should face such consequences at the hands of an algorithm, a rigid checklist, or an unreachable bureaucratic layer without a guaranteed path to a human decision-maker who can weigh their specific material circumstances.
[P1.2] Automated or rigid rules-based systems lack the capacity to account for the unique nuance that defines real human lives. A single parent working irregular hours may miss a benefits recertification deadline due to a sudden family emergency; an algorithm will auto-terminate aid, but a human reviewer can waive the penalty after hearing their story. An asylum seeker’s credible fear claim may hinge on unspoken cultural context and personal testimony that a predictive model cannot parse, yet a human officer can evaluate that testimony on its merits.
[P1.3] Rigid systems also embed historical biases that are hard to audit: initial eligibility screenings for public programs have long disproportionately flagged low-income people of color for denial, a pattern that human review can interrupt before irreparable harm is done.
[P1.4] A core legal right to a human decision-maker creates enforceable accountability, not optional discretion for agencies to grant or withhold. If an automated system errs, there is no individual to question, no rationale to challenge, no clear path to redress. A human decision-maker can be required to explain their reasoning in plain language, face appeals, and be held to standards of fairness that apply to all. This is not a call to slow government down, but to ensure its most consequential actions respect the dignity, individuality and specificity of the people they affect.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that human decision-makers inherently provide superior outcomes in high-stakes cases, yet this overlooks both the limits of human capacity and the advantages of structured processes at scale. Government decisions on benefits, immigration, and pretrial matters number in the tens of millions annually. Mandating human review at the initial stage for all such matters creates immediate resource shortages that force prioritization or backlog. Individuals waiting years for a human reviewer lose access to necessary support or face extended uncertainty, outcomes that rigid timelines in automated systems sometimes avoid by enabling faster initial processing followed by targeted human appeals.
[C1.2] Human discretion also introduces variability that structured systems can mitigate. Officers handling the same type of claim may apply standards unevenly depending on their experience, current workload, or even the order of cases reviewed that day. This produces outcomes difficult for applicants to anticipate or systematically challenge. Standardized criteria, however, permit analysis of aggregate data to detect and adjust for patterns of misapplication across the entire population affected by a given rule.
[C1.3] The emphasis on personal nuance as a reason for mandatory human review ignores how that same nuance often results in unequal treatment. Caseworkers interpreting discretionary exceptions for benefits or asylum claims frequently reach divergent results on similar facts, creating a lottery effect based on which reviewer is assigned. Clearer rule sets allow for more uniform evaluation while still permitting later human adjustment where clear error is shown.
[C1.4] The focus on accountability through human explanation overlooks the practical effect of requiring humans at the outset. When volume prevents prompt human review, people suffer prolonged harm from erroneous initial denials or restrictions. Existing appeal processes already channel difficult cases to humans for reconsideration without mandating that every case begin with individualized human attention. The legal right therefore risks prioritizing form over the substance of timely and fair government action.
