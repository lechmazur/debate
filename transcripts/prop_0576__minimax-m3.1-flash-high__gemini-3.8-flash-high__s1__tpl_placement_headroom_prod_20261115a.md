[P1.1] Religious organizations should not receive tax advantages when their primary activity is overt partisan political action rather than worship, charity, education, or spiritual service. This is not an attack on religion; it limits a public subsidy for activity organizations can perform without religious or charitable justification.
[P1.2] Tax exemptions are a transfer from taxpayers, so they should advance purposes society recognizes as worthy. Churches and religious nonprofits provide genuine public goods: shelter, food assistance, hospital care, education, counseling, and community support. Overt partisan campaigning serves a different purpose: electing candidates and advancing a platform. If an organization is principally a political operation, retaining charitable status merely because it uses religious language turns exemption into a reward for political influence.
[P1.3] Consider a congregation whose core ministry is spiritual care and disaster relief; its tax treatment should remain secure. Now consider an affiliated political organization that openly recruits voters for a party, attacks rival candidates, and uses the congregation’s tax-exempt resources for electoral objectives. The decisive issue is not whether religion appears in the materials. Religious groups may legitimately speak on public questions.
[P1.4] The issue is what the organization is actually doing: sustaining a religious or charitable community, or operating principally as a partisan political actor.
[P1.5] That distinction protects religious freedom without subsidizing partisan power. A rule against religious groups’ political speech would punish citizens for worshipping, associating, or expressing moral views. A narrower rule conditions benefits on institutional purpose: organizations that cross from primarily spiritual or charitable work into overt partisan political activity should lose the advantages tied to that status. This preserves religious liberty and political equality because it asks what activity receives the subsidy, not what beliefs the recipient holds.
[P1.6] Tax law should reward religious service, not merely religious affiliation in partisan politics.

[C1.1] Tax exemption is not a cash subsidy awarded by the state for good behavior; it is a vital constitutional shield designed to keep the government out of the pulpit. When the state claims the authority to revoke exemptions based on whether an institution is "primarily" spiritual rather than "overtly partisan," it inevitably crowns tax auditors as arbiters of theological integrity.
[C1.2] The fatal flaw in this proposal lies in the false dichotomy between faith and political action. For many traditions, engaging secular power is not a partisan diversion; it is the direct expression of spiritual mandate. When historic Black churches organized voter registration drives, endorsed candidates who pledged to dismantle segregation, and mobilized their communities, they were exercising deep religious conviction. Under the proposition, a hostile state apparatus could easily brand such moral leadership as disqualifying partisan activity.
[C1.3] History demonstrates that whenever the state polices religious expression, selective enforcement follows. Bureaucrats inevitably punish dissenting, minority, or unpopular faiths while granting leeway to mainstream institutions that align with the ruling party.
[C1.4] Moreover, treating tax exemption as a conditional public gift distorts the principle of separation. We do not exempt religious institutions simply to reward them, but to prevent the state from acquiring coercive financial leverage over conscience. The power to tax inherently carries the power to control. If tax authorities must monitor Sunday sermons, scrutinize congregational budgets, and parse pastoral endorsements to determine institutional purity, religious autonomy is replaced by intrusive state surveillance.
[C1.5] Revoking exemptions does not safeguard political integrity; it arms the state with a financial weapon to silence religious voices that dare to challenge political authority. The moment the government decides which prayers are genuinely spiritual and which are impermissibly political, genuine religious liberty ceases to exist.
