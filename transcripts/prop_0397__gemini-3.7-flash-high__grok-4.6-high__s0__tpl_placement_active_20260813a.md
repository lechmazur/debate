[P1.1] Bankruptcy exists for a single, essential purpose: to give honest, overwhelmed debtors a fresh start when financial distress becomes permanent. Yet under the current system, student debt is subjected to an anomalous and nearly impossible standard. While bankruptcy courts routinely discharge gambling debt, failed business loans, and reckless consumer spending, student loans remain tethered to borrowers for life, even in the wake of permanent illness, credential fraud, or severe economic displacement.
[P1.2] This carve-out is both morally indefensible and economically counterproductive.
[P1.3] First, bankruptcy contains natural, severe deterrents that prevent abuse. Filing for bankruptcy is not a painless escape hatch; it is a humiliating legal process that liquidates non-exempt assets, severely damages credit ratings, and impairs access to housing, employment, and capital for up to a decade. No solvent professional destroys their financial life to walk away from manageable debt. The proposition explicitly limits relief to those facing a genuine, *long-term inability to repay*—ensuring that discharge is reserved for cases where repayment is functionally impossible.
[P1.4] Second, keeping unpayable debt on the balance sheets of destitute citizens generates no real recovery. Creditors and taxpayers collect mere pennies on the dollar through protracted administrative churn, aggressive collections, and wage garnishments that push families deeper into poverty. In return, the broader economy suffers a massive deadweight loss: millions of productive individuals are permanently barred from purchasing homes, starting businesses, or saving for retirement, ultimately shifting greater dependency onto social safety nets.
[P1.5] When an educational investment fails to yield the capacity to pay, treating that debt as uniquely immortal perverts the foundational purpose of bankruptcy law. Making student debt realistically dischargeable when repayment is impossible restores basic parity to the legal system, disciplines reckless lending, and reaffirms a fundamental economic principle: when catastrophic failure occurs, the law must offer a path to recovery, not a life sentence.
