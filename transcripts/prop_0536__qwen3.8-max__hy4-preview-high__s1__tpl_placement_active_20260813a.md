[P1.1] Older adults should have stronger legal protection against age-based algorithmic exclusion because automated systems do three things that ordinary law handles poorly: they make age decisions at scale, hide them behind neutral proxies, and leave victims without meaningful recourse.
[P1.2] Start with hiring. A resume screener can ignore birthdate yet still screen out older workers by penalizing graduation years, long job histories, or “culture fit” signals correlated with age. The result is the same unlawful exclusion, but faster, quieter, and harder to detect. Existing age-discrimination rules assume a human decision-maker and direct evidence; they fit badly with models whose logic is statistical, opaque, and constantly updated.
[P1.3] Insurance and finance present the same problem in more consequential forms. An underwriting or credit model might rely on health indicators, device behavior, transaction patterns, or neighborhood data that function as stand-ins for age. A 66-year-old may be denied coverage, charged more, refused a loan, or pushed into a restricted account without ever knowing which variable mattered. When age is used as a blunt shortcut for risk or profitability, the effect is exclusion from basic economic life.
[P1.4] The opposition will likely say current law or market competition is enough. But law that cannot see the decision is not enough. Stronger protection should require disclosure when algorithms affect hiring, insurance, or credit; audits for age disparate impact; limits on age and age proxies unless specifically justified; and a realistic right to contest adverse outcomes. That is not banning actuarial judgment or legitimate risk assessment.
[P1.5] It is insisting that exclusion cannot be automated into invisibility.
[P1.6] The central question is who bears the burden when a model quietly closes doors. Under the status quo, older applicants must somehow prove what a proprietary system did to them. The proposition places the duty on decision-makers to show their systems are fair, necessary, and reviewable. That shift is essential, because algorithmic age bias is not hypothetical; it is the predictable output of models optimized for efficiency without civil-rights constraints.
