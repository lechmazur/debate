Electrifying buses wins that race because Latin American cities already move on buses. From Bogotá and Mexico City to São Paulo and Santiago, buses and BRT carry the overwhelming share of public trips, including in low-income peripheries rail rarely reaches. Replacing those diesel fleets attacks emissions where trips already happen, this decade.
[P1.2] Speed and cost make the gap decisive. An electric bus program can be tendered, delivered, and put on existing roads and depots within a political cycle, route by route. A metro line requires years of studies, land expropriation, tunneling, utility relocation, and billions in upfront capital before it carries a single passenger — and its construction burns vast amounts of concrete and steel upfront. When budgets are scarce, one rail corridor consumes funds that could electrify thousands of buses across an entire city.

[P1.4] Crucially, electrification leverages the region's relative strength: comparatively hydro-rich electricity in much of Latin America. [P1.5] If the goal is maximum emissions avoided per dollar and per year before 2035, governments should electrify the fleet people already ride, at citywide scale, before betting the near term on new rail lines.
