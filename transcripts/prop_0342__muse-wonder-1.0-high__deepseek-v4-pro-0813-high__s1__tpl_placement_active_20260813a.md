[P1.1] Schools teach reading not because every child will become a novelist, but because no child can learn, work, or participate in life without it. That is now true of AI and media literacy. Students do not occasionally encounter manipulated information; they live inside systems that rank, recommend, generate, and personalize it for them all day. If they cannot ask how a claim was produced, why it was shown to them, and what evidence would change their mind, they are not literate in the world they inhabit.
[P1.2] Making this a core subject is the difference between exposure and competence. An optional module means an assembly on fake news, a lesson squeezed in by a teacher, or a unit only some schools can afford. Manipulation does not work that way. It is repetitive, emotional, and adaptive, exploiting identity, outrage, and belonging.
[P1.3] Resisting it requires a progression built over years: checking sources laterally instead of reading vertically, recognizing engagement incentives, understanding that AI systems predict plausible language rather than verify truth, spotting synthetic media, and noticing when urgency is being manufactured to bypass reflection.
[P1.4] This is not an add-on about apps that will date in a year. The durable skills are epistemic: triangulating sources, distinguishing evidence from assertion, understanding data, incentives, and probability, and knowing when to slow down, search independently, or withhold judgment. Taught well, those habits strengthen history, science, and English rather than compete with them, because every subject depends on students evaluating sources they find and outputs they generate.
[P1.5] The alternative leaves the most vulnerable students least protected. Affluent families can buy tutoring, discuss news at home, and model careful AI use; students without that support get the algorithm raw. A core curriculum, with trained teachers, protected time, and assessment, is how schools equalize a survival skill instead of treating it as enrichment.
[P1.6] We already made this decision for print. The information environment has changed faster than the timetable. Schools should catch up.

[C1.1] "Core subject" is not a stronger optional module. It means mandatory standards, tests, teacher certification, textbooks, and protected timetable slots. Those slots come from somewhere; in most schools the realistic cuts are history, civics, science, music, or languages—the background knowledge students need before they can judge a claim. An overloaded timetable cannot absorb another tested subject without leaving students less informed, and less informed students are easier to manipulate.
[C1.2] The reading analogy [P1.1] fails. Reading rests on a stable symbol system and decades of curricular consensus. AI and manipulation-resistant media literacy depend on fast-moving platforms, recommendation incentives, synthetic media, and contested speech norms. Standardizing that as a core subject invites political capture, because deciding what counts as manipulation is not a settled knowledge base.
[C1.3] What looks essential today becomes obsolete before a curriculum cycle ends. Core status locks in stale vendor content and checklist thinking; optional or embedded modules can adapt without turning an ed-tech fad into a permanent mandate.
[C1.4] The skills themselves are not a separate subject. Lateral reading, source triangulation, evidence versus assertion, recognizing manufactured urgency—these are disciplinary habits best practiced inside history, science, and English. A standalone media/AI class often becomes generic cautionary material, memorized for a test, while displacing the knowledge-rich subjects that make resistance durable. The proposer's own durable skills are mostly the work of existing disciplines.
[C1.5] The equity argument cuts the other way. An unfunded core mandate forces the least-resourced schools to adopt superficial compliance—scripted modules, outsourced platforms, tests without trained staff—while affluent schools get rich integration. Core status does not equalize; it widens the implementation gap.
[C1.6] The real choice is not core versus nothing. Schools can embed these practices across the curriculum, train teachers, and offer targeted optional modules. That is more flexible, less costly, and less likely to be captured by whatever media panic is current. A core subject is a heavy, rigid answer to a fluid problem.
