[P1.1] The kafala system gives a single employer total legal control over a migrant worker's ability to stay in the country, change jobs, or leave. That structural fact is not a flaw in the system's implementation—it is the system's design. And that design produces predictable, systematic harm.

Under kafala, she cannot quit, cannot switch employers without her sponsor's written permission, and cannot legally remain in the country if she runs.
[P1.3] She is not an employee in any meaningful sense—she is a captive. The abuse she faces is not incidental; it is enabled by a legal architecture that removes her exit option entirely.
[P1.4] The proposition's remedy is simple and precise: make the visa portable and worker-held. That single change dissolves the chokehold. If a worker can switch employers without sponsor consent, exploitation loses its mechanism. An employer who withholds wages or confiscates documents no longer holds the threat of deportation over a worker's head, because the worker can simply leave.
[P1.5] Critics will argue reform threatens labor market stability or that Gulf economies depend on predictable labor supply. But portable visas do not mean unlimited migration—they mean workers can move between employers within the existing visa framework. This actually improves labor market efficiency. When workers can respond to better wages and conditions, employers must compete for labor rather than capture it.
[P1.6] The result is upward pressure on wages and compliance with contracts, which Qatar's partial 2021 reforms began to demonstrate.
[P1.7] The moral and economic cases align here. Kafala suppresses labor mobility through legal coercion. Portable visas restore what every functioning labor market requires: the worker's ability to say no. Without that ability, no labor protection law, no complaint hotline, no diplomatic promise means anything—because the worker cannot act on them without losing the legal right to exist in the country.
[P1.8] That is the central question. Everything else follows from it.

[C1.1] The proposition's causal chain has a fatal weak link: it assumes visa control is the exclusive mechanism of abuse. It isn't. My case rests on three failures that fully unconditional worker-held portability cannot fix and may worsen.
[C1.2] First, target mismatch. PRO's own example is a domestic worker in a private household. In most Gulf states, domestic workers are explicitly carved out of the general labor code—no standard contract, no labor inspectorate access to private homes, no minimum wage enforcement. Changing who holds the visa does nothing when the underlying labor law never covered her in the first place.
[C1.3] The reform fixes a mechanism that isn't actually the binding constraint for the population PRO cites as the paradigm case.
[C1.4] Second, incentive reversal. Employers currently absorb recruitment fees, visa costs, and housing because kafala guarantees they retain the worker long enough to recoup that investment. Strip employer permission from the equation entirely, and rational employers respond by shortening contracts, front-loading fees onto workers, or simply refusing to hire workers seen as flight risks—precisely the low-skill, high-vulnerability workers PRO wants to protect. You don't eliminate exploitation; you shift it earlier in the relationship, into the recruitment stage, where it's harder to police.
[C1.5] Third, institutional capacity. A "portable visa" is only as real as the government agency that processes the transfer. Gulf labor ministries already struggle with backlogs, and workers report month-long waits even for routine permit actions. Removing employer consent doesn't remove the state bottleneck—it just relocates the choke point from a private sponsor to an under-resourced bureaucracy, with no guarantee of faster resolution.
[C1.6] The better path is what Qatar and the UAE have already begun: regulated mobility with notice periods, contract-completion safeguards, and enforceable minimum standards—reform that closes the recruitment-debt loophole instead of opening a new one. The proposition's absolutism, not incremental regulation, is the risk here.
