[P1.1] A free society is not merely a safe society. Prisons can be safe. What distinguishes freedom is that people may think, associate, worship, love, and criticize without being watched by the state. That is why privacy must rank above security when the two collide.
[P1.2] The core mistake is treating security as the master value and privacy as a luxury. Security is instrumental: we want it so people can live freely. Privacy is part of what freedom is. A person who cannot have an intimate conversation, seek medical care, read, or organize without fear of being logged and analyzed is not made free by the cameras that protect her.
[P1.3] She is made cautious. A society of cautious people is already less free, even if it is orderly.
[P1.4] Surveillance powers are routinely justified by emergencies, and then they expand: metadata retained, cameras networked, databases shared, dissent mapped. The benefits are often diffuse and speculative; the costs are concentrated and permanent. The activist, the abused spouse, the whistleblower, the religious minority, the person exploring an unpopular idea—these are the people who pay first. Security can be restored after an attack; a record of someone’s private life cannot be un-collected.
[P1.5] Nor is this a counsel of paralysis. A free society can pursue genuine security through targeted warrants, narrow data collection, transparency, and accountability. That is exactly the priority I am defending: privacy sets the default, and security must justify each intrusion. Reverse it—security first, privacy later—and you get the “nothing to hide” state, where the burden falls on citizens to prove they deserve solitude.
[P1.6] The question is not whether we want safety. Of course we do. The question is what kind of safety we are buying, and at whose expense. If security is purchased by making everyone a suspect, it has protected the state from its people rather than the people from harm.
[P1.7] Privacy matters more because it is what keeps security from becoming domination.
