[P1.1] A man dies leaving four thousand dollars of books on a shelf and four thousand dollars of books in an account. His daughter inherits the shelf. The account closes, and the books vanish. Nothing about her father's payment changed; nothing about the seller's costs changed.
[P1.2] Only the format changed — and a clause he never negotiated decided that his family gets nothing. That is the injustice this motion corrects.
[P1.3] The core of CON's case will be the word "license." You bought access, not property, so there is nothing to inherit. But that is the very label the motion contests; repeating it is not an argument. Non-transferability is a drafting choice made by vendors with no bargaining on the other side, and legislatures routinely void contract terms that offend a deeper public policy — unwaivable warranties, minimum tenant protections, terms that survive death in insurance and pensions.
[P1.4] Inheritance is such a policy. Every legal system treats a person's accumulated property as passing to their family by default; the question is whether a terms-of-service checkbox should be allowed to quietly repeal that default for an entire category of wealth.
[P1.5] Note what the guarantee requires: access passes to one heir, one account, no copying, DRM intact, no resale market. The seller keeps its price, its restrictions, and its royalties — already paid at purchase. A bequest is not a second sale. So the harm CON must show isn't piracy or lost revenue; it's the loss of a windfall they engineered, the right to delete a customer's paid library the moment he dies.
[P1.6] And these are not just files. They are annotated research libraries, purchased film collections, the marginalia of a life. CON must explain why a father's paper library is heritage and his digital one is a subscription that expires with his heartbeat.

[C1.1] The daughter in [P1.1] is a sympathetic image, but it hides the real question. The motion does not ask whether inheritable digital libraries would be nice. It asks whether law should *guarantee* inheritance for goods that were sold, and priced, as non-transferable. That guarantee would cost buyers more than it gives heirs.
[C1.2] First, the price was never "the same." PRO says "nothing about the seller's costs changed" and the bequest is "not a second sale." But a digital licence is cheap precisely because it is bounded: one user, one lifetime, no secondhand market. Rights holders price on that assumption.
[C1.3] Lengthen the term to cover heirs, and potentially their heirs, and the product becomes more valuable, so its price rises or it disappears. The likeliest result is a sharper shift to subscriptions and rentals, where nothing accrues and nothing passes. A mandate meant to protect ownership pushes the market further from it.
[C1.4] Second, retailers often cannot give what the guarantee demands. A digital bookstore or film platform usually holds distribution rights licensed from publishers and studios, who in turn hold them from authors, under territorial and time-limited terms. When those deals lapse or a platform shuts down, even living customers can lose titles. A statutory promise that every library "can pass" either rewrites contracts between parties who never dealt with the deceased, or becomes a guarantee the law cannot deliver.
[C1.5] Third, transfer-on-death is a laundering route. Non-transferability matters because transfer is the gateway to resale and sharing. Once law creates a mandatory channel, gray markets follow: "estates" handing whole account libraries to relatives, friends, or buyers.
[C1.6] Finally, the honest fix is disclosure and choice. Require clear labelling. Let sellers offer a transferable tier at a transparent price, and let buyers who value legacy pay for it. Choice protects families.
[C1.7] A blanket guarantee taxes every reader for a promise few will use.
