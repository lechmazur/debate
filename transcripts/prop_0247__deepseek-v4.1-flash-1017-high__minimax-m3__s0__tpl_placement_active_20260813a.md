[P1.1] A meaningful tax on sugar-sweetened beverages changes the price of harm and lets every shopper feel the signal. The case is simple: price works. These drinks are highly substitutable—water, unsweetened tea, coffee, reformulated lower-sugar versions. When the price gap widens, purchases fall, manufacturers reformulate, and the whole population shifts, not just the motivated few who read labels.
[P1.2] The health payoff is not abstract. Sugary drinks are a major source of added sugar with little nutritional value, and they feed obesity, type 2 diabetes, and dental decay. Those costs are brutal and regressive already: they fall hardest on lower-income families and on public health systems. A tax that cuts consumption therefore delivers its largest health gains to the people who most need them.
[P1.3] The regressivity objection compares the tax to a fantasy of zero costs. Lower-income households may pay more tax at the till, but they also bear more disease, lost work, and medical bills under the status quo. The right comparison is tax plus better health versus no tax plus preventable illness. And revenue can be recycled—child health programs, dental care, or rebates—so the net package can be progressive even when the levy itself is not.
[P1.4] Opponents will say education is fairer. But education alone is slow and weak against cheap, heavily marketed sugar. A price signal reaches everyone, including heavy consumers who ignore labels. If a policy meaningfully reduces a major source of preventable disease, and its revenue can protect the poor, the disproportionate burden is a design problem, not a veto.
[P1.5] That is why the proposition is right.
