[P1.1] Combat is not ordinary procurement. In a democracy, lethal force is supposed to travel through a public chain: elected authorization, uniformed command, military law, budgets, casualty reports, legislative scrutiny, and voters who can punish the government. Private military contractors in combat roles sever it. The trigger-puller works for a company under a commercial contract, answerable to managers and shareholders; the escalation and moral burden fall on citizens who never clearly authorized that agent.
[P1.2] The line is combat, not every service contract. A caterer or mechanic does not normally choose when coercion becomes lethal. Armed contractors at checkpoints, on convoy protection, in raids, advising under fire, or operating weapons can decide who is threatened, who is killed, and whether a local clash becomes an international incident. That is sovereign power, and it cannot be made democratic by calling it "security" in a contract.
[P1.3] The accountability harm is structural. Officials wary of a troop cap, a hostile vote, or public opposition can add armed personnel through contract modifications, subcontractors, and opaque task orders. When abuse occurs, responsibility can slide among commander, contracting officer, corporate supervisor, host government, and foreign jurisdiction; clauses may be proprietary; the individual may have left the country. Parliament can scrutinize a deployment, voters can punish casualties, and journalists can count uniforms, but those controls blur when the state can answer that the violence was private.
[P1.4] Regulation cannot cure that. Licensing, audits, and reporting duties may reduce misconduct, but they leave governments free to convert public war into less visible capacity. Profit gives another loyalty: a firm may lobby to renew the mission, while citizens bear the risk without a clear vote. Nor does efficiency answer legitimacy: a democracy gains no authority by fighting more cheaply or with fewer uniforms.
[P1.5] If elected leaders cannot authorize combat through publicly answerable forces, buying around that refusal is not flexibility; it is evasion. Combat roles in international conflicts must therefore be banned.

[C1.1] A private contractor cannot authorize a war, appropriate a dollar, or set his own mission. An elected government hires him, funds him, defines where he may operate and when he may fire, and can cancel the contract, bar the firm, or prosecute its employees. That is delegation under democratic control, not its disappearance — just as democracies use civilian intelligence analysts and arms makers without pretending every hand supporting force must be a salaried soldier.
[C1.2] The decisive question is who decides and who can be punished. Voters do not lose that power because a checkpoint guard wears a company patch. When contractors killed civilians in Baghdad's Nisour Square, the violence did not vanish as private: it produced investigations, prosecutions, and lasting political cost for the government that hired them. Uniforms guarantee nothing either; regular forces have committed abuses under classification and command secrecy.
[C1.3] Accountability comes from jurisdiction, disclosed contracts, reported casualties, and legislative funding — rules a ban discards for a symbol.
[C1.4] A ban would make force less, not more, accountable in practice. Governments facing an evacuation, embassy defence, or aid convoy to protect will not simply do nothing; they will turn to classified special forces, local militias, or foreign proxies over whom parliament has even less contractual and legal grip, or relabel combat as 'security.' Regulation can forbid off-books task orders, require reporting to legislatures, and extend criminal jurisdiction in theatre — preserving oversight without pretending prohibition removes demand.
[C1.5] Contractors also give elected leaders a limited, reversible option between inaction and mass deployment. That can express democratic restraint: protect civilians or withdraw personnel without a draft, an open-ended occupation force, or endless rotations for regular troops. Removing the option does not return power to voters; it narrows choices their representatives may make, while authoritarian states and their proxies keep the tool. Keep the decision public and punish abuse whoever commits it, but do not confuse a uniform with accountability.

A young soldier at a checkpoint also decides in seconds whether a car is a threat, kills irreversibly, and may create an incident citizens never approved in advance. Democratic accountability has never meant prior consent to each trigger pull. It means an elected government authorizes the mission, sets rules of engagement, can investigate, punish and terminate it, and answers electorally.
[C2.2] Contractors can operate under those same orders.
[C2.3] Nisour Square cuts the other way: killings were traced to a firm, investigated, prosecuted, and attached politically to the hiring government. That exposure followed scandal distinguishes nothing—uniformed abuses also emerge through leaks and photographs despite a named chain. Diffusion among commander, contracting officer and company is a defect of badly drafted contracts, not of a patch. Mandate one accountable authority, in-theatre criminal jurisdiction, preserved records and command-approved fire rules, and the chain can be named, audited and punished.
[C2.4] Visibility likewise does not require uniforms. Require contractor casualties and costs to be reported and funded in identifiable appropriations, and voters can punish contractor wars—as they did after Nisour. The remaining objection prefers uniformed deaths because they generate greater political resistance. That treats soldiers as tripwires for public opinion, not accountability.
[C2.5] The promised functional ban collapses at the embassy gate. If a guard may return fire, lethal discretion remains; if not, the choice is abandonment, local guards beyond domestic courts, or classified special forces whose operations and costs can be hidden far more completely than a contract. Calling special forces state actors answers legal status, not their superior visibility [P2.5].
[C2.6] A ban therefore does not force force into daylight. It drives the same demand toward darker channels while discarding contract, funding and jurisdiction—the very handles by which democracies actually grip it.
