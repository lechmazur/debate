[P1.1] Lock-in is the only cost in public IT procurement that compounds. The sticker price of a single-vendor integrated stack is quoted once; the price of being unable to leave it is quoted every renewal, every migration, every integration the vendor declines to build. My case is simple: for school and health systems, procurement rules should prefer open standards and switching portability because those are the only mechanisms that keep future prices competitive and future data usable.
[P1.2] Start with what the public is actually buying. In both cases the asset is the data and the workflow, not the brand of the dashboard.
[P1.3] When the contract runs on proprietary schemas, the vendor owns the exit, and the public owns the risk.
[P1.4] The mechanism is not complicated. Where those rules exist, buyers negotiate as customers; where they don't, they negotiate as hostages.

[C1.1] Let’s be clear about who bears the cost of this debate’s elegant theory. The proposition prioritizes the abstract, future value of switching. My position prioritizes the concrete, present burden of making things work for a nurse with a terminal patient or a teacher with thirty kids in the room.
[C1.2] In schools and clinics, staff are not software integrators. Their primary job is education or care, not negotiating API schemas between a student information system, a learning platform, and three other "best of breed" tools stitched together with custom code. The integrated stack, from a single vendor, is often the only viable way to get a coherent, functioning interface into the hands of a frontline professional who has minutes, not hours, to complete a task. Fragmenting this environment under a "switching portability" mandate doesn't create freedom; it creates a new, hidden cost: the cognitive load of workflow disruption and the operational risk of system incompatibility.
[C1.3] The real-world data on this is cautionary. Many public sector attempts to mandate open standards and multi-vendor integration have collapsed under their own complexity. They demand a level of in-house technical expertise and long-term management capacity that most school districts and local health systems simply do not possess. The result is often not a dynamic, competitive market, but a prolonged, expensive procurement nightmare where nothing works well together, and the "portability" is theoretical because no one has the resources to execute a migration.
[C1.4] The integrated stack offers a clear value proposition: predictable performance, a single point of accountability, and a functional system that arrives as a product, not a project. For institutions where downtime has immediate human consequences—a misscheduled vaccination, a lost special education record—this reliability is not a luxury. It is a core requirement. The PRO’s case mistakes a possible future benefit for a definite present cost, a cost paid by the very people the procurement is meant to serve.
