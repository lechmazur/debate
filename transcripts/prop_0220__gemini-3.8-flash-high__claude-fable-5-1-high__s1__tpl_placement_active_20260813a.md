[P1.1] Long-run household financial security is not held back by a lack of tax incentives; it is held back by human inertia. Expanding tax deductions assumes households fail to save because the financial return is slightly too low. In reality, millions fail to save because navigating retirement plans requires active effort, paperwork, and overcoming severe present bias.
[P1.2] Automatic enrollment solves this foundational friction by making saving the default. Instead of demanding that an employee research funds and actively sacrifice take-home pay, auto-enrollment harnesses behavioral inertia. When saving is the path of least resistance, the human tendency to stick with the status quo works *for* the worker rather than against them. While employees retain full autonomy to opt out, the vast majority remain enrolled, building wealth they otherwise never would have accumulated.
[P1.3] By contrast, increasing tax deductions is fundamentally ineffective at broadening security because it is an upside-down subsidy. Deductions disproportionately reward high earners in top brackets who already possess disposable income and established savings habits. For them, a higher deduction cap merely subsidizes the shifting of existing capital into tax-sheltered accounts, generating negligible new savings.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, low- and middle-income families—those facing the most acute retirement deficits—receive trivial relief from deductions due to lower marginal tax rates. A policy that provides the weakest push to those facing the greatest risk cannot deliver widespread security.
[P1.5] Long-run security requires starting early and compounding consistently across the entire workforce. Auto-enrollment converts millions of non-savers into persistent asset builders from their very first paycheck. Expanding deductions merely pays the affluent to do what they were already doing. Changing the default creates real, broad-based wealth; tweaking tax rates simply rewards existing advantage.

[C1.1] The motion asks about long-run household financial security, not participation rates. Those are different things, and auto-enrollment's record shows why the gap matters.
[C1.2] First, auto-enrollment buys enrollment with anchoring. Employers set defaults low—typically around three percent—because a high default triggers opt-outs and payroll complaints. The same inertia that keeps people in the plan keeps them at that number for years. Workers who would have chosen eight or ten percent under a voluntary regime drift down to the default.
[C1.3] Headline participation rises; average contributions among people who would have saved anyway often fall. A plan full of three-percent savers is not retirement security; it is a pension-shaped placebo.
[C1.4] Second, small auto-enrolled balances leak. When a job ends, a balance of a few thousand dollars is routinely cashed out, penalized, and spent. Hardship withdrawals and loans hit hardest exactly among the passive savers auto-enrollment recruits, because they never chose the commitment. Someone who never made a decision to save has never made a decision not to withdraw.
[C1.5] Third, coverage. Auto-enrollment only reaches workers at employers that offer plans—roughly half the private workforce. The gig driver, the small-shop employee, the self-employed contractor get nothing. A larger deduction for voluntary contributions attaches to the person, through IRAs, wherever they work and however often they change jobs.
[C1.6] Fourth, "security" includes the next six months, not just the next forty years. Defaulting a paycheck-to-paycheck household into an illiquid account while it carries card debt at twenty-plus percent doesn't build wealth; it swaps liquid solvency for locked savings with penalties attached. Voluntary contributors save when they can afford it, which is why the money stays.
[C1.7] Expanded deductions reward the thing that actually produces security: chosen, sustained, portable saving by people who understand what they are doing. Auto-enrollment manufactures accounts. Deductions grow savers. The motion asks which delivers security in the long run, and only one of these mechanisms survives the first job change.
