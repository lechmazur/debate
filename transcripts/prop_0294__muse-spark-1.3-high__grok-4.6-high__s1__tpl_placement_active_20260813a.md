[P1.1] Who decides what children learn about war and empire should not be whoever won the last school board election.
[P1.2] When politicians control history narratives directly, the incentive is not accuracy but loyalty. Governments in power whitewash their own crimes, opposition parties promise to rewrite the books when they return, and textbooks swing with each cycle. Students learn that history is propaganda, teachers are forced to teach myths they know are false, and minority communities see their own experiences erased for the sake of a flattering national story.
[P1.3] Independent scholarly review reverses those incentives. Historians are trained to weigh archives against each other, to publish evidence that others can check, and to correct mistakes when new documents emerge. That process is imperfect, but it points toward truth rather than toward applause. A textbook vetted by specialists in military history, colonial history, indigenous sources, and pedagogy is far more likely to get basic facts right, to present contested interpretations as contested, and to survive a change of government without needing a rewrite.
[P1.4] That stability matters for learning itself. You cannot teach critical thinking from a book designed to prevent questioning. Students who read about both the courage of soldiers and the atrocities of war, both the building of nations and the dispossession behind them, develop a tougher, more adult patriotism — loyalty that can face failure and reform it.
[P1.5] None of this removes democratic accountability. Elected bodies can still set broad standards, approve processes, and ensure age-appropriateness. But the drafting and fact-checking of sensitive narratives should be insulated, just as we insulate vote-counting and trials from direct political pressure.
[P1.6] History belongs to the public, not to the temporary officeholder. Put scholars in charge of getting it right, and let politicians debate what to do with the truth.

[C1.1] History in a public school is not a journal article. It is a civic act: a community deciding what its children should remember about war, belonging, and the country's past. That decision cannot be outsourced to a review panel and still remain public.
[C1.2] The proposition treats elected control as contamination and scholarly review as hygiene. That is a category error. Politicians can be voted out. School boards can be replaced.
[C1.3] Parents can show up. Independent scholarly review, once insulated, answers to professional networks and prevailing academic fashion. Those incentives are not accuracy. They are status.
[C1.4] When one orthodoxy captures the journals, it captures the textbooks, and no election can pry it loose. A parent and a tenured reviewer will not weigh the same chapter on empire or the last war. Only one of them faces voters.
[C1.5] Academic consensus already hardens. Colonial history was taught for long stretches as a civilizing mission, then as original sin without remainder. Each shift claimed the authority of scholarship. Each was also a political mood.
[C1.6] Stronger insulation would not have prevented those swings. It would have made them slower, more elite, and harder to contest.
[C1.7] Stability is not truth. A book that survives a change of government because no elected official can touch it is not more honest. It is more captured. Students do not learn critical thinking from premises pre-certified by specialists who share the same training and often the same politics.
[C1.8] None of this requires whitewashing. Elected bodies can demand multiple perspectives and primary sources. What they must not do is surrender the last word on national identity to people the public did not elect and cannot remove. War, empire, and patriotism are not technical questions like vote-counting.
[C1.9] They are the substance of self-government.
[C1.10] If history belongs to the public, the public must remain able to rewrite it. Insulation is not neutrality. It is a transfer of power.

[P2.1] Elections are a terrible fact-checker. A school board that deletes a massacre or inflates an empire can be voted out years later, but the lie has already been taught to thousands of children who cannot vote at all. Accountability after the damage is not accountability. The promise in [C1.4] that parents can just show up ignores who actually shows up: the most mobilized faction, the loudest identity bloc, the party with a myth to protect.
[P2.2] Scholars have bad incentives too — status, fashion, orthodoxy. The difference is that scholarly incentives can be checked by evidence. A historian who claims a war was clean must cite archives others can open. A rival can publish the contrary orders, the photographs, the colonial records, the testimony.
[P2.3] That is how the old civilizing-mission story fell: not because politicians grew generous, but because researchers forced open files that elected textbook committees had happily taught for decades. Political control did not prevent that orthodoxy. It protected it.
[P2.4] Insulation does not mean an irremovable priesthood writing beyond challenge. It means a mixed panel — military historians, colonial specialists, indigenous scholars, teachers — working in public, footnoting claims, marking disputes as disputes. That is harder to capture than a 5-4 board majority rewriting a chapter the summer after an election.
[P2.5] And war and identity are not licenses to invent the past. A community absolutely should decide what its history means, what courage to honor, what guilt to carry. It cannot decide by vote that dispossession did not happen or that prisoners were not killed. Meaning belongs to self-government; facts do not.
[P2.6] Leave politicians the standards, the process, the debate over significance. Take away their power to dictate the record.

[P4.4] And process without instant firing is not unaccountable; it is how every truth-seeking institution works. You cannot fire a lab result, you replicate it.
