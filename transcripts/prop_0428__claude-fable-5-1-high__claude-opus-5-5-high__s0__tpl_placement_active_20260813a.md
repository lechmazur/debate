[P1.1] Every neighborhood already runs a quiet experiment in what happens when worry wins. Walk past the ground-floor storefront that has sat empty for three years because a board game club couldn't meet the parking minimum. Look at the block where the last café closed at six and nothing replaced it, so anyone who wants company after work has a choice between a bar and their couch. That is the status quo the motion challenges, and it has costs that never get read into the record because nobody files a complaint about loneliness.
[P1.2] The clash here is simple: a worry is not a harm. Noise and parking concerns are predictions, and neighborhoods have tools for the moments when predictions come true—decibel limits, conditional hours, licensing that can be revoked. What we lack is any tool for the harm on the other side: the retiree who talks to no one all week, the teenager with nowhere to go but a screen, the night-shift nurse whose only social option closes before her shift ends. Low-cost venues are precisely the ones that serve these people, and precisely the ones a parking objection kills, because a knitting circle cannot afford to buy a lot.
[P1.3] Notice what these venues actually are. A pottery studio, a chess café, a late-hour tea room—these are not nightclubs. They are low-intensity by nature, and low-cost venues draw walk-in patrons from the surrounding blocks, which is the opposite of a parking problem. A lit window at eleven at night with people inside is also the cheapest public safety measure a street can have; dark blocks are the dangerous ones.
[P1.4] Finally, ask who is in the room when the worry is voiced. Zoning meetings skew toward long-tenured homeowners with free evenings. The renters, young workers, and newcomers who would fill these spaces are, by definition, absent. Allowing more venues corrects that imbalance rather than overriding the community.
[P1.5] CON must show that manageable, reversible inconveniences outweigh permanent, invisible isolation. It cannot.
