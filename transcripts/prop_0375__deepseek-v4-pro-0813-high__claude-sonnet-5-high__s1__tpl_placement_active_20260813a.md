[P1.1] Sustained low body mass is one of the oldest performance tools in sport. In weight-class events, reaching a lower class without arriving depleted gives a size advantage; in endurance events, less mass improves watts per kilogram and heat tolerance. GLP-1 drugs offer a pharmaceutical shortcut to that state: they blunt appetite, reduce body weight, and make prolonged calorie deficit easier to sustain. That is a direct intervention on a variable that decides medals.
[P1.2] Yet current regulation treats GLP-1s as ordinary diabetes or obesity medications, not as possible performance aids. Athletes without obesity can obtain them off-label for weight control. A boxer who stays at a lower class without fighting hunger, a lightweight rower who makes weight with less stress, a cyclist who reaches climbing weight while defending training intensity—these are material advantages. The advantage is uncertain in size, and that uncertainty strengthens the case for scrutiny, not against it.
[P1.3] The unfairness is structural. Not every athlete has equal access, willingness, or medical cover to use a prescription drug for body-composition control. When non-obese athletes use GLP-1s for weight management, the field tilts toward those with permissive doctors or fewer qualms. Normal dieting is allowed, but method, safety, and equal access still matter.
[P1.4] Health risks compound it. In lean, training athletes, GLP-1s may cause muscle loss, nausea, and impaired fueling during hard work. If sport normalises them for weight cuts, it risks encouraging a dangerous shortcut and worsening energy deficiency.
[P1.5] Stricter scrutiny does not require an immediate ban. It means monitoring prevalence, requiring clear therapeutic justification, and studying performance effects before use becomes entrenched. When a drug makes the hardest part of weight-class and endurance sport—staying light while training hard—easier, sport should treat it with suspicion, not wait for a scandal.
