[P1.1] The case for community-note style systems is that they treat political misinformation as an information problem, not a disposal problem. The harm of a false voting claim is not that the post exists; it is that people see it and believe it. A visible correction attached at the point of exposure directly reduces that harm. Proactive takedown often removes the post but not the claim: it migrates to screenshots, group chats, and less-moderated platforms, where no context can be attached.
[P1.2] A deleted post becomes a censorship story; a community-noted post loses credibility in place.
[P1.3] This matters most because political speech is high-value. Democratic accountability requires claims about candidates, elections, and protests to be contestable. Proactive removal forces private moderation teams to make fast, high-stakes calls about truth, and their mistakes suppress legitimate speech when it matters most. Community notes preserve the speech while correcting it, lowering the cost of error and reducing the appearance of partisanship.
[P1.4] The legitimacy point is practical. Political misinformation thrives when audiences distrust central fact-checkers and platforms. Community-note systems distribute judgment across competing users, display the reasoning, and attach evidence. That makes corrections more credible than an invisible removal decision.
[P1.5] They also scale: a viral false claim can be flagged before moderators review it, and ambiguous political spin can receive context without binary censorship.
[P1.6] Takedowns should remain for genuinely unlawful or dangerous material. But for ordinary political-content governance, context should be the default: it reduces belief, preserves accountability, scales across an endless stream of misleading claims, and avoids making every moderation error a free-speech scandal.

[C1.1] The central mistake is treating context as a substitute for interruption. For political content, reach and timing are the harm. A false polling-place claim, a fabricated video of a candidate, or a call to confront officials at a protest can do its damage in the hours before any note achieves consensus. Community notes are reactive and slow by design.
[C1.2] They require enough contributors to agree, which is hardest exactly when a claim is polarizing, fast-moving, or pushed by coordinated accounts. A note that appears after the vote, the rally, or the viral peak is not governance; it is an epitaph.
[C1.3] Proactive takedowns can act on the distribution layer: remove the post, demote the account, disrupt the network, prevent the recommendation engine from amplifying it. Notes only annotate the post. Many users never open the context, and some share it precisely because the note gives the false claim a controversy halo. On the most dangerous content—voter suppression, intimidation, incitement, hacked materials, synthetic impersonation—context is not a remedy.
[C1.4] If a deepfake audio clip is left up with a label, the platform is still distributing it. If a false election date is left up with a correction, it is still reaching people who will act before reading.
[C1.5] The proportionality argument is backwards. Community notes are useful for ambiguous spin, local disputes, and cases where removal would be overbroad. They should not be the bigger part of political-content governance, because they cannot stop coordinated manipulation. Adversaries can brigade consensus, exploit niche languages, or keep a claim below the note threshold while it spreads.
[C1.6] And a system that depends on cross-partisan agreement will systematically fail on the claims that most need correction.
[C1.7] The real choice is not speech versus censorship. It is whether platforms interrupt demonstrable harms, or append context while the harm scales. Keep notes as a supplement. Do not make them the primary defense.
