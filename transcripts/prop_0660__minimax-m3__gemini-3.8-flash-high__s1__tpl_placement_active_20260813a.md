[P1.1] Knowledge work doesn't end when the laptop closes. It bleeds into evenings, weekends, and vacations through emails, chat pings, and the low-grade anxiety of an unread inbox. [P1.2] The welfare gain is concrete. Workers report burnout, broken recovery, and strained relationships when availability is presumed unlimited. A law that fixes the end of the workday restores these directly—no manager-by-manager negotiation, no quiet pressure to "just reply to this one thing." It also lowers the cost of saying no, which matters most for the workers least able to negotiate individually: junior staff, contractors, and anyone whose job security feels thin.
[P1.3] Now the productivity objection. Two things matter here. First, in knowledge industries, output is bottlenecked by cognition, not hours. Exhausted people write worse code, produce shallower analysis, and solve fewer novel problems.

Once a clear boundary exists, both sides adapt: workers batch communications, managers stop sending low-value late messages, and the work that survives is the work that actually mattered.
[P1.5] The trade-off is also asymmetric. Productivity losses are recoverable—overtime, better tools, smarter processes. Burnout, family breakdown, and chronic sleep debt are not. One side gains durably; the other gives up temporarily.
