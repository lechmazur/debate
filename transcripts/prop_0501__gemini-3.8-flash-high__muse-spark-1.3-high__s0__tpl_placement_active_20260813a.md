[P1.1] For decades, central banking dogma assumed that keeping consumer price inflation near two percent was sufficient to guarantee economic stability. The 2008 financial crash and subsequent boom-bust cycles proved that assumption disastrously wrong. In the years leading up to 2008, consumer price indices registered calm, predictable inflation. Yet beneath that tranquil surface, unprecedented credit expansion was inflating an enormous housing bubble that ultimately shattered the global financial system.
[P1.2] Assets, especially housing, are fundamentally distinct from the goods in a typical consumer basket. Nobody buys groceries on eighty percent bank leverage. Housing, by contrast, is the primary collateral backing the modern financial sector. When residential property prices decouple from wage growth and fundamental rents, the resulting collapse does not merely trim paper wealth; it impairs bank balance sheets, triggers systemic credit freezes, and forces deep, protracted balance-sheet recessions.
[P1.3] Targeting only consumer prices while ignoring real estate is like checking the cabin temperature while water pours into the engine room.
[P1.4] The orthodox doctrine—that central banks should ignore bubbles on the way up and simply "clean up" with emergency liquidity after they burst—has failed. This asymmetric approach guarantees moral hazard: speculators privatize the gains of credit-driven asset booms, while the public bears the crushing social and economic cost of bailouts, austerity, and prolonged stagnation. Furthermore, unchecked housing appreciation locks entire generations out of shelter and wealth accumulation, driving destabilizing inequality.
[P1.5] Central banks already dictate the cost of mortgage credit and oversee macroprudential standards. Explicitly mandating asset-price stability, particularly in housing, removes the institutional excuse to look away while speculative debt overheats the real estate sector. Central banks must lean against systemic asset bubbles before they explode. True price stability cannot exist when the most critical asset ordinary citizens rely upon is left to the mercy of speculative credit cycles.
