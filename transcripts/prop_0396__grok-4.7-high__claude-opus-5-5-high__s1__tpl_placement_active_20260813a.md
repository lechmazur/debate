[P1.1] Credit reports exist for one job: forecasting whether someone will repay debts they choose to take on. They work when past behavior reveals habits, from rent and car notes to credit cards paid on time. Medical debt fails that test. It records an illness, not a decision.
[P1.2] Nobody shops for a heart attack the way they shop for a loan. The bill arrives after an emergency visit, a cancer diagnosis, or a child's surgery, inflated by denials, coding errors, and prices the patient never meaningfully accepted. Two neighbors can share income and payment habits; only the one who gets sick looks like a worse risk. That is noise, not a forecast of character.
[P1.3] Ordinary delinquency speaks to budgeting and willingness to honor contracts. Medical debt speaks to biology and a billing system full of surprise charges and out-of-network bills. Many of those balances are disputed, reduced, or paid once insurance finally processes. Reporting them early converts an administrative mess into a lasting mark of unreliability.
[P1.4] Correlation is not the same as a meaningful indicator. A person facing a five-figure hospital bill may miss that one obligation and still pay every voluntary debt on time. The medical line does not show how they treat lenders. It shows that an unpredictable shock exceeded cash on hand.
[P1.5] Scoring models already see real strain through missed rent, cards, and loans. Adding the hospital bill double-counts hardship and imports errors no voluntary credit file would contain.
[P1.6] The penalty then manufactures the risk it claims to measure. A lower score can mean a denied apartment, a rejected job, or costlier borrowing just as savings disappear. Lenders do not get a sharper picture of future repayment. They get a score that punishes illness.
[P1.7] Illness is not a meaningful indicator of future repayment behavior. Medical debt should not appear on consumer credit reports.

[C1.7] They tighten standards for everyone or lean on cruder proxies such as income bands and neighborhoods. Patients who pay faithfully lose the ability to show it.
