[P1.1] A restaurant menu lists $20 for pasta. Then it expects $4 more, or you are the villain. That is not generosity, it is a hidden labor cost collected through guilt.
[P1.2] Tipping forces workers to live with income volatility that employers should absorb. A slow Tuesday, bad weather, or a cheap table means servers take home less for the same work. No other skilled job makes pay depend on the mood of ten strangers a night. When base pay is low and tips fill the gap, management outsources wage risk onto the people least able to bear it.
[P1.3] It also bakes discrimination into the paycheck. Research consistently finds tips vary with race, gender, age, and appearance, not effort or quality. The link between service quality and tip percentage is tiny. What tipping actually incentivizes is tolerating harassment and deference to abusive customers because confrontation costs rent money.
[P1.4] That is a power structure no modern workplace should keep.
[P1.5] An included service charge fixes the transparency problem. Raise base wages to a livable, predictable level and put the real cost on the menu. Customers see the true price before they order and decide freely, without performing moral math at the table. That is more honest than pretending food is cheaper and then shaming diners into topping up payroll.
[P1.6] For restaurants, stable pay means stable teams. Kitchens and floors stop fighting over an arbitrary divide between tipped and untipped staff. You can reward teamwork, train professionally, and retain people instead of churning through them. The best restaurants in much of the world already run this way and compete on quality, not guilt.
[P1.7] Pay labor directly. Price honestly. If service truly matters, fund it like you mean it, on the bill, in the wage. Stop making dignity a gratuity.

[C1.7] Voluntary tipping rewards hustle, memory, and warmth. Flat systems reward attendance. Past no-tip experiments largely collapsed for the same reasons: staff quit and guests hated sticker shock.
