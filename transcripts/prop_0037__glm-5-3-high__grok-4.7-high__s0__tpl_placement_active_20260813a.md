[C1.1] Prohibition is not a plan. Banning private cars from entire central districts trades a solvable pricing problem for an unmanageable access problem, and the costs fall hardest on the people the ban claims to protect.
[C1.2] Congestion pricing works because it targets the harm. Drivers pay for the space and delay they consume, so wasteful trips drop while necessary ones continue. Schemes in London, Stockholm, and Singapore cut traffic and emissions without emptying their centers of every private vehicle. Those charges also funded bus and rail upgrades, so the alternative got better instead of being taken on faith.
[C1.3] A ban cannot replicate that selectivity. Once private cars are illegal, every exception—disability, night shifts, tools, medical visits—becomes a permit regime. The night-shift nurse and the tradesperson hauling tools do not vanish; they queue for exemptions that the wealthy will navigate faster. Ability to pay is replaced by ability to lobby.
[C1.4] That is not a fairer principle. It is a worse filter.
[C1.5] Street space can be reallocated without outlawing cars. Cities already convert lanes to buses and bikes, price or remove curb parking, and pedestrianize individual streets while private vehicles remain lawful on the network. What a district-wide ban adds is collateral damage: shops that lose customers who cannot haul purchases on transit, residents stranded when service is poor, and congestion shoved onto the ring roads that lower-income neighborhoods often border. Pricing, unlike a ban, generates the revenue that builds the alternatives.
[C1.6] A ban demands those alternatives first and offers no mechanism to pay for them.
[C1.7] Certainty is not a virtue when the policy is the wrong one. Cities need fewer car trips downtown, cleaner air, and streets that serve people. They do not need a rule that pretends every private journey is equally illegitimate. Price the externality.
[C1.8] Keep the option. Do not ban the city out of its own mobility.
