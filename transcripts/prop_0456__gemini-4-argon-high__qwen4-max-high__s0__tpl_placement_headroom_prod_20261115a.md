[P1.1] Generative AI does not conjure art from the ether; it distills the accumulated labor of human writers, musicians, and filmmakers. Yet the creative economy currently faces a false binary between two broken extremes: uncompensated extraction or licensing paralysis. A compulsory licensing scheme is the only mechanism that preserves both technological progress and the economic survival of human creators.
[P1.2] First, training without compensation is economic cannibalism. When a human reads a novel, they absorb inspiration; when a studio or label ingests millions of copyrighted works into a neural network, they are manufacturing a commercial substitute designed to outcompete the very artists they scraped. Consider a music label training an audio model on decades of session musicians' recordings to generate endless, royalty-free background tracks, or a publisher training an engine on freelance illustrators to automate book covers. Allowing this for free under the guise of "fair use" forces creators to subsidize their own obsolescence.
[P1.3] If the underlying human ecosystem is starved of revenue, the well of original human culture eventually dries up.
[P1.4] Second, requiring individual, upfront permission for every single work is logistically impossible. Useful generative models require vast, diverse datasets spanning millions of texts, tracks, and films. Negotiating bespoke contracts with millions of fragmented rightsholders creates an insurmountable transaction-cost bottleneck. In a strict opt-in regime, either AI development halts entirely, or only the largest conglomerates survive by cutting backroom deals that still leave everyday creators behind.
[P1.5] Compulsory licensing solves both failures simultaneously, and proven precedent shows it works. Just as mechanical licenses and digital broadcast collectives allow services to play music without tracking down every songwriter, a statutory AI license removes the permission bottleneck while mandating fair, standardized remuneration. Publishers, labels, and studios gain the frictionless scale required to innovate, and working artists get paid.
