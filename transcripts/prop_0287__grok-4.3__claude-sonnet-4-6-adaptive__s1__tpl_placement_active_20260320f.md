[P1.1] Fixed terms for constitutional justices tie judicial power to ongoing democratic consent instead of freezing the preferences of appointments made a generation earlier. Lifetime tenure lets justices appointed in one era control constitutional outcomes long after the electorate has shifted, turning the court into an institutional veto that current majorities cannot revisit without amending the constitution itself. That structural distance erodes the court’s claim to speak for a living constitutional order.
[P1.2] The same arrangement intensifies appointment battles because each vacancy carries decades of downstream power. Fixed, non-renewable terms lower those stakes by guaranteeing turnover on a predictable schedule, which reduces the incentive to treat every nomination as existential and thereby cools the politicization already visible in lifetime systems.
[P1.3] Independence is not destroyed by this rotation. A single non-renewable term still shields sitting justices from removal or financial retaliation while they serve; the only lever removed is the ability to extend personal tenure by pleasing the next government. European constitutional courts that adopted this model decades ago have maintained high rates of rulings against the governments that appointed them, showing that decisional autonomy survives without lifetime insulation.
[P1.4] The legitimacy deficit of indefinite tenure is therefore the heavier cost. When citizens conclude that constitutional rules are enforced by unelected officials whose views no longer reflect any recent democratic choice, voluntary compliance falls and political actors feel justified in testing institutional boundaries. Fixed terms address that source of friction directly while preserving the practical protections that matter for day-to-day independence.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a category error about where judicial legitimacy originates. Courts are not legislatures. They derive authority not from reflecting popular preferences but from being visibly insulated against them. When a justice rules against the government that appointed her, or against a strong public consensus, the system works as designed.
[C1.2] Fixed terms corrode that mechanism at its root.
[C1.3] The structural damage runs deeper than PRO acknowledges. A justice in the final years of a fixed term faces inevitable post-court life — a law firm partnership, an academic chair, a political appointment. That creates an audition problem that lifetime tenure avoids. Justices permanently shielded from post-service career pressure have less incentive to cultivate powerful actors who will later evaluate them on exit.
[C1.4] Non-renewable terms address only one channel of manipulation — re-appointment by government — while leaving the post-service lever fully exposed and structurally guaranteed.
[C1.5] The legitimacy argument also misfires on its own terms. PRO claims courts whose personnel no longer reflect recent democratic choices lose public compliance. But compliance with constitutional courts historically peaks precisely when those courts issue counter-majoritarian rulings that protect minorities — rulings that are, by definition, out of step with current democratic preferences. Tying judicial turnover to electoral cycles sends an implicit public message that the court should track those preferences.
[C1.6] That message undermines the exact institutional logic that makes constitutional protection worth having in the first place.
[C1.7] As for reducing appointment battles: predictable vacancies do not lower political stakes — they schedule them. Knowing a seat opens every eighteen months means every election cycle is contested partly over the next several appointments. Fixed terms convert episodic high-stakes confirmation fights into permanent constitutional campaigning.
[C1.8] The trade-off the motion describes is real. The independence damage is structural and self-compounding; the legitimacy gain is illusory because it rests on a false model of what courts are supposed to be.

[P2.1] Fixed terms correct rather than contradict the insulation principle. A justice insulated for eighteen years still decides without fear of removal, yet the system no longer treats that insulation as permanent veto power over every future electorate. The CON claim that legitimacy requires total disconnection from democratic turnover confuses means with end: insulation protects the decision process; it does not justify freezing constitutional interpretation in the preferences of long-departed presidents or parliaments.
[P2.2] The audition problem is real but misweighted. Post-service careers already exist under lifetime tenure; justices routinely join firms, boards, or universities after retirement. Non-renewable terms simply make the end date predictable instead of discretionary. They remove the single most dangerous lever—government control over continued tenure—while the shorter horizon limits the period any justice can angle for future favor.
[P2.3] The structural guarantee of exit actually reduces the incentive to cultivate patrons during service, because reappointment is off the table.
[P2.4] Compliance data points the opposite direction from the counter-majoritarian argument. Courts retain public support longest when majorities perceive that the bench itself remains open to revision rather than locked into generational capture. When rulings appear permanently detached from any recent electoral signal, political actors treat them as obstacles to be circumvented rather than constraints to be obeyed. Fixed terms transmit the clearer message that the court is an enduring institution whose membership rotates on a known schedule, not an unaccountable oligarchy.
[P2.5] Scheduling battles does not equal intensifying them. Predictable vacancies convert confirmation fights from once-in-a-generation events into routine legislative business whose stakes are bounded by term length. Lifetime vacancies, by contrast, remain zero-sum contests over decades of control, which is why nominations already function as permanent campaign issues. The legitimacy gain from restoring rotation therefore exceeds the marginal independence cost, because the independence that matters—decisional freedom while on the bench—survives intact.
