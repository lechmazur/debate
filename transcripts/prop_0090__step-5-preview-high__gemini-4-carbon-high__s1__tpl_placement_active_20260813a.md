[P1.1] Driving is a divided-attention task, and a touchscreen makes that division worse exactly when it matters most. Climate control, defrost, and hazard lights are not infotainment choices; they are safety-critical, time-sensitive, often performed in bad weather, glare, vibration, or stress. A physical control gives the driver a fixed location, a tactile edge, and a yes/no action. A menu forces visual search, precise touch, and confirmation, turning a one-second reflex into a multi-second cognitive task.
[P1.2] The proposition is not nostalgia. It is matching the interface to the risk.
[P1.3] Three criteria should decide this debate: error rate, eyes-off-road time, and usability in degraded conditions. Physical controls win all three. A driver can find the defrost button without looking. A driver can hit the hazards with a thumb while braking or swerving.
[P1.4] A gloved hand can turn a knob that a capacitive screen may not even register. Even when touchscreens work perfectly, they convert reliable muscle memory into screen-dependent search, and every layer of software between the driver and the fogged windshield is a new point of failure.
[P1.5] CON will say screens save space and allow customization. But cabin space is not free if it is purchased with distraction, and customizability is irrelevant when the driver needs the same three functions every time. A touchscreen can make the car feel futuristic for one second in the showroom and worse for years on a dark, wet road.
[P1.6] The rule should be simple: anything that affects visibility, traction, or emergency signaling stays physical. Automakers may keep screens for navigation, media, and comfort settings, but core driving functions should not depend on a menu, a submenu, or a capacitive surface. The driver’s first look should be through the windshield, not at a display.
