[P1.1] A purchased ticket is not a mere revocable pass; it is a prepaid entitlement to a seat. If a fan cannot attend because of illness, work, travel failure, or a family emergency, that value should not silently expire for the sole benefit of the original seller. Digital ticketing should make transfer easier, not become a tool for converting fans’ purchases into non-transferable, platform-locked permissions. The default legal rule should therefore be that a fan may transfer a digital ticket peer-to-peer.
[P1.2] Blanket no-transfer policies are the wrong response to scalping. They target ordinary fans more effectively than professional scalpers, who use bots, multiple accounts, and identity workarounds. A parent who cannot go and wants to send her sister, or a friend holding two tickets after a companion gets sick, should not need the organizer’s permission. The law should protect that ordinary alienation right.
[P1.3] The motion already accommodates legitimate anti-scalping concerns. Organizers may impose limits, but only if narrowly tailored. Face-value exchanges, capped resale, verified fan-to-fan transfer windows, or waiting lists can reduce speculative resale without stranding tickets. An ID-locked, no-transfer system is not narrow: it eliminates the entire lawful secondary use to prevent a subset of abuse.
[P1.4] That fails the least-restrictive-means test embedded in the proposition.
[P1.5] There is also an incentive problem. Without a default legal right to transfer, organizers and platforms have every reason to lock tickets into official resale channels that charge a second round of fees. Peer-to-peer transferability creates competitive pressure on those fees and gives fans an exit when plans change. The anti-scalping exception should be an exception, not a backdoor for total control.
[P1.6] The legal baseline should therefore be transferability, with the burden on organizers to show that any restriction is genuinely narrow and anti-scalping—not merely convenient for the issuer.

[C1.2] Consider what happens the day after this becomes law. Every organizer who caps resale at face value gets sued by a scalper claiming the cap isn't "narrowly tailored." Every ID-check requirement gets challenged as overbroad. The exception PRO promises—designed to reassure us that anti-scalping limits survive—becomes a litigation treadmill where deep-pocketed resale platforms pick off restrictions one by one.
[C1.3] The narrow tailoring standard, borrowed from constitutional doctrine, does not transplant cleanly into commercial contract regulation. It demands courts second-guess business judgments about bot threats, venue security, and dynamic market conditions they have no competence to assess.
[C1.4] The deeper flaw is conceptual. A digital ticket is not a chair you own and can sell to whomever. It is a revocable license to enter a specific venue at a specific time, subject to terms the organizer sets. Performers and organizers have legitimate interests in controlling who fills those seats—crowd safety, fan identity, brand covenants with artists who do not want their audiences brokered by scalpers.
[C1.5] The proposition treats these as pretextual, but that assumption is wrong. When a sports team restricts transfer to prevent visiting-team fans from buying blocks of seats, that is not anti-scalping at all—it is a legitimate crowd-management choice the proposition would override.
[C1.6] PRO's claim that scalping is a "subset of abuse" understates the problem. Professional scalpers dominate secondary markets; they are not a fringe case. A default transfer right with a narrow exception primarily empowers them, because they have the infrastructure to exploit it, while ordinary fans gain a benefit they could receive through simpler tools—official resale, name-change windows, or will-call transfer. The proposition trades a scalper's paradise for a marginal fan convenience, enforced by a standard no court can administer.
