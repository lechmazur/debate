[P1.1] Birth rates are not a checkout-counter decision where a coupon changes whether a family has a second child. They are a long-horizon commitment shaped by monthly bills, space, time, and uncertainty. That is why housing affordability and childcare outperform one-off baby bonuses: they change the recurring constraints that determine whether couples reach the family size they already say they want.
[P1.2] A one-off bonus is small against the lifetime cost of raising a child, and worse, it is temporary. It can pull a birth forward—couples may take the cash and have a child a year earlier—without raising completed fertility. The policy looks successful for one year and then the birth rate falls again. It also does little for the second child, which is where East Asia’s fertility gap is sharpest.
[P1.3] A family already stretched by rent and childcare does not become secure because of a payment that covers a stroller and a few months of formula.
[P1.4] Housing and childcare attack the actual bottlenecks. In East Asian cities, family formation is often gated by housing: space, down payments, rent, and long commutes. Childcare is gated by availability, hours, and cost—especially for women deciding whether a second child is compatible with employment. A guaranteed childcare place near home, or secure rental housing near work and school, changes the answer to “Can we manage another child?” every month, not just once.
[P1.5] The right test is completed fertility, not immediate conceptions. One-off bonuses are visible and easy to announce, but shallow. Affordable housing and reliable childcare reduce the recurring cost and time conflict that make couples stop at one child. The opponent must show that a transient transfer beats a permanent reduction in the constraints families face for years.
[P1.6] That is the comparison, and structural affordability wins it.
