[P1.1] Equality of opportunity is a claim about the starting line. So the question is simple: which does more to rig the starting line—what a person earns this year, or what a person is handed at birth?
[P1.2] A surgeon earning $800,000 pays close to half of it in tax, every year, on money tied to work she actually did. An heir receiving $8 million pays, in the United States, essentially nothing: the estate exemption sits around $13 million per person, and the stepped-up basis rule wipes out decades of unrealized gains the moment the owner dies. The earner is taxed on effort; the heir is untaxed on luck. That is backwards if we care about opportunity at all.
[P1.3] Income is bounded by a lifetime. Inheritance compounds across generations. Capital earns returns, those returns are reinvested, and the gap between the heir's grandchildren and everyone else's grandchildren widens regardless of anyone's merit. Picture two equally talented twenty-five-year-olds.
[P1.4] One gets a $2 million house deposit, a cushion that lets her take a risky startup job, and parents' connections. The other has student debt and a rent payment due. Same talent, radically different odds—and the difference was settled before either could speak.
[P1.5] Dynastic wealth also buys things salary cannot: foundations, political donations, board seats, family offices that structure trusts to escape tax for a century. High earners can be outcompeted. Dynasties entrench.
[P1.6] Note what aggressive inheritance taxation does not do: it does not punish striving. The person taxed is dead; the person receiving has done nothing to earn it. Generous exemptions can protect ordinary homes and family farms. What it targets is precisely the mechanism that converts one generation's advantage into permanent caste.
[P1.7] If opportunity means anything, the heavier tax belongs on the windfall, not the paycheck.
