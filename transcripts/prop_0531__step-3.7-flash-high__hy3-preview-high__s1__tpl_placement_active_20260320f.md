[P1.1] Public memorials in post-civil conflict societies are not neutral historical markers: they are foundational tools for building the shared trust required to prevent a return to violence. Prioritizing fully separate competing ethnic narratives for these sites guarantees that collective grief will be weaponized to entrench division rather than foster reconciliation. Separate ethnic framing of memorials transforms sites meant to honor loss into permanent grievance monuments. Each group’s exclusive centering of its own victimhood erases the universal, cross-community suffering inherent to civil conflict, while implicitly framing other groups’ pain as illegitimate or secondary.
[P1.2] For decades, Northern Ireland’s sectarian murals celebrating only one community’s militia or suffering have served as routine flashpoints for street violence, a direct result of framing collective trauma through exclusively ethnic rather than civic lenses. Even where no immediate violence erupts, separate narratives embed the logic of the conflict into public space, making cross-community empathy impossible by design. In post-war Bosnia, competing Serb, Bosniak, and Croat memorials that frame the conflict as an unprovoked attack on their respective groups have enshrined historical revisionism in public space, blocking the development of a shared civic identity for more than two decades.
[P1.3] A shared civic account of violence, by contrast, does not erase specific group suffering: it contextualizes that suffering within the broader collective harm inflicted on all citizens by civil conflict. This framing avoids the trap of competitive victimhood that plagues segregated narrative projects, while ensuring memorials are legitimate to the entire polity rather than perceived as ethnic property. Post-conflict Sierra Leone’s national peace memorials, which center the shared experience of civilian loss and child soldier recruitment across Mende, Temne, and other ethnic groups rather than separate group-specific narratives, have proven far more effective at building a national identity that transcends the conflict’s fault lines.
[P1.4] These shared narratives do not demand that groups erase their unique trauma, only that they reject the false zero-sum framing of victimhood that fuels future conflict.
