[C1.1] Public trust in a democracy rests on one prior belief: that power changes hands because votes were counted as cast. Legal corruption corrodes faith that government serves everyone fairly. Outright voter fraud corrodes faith that government is chosen by the people at all. The second injury is deeper, because it attacks the mechanism by which every other injury is supposed to be repaired.
[C1.2] That is why fraud does outsized damage even when it is localized. When a North Carolina congressional election had to be thrown out over absentee-ballot fraud, or a London mayoral election was voided by an election court, the lesson for ordinary voters was not limited to one district or one politician. If ballots can be harvested, altered, or invented there, citizens ask why theirs are safe anywhere. A captured regulator makes people distrust an agency; a fraudulent count makes them distrust their neighbors, poll workers, opponents, and the peaceful transfer of power itself.
[C1.3] Capture, by contrast, is diffuse and politically contested. One voter sees a donation or a lobbying meeting as corruption; another sees constituency representation, expertise, or a lawful policy dispute. That disagreement is unhealthy, but it is still processed through trusted procedures: investigations, elections, courts, and reforms can punish excess and rewrite rules precisely because voters believe the next election is real. People who denounce “the swamp” still campaign, donate, and vote, expecting their ballots to decide who drains it.
[C1.4] Fraud removes that expectation. It turns losers into alleged victims of theft, winners into usurpers, and concession — the act that makes democracy self-enforcing — into surrender. Claims about fraud gain force not from abstract cynicism about banks or lobbyists, but from the uniquely explosive idea, made plausible by real cases, that the count itself was stolen. A system can survive voters thinking officials are too close to industry.
[C1.5] It cannot easily survive voters thinking their votes do not determine who governs.
