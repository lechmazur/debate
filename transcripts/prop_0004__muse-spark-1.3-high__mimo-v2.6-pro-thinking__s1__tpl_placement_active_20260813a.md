[P1.1] The five-day, forty-hour week was a compromise for factories a century ago, not a law of nature. Since then output per worker has soared through computers, automation, and logistics, yet the dividend went almost entirely into profits and executive pay while ordinary hours barely budged. Legally resetting full-time to four days with no pay cut simply returns overdue productivity gains as time.
[P1.2] The core economic fear is backwards. Most lost output on the fifth day was never real. Offices are drowning in bloated meetings, inbox churn, presenteeism, and exhaustion-driven mistakes. When firms compress deadlines to four days they cut the waste, not the work: shorter meetings, fewer interruptions, better prioritization.
[P1.3] That is why large pilots in places like Iceland and Britain found revenue and output generally held steady while sick days, quits, and burnout fell sharply. Paying people for results, not seat time, makes business sense.
[P1.4] The human payoff is enormous and urgent. Chronic overwork fuels anxiety, depression, sleep loss, cardiovascular strain, and a caregiving crunch that pushes parents — especially mothers — out of full-time work. An extra guaranteed day off is not leisure trivia; it is time for children, elderly parents, doctors, education, and rest. That keeps experienced workers attached to the labor force, eases hiring shortages, and reduces public health costs.
[P1.5] A shorter week is preventive medicine and family policy at once.
[P1.6] Only law can deliver this. Left voluntary, any firm that offers four days fears being undercut by rivals who grind five. A legal standard solves the coordination trap by moving the overtime threshold together, so the fifth day becomes premium, not expected. The word most matters: hospitals, police, and continuous plants would still cover seven days through rosters — but no individual should be classed as part-time for working 32 hours.
[P1.7] And full pay is non-negotiable, otherwise we create a pay cut disguised as reform and punish the workers who created the productivity.

[C1.2] Start with arithmetic the PRO case cannot erase. Thirty-two hours at the same base pay is a legally enforced 25% jump in hourly labor cost for every employer covered. In a software team, maybe you can delete meetings and reclaim it. In a care home, a hospital ward, a supermarket, a warehouse, or a school, the fifth day's output was real.
[C1.3] Someone must supervise patients, staff the till, and cover the shift. You cannot "prioritize" away eight hours of bedside care. Those employers face three options: hire roughly a quarter more staff in labor markets already short of nurses and carers, cut hours of service to residents and customers, or raise prices in sectors where margins are thin and demand is inelastic. None of those is progress for ordinary people.
[C1.4] The first is impossible at scale, the second is rationing, the third is a pay cut in disguise — for consumers.
[C1.5] Note also who quietly loses. Workers on hourly contracts who want a fifth shift lose legally available overtime and income. The proposition quietly moves the overtime threshold, so the same fifth day now costs premium rates — pricing the extra hours out of existence exactly when household budgets are strained. And "no one classed as part-time at 32 hours" is a labeling promise, not a labor market: employers respond to real hours, and the workers least insulated — young, low-tenure, mothers returning part-time — absorb the adjustment through fewer offers and worse schedules.
[C1.6] Finally, the pilots. They mostly ran in public and professional workplaces, with volunteers and hours that were already partly slack, and no legal pay mandate behind them. Extrapolating from self-selected offices to coverage-heavy, thin-margin employment is precisely the error this motion commits.
[C1.7] The existing model already permits what works. What it forbids is compulsion.
