[P1.1] Land is the scarcest resource in dense cities. Continuing to allocate permanent burial plots in public cemeteries locks that land away forever—for one body, one family, for centuries. That is an indefensible waste when housing shortages, parks, and infrastructure demand every available acre.
[P1.2] Composting and alkaline hydrolysis solve this. Human composting transforms remains into nutrient-rich soil within weeks, so the same space can serve successive generations or return to living use. Alkaline hydrolysis dissolves soft tissue with a gentle, water-based process, leaving sterile liquid and minimal bone remnants that occupy almost no permanent ground and use far less energy than flame cremation. Both eliminate embalming fluids, concrete vaults, and metal caskets that leach toxins and sequester carbon for no social purpose.
[P1.3] Permanent plots create irreversible scarcity. Once a cemetery fills, it stays full; expansion is often impossible or seized from living neighborhoods. Transition multiplies capacity: a composting bed or hydrolysis system processes far more remains per year than a single sealed grave. Memorialization moves to shared gardens, columbaria, or digital records—the same adaptation already accepted after cremation became common.
[P1.4] Cultural preference for earth burial is real but not absolute. Societies once resisted cremation; land pressure reversed that. Dignity does not require eternal private real estate. It requires respectful processing of the body and space for grief that does not bankrupt the city’s future.
[P1.5] Public cemeteries exist for the public, not as permanent private claims. In land-scarce cities, composting or alkaline hydrolysis is the only fair, sustainable allocation of finite ground. Permanent burial privileges habit and the dead over the living and reason.
