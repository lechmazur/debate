[P1.1] Unpaid internships are often sold as experience, but when the internship is not genuinely educational, the “experience” is simply unpaid labor with a better résumé. Governments should heavily restrict them because the harms are not confined to the individual worker: employers can replace paid jobs with free labor, ordinary graduates can be crowded out of entry-level employment, and workers with fewer financial resources are pushed to accept the worst terms.
[P1.2] Consider a small company that needs two temporary office assistants. If both are unpaid, one real vacancy disappears while the company receives full-time work at no wage cost. If the workers are students whose courses award credit, the placement may justify part of that bargain because instruction and assessment replace some of the employer’s training role. If there is no meaningful learning, supervision, or academic credit, that justification largely vanishes.
[P1.3] A heavy restriction, with enforceable definitions and meaningful penalties, would block this substitution and make violations cost more than the wage saved. It should not be an absolute ban on every unpaid placement: genuine educational internships can remain when the worker receives structured learning, qualified supervision, a bounded schedule, and a real connection to a course or credential.
[P1.4] The decisive distinction is between training and displacement. If the employer primarily gains a worker’s productive output, while the supposed trainee merely gets access to a building and a line on a résumé, the arrangement is labor exploitation masquerading as education. Restricting it protects paid work, improves fairness, and keeps “work experience” from becoming a private fee charged in the form of lost wages. The burden should fall on employers to demonstrate that a placement is genuinely educational; ambiguity otherwise lets the most abusive practices flourish.

[C1.1] Heavy restrictions on unpaid, non-educational internships will not deliver the fairer labor market proponents promise; they will slam shut the lowest-stakes on-ramp to professional work for people who lack family connections, wealth, or perfectly linear career paths, while doing nothing to curb the informal privilege that already skews access to good jobs. The core flaw in the pro-side case is its false binary between exploited unpaid labor and protected paid work. For cash-strapped small organizations—local community nonprofits, small arts outlets, scrappy startup teams, grassroots campaign shops—most short unpaid stints are not replacing paid roles: those roles would never exist as paid positions at all, because the organization simply does not have the budget to take on the risk and cost of a formal entry-level hire for work that is often intermittent, project-based, or low-volume.
[C1.2] Heavily restrict those placements, and those opportunities do not convert to paid jobs; they vanish entirely, leaving no path for the retail worker trying to switch to nonprofit outreach, the immigrant building local professional references, or the caregiver re-entering the workforce after a decade out who cannot compete on recent paid experience alone. Adults can weigh whether a short stint is worth the tradeoff to build contacts and portfolio work, rather than being barred from that choice. Worse, rigid, penalty-heavy rules that only carve out formally “educational” placements tied to for-credit coursework will lock out people no longer enrolled in school entirely.
[C1.3] Those opportunities will not disappear; they will move off the books, offered only to friends and family of existing staff through unadvertised channels. The end result is a more exclusionary labor market, not a fairer one, where the only people who get early career shots are those who can afford tuition for credit-bearing placements or already have an inside track to hiring decision-makers.

[P5.4] Unpaid work is not a gift to the applicant; it is a wage payment made by the applicant. Heavy restrictions should stop employers from collecting that payment for ordinary output while preserving education. The burden belongs to the party claiming its placement is educational. If the worker receives instruction, restriction protects the bargain; if the employer receives ordinary output, it prevents displacement.
