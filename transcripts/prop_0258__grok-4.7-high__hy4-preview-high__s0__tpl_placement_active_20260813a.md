[P1.1] A law that ordinary people cannot read is not a law they can obey, contest, or use to judge their representatives. Requiring legislatures to draft statutes in plain language is a demand that government speak to the governed. Some technical precision may be lost. That is the right price for a legal system that stays public rather than becoming professional property.
[P1.2] The rule of law requires that people can discover their duties without an interpreter. Criminal prohibitions, tax obligations, and housing rules all claim to guide conduct in advance. If a careful non-lawyer still cannot tell what is required or forbidden, the statute has failed. Complexity does not prevent injustice; it manufactures it.
[P1.3] Ordinary people break rules they never fairly understood, while sophisticated actors use the same fog to find gaps. Inaccessible precision is precision for the few.
[P1.4] Exact wording is said to close loopholes and limit litigation. Much of what passes for precision does neither. Nested cross-references and hedges like "reasonable" send disputes to court. Plain drafting does not abolish judgment.
[P1.5] It forces the legislature to state the operative rule in words a citizen can follow and to define necessary terms in the same register. Short definitions, examples, and later case law can carry residual detail. What it removes is the habit of hiding policy choices inside syntax.
[P1.6] The distributional stakes decide the question. Opaque statutes tax those who cannot afford counsel and shield drafters who prefer not to be understood. Voters cannot judge a bill they cannot parse. A defendant cannot know the elements of an offense until a lawyer translates them.
[P1.7] Accessibility corrects that imbalance even when a phrase is slightly less airtight.
[P1.8] When precision and public understanding conflict, understanding should prevail. A statute no one outside the profession can use does not govern; it mystifies. Legislatures should be required to write so the people bound by the law can read it.

[C1.1] The proposition is not really a call for clear drafting; it is a mandate to sacrifice precision whenever the two conflict. That is dangerous, because precision is often the only barrier between citizens and official discretion.
[C1.2] My opponent says that when precision and public understanding conflict, understanding should prevail [P1.8]. The risk runs the other way. My opponent also claims 'Opaque statutes tax those who cannot afford counsel' [P1.6], but vaguer text does the opposite: it transfers decisions to police, agencies, and prosecutors, who then exercise discretion with little textual restraint. Detailed limits, not plain words, are what a poor defendant can actually hold the state to.
[C1.3] The word 'plain' is not self-defining. Ordinary usage shifts with context, and forbidding drafters from resolving that context with defined terms, cross-references, and distinctions removes load-bearing clarity. Take a tax credit. A precise version ties eligibility to defined household, support, and income tests.
[C1.4] A plainer rewrite—'if the child lives with you most of the time'—reads better and invites fights over shared custody, temporary absence, and informal support. Ceding precision either sweeps in people the legislature never meant to cover or excludes people the statute was meant to help.
[C1.5] Criminal law sharpens this. Accessible but open words like 'harass' or 'serious', without settled boundaries, invite arbitrary enforcement; the complexity that cabins official discretion protects people who cannot hire lawyers.
[C1.6] None of this opposes clarity as an aspiration; good drafting explains itself where it can. It rejects trading away the tools that make legal rules predictable under pressure. A statute is a command backed by coercion, not a user manual: the test should be whether it yields stable, reviewable answers, not whether it reads like a brochure. Voters already judge laws through summaries and advocates; defendants already need advice.
[C1.7] Requiring loss of precision will produce prettier texts and less real fairness.
