[P1.1] Writing to the brain is not another app or wearable. A step counter reads your body; a stimulating interface changes the organ that makes you you. That difference demands medical control, not a checkout cart.
[P1.2] The brain is excitable, interconnected, and plastic. A small electrical, magnetic, or ultrasound pulse delivered to boost focus, mood, or gaming immersion does not stay in one neat lane. Nearby circuits for seizure threshold, impulse control, sleep, anxiety, and memory can be nudged as well, and repeated nudging can lock in. A consumer cannot feel a shift in plasticity coming, cannot calibrate for their own epilepsy risk, medication interactions, developmental stage, or mental-health history, and cannot uninstall a persistent change the way they delete an app.
[P1.3] Second, a write-capable device is a remote control for minds. Any ordinary consumer gadget will be hacked, spoofed, or monetized — that is the history of every connected product. When the payload is brain stimulation, a breach is not identity theft. It is unwanted mood alteration, impaired judgment behind the wheel, or coerced attention.
[P1.4] App-store consent screens and firmware updates are absurd safeguards for direct neural influence.
[P1.5] Third, consumer markets depend on informed, reversible, individual choice. Brain stimulation fails all three. Effects are opaque and delayed, marketing will sell happiness and productivity to teenagers and distressed adults least able to weigh long-term costs, and social pressure will do the rest once employers, teams, or games expect the boosted brain.
[P1.6] We do this for far less intimate powers. We do not sell prescription drugs, surgical tools, or X-ray machines as lifestyle gadgets because dose and targeting matter and mistakes injure. Stimulating the brain is more intimate still: it acts on agency itself.
[P1.7] Tightly regulated medical use does not abandon the technology. It puts dose, targeting, screening, monitoring, and adverse-event tracking under clinicians and regulators before profit scales harm.

[C1.1] A low-current headband that helps someone focus or fall asleep is not brain surgery, and locking it behind a diagnosis confuses the organ it touches with the risk it actually poses.
[C1.2] Risk scales with intensity, invasiveness, and reversibility — not with the word “brain.” The consumer devices at issue are non-invasive and low-dose, with effects that are modest and temporary compared to drugs, alcohol, sleep deprivation, and intensive training adults already use to alter their mental state. We sell electrical muscle and pain stimulators, bright-light therapy, and psychoactive caffeine over the counter because we cap output, warn against contraindications, and set manufacturing standards. The same proportional toolkit fits here: locked intensity limits, automatic shutoffs, age gates, screening prompts, and adverse-event reporting.
[C1.3] Medical-only restriction also gets autonomy backwards. It tells healthy adults they may not try to improve attention, mood, learning, meditation, or accessibility unless a clinician first labels them patients. That is not protection; it is gatekeeping. It raises prices, creates waiting lists, and reserves enhancement for people who can afford specialist clinics, while students, shift workers, disabled users seeking non-medical communication or control tools, and ordinary experimenters are shut out.
[C1.4] Worse, the restriction would make safety worse, not better. Demand for cognitive help will not disappear because a store cannot sell it. It will move to do-it-yourself rigs, imported headsets, and borrowed devices with no dose limits, instructions, or recall system at all. A legal consumer market is what lets regulators demand testing, trace failures, force updates, and pull bad products.
[C1.5] A clinic-only market hides experimentation and slows the evidence-gathering needed to distinguish real risks from speculation.
[C1.6] Yes, stimulation deserves serious rules — especially on marketing claims, data security, and use on children. But serious rules are not the same as a medical monopoly. Adults routinely make consequential choices about their own minds. They deserve regulated access, honest information, and safe devices, not a prescription for permission to think differently.

A medical channel does the opposite: supervised dosing, contraindication screening that actually happens, and mandatory adverse-event tracking that builds the evidence the other side says it wants.
[P2.6] Autonomy is not the freedom to be remotely stimulated, hacked, or productivity-pressured into rewiring yourself. Protecting the mind's independence is what makes future freedom possible.

[C2.5] That is why the black-market answer backfires. Restriction raises price and friction — conditions that sustain illicit stimulant markets and DIY rigs. A legal, traceable product undercuts both with a safer standardized alternative regulators can test, monitor, and pull. If the fear is teen marketing or employer pressure, ban that marketing and coercion directly, rather than denying every adult a sleep or focus aid.
