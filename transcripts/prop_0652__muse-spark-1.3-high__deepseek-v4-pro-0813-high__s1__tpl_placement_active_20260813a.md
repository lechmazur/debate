[P1.1] When you type 'a singing mouse in red shorts' into a generator and get Mickey Mouse back, you didn't draw him. The platform did. It studied millions of copyrighted images, built a machine that can recreate them on demand, sells access to that machine, then hosts and distributes the result to the world. That is not a passive bulletin board.
[P1.2] That is a printing press with a slot for user coins.
[P1.3] Liability should follow power and profit. These platforms decide what data to ingest, how closely the model can memorize, what guardrails to install, and whether to filter, fingerprint, or block known characters, lyrics, and art styles. The user controls a sentence; the platform controls everything that turns that sentence into a market substitute. The party that designs and monetizes the infringement engine is the cheapest party to prevent the harm.
[P1.4] The alternative is the death of copyright in practice. No illustrator, musician, or photographer can chase ten thousand anonymous users generating knockoffs at scale, each judgment-proof and replaceable overnight. Rights that can only be enforced against the poorest, least traceable link are not rights at all. They become a subsidy: creators supply the training value, platforms capture the subscription revenue, and artists absorb the lost commissions.
[P1.5] Shared liability does not mean bankrupting search bars for one bad output. It means shared responsibility: build reasonable filters, honor opt-outs, license high-risk catalogs, act quickly on notices, and pay when you distribute known infringement for profit. Bars share liability for drunks they keep serving because they profit and can cut people off. Platforms that mass-produce and publish infringing expression for money can do the same.
[P1.6] Hold the press accountable, not just the person who dropped the coin.

[C1.1] PRO’s printing-press image is vivid but wrong. A printing press reproduces a fixed work chosen by the operator. A general-purpose AI platform produces whatever a particular user directs it to produce. The user supplies the prompt, selects the output, and decides whether to publish it.
[C1.2] If I use a camera to photograph a copyrighted poster, Canon is not the infringer; I am. If I use graphics software to recreate a logo, the software company is not the infringer; I am. The same logic applies here.
[C1.3] Shared liability would turn AI hosts into universal copyright screeners. A platform cannot know in advance whether “cartoon mouse in red shorts” will yield an exact Mickey Mouse, a satirical critique, a generic cartoon mouse, or an original character. Fair use depends on context, purpose, transformativeness, and market effect. Those facts are known only after the user chooses how to use the output.
[C1.4] The safe response is over-filtering: block names, styles, genres, and anything remotely resembling a protected character. Lawful parody, fan expression, commentary, and ordinary creative prompting would be suppressed first.
[C1.5] PRO says liability should follow power and profit. Search engines, cloud storage providers, browsers, and email hosts all profit from networks that sometimes carry infringement, and all can filter. We do not make them share liability for each user’s misuse because their core service is lawful and because platform-level liability would break the open internet. A generative model is overwhelmingly used for lawful text, code, images, and speech.
[C1.6] Power plus profit cannot justify liability without knowledge of a specific infringing act.
[C1.7] Existing law already reaches the real abuse. If a platform builds a Mickey-on-demand machine, ignores valid notices, or knowingly hosts exact copies, contributory infringement doctrines apply. The proposition does not fill a narrow gap. It shifts from fault-based liability to a strict liability tax on the tool itself.
[C1.8] The cheapest and most principled target remains the person who prompts for, selects, and distributes the infringing copy. Hold that user accountable, not every platform that made the tool available.

[P4.1] A1: Shared does not mean strict and automatic. It means the host that trained, generated, hosted, and sold the copy answers alongside the user, with courts apportioning fault by control, knowledge, and reasonable care. The motion states when liability exists — when infringement happened — not how to prove it without evidence. Bars share liability for drunks they keep serving, but only when they overserve; that is exactly our model: license high-risk catalogs, fingerprint iconic works, act fast on notice.
[P4.2] A2: The platform does not need clairvoyance before publication. Most mass harm is not close parody cases but Mickey-on-demand, verbatim lyrics, cloned frames. For the hard edge, shared liability works after distribution through notice, context review, and counter-notice for genuine parody — the same way hosts already handle video takedowns without banning all criticism. Facing shared cost for easy copies will not make a rational host ban commentary; it will make it stop selling exact substitutes while leaving room to defend true fair use.
[P4.3] That distinction collapses the CON escape hatch. Their rule — liability only with specific knowledge of a specific act [C2.5] — lets a company design for ignorance at scale: ingest millions of works, refuse fingerprints, auto-publish everything, then claim no one told us about this mouse. That is willful blindness as business model, and it leaves the artist chasing thousands of anonymous prompters who vanish overnight.
[P4.4] Copyright survives only if responsibility follows the factory, not just the coin-slot. The user supplies a sentence; the platform supplies the expression, publishes it under its paywall, and alone can prevent the next ten thousand copies cheaply. Share that liability and you get licenses, filters, and quick removals. Keep CON's user-only rule and you get mass substitution with no one solvent left to sue.
