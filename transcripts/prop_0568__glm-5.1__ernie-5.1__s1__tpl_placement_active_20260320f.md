[C1.1] Mandating cash acceptance for essential goods sounds compassionate, but it actually entrenches the very exclusion it claims to fix while imposing real costs that fall hardest on low‑income shoppers. Retailers already bear the expense of counting, securing, transporting, and insuring cash. Adding a legal requirement to keep cash registers open for groceries and medicine forces them to allocate staff, space, and capital that could otherwise lower prices or expand services. Those costs are passed on to every customer, raising the price of the very essentials the policy seeks to protect.
[C1.2] The unbanked are not left with no options. Federal programs such as SNAP, WIC, and Medicaid already provide electronic benefit cards that work at any major retailer. Prepaid debit cards, mobile wallets linked to bank accounts, and community‑run cash‑to‑card kiosks give cash‑holding households a digital bridge without forcing stores to handle physical currency. In cities where cashless pilots have run, participation in assistance programs actually increased because transactions became faster and more traceable.
[C1.3] Security is another hidden burden. Cash draws robbery, both at the store and in transit. Stores that must keep large cash drawers become targets, endangering employees and shoppers alike. A cashless environment reduces violent theft and frees staff to focus on service rather than vault management.
[C1.4] From a public‑policy perspective, the proposition treats cash as a public good, but cash is a private medium that imposes externalities—counterfeiting, money‑laundering, and lost tax revenue. Requiring its acceptance locks retailers into an inefficient system and slows the rollout of cheaper, faster payment innovations that benefit everyone, including the most vulnerable.
[C1.5] Finally, the regulatory reach is disproportionate. Major retailers already comply with health, safety, and accessibility rules; adding a cash mandate creates a new compliance layer that offers marginal benefit while generating measurable cost. The net effect is higher prices, reduced store viability in low‑margin areas, and a slower march toward the digital inclusion that truly lifts the unbanked out of exclusion.
