[C1.1] The case for social media mistakes breadth for depth. The question is not which platform touches more people; it is which medium most reshaped how democratic politics works. Cable news did more: it built the machinery of modern polarization before social media existed and still supplies its fuel.
[C1.2] Start with the original design. Twenty-four-hour cable news turned politics into continuous, profitable tribal combat. Fox News showed in the mid-1990s that partisan identity could be a business model; MSNBC later built the mirror image on the left. Shouting panels, insulting chyrons, hosts who replace reporting with combat—these trained viewers to experience politics as loyalty to one side and loathing for the other.
[C1.3] That was the product, not an accident.
[C1.4] Social media reach is real but weak evidence of polarization. Most social media use is non-political: friends, sports, entertainment, shopping. Cable's smaller audience is older, habitual, high-turnout, and deliberately watching politics. These are the voters who dominate primaries, fund campaigns, and reward polarization.
[C1.5] A platform that reaches everyone shallowly does not polarize more than one that reaches the decisive political audience deeply.
[C1.6] The deeper flaw is that social media is downstream of cable. The most viral political clips are often cable segments: a hearing moment, a prime-time monologue, a panel clash. The algorithm may carry the clip further, but the ideological frame, trusted host, and outrage script were produced by cable. Remove social media, and cable still beams the same partisan story every night.
[C1.7] Remove cable, and the feed loses much of its most polarizing raw material.
[C1.8] Timing also favors cable. The long rise in partisan sorting, obstruction, and us-versus-them politics in the United States tracks the rise of 24-hour partisan television in the 1990s and 2000s, before algorithmic feeds were mainstream. Social media accelerated an existing polarization; cable created and institutionalized it.
[C1.9] Cable did not merely polarize an audience. It polarized the producers, politicians, and party activists who set the terms for everyone else—and then handed the feed its script.
