[C1.1] The motion asks for more than privacy. It asks to make anonymity a legally guaranteed feature of state money, and that mandate is what I oppose. Confidentiality is a legitimate design goal; a statutory right to untraceable transfers below a fixed ceiling is a different claim, and PRO must defend the mandate, not the value behind it.
[C1.2] The comparison to cash hides what changes when bearer-style money goes digital. Notes are bounded by physics: you stand near me, you carry them, and they move at human speed. Anonymous digital value travels instantly, remotely, and programmably. A legally blessed threshold becomes a structuring toolkit — split proceeds across wallets, cycle them through counterparties, and cash out at whatever points remain.
[C1.3] PRO says deposits create scrutiny, but that assumes the receipts arrive at a bank. They need not: agents, gift instruments, crypto exchanges, and peer markets absorb small anonymous balances without ever creating the tidy trail PRO relies on.
[C1.4] A fixed legal threshold is brittle in ways no legislative number can anticipate. It cannot adjust for inflation, for a wave of elder scams, or for coercion. Once a transfer is anonymous, no one can reverse a scam, return stolen funds, or identify who pressured a frightened payer. The victim is told the system was designed to be unaccountable to them.
[C1.5] The better instrument is strong privacy without mandatory anonymity: strict data minimization, purpose limits written into law, independent oversight, and verification tiers scaled to amount. That protects a worker's lawful contribution from casual snooping while keeping recourse for victims and tools proportionate to real crime. The state may choose to offer anonymity where law and risk allow. It should not be ordered to build a channel it cannot govern.
