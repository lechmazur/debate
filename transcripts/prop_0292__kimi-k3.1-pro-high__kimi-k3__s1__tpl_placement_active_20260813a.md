[C1.1] The harm PRO describes is real, and I won't minimize it. But notice what their case never shows: that a private lawsuit actually reaches the people causing it. Three structural failures.
[C1.2] First, the wrong defendants. The typical perpetrator is an anonymous account, a broke ex-partner, or an uploader beyond the court's jurisdiction. A damages award against such a defendant is a piece of paper. PRO answers that suits can unmask anonymous creators — but existing torts already allow John Doe discovery; no statute can make a foreign troll solvent.
[C1.3] The remedy evaporates exactly where the abuse is worst.
[C1.4] Second, the wrong victims. Civil litigation rewards plaintiffs with money, time, and counsel. The teacher and teenager in PRO's own examples are the least able to fund a multi-year suit and endure discovery into their intimate lives. And filing a complaint republicizes the imagery — the Streisand effect is not a side risk but the mechanism.
[C1.5] A remedy usable mainly by celebrities is not victim control; it is victim triage.
[C1.6] Third, the wrong way to narrow speech. PRO treats "some speech protections" as a tidy marginal cost, but a private right of action chills through the price of defense, not careful judgments. Culpability standards are not self-executing; every sharer, archivist, or satirist must litigate to prove protection. Platforms will respond with automated over-removal, and threat letters will be wielded the way defamation claims already silence critics: a censorship infrastructure leased to anyone with a lawyer, while the actual perpetrator never appears.
[C1.7] None of this means doing nothing. Criminal statutes carry demanding burdens because punishing speech-adjacent conduct should be hard. Takedown duties and targeted injunctions stop circulation without a damages market, and jurisdictions are already extending intimate-image and appropriation law to synthetic media.
[C1.8] PRO trades a general liberty for a remedy that mostly cannot reach the harmed. On their own metric — helping victims — this fails.
