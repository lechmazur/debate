[P1.1] The word that decides this debate is "near-term." Over the next twenty years, a ton of methane traps roughly eighty times the heat of a ton of carbon dioxide, and methane leaves the atmosphere within about a decade. Plug a leak today and the warming avoided shows up within years. Capture a ton of CO2 and you have shaved a sliver off a problem that unfolds over centuries.
[P1.2] On the timescale the motion specifies, these are not comparable currencies.
[P1.3] Now compare what a dollar buys. Leak detection and repair is unglamorous: quarterly surveys with infrared cameras, swapping a worn compressor seal, retrofitting pneumatic controllers, tightening a flange. The abated gas is product, so much of this pays for itself or costs a few dollars per ton of CO2-equivalent. Carbon capture typically runs tens to over a hundred dollars per ton before you add pipelines, injection wells, and decades of monitoring.
[P1.4] Spend the same money and methane repair buys an order of magnitude more avoided warming.
[P1.5] Speed compounds the gap. Oil and gas emissions are dominated by a small share of super-emitting sites: a single malfunctioning tank hatch or unlit flare in the Permian can vent as much gas in a day as hundreds of normal wells. Satellites, aircraft, and ground crews can locate these within months and operators can fix most within days. CCS, by contrast, means multi-year permitting, construction, and a track record of flagship projects capturing well below their promised rates, with much of the captured CO2 sent to push more oil out of the ground.
[P1.6] So the comparison is simple: cheap, fast, proven, and aimed at the most potent short-lived pollutant we emit, versus expensive, slow, underperforming, and aimed at the slowest-acting one. Equivalent dollars, radically unequal near-term returns. Unless CON can show carbon capture delivering comparable warming avoided per dollar within the next two decades, the motion stands.
