[P1.1] Social media accounts are not optional hobby profiles—they are indispensable infrastructure for modern life: small businesses rely on them to reach customers, activists use them to organize, and marginalized communities depend on them for support and connection. A sudden, unexplained ban can destroy a livelihood, silence dissent, and erase a person from public life with zero recourse, all at the hands of unaccountable corporate moderators.
[P1.2] Right now, platforms operate with no required transparency. Automated moderation regularly flags content out of context: a historian posting archival footage of Nazi atrocities gets banned for “hate speech,” a sex educator sharing accurate reproductive health information gets flagged as “adult content.” In 2020, thousands of racial justice organizers were suspended mid-protest for sharing videos of police violence, cutting them off from the coordination tools they needed to advocate for their own safety. These errors are never corrected, because there is no required appeal process, no requirement to share the reason for a ban, and no oversight of platform decision-making.
[P1.3] Requiring notice, appeal, and reasoning for bans is not a radical ask. We already apply this baseline to far less powerful actors: landlords cannot evict tenants without cause and a hearing, employers cannot fire workers without documented justification and review. This requirement would not stop platforms from removing actual harmful content: hate speech, CSAM, incitement to violence, or coordinated harassment would still be removable, as long as platforms tell users what rule they violated, give them a chance to explain context, and provide a meaningful path to overturn erroneous bans.
[P1.4] The burden on platforms is trivial. They already maintain internal systems to track content violations and review appeals for their paid advertising policies; extending that same basic accountability to regular users costs them almost nothing. The upside is enormous: millions of people would no longer live in fear that a single erroneous algorithm flag can erase their business, their community, or their ability to participate in public life without any way to fight back.

[C1.2] The practical effect is slower enforcement against coordinated abuse. Slowing those judgments to satisfy due-process forms simply extends the window in which harm spreads.
[C1.3] Market exit already supplies the functional equivalent of recourse. [C1.4] Once government defines acceptable procedures, it also gains leverage to shape what counts as a valid reason. Platforms anticipating regulatory scrutiny will err on the side of retaining borderline accounts that later prove harmful, or will over-document in ways that chill borderline speech to avoid liability. The result is neither more accountability nor clearer rules, only slower, more politicized moderation.
