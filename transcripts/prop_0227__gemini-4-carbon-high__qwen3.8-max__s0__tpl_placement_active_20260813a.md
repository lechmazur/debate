[P1.1] Ransomware thrives for one simple reason: it pays. Today, when attackers lock a hospital or company network, the victim faces a brutal collective action trap. Paying feels locally rational when it looks cheaper than weeks of downtime, yet it is globally catastrophic. Every payout becomes venture capital for criminal syndicates to buy zero-day exploits, hire developers, and hit ten new targets tomorrow.
[P1.2] Leaving ransoms legal lets today’s panic finance tomorrow’s attacks. Only a legal prohibition breaks this business model at its root.
[P1.3] Critics worry about essential services, like a local clinic or municipal utility, left paralyzed without a decryption key. Yet trusting extortionists is a dangerous illusion: decryptors routinely corrupt data, stolen files get leaked anyway, and payers paint a target on their backs for repeat extortion. Still, banning payments alone would abandon victims in their darkest hour. That is precisely why this policy pairs prohibition with funded victim support.
[P1.4] Instead of wiring millions to syndicates, public funds deploy emergency incident response, rapid system rebuilding, and bridge financing so vital operations survive the outage. Money shifts from subsidizing criminals to rescuing victims.
[P1.5] Nor will a ban merely drive major payments underground. Lucrative ransomware depends on above-board institutional machinery: cyber-insurers reimbursing demands, compliance teams clearing transfers, and corporate boards approving payouts. Make payment illegal, and two incentives flip overnight. First, insurers stop underwriting extortion and executives refuse to risk prison over an IT failure, drying up the massive institutional payouts that sustain syndicates.
[P1.6] Second, knowing extortion is off the table, organizations finally invest in offline backups beforehand, while state relief gives hit victims every reason to report breaches immediately to unlock aid rather than pay in secret. Because major economies supply the vast majority of ransom revenue, bans across key jurisdictions starve the entire ecosystem, crippling the shared criminal tooling and affiliate networks that prey on victims worldwide. Stop funding the arsonists; fund the firefighters instead.
