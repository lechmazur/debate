[P1.1] Housing inflation is not just another price spike; it is the fastest way to turn macroeconomic instability into permanent inequality. When rents and home prices outrun incomes, young workers, renters, and low-income families bear the burden first. They cut consumption, delay family formation, and transfer wealth upward to existing owners. If a central bank tolerates that because it fears a mild recession, it is choosing to protect short-term output at the cost of long-term social and financial stability.
[P1.2] The core of our case is that housing inflation is dangerous precisely because it becomes self-reinforcing. Rising prices feed expectations of further gains, encouraging leverage and speculative demand. Banks loosen underwriting, households take larger mortgages, and the financial system becomes more exposed to a future correction. The longer policy waits, the greater the chance that the correction arrives as a disorderly crash rather than a controlled slowdown.
[P1.3] A recession induced by firm policy is a cost; a housing bubble left to burst is a crisis.
[P1.4] Monetary policy is blunt, but the alternative is worse. Central banks cannot zone land or build homes, yet they can restrain the credit impulse that turns supply shortages into asset manias. If policymakers hesitate until housing inflation disappears through collapse, the recession will be deeper and the damage more persistent: foreclosures, negative equity, bank losses, and frozen credit. Accepting some recession risk now is the lesser evil.
[P1.5] The proposition does not require central banks to crave recession. It requires them to treat housing inflation as a priority when it threatens price stability and financial stability. That priority is justified because housing shocks spread through the whole economy, and because the most vulnerable cannot hedge with property wealth. The harder task is preventing inflation expectations from becoming embedded; once they do, the ultimate recession risk becomes unavoidable.

[C1.1] Housing inflation has one dominant cause in most countries: not enough homes are being built, because of zoning restrictions, permitting delays, and land scarcity. Interest rates do not zone land or approve permits. What raising rates does do is choke off construction financing, which means fewer homes get built, which means the shortage that caused the inflation in the first place gets worse. Central banks prioritizing housing inflation through rate hikes are treating a supply disease with a demand-side drug, and the side effects land on the whole economy.
[C1.2] Consider who actually gets hurt when a central bank engineers a recession to cool housing prices. It is not primarily the speculators PRO worries about. It is the construction worker who loses his job when housing starts collapse. It is the renter who now faces both stagnant wages and layoffs, on top of whatever rent she was already paying.
[C1.3] It is the small business that closes because credit tightened economy-wide, not just in the mortgage market. A recession does not selectively discipline asset speculators; it broadly destroys income and employment, and low-income, low-asset households are the first fired and the last rehired. PRO's own concern for the vulnerable cuts against their proposal.
[C1.4] There is also a serious mismatch of tools. Interest rates are blunt instruments that move the entire economy at once. If housing inflation is a localized, structural problem, the right response is macroprudential: loan-to-value caps, stress tests, tighter underwriting standards, targeted lending rules. These cool speculative credit in the housing sector specifically, without forcing manufacturing, retail, and services into recession to solve a problem centered in real estate.
[C1.5] Finally, note the asymmetry PRO ignores: a policy-induced recession is not a hedge against a crash, it is a guaranteed cost stacked on top of whatever housing correction eventually happens anyway, since monetary tightening rarely deflates housing gently. Broad economic pain now, plus the same fragility later, is a worse bargain than targeted tools that address the actual cause.
