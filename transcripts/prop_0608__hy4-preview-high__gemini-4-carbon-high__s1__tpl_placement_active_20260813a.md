[P1.1] Corporate net-zero should be a claim about changed steel, fuel, and code—not about cheap paper promises from someone else's forest. That is why the law should cap offsets at a small share of a company's footprint and require the overwhelming majority of reductions to happen in its own value chain.
[P1.2] Start with what offsets actually do. In practice many crediting projects are not additional, not permanent, and not leak-proof. A tree plantation may have been planted anyway; a protected forest may avoid logging that shifts next door; stored carbon can burn in a single fire season. When a firm buys these credits and subtracts them from its emissions ledger, it is often booking savings that either would have happened or may not last.
[P1.3] The atmosphere does not care about accounting optimism. It cares about molecules, and molecules only fall if the emitting activity changes.
[P1.4] The second problem is incentive. If a company can satisfy a net-zero pledge by writing checks for cheap credits while its factories, fleets, and suppliers keep expanding, the pledge becomes a license to delay. Voluntary guidelines have not solved this: firms pick the weakest credible standard and disclose around real cuts. A legal ceiling removes the escape hatch.
[P1.5] It forces capital into the unglamorous work: electrifying heat, redesigning products, greening grids, shifting suppliers. Those reductions are slower and costlier, which is exactly why companies will not make them unless law requires it.
[P1.6] The central clash is therefore simple. CON will defend flexibility and cost-effectiveness: let firms cut where it is cheapest, including outside the fence. But flexibility is not the goal. Climate stability depends on decarbonizing the real economy, not on relocating the ledger.
[P1.7] A small residual allowance—roughly one-tenth—still funds hard-to-abate gaps and removals, while the mandated majority guarantees that net-zero means the company changed its business, not just its paperwork.

A ton of carbon kept out of the sky by capturing landfill methane or retiring coal in an emerging grid cools the planet identically to a ton cut inside a firm’s own facilities, often years sooner and at far greater scale per dollar.
[C1.2] [P1.2] attacks non-additional, flammable forest projects as though "external" meant "fake." That conflates credit quality with credit quantity. Notice the term is net-zero, not gross-zero: it literally means balancing remaining emissions with equivalent mitigation. If bad credits cheat the atmosphere, the law should ban low-integrity offsets, enforce permanence, and require firms to disclose gross emissions separately from credits, not outlaw genuine external reductions just because they cross a legal boundary.
[C1.3] Worse, capping offsets at "roughly one-tenth" [P1.7] collides with industrial reality. A software firm’s footprint looks nothing like an airline’s, a cement producer’s, or a manufacturer’s spread across thousands of shared overseas suppliers and fossil-heavy grids it cannot unilaterally rebuild. Across hard-to-abate sectors and fragmented Scope 3 value chains, commercial in-house substitutes cannot yet eliminate most emissions. Blocking those firms from buying verified external mitigation will not conjure cheap synthetic jet fuel into existence; it chokes off private climate finance to developing economies and starves nascent engineered carbon-removal technologies of the early demand needed to drive down costs.
[C1.4] Finally, [P1.5] mistakes a label rule for an emissions mandate. Making a "net-zero" claim is voluntary. Threatened with legal liability unless they achieve near-total internal cuts that technology or supplier leverage cannot deliver, rational firms will not spend themselves into insolvency. They will simply drop net-zero pledges and stop buying credits, without burning a liter less fuel.
[C1.5] Under [P1.3]’s own molecule test, trading real external reductions for corporate retreat leaves the planet hotter just to purify a label.
