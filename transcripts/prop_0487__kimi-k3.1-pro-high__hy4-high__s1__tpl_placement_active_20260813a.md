[P1.1] A government’s first duty is to prevent harms from which society may never recover. Asteroid impacts are rare on a human timescale, but their consequences can be civilizational, and no private actor has the responsibility or incentive to maintain a planetary response capability. The correct standard is therefore not “How many people die in an average year?” It is “Should we remain unable to act when action is possible?”
[P1.2] Research alone leaves that answer as no. Telescopes can provide warning; they cannot provide deflection. The 2013 Chelyabinsk airburst injured roughly 1,500 people without any advance warning, while Tunguska devastated a vast forest. Neither was a civilization-ending event. Both expose the folly of equating low frequency with preparedness.
[P1.3] A small nudge delivered early can be far easier than a desperate, high-energy intervention late, yet the early option depends on hardware, mission expertise, launch arrangements, and decision procedures that cannot be invented overnight.
[P1.4] We also no longer need to speculate about whether deflection is physically possible. NASA’s DART mission demonstrated that a spacecraft impact could alter an asteroid’s orbit. But a successful experiment is not a standing capability, just as a prototype vaccine is not a vaccination system. Permanent infrastructure means sustained tracking, maintained and periodically tested deflection technology, practiced mission teams, compatible launch plans, and clear international protocols.
[P1.5] Its components must survive budget cycles and be replaced as technology changes. Otherwise, knowledge accumulates on paper while operational readiness decays.
[P1.6] This is proportionate insurance, not panic spending. Deployment can be phased, civilian-led, internationally supervised, and scaled according to the threat; it need not mean hair-trigger weapons waiting in orbit. It also generates scientific and spaceflight benefits, but its core justification stands without them: preserving the option to prevent irreversible catastrophe.
[P1.7] The opposition must defend a strange gamble—keep studying the danger, but accept institutional unpreparedness until a deadline appears. Warning without the maintained capacity to respond is not planetary defense. It is planetary observation. Governments should build the missing capability before the sky supplies the deadline.
