[P1.1] Consent alone cannot make a transaction ethical, for three reasons it can never capture on its own. First, formal consent can exist alongside severe structural coercion that strips the “choice” of any moral weight. A worker who “consents” to take a job with unprotected toxic exposure because the only alternative is homelessness and their child needs medication does not face a gun to the head, but the transaction exploits power imbalance to push life-shortening harm no fair framework would call ethical just because someone signed a form.
[P1.2] No one calls a mugging ethical because a victim “chose” to hand over a wallet instead of being stabbed; the logic does not shift when coercion is structural, as unequal bargaining power strips choice of the freedom consent is meant to guarantee. Second, even fully uncoerced, informed consent between two parties cannot waive harms that spill over to people who never had a chance to agree. If a wealthy outsider pays a riverside landowner a sum to dump untreated mining waste upstream that poisons the drinking water for an entire downstream town, the landowner’s enthusiastic consent does not erase the harm to every neighbor who had no seat at the bargaining table.
[P1.3] Consent only tracks the preferences of the people signing off, not the silent third parties who bear the brunt of the deal. Third, some transactions erode the conditions that make meaningful consent possible long-term. A predatory lender who secures signed “consent” for a 400% annual interest loan a borrower can never repay traps them in a cycle of debt that strips away future autonomy, turning a one-time procedural yes into a permanent loss of the capacity to make free choices later.
[P1.4] Consent is a necessary guardrail for ethical exchange, but it is never, on its own, enough.

When we say “consent alone,” we mean exactly that: no hidden coercion, no material deception, no incapacity, and no silent bystanders left to absorb the costs. Under that definition, consent is not merely a guardrail alongside other criteria; it is the complete criterion for ethical exchange, because it is the only procedure that treats each person as the sole author of their own obligations.
[C1.2] Take the worker who accepts toxic exposure because the only alternative is homelessness for a child. That is not genuine consent; the background conditions of desperation negate the voluntariness consent requires. The riverside dumping deal is unethical not because consent is insufficient, but because the downstream town never consented—and their consent is part of the “alone” that the proposition dismisses. The 400% loan is predatory because the borrower’s capacity to understand and refuse was compromised by the structure of the offer.
[C1.3] In each case, the wrongness traces to a missing element of consent, not to a surplus of it. If consent were truly present—voluntary, informed, and inclusive of all affected parties—none of these harms would exist.
[C1.4] Some may worry that consensual deals can still produce harsh outcomes, like a poor person selling a kidney. But if the choice is truly voluntary, informed, and free of harm to non-consenting third parties, then respecting it is the only ethical option; overriding it would be paternalism that violates autonomy. Ethics in transactions is about respecting persons as authors of their own lives. Consent, properly understood, is the only mechanism that does this.
[C1.5] The claim that consent alone is not enough attacks a straw man. Real consent alone is enough.
