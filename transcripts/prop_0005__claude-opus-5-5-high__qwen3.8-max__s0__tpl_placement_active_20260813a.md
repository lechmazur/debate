Millions of people never reach a doctor because they do not recognize their symptoms, feel ashamed, or have been told to live with pain. A mass-media ad can be the first lawful nudge that says: this condition is real, there is a treatment, ask a professional.
[C1.2] For depression, migraine, erectile dysfunction, or overactive bladder, that nudge can break silence and stigma. Banning it does not make patients more rational; it makes them quieter and sicker.
[C1.3] Prescription status does not mean patients should be kept ignorant. It means the final decision is still mediated by a clinician. Direct-to-consumer advertising does not remove that gatekeeper: no one walks out of a television commercial with a prescription. It gives patients questions they might never have known to ask.
[C1.4] A doctor can still say no, choose a generic, recommend lifestyle change, or order tests. The exam room remains the decision point; the ad only gets the patient there.
[C1.5] Prohibition also pushes information into worse channels. If regulated television and print ads disappear, people will still seek answers online, where claims are less accountable, risk warnings are absent, and influencers or counterfeit sellers fill the vacuum. Regulated mass-media advertising is imperfect, but it is one of the few commercial speech channels that can be required to disclose risks and avoid false promises. Even if some ads oversell new drugs, the remedy is targeted enforcement against misleading claims, mandatory plain-language risk summaries, and limits on emotional manipulation—not a total ban.
[C1.6] The proposition treats patients as fragile consumers who must be protected from information. We should treat them as adults who deserve prompts, doctors who can handle questions, and a system that uses truthful advertising to identify untreated illness rather than suppress it. The motion is overbroad, paternalistic, and dangerous.
