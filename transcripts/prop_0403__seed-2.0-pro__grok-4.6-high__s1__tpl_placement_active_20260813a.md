[P1.1] When the public puts up hundreds of millions for a stadium, that is not a gift. It is a contract. Nobody hands you that kind of money so you can pack the trucks and leave three years later.
[P1.2] Public subsidies do not just pay for concrete. Cities tear down neighbourhoods, redirect school funding, defer road repairs, all on the explicit promise that this team will be the community's anchor for generations. When an owner takes that money then relocates at the first better offer, it is not just bad business—it is theft by loophole. We have all watched this play out again and again: mid-market cities approve tax hikes, build shiny new arenas, then get abandoned the second a richer market dangles a sweeter deal.
[P1.3] This rule does not ban relocation. It just says if you took major public funds, you do not get to unilaterally break the deal. A binding community approval process is not some unreasonable barrier. It is the most basic standard of accountability for anyone spending public money.
[P1.4] If the team has a good, fair reason to leave, they can make that case and win approval. If they cannot win that argument, they should not be allowed to go.
[P1.5] Opponents will invoke owner rights and business flexibility. Those rights end the second you cashed the public's cheque. There is no other area of public spending where we hand over hundreds of millions with zero enforceable conditions on the service we were actually paying for. No other contractor gets to take the money and walk away without delivering what was promised.
[P1.6] This is not radical regulation. This is just enforcing the most basic terms of the deal that everyone pretended existed when they asked for the tax increase.

[C1.1] A subsidy is not a deed. When a city writes a check for a stadium, it buys concrete, lease terms, and whatever exit penalties it bothered to negotiate. It does not buy the franchise or a veto over whether that business can move.
[C1.2] The proposition treats public money as if it converted a private club into a public utility. That is the error that should decide this debate. Cities already have the tool they claim to want: a contract. Long leases, clawbacks, exit fees, repayment of remaining debt—those are enforceable.
[C1.3] A binding community approval process is not contract enforcement. It is a political hold-up after the ink is dry.
[C1.4] Who is the community? The city that paid, the county that didn't, the suburbs that fill the seats? What majority? Who drafts the question?
[C1.5] That process will not be a sober hearing on whether the owner has a fair reason to leave. It will be a campaign. A referendum is not a court. It is a second negotiation with no duty to be fair.
[C1.6] Officials will extract another subsidy as the price of yes. Capital will be trapped in markets that no longer support it.
[C1.7] The stadium was built. The games were played. What was never purchased is perpetual occupancy. Trapping a franchise does not restore demolished neighbourhoods or school budgets.
[C1.8] It leaves a weaker product and a standing invitation to pay again. Factories that took tax breaks can still close. Sports should not be the exception.
[C1.9] If an owner breaches a lease, collect the money. If a city signed a sloppy deal, that is failed bargaining, not a warrant for a new political veto. Mobility disciplines cities that overpay and owners who overpromise. Replace that with a referendum and you do not get accountability.
[C1.10] You get a more expensive cage.

[P2.2] The factory analogy does not hold. When a manufacturing plant takes public incentives, it employs thousands locally, generates decades of payroll and property tax, and leaves behind usable industrial land. A sports team takes ten times as much public money, creates barely one hundred permanent local jobs, siphons almost all ticket revenue out of the community, and leaves behind a single-purpose stadium that is worthless without them. Everyone knows this when they vote for the subsidy.
[P2.3] The public is not paying for concrete. They are paying for the team to stay. What is called political hold up is far better than the status quo hold up we see now: owners negotiating secret out-of-state deals, announcing relocation after midnight, and skipping town before anyone can react. A public approval vote will never be perfect.
[P2.4] But it is infinitely more fair than letting one wealthy owner unilaterally break the promise the entire subsidy was built upon. If written leases were really the sufficient protection opponents claim, team owners would not spend millions lobbying to stop this exact rule from passing. They know exactly what they are getting away with.
