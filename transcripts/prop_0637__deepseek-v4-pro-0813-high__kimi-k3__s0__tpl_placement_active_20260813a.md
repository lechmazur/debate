[P1.1] Blocking by-right density near transit does not prevent displacement; it displaces displacement. In a high-demand rail neighborhood, refusing to let new homes be built means higher-income arrivals compete for the existing stock. Older apartments get renovated, rents climb, and long-time tenants are pushed to cheaper peripheries with worse transit. The same pressure then appears in the next neighborhood.
[P1.2] This is not stability—it is a rolling displacement crisis moved out of sight.
[P1.3] By-right rules matter because discretionary review turns housing production into a war of attrition. Each project near transit must survive years of hearings, design revisions, and veto points. That delay is not neutral: while supply is frozen, scarcity worsens and land values rise, making demolition or conversion of older occupied buildings more profitable. Legalizing density by right shortens that speculative window and lets new homes reach the market before displacement pressure becomes irreversible.
[P1.4] The displacement concern is real, but the answer is targeted protection, not banning the rule. Municipalities can pair by-right density with tenant right to counsel, right of first refusal, replacement units, anti-harassment protections, and affordable set-asides in larger buildings. The motion does not force a choice between housing supply and tenant protection; it rejects the false choice that excluding new neighbors is the only way to protect current ones.
[P1.5] Above all, near transit is exactly where new density belongs. A new home next to frequent rail or bus service means fewer car-dependent commutes, lower emissions, less pressure to pave fringe land, and access to jobs for people who cannot afford to drive everywhere. If density is refused there, growth does not vanish; it moves to places with worse infrastructure and higher total displacement. Short-term local risk must be weighed against the long-term harm of deepening scarcity in the neighborhoods best able to absorb growth.
[P1.6] That comparison favors allowing density by right.

Mandatory inclusionary rules attached to by-right density are stronger than a veto that may simply stop housing.
[P2.6] Finally, the motion is not “density without protection.” It says municipalities should allow density by right; it does not require abandoning mitigation.
