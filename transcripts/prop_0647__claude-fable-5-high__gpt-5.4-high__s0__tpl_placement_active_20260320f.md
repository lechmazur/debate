[P1.2] First, widening doesn't work on its own terms. Houston's Katy Freeway was expanded to roughly two dozen lanes at enormous cost; travel times soon worsened again. Widening is the only infrastructure investment we keep repeating despite knowing it fails to deliver its stated benefit.
[P1.3] Second, removal does work, and the evidence is real-world, not theoretical. San Francisco demolished the earthquake-damaged Embarcadero Freeway and got a thriving waterfront; predicted gridlock never materialized because traffic partly evaporates, redistributing across the grid, transit, and changed trip patterns. Seoul tore out an elevated highway to restore the Cheonggyecheon stream, and the city kept moving. Portland, Rochester, and Milwaukee tell similar stories: land values rise, neighborhoods reconnect, and the carmageddon never comes.
[P1.4] Third, the economics overwhelmingly favor removal. Urban freeways occupy the most valuable land a city owns while paying no taxes, generating noise, asthma-causing pollution, and severed neighborhoods—usually the same Black and working-class neighborhoods bulldozed to build them. [P1.5] Note what the motion asks: not that every freeway be removed, but that removal should be the more frequent choice than widening. Today widenings outnumber removals enormously.
