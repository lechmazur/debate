[P1.1] Studios should spend more on mid-budget original movies because the industry's long-term viability depends on creating new stories rather than endlessly recycling old ones. Franchise sequels may deliver more predictable box-office returns in the short term, yet that predictability becomes a trap. Audiences grow bored with familiar heroes, formulas, and spectacle, producing visible fatigue: softer later entries, declining cultural excitement, and thinner theatrical habits outside surefire event films.
[P1.2] Mid-budget originals reverse the decay. Their costs allow studios to absorb misses without wrecking a slate, while a single hit yields outsized percentage returns, new intellectual property, and fresh stars or directors. Films such as Get Out or A Quiet Place began as contained originals, generated massive profits, and quickly expanded into franchises or cultural landmarks. Even standalone successes build prestige and talent pipelines that strengthen everything a studio releases, including the next franchise installment.
[P1.3] Without sustained investment in this range, Hollywood cannibalizes itself. Safe franchise bets only stay safe while the creative engine that invents characters, worlds, and directors still functions. Starve mid-budget originals and you eventually lose the audiences who treat cinema as more than brand maintenance, along with the artists who make blockbusters worth watching. Spending more on originals is therefore not risk for its own sake; it is the disciplined hedge that creates tomorrow's predictable earners rather than milking today's until they dry up.
[P1.4] Short-term certainty is worthless if the entire theatrical model loses vitality and relevance.

[C1.1] The claim that studios must spend more on mid-budget originals treats box office as the only ledger that matters. It isn't. Marketing and distribution for a theatrical release routinely cost as much as the production budget itself, which means a "mid-budget" original at fifty million dollars in production can require another fifty million to open wide — and if it flops, that capital is gone with nothing to show for it. A franchise sequel absorbs the same marketing spend but starts with built-in awareness, merchandising, streaming residuals, and theme-park or licensing tie-ins that recoup losses even when reviews are mediocre.
[C1.2] That is not laziness; it is prudent capital allocation under real financial constraints.
[C1.3] PRO's examples — Get Out, A Quiet Place — are survivorship bias dressed up as strategy. For every original that breaks out, there are dozens of mid-budget theatrical originals that quietly vanished after one weekend, and studios don't get to know in advance which is which. Asking a public company to deliberately choose the statistically worse bet, sequel after sequel, film after film, "even if" the safer bet stays safer, is asking shareholders to subsidize speculative art with certain money.
[C1.4] That's not hedging; that's gambling with someone else's return.
[C1.5] The creative pipeline argument also ignores where original storytelling actually lives now. Streaming platforms, not theatrical mid-budget slots, have become the home for original ideas, new directors, and untested IP, at a fraction of theatrical marketing risk. Studios already get the "creative engine" benefit PRO wants, just through a cheaper channel. Theatrical mid-budget originals aren't the only way to cultivate talent or story diversity — they're simply the most expensive way to do it in a shrinking theatrical window where audiences have shown, repeatedly and expensively, that only event-scale films reliably pull them off the couch.
[C1.6] Predictability isn't a trap here. It's the only thing keeping the model solvent enough to fund anything else.
