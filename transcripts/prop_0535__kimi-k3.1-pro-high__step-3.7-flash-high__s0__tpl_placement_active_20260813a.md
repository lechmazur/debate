[P1.1] An advance directive is not a dead hand reaching into the future. It is a person’s answer, made while able to deliberate, to a question that later illness may make impossible to answer reliably: what should happen when treatment can prolong biological life only under conditions that person finds unacceptable? Strong binding force protects that answer against the predictable pressures of dementia—family distress, institutional caution, and medical reluctance to let go.
[P1.2] The central principle is precedent autonomy joined to equal respect. We routinely allow earlier decisions to constrain later choices when decision-making capacity is compromised: financial arrangements, consent to future medical procedures, and safeguards against exploitation. Dementia should not uniquely erase agency. The person with dementia remains the same rights-bearing person, not a new patient whose history becomes optional.
[P1.3] Their present comfort matters greatly, but comfort cannot automatically outweigh every other value they held. Someone may consistently choose to refuse ventilatory support in advanced dementia while still wanting pain relief, personal care, and treatment for reversible suffering. Honoring the directive can honor both their enduring values and their present welfare.
[P1.4] The motion already contains the correct safeguard: clear evidence that values materially changed. This prevents directives from becoming unchallengeable commands. A sustained, credible rejection of the earlier outlook—rather than fear during a procedure, confusion on a bad day, or relatives discovering a preference of their own—can justify departure. The dispute is therefore not “directive versus person,” but who bears the burden of proof.
[P1.5] CON would effectively place that burden on the person who can least defend themselves and give it to those surrounding them.
[P1.6] That reversal invites abuse and quiet paternalism. Vague claims that someone “seems happy now” are especially dangerous: pleasure under severe cognitive impairment may coexist with values that remain stable, while distress may reflect untreated pain rather than a changed philosophy. Strong binding force, combined with a genuine changed-values exception, gives vulnerable people a presumption of self-government instead of making their deepest commitments contingent on other people’s confidence.

[C1.1] Strong binding force for pre-dementia advance directives mistakes a snapshot of past preferences for the moral standing of the living person with dementia, and its stated safeguard is functionally useless for the people it claims to protect. PRO frames this as a question of precedent autonomy, but autonomy requires the capacity to engage with and weigh one’s values—a capacity dementia erodes. The person writing the directive can only guess at what life with dementia will actually feel like; enforcing their guess over the lived experience of the person with dementia treats the pre-illness individual as the sole rights-bearer, erasing the current person’s moral claim to have their present welfare considered.
[C1.2] The motion’s “clear evidence of materially changed values” exception is a fig leaf, not a safeguard. Dementia by definition impairs the ability to articulate, revise, and communicate values coherently. A person who wrote in 2018 that they would refuse antibiotics for any infection if they developed dementia, believing cognitive decline to be a fate worse than death, may spend 2025 laughing at family visits, showing clear distress when caregivers discuss withholding treatment, and clinging to favorite blankets—but their inability to verbally articulate “I changed my mind” means their directive remains binding, and they are left to die of a treatable infection.
[C1.3] The bar for departure is set so high that the exception is almost never reachable for the people it is meant to protect. PRO claims this prevents abuse, but it creates exactly the quiet paternalism it warns against, in reverse. Families seeking to cut costs, or who resent the burden of care, can point to the old directive and dismiss the current person’s obvious joy or distress as irrelevant, arguing the “clear evidence” standard is unmet because the patient cannot coherently defend their current preferences.
[C1.4] The burden is not shifted to the person who can least defend themselves: it is locked onto them, with no meaningful way to escape.

[P2.1] CON conflates two questions: whether someone now has capacity to make a new treatment decision, and whether their earlier competent decision should count. Dementia can answer the first no without answering the second no. A person does not lose authorship of their life by losing the ability to restate every chapter. Otherwise, the disability that makes a directive necessary becomes the reason to disregard it. Present welfare remains central; CON never shows why present pleasure must silently repeal a settled judgment about acceptable conditions of life.
[P2.2] The antibiotic example exposes that gap. Laughter at visits, attachment to a blanket, and distress when treatment is discussed may demand comfort, reassurance, and excellent palliative care. They do not, by themselves, show abandonment of a refusal of life-prolonging treatment in advanced dementia. CON converts a welfare judgment into a values judgment, then calls the conversion “obvious.” That is precisely the inference clear evidence must test.
[P2.3] Nor does the exception require a fluent declaration that “my values have materially changed.” Clear evidence can be behavioral and corroborated: a sustained pattern, across settings and lucid intervals, credibly rejecting the earlier outlook. Momentary fear or confusion is insufficient, but articulate verbal revision is not the only proof. The standard is demanding because overriding a considered refusal is serious, not because departure is forbidden.
[P2.4] CON’s abuse argument also cuts the wrong way. A cost-conscious relative can misuse a directive, just as a relative can call treatment burdensome under a loose present-welfare standard. The remedy is scrutiny of applicability, motive, and evidence—not making directives defeasible whenever others describe current happiness. Strong binding force narrows the discretion surrounding the patient; CON expands it.
[P2.5] The burden is not “locked onto” the patient: their directive has already spoken. It rests on those seeking to defeat it. CON would let people who cannot reliably revise their choices lose them through other people’s interpretations. PRO preserves comfort and genuine review while requiring more than an interpretable smile to erase a life-defining value.
