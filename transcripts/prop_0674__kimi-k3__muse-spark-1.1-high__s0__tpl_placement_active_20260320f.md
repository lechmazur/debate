[P1.1] Deterrence is not bought with signatures; it is bought with demonstrated cost. That fact decides this debate. The question is not which path ends the fighting fastest — it is which path makes the next Russian war less likely. On that metric, sustained aid wins decisively.
[P1.2] Start with the track record. Georgia in 2008 met a muted response; Crimea in 2014 met sanctions but no serious arming of Ukraine; the Minsk agreements froze the war on paper while Moscow rebuilt and returned for the whole country in 2022. Each concession was read in the Kremlin as a price signal — aggression is affordable. An immediate settlement now, with Russian troops on Ukrainian soil, repeats that lesson at higher stakes: invade, dig in, wait for the West to tire, keep what you took.
[P1.3] Now consider what actually shapes that calculus. Deterrence is capability times resolve, and aid supplies both. Every month Ukraine holds the line with Western weapons, the expected return on expansion falls — not only for Russia's next move in Ukraine, but for anyone watching from Moldova to the Baltics to Beijing. Aggression that visibly fails, and visibly costs, is aggression that does not get repeated.
[P1.4] A settlement under fire demonstrates the opposite: that force still pays. And this deterrent fights in Ukrainian hands, not under NATO flags; a rearmed, victorious Russia on the alliance's border is the real escalation risk.
[P1.5] The negotiated alternative is not peace; it is an intermission. Russia's economy is on a war footing and its stated aims extend well beyond the land it currently holds. A deal signed without enforcement power is a rearmament window — precisely what Minsk became. The only security guarantee Moscow has ever respected is a defended border.
[P1.6] So the choice is stark: arm the deterrent now, or negotiate a pause that schedules the next invasion. One path makes wider aggression costlier every year it continues. The other advertises that it pays.
