[C1.1] “Skin in the game” puts skin in the wrong place. A university provides instruction; it does not underwrite a federal loan, set interest rates, control recessions, employ graduates, or choose repayment plans. Repeated poor earnings-to-debt ratios still do not isolate school fault: default is a repayment event shaped by health shocks, family obligations, regional wages, graduate study, and post-school choices. Treating it as a refundable defect in the program turns a degree into a warranty on the labor market.
[C1.2] The rule would punish the students it claims to protect. A graduate with family savings rarely defaults; a first-generation graduate with the same salary and no cushion can. If schools pay when that borrower defaults, the cheap response is not better teaching but admitting fewer low-income students, steering them from modest-wage fields, demanding family resources, or raising prices to build reserves. That is not value; it is risk selection.
[C1.3] Many low-paying programs are not scams. Teaching, social work, counseling, public health, ministry, and local-service work can have poor earnings relative to debt while producing real public value. An earnings-to-debt penalty tells a public university that accounting is safer than elementary education in a rural district. PRO’s repeated patterns are information, but they are also incentives; this one points away from access and public-service labor.
[C1.4] The feedback is blunt and late. Cohorts borrow under one economy, graduate under another, and default years later; administrators cannot reteach them, reset wages, or rewrite loan terms retroactively. A small charge is paperwork folded into tuition. A large charge is a reason to abandon whole categories of students.
[C1.5] Neither gives timely educational guidance.
[C1.6] PRO dismisses transparency and anti-fraud rules as slow. Slow is better than pricing noise retroactively. Accountability belongs where control exists: clear disclosures, outcome data, accreditation review, and discipline for deception or educational failure. Making universities co-insure federal loan losses is not consumer protection; it is tuition-funded insurance against risks universities do not control.
