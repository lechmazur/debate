[P1.1] Live face recognition on AR glasses is not a sharper pair of eyes. It turns a crowd into a searchable database at the speed of a glance. In a station, protest, or shop, a passerby can be named, linked to online traces, and sorted by reputation, workplace, or perceived risk without knowing the query occurred. Most public life should keep practical obscurity; only tightly accountable professional uses should cross that line.
[P1.2] The central harm is asymmetry. Ordinary seeing is reciprocal and limited: I may notice you, but I do not instantly get a dossier, and you can usually tell I am staring. AR recognition is silent, scalable, and portable. The wearer gets certainty or its appearance; the bystander gets no consent, notice, correction, or realistic exit from public space.
[P1.3] A stranger on a train who identifies a commuter can follow, message, harass, or profile her before she knows anything happened. At a rally, it can chill lawful speech by making attendance a durable record of association. A mistaken match with a banned customer or suspect becomes immediate exclusion because the overlay looks authoritative.
[P1.4] Consent rules and misuse penalties do not solve this. The people identified are not the users who accepted an app's terms; they are everyone in the frame. Opt-out codes are useless to someone who never sees the device. Punishing misuse cannot restore anonymity or undo an approach, denial of service, or threat that already occurred.
[P1.5] A public default ban fits the actual risk.
[P1.6] Professional exceptions prove the distinction. An officer under specific lawful authorization, a clinician in controlled intake, or a security worker checking a defined access list can be limited by necessity, scope, logs, training, supervision, and sanctions. Ordinary wearers cannot. Convenience, networking, and "remembering names" are thin interests beside a default rule allowing any stranger to make any passerby continuously readable.
[P1.7] Public freedom depends on being visible without being automatically identified.
