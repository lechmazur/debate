[P1.1] Every year, thousands of foreign-trained doctors, engineers, and nurses arrive in countries desperately short of exactly those skills — and then spend years driving taxis, stacking shelves, or re-taking exams they already passed, because their credentials sit in a queue. Faster recognition is the fix.
[P1.2] Start with the stakes. Underemployment of skilled immigrants is well documented: a large share of skilled migrants work far below their qualifications. The cost falls everywhere — on the migrant, on patients waiting for care, on a treasury that loses the taxes of a physician and gains the taxes of a delivery driver. Labor-market testing does nothing about this, because the bottleneck in licensed professions is not employer demand; hospitals are begging for staff.
[P1.3] The bottleneck is the license.
[P1.4] Second, labor-market testing is a weak instrument for its own stated goal. It asks employers to advertise jobs they have already decided to fill, generating months of delay and paperwork theater that everyone involved knows how to game. It measures whether an ad was posted, not whether a worker is scarce. And in credential-gated fields it is redundant by design: where a license is required, the supply of qualified locals is already exhausted.
[P1.5] Third — and this is the comparison that decides the debate — faster recognition protects domestic workers better than testing does. When a qualified engineer is blocked from engineering, she does not disappear; she competes in the low-wage market instead, pressing down wages exactly where workers are most exposed. Recognizing her credential moves her out of that competition and into a shortage occupation at a regulated standard. Faster recognition is not lower standards: it is the same assessment done in months, not years, with bridging where gaps exist.
[P1.6] The choice is between a policy that converts wasted talent into licensed professionals and one that certifies a job ad ran. One matches skills to needs; the other administers scarcity. Prioritize recognition.
