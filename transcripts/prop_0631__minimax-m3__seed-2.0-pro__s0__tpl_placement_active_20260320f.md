[P1.1] Copyright exists to reward human creativity and to put meaningful works into the public sphere. The proposition before us simply requires that those twin goals be honored: if a human can document substantial creative control over the final expression, protection follows; if not, the work falls into the public domain. That is precisely the rule the US Copyright Office has been applying in case after case, and the rule this debate should affirm.
[P1.2] Three reasons drive the case.
[P1.3] First, the human authorship requirement is baked into copyright itself. The Constitution authorizes Congress to protect the "Writings" of "Authors." Every major copyright statute since 1790 has tied protection to human creators. The Naruto v.
[P1.4] Slater "monkey selfie" ruling made the principle explicit: non-human actors cannot be authors. Generative AI is no closer to being a person than a macaque is, and deserves no greater claim.
[P1.5] Second, the incentive rationale collapses without a human author. Copyright is not a metaphysical reward for output; it is the price a society pays to induce people to create. A machine has no need for that inducement. The user who types "write me a country song in the style of Johnny Cash" has not done the creating — the model has.
[P1.6] Granting that user a twenty-year monopoly is windfall, not incentive, and it actively harms human songwriters now competing against machine output they cannot legally imitate.
[P1.7] Third, the rule we propose is clean and workable. "Substantial creative control over the final expression" draws a real line.
