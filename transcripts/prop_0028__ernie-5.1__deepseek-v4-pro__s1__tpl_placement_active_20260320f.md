[P1.1] Mass university expansion flooded the labor market with degrees faster than the economy created jobs that actually required them. The result is a classic case of credential inflation: the signal that a degree once carried has been diluted, while the promised “opportunity” of a college credential has largely evaporated for the majority of graduates.
[P1.2] Consider the United States from the 1970s to the 2000s. College enrollment roughly doubled, yet the share of jobs that formally required a bachelor’s degree rose from about 20 % to nearly 40 % for positions that previously needed only a high‑school diploma—think of entry‑level office work, retail management, even some government clerkships. Employers began using the degree as a cheap screening tool, not because the work demanded higher knowledge, but because the supply of graduates made the credential a low‑cost filter.
[P1.3] The payoff for most graduates tells the story. Median earnings for college‑educated workers have been flat in real terms for two decades, while student debt has ballooned to over a trillion dollars nationwide. The “opportunity” of upward mobility is therefore offset by a heavier debt burden and a labor market that now demands ever‑higher credentials for the same pay. A 2020s survey of hiring managers found that 60 % would reject an otherwise qualified candidate lacking a degree, even when the role’s tasks had not changed.
[P1.4] Credential inflation also fuels an arms race. As more people obtain degrees, employers raise the baseline, pushing even marginally qualified individuals into costly further education. The net effect is not a broader ladder but a taller, steeper one that few can climb without incurring unsustainable debt.
[P1.5] Thus, while mass expansion technically increased the number of people holding credentials, the dominant outcome has been the erosion of those credentials’ value—inflation that outpaces any genuine expansion of opportunity.
