[C1.1] Reformulation sounds like a clean fix until you ask what actually happens when you cap salt, sugar, or fat. Food companies are not public health agencies. When mandatory caps hit, manufacturers do not simply remove the targeted ingredient and sell a healthier product. They reformulate to maintain palatability and shelf life—often by adding other ingredients that carry their own risks.
[C1.2] Reduce sugar, and you may get more artificial sweeteners, which some evidence links to glucose intolerance and altered gut microbiomes. Reduce fat, and you get more refined carbohydrates or emulsifiers. The health impact of these substitutions is not neutral, and in many cases we do not yet know the long-term effects. Reformulation by mandate trades one known problem for potentially several unknown ones.
[C1.3] More fundamentally, reformulation leaves the underlying architecture of the food system untouched. People eat ultra-processed foods not because they contain slightly too much sodium, but because these products are engineered to be hyper-palatable, aggressively marketed, and cheaper than whole alternatives. Tweaking nutrient profiles does nothing to address caloric density, fiber deficiency, or the behavioral economics of food choice. A slightly less salty chip is still a chip, and the chronic disease epidemic is driven by far more than micronutrient thresholds.
[C1.4] Education and labeling, for all their imperfections, can shift demand toward fundamentally different food categories—more home cooking, more whole foods, more plant-forward diets—rather than marginally better versions of the same processed products. Chile's warning labels drove substantial product reformulation, yes, but also measurably reduced purchases of products carrying warnings and increased purchases of unlabeled alternatives. Information changed the category mix, not just the nutrient profile.
[C1.5] Finally, the argument that reformulation reaches everyone equally ignores that health disparities stem from more than nutrient exposure. They reflect differential access to healthcare, stress, environmental exposures, and time poverty. Marginal reductions in salt intake do not close those gaps. Building food literacy and creating genuine alternatives does.
