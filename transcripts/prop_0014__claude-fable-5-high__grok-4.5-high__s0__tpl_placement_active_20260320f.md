[P1.1] For decades, deficit hawks cried wolf while interest rates stayed near zero and the sky never fell. That era is over, and the arithmetic has changed in a way that makes this motion not ideological but simply descriptive of our situation.
[P1.2] Consider three facts, all approximate but directionally undeniable. First, federal debt held by the public now sits at roughly 100 percent of GDP and climbing—territory the United States has only occupied once, briefly, after fighting a world war. Second, annual interest payments have surged past what we spend on national defense, approaching a trillion dollars a year. That is money that buys no schools, no bridges, no missiles—it services yesterday's consumption.
[P1.3] Third, deficits are running near six to seven percent of GDP during peacetime and near full employment, exactly when textbook economics says we should be consolidating.
[P1.4] The core mechanism is compounding. Every year of inaction raises the interest bill, which raises the deficit, which raises the debt, which raises the interest bill. This is not a problem that waits patiently; it accelerates. And the higher-rate environment since 2022 means old debt is rolling over at double or triple its previous cost.
[P1.5] Notice what the motion actually asks. Not austerity. Not slashing Social Security tomorrow. It says deficit reduction should take priority over *most new* spending commitments—that new programs must clear a higher bar of necessity.
[P1.6] That is a modest, prudent claim. Genuine emergencies and high-return investments can still pass it.
[P1.7] The deeper stake is fiscal capacity itself. America could respond massively to 2008 and to COVID precisely because it had room to borrow. [P1.8] The choice is not spending versus cruelty. It is whether we govern the debt now, on our terms, or let bond markets govern it later, on theirs.
