[P1.2] Start with prohibition. Bans do not delete demand; they relocate it. When Thailand, India, and Nepal closed their doors after scandals like the Baby Gammy case, the industry did not evaporate — it migrated to jurisdictions with thinner oversight, and arrangements moved further into the informal economy, where a surrogate has no contract worth invoking, no independent lawyer, and no leverage when a client walks away from a disabled newborn. A ban is a promise of protection that dissolves precisely at the moment protection is needed, because the woman who needs help must confess to an illegal act to ask for it.
[P1.3] Now unconditional legality: the world that produced those scandals. Agencies transferring multiple embryos to raise success rates at the surrogate's medical expense. Payment structured so the final installment is hostage to delivery. Consent forms in a language the woman does not read.
[P1.4] Children like Baby Manji, born to no legally recognized parent in either country, stateless while lawyers argue.
[P1.5] Conditionality is the only regime that touches the actual failure points, and it has real teeth, because the money and the intending parents come from rich countries with regulatory reach. Recognize parentage only for accredited arrangements, and every commissioning parent becomes an enforcement agent — they will not risk a child who cannot come home. Require escrow so payment cannot be weaponized. Require independent counsel and independent physicians for the surrogate, medical coverage extending past delivery, and single-embryo-transfer limits.
[P1.6] Publish and audit the accreditation list.
[P1.7] That is how we regulate every valuable but dangerous cross-border service. The choice is between rules with consequences and a black market with none. CON must defend one of those two. Neither is defensible.
