[P1.1] Democracies draw the voting line somewhere, but it should track who is governed, not an arbitrary age. Sixteen-year-olds work, pay tax on wages, and are bound by laws on school, speech, policing, climate policy, and war. In many countries they can already drive, leave school, or consent to medical treatment. The objection is never that they are unaffected; it is that they are supposedly unfit.
[P1.2] That is the real clash: exclusion must be justified, and “maturity” is doing all the work.
[P1.3] Start with stake. A sixteen-year-old stacking shelves pays tax and will live decades with decisions made now, on carbon, debt, housing, and AI. An electorate that skews older discounts those long-term costs. Lowering the age to sixteen does not grant a new privilege; it removes a mismatch between those who bear policy and those who choose it.
[P1.4] Capacity is the stronger half, not the weaker one. By sixteen, basic reasoning about candidates and parties is largely in place; what lags is impulse control in hot, immediate situations, not considered political judgment. Sixteen-year-olds are also reachable: still in school, where a first vote can be paired with real civics rather than picked up later in isolation. The habit mechanism matters: voting at sixteen, with peers and classroom discussion, builds turnout for life, while waiting until eighteen lets nonvoting harden.
[P1.5] This is not theory. Austria enfranchised sixteen-year-olds nationally; Scotland did so for major referendums and devolved elections; Brazil and others allow voting at sixteen. The sky did not fall. Turnout did not collapse, and there was no childish takeover of policy.
[P1.6] The CON will say sixteen is too young. But adults also vote from identity, misinformation, and weak information; we do not disenfranchise them. The remedy for inexperience is education, not silence. Sixteen-year-olds are governed, taxed, and capable.
[P1.7] Democracy works better when those who inherit the future help choose it.

[C1.1] Voting is not a reward for being affected by policy; it is the assignment of coercive power over a whole political community. That is the line CON is defending. Sixteen-year-olds are unquestionably governed, taxed, and often capable, but the law already knows how to handle partial capacity: it grants supervised, domain-specific permissions—driving on a permit, limited work, medical consent in some places—while withholding alcohol purchases, contracts, jury service, and adult criminal responsibility. The ballot is not another permit.
[C1.2] It chooses the officials who tax, imprison, deport, and make war.
[C1.3] PRO’s “who is governed” test cannot be the boundary. Visitors, temporary residents, and future generations are governed by policy without voting; being affected does not create jurisdiction. Nor does paying wage tax. Minors are taxed because they use public goods and earn income, not because they are legally independent members of the electorate.
[C1.4] Capacity is where the case really fails. Sixteen-year-olds may reason about familiar issues, but national elections demand comparative judgment about debt, monetary policy, criminal law, alliances, and constitutional tradeoffs. Adolescents are also more peer-sensitive and less stable in long-horizon choices, especially online. Schools can improve that, but schools are not neutral: teacher-led first voting risks institutional encouragement and unequal turnout, giving an extra voice to already-advantaged students.
[C1.5] The examples PRO cites do not prove the motion. Scotland and Brazil involve devolved or nonpresidential contexts; referendums are single-issue and emotionally simple. No evidence that Austria or Scotland shows disaster is a low bar for changing every national franchise. If affectedness is the test, there is no reason to stop at sixteen rather than fourteen.
[C1.6] PRO’s answer—that adults also vote from identity or misinformation—confuses a threshold with a purity test.
[C1.7] CON’s principle is cleaner: expand civic education and youth consultation, but keep the franchise where the law places adult responsibility.
