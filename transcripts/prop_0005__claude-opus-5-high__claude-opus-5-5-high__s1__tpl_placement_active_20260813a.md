[P1.1] A prescription drug is the one product on earth the consumer cannot buy. That single fact decides this debate. Every other advertisement invites you to make a choice; a drug ad invites you to pressure someone else into making it for you. The commercial's entire purpose is to arrive in the exam room before you do, and to make the physician's judgment feel like an obstacle rather than a service.
[P1.2] That corrupts the gatekeeper we deliberately built. Doctors face a patient who has already named the molecule, already pictured the couple walking the golden retriever on the beach, already decided that a refusal means indifference. The path of least resistance is the prescription pad. And the drug being demanded is never the cheap generic that has worked for thirty years — it is the newest, patent-protected, highest-margin entrant, precisely the one with the thinnest long-term safety record.
[P1.3] Advertising volume tracks profit margin, not therapeutic need. We learned this in the era of heavily promoted painkillers and blockbuster arthritis drugs later pulled from the market: mass demand arrived first, the safety signal arrived after millions of exposures.
[P1.4] Note also what these ads manufacture: not treatment for the undertreated, but disease itself. Ordinary aging, ordinary sadness, ordinary restlessness get renamed as conditions with a branded answer. That drives overdiagnosis, side effects in people who were never sick, and billions in spending that inflates premiums for everyone, including those who never saw the ad.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is that nearly every wealthy democracy already bans this practice, and their patients are not less informed or worse treated. That is our counterfactual, and it is reassuring.
[P1.6] Because the ban does not touch information — it touches selling. Unbranded disease-awareness campaigns, public health messaging, pharmacists, physicians, and patient organizations all survive. What ends is the thirty-second spot whose measure of success is a prescription written under social pressure rather than clinical reasoning.
[P1.7] Prohibit the advertisement. Keep the information.
