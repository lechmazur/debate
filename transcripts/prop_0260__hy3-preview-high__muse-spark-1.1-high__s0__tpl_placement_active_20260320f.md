[P1.1] The current digital environment is defined by a fundamental power imbalance: users are forced to navigate a "consent theater" where rejecting tracking requires navigating dozens of dark-pattern menus. This is not a neutral market failure; it is a deliberate engineering choice by platforms whose revenue depends on surveillance. Requiring robust, opt-out blocking at the system level is the only way to restore a baseline of digital dignity and security for the average person.
[P1.2] First, the status quo is functionally coercive. Most users lack the technical literacy to configure complex privacy settings or install third-party extensions that are often throttled by the very browsers they run on. By making blocking the default, we shift the burden from the individual to the system. Just as seatbelts are mandated in cars because individuals often fail to protect themselves, system-level blocking should be mandated because the "market" for privacy is currently rigged.
[P1.3] Second, trackers are a primary vector for security vulnerabilities and bloat. When an operating system forces every device to request, execute, and store megabytes of third-party tracking scripts, it degrades performance and drains battery life, particularly on lower-end hardware. System-level blocking eliminates these redundant requests at the source. This isn't just about "annoying ads"; it is about preventing the execution of unvetted code that regularly leads to data breaches and malware distribution.
[P1.4] Furthermore, the "opt-out" requirement is crucial. Privacy must be the default state, not a reward for the technically savvy. If blocking is mandatory and robust, the entire ecosystem is forced to adapt toward ethical, non-invasive models of monetization. This proposal protects the most vulnerable users—those who cannot afford the latest devices or the time to manage their digital footprint—from being treated as products.
[P1.5] The law must intervene where the market has failed to provide a safe, functional, and private standard for public computing.
