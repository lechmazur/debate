[C1.1] PRO claims that preemptive preparation is "cheap" insurance. It is not. Preemptively establishing massive new entitlements like wage insurance and expanded income support based on speculative AI anxiety is a profound misallocation of resources. Committing billions to these programs before we understand the shape of the future labor market means diverting capital from urgent, tangible crises to fund a hypothetical one.
[C1.2] PRO demands we build "skills infrastructure" immediately. But retrain for what? AI shifts the economy in entirely unpredictable ways. In the 1990s, governments could not have retrained typists to become cloud computing architects because those roles did not yet exist.
[C1.3] If the state funds massive retraining programs before knowing which new industries AI will create and which specific human skills will remain scarce, it will inevitably train workers for the wrong jobs. We risk creating a pipeline to nowhere, wasting taxpayer funds and workers' time on misaligned curricula.
[C1.4] Furthermore, PRO confuses the automation of *tasks* with the destruction of *jobs*. As [P1.2] notes, AI accelerates drafting and coding. But historically, when technology automates mundane tasks, it boosts worker productivity, lowers costs, and *increases* demand for the final product. A junior developer who uses AI to write boilerplate code ten times faster does not become unemployed; the firm takes on more projects and builds more software.
[C1.5] By preemptively preparing income support for mass unemployment, PRO treats an impending productivity boom as if it were a natural disaster.
[C1.6] Finally, establishing heavy labor-market interventions now will rigidify the economy exactly when it needs agility. If governments step in early to subsidize wages or pay workers to sit in speculative training programs, they artificially slow the natural, productive reallocation of labor that transformative technology requires. We must not build a permanent, restrictive welfare state to preempt an unemployment crisis that economic history, current labor shortages, and AI's actual productivity mechanisms strongly suggest will never arrive.
