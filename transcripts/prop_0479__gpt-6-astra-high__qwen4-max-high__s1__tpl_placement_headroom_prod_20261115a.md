[P1.1] Major policy mistakes often survive because the people making the forecast also have a stake in defending the plan. Subsidized prediction markets create a standing reward for showing that official confidence is misplaced. Governments should make that independent challenge a formal input, not leave consequential forecasts mainly inside panels and ministries.
[P1.2] Consider a proposed flood barrier. Officials forecast completion dates, cost overruns, and protection levels. Clearly specified contracts can aggregate judgments from engineers, contractors, insurers, and informed outsiders who would never sit on the same advisory panel. Someone who thinks the official timetable is unrealistic can gain by backing that judgment.
[P1.3] The market makes disagreement visible before construction, rather than after an inquiry.
[P1.4] Subsidies matter because better public forecasts benefit everyone, while researching them costs individual participants time and money. A public subsidy supplies trading incentives where commercial interest alone would be too weak. Unlike commissioning another report, it rewards successful forecasting rather than institutional access, and allows estimates to change as evidence arrives.
[P1.5] Formal use means publishing relevant market probabilities alongside official forecasts in major decision documents, tracking both against outcomes, and requiring an explanation when policy rests on assumptions markets strongly dispute. Market forecasts become an independent pillar of appraisal, not an optional appendix. Experts still explain mechanisms; elected officials still choose goals.
[P1.6] But neither should enjoy an unchecked monopoly over claims about what will happen.
[P1.7] Markets need clear settlement rules, conflict restrictions, and safeguards against manipulation. Their influence should depend on demonstrated forecasting performance and participation quality, not blind faith in prices. Yet these are reasons to design and evaluate them, not preserve dependence on institutions vulnerable to groupthink and political pressure. The choice is not markets instead of judgment.
[P1.8] It is judgment disciplined by a public, continuously updated test of its factual assumptions.
