[P1.1] A return-to-office mandate is one of the largest unilateral interventions a company can make in its employees' lives. It reshapes commutes, childcare arrangements, disabilities accommodations, and the geographic pool employers can hire from. When a decision carries that weight, "culture" cannot be the whole answer. Culture is a means, not an end, and means demand justification.
[P1.2] The proposition targets the weakest version of the argument employers currently offer. Broad culture talk collapses two very different claims into one: that in-person work produces specific, identifiable benefits for specific roles, and that presence creates intangible social cohesion. The first claim is testable. The second, even when true, is not self-evidently worth the costs imposed on workers, especially those with caregiving duties, chronic illness, or long commutes.
[P1.3] Role-specific justification forces the employer to show which of those benefits actually attach to, say, a software engineer whose code reviews happen in pull requests, versus a junior relationship banker whose client pipeline is built face-to-face.
[P1.4] Consider what role-specific scrutiny would surface. For a claims processor, the mandate would have to show measurable throughput or error-rate gains that remote work suppresses. For a litigator, perhaps in-person war-rooming genuinely shortens case timelines. For many white-collar roles, the honest answer is that the evidence is thin, contested, or task-dependent, and a serious employer should be willing to say so out loud rather than hide behind slogans about trust and belonging.
[P1.5] Broad culture assertions also create a loophole large enough to defeat the policy's purpose. Managers who personally dislike remote work, or who prefer to see bodies in seats, can dress that preference as culture and sidestep scrutiny entirely. Role-specific reasoning makes that move harder, because the manager must point to the work itself.
[P1.6] Finally, accountability flows downward. Employees, regulators, and investors are all increasingly entitled to ask why a major policy was imposed. A company that can answer only with culture has, in effect, answered nothing. The proposition simply requires that the answer be a real one.

[C1.1] A large enterprise is not an agglomeration of independent contractors sharing a logo; it is an interdependent ecosystem. Workplace design cannot be reduced to isolated task metrics without dismantling the cross-functional fabric that makes an organization greater than the sum of its parts. Culture, mentorship, and spontaneous collaboration are enterprise-level assets that cannot be captured on a role-by-role spreadsheet.
[C1.2] First, in-person presence generates systemic positive externalities that role-specific assessments inherently miss. An experienced engineer’s individual coding output might look identical from a home office, but their physical presence dramatically accelerates the onboarding of junior colleagues, resolves cross-departmental blockers in passing, and builds social capital. When employers are forced to justify mandates solely through narrow, role-specific metrics, they are blind to these collective benefits. The value of presence lies in the interaction between different roles, not within isolated job descriptions.
[C1.3] Second, fracturing policy into role-by-role justifications breeds administrative paralysis and toxic internal inequity. Evaluating thousands of distinct job codes creates an endless cycle of subjective boundary-drawing and departmental resentment. If marketing or sales teams are mandated into the office while data analysts are exempted, it establishes an arbitrary two-tier caste system that damages morale and destroys cross-functional alignment. A coherent enterprise requires shared baselines, not balkanized privilege.
[C1.4] Finally, dismissing culture as vague rhetoric creates a dangerous false dichotomy. Culture—the shared norms, institutional memory, and mutual trust that sustain high-performing teams—is a concrete commercial imperative. Forcing employers to defend holistic organizational strategy through atomized task justifications compels companies to optimize for easily quantifiable short-term inputs at the expense of long-term enterprise health. Large employers must be permitted to manage the whole organization, not just its disconnected parts.
