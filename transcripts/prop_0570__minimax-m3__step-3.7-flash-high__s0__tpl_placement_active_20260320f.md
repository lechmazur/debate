[P1.1] The proposition rests on a clean distinction: tasks where AI is genuinely useful, and tasks where substituting it for human judgment would corrupt the skill we are trying to teach. Mastery practice belongs to the first. Writing feedback belongs to the second.
[P1.2] Start with mastery practice. Drill, retrieval, procedural fluency — these are exactly what AI tutoring handles well. The answer is right or wrong. The student needs infinite patience, immediate correction, and adaptive difficulty.
[P1.3] A student practicing quadratic equations, balancing chemical equations, drilling subjunctive forms — none of these tasks turn on judgment. AI can scaffold, hint, and re-explain without fatigue. Using it here is efficient, not a compromise.
[P1.4] Writing feedback is a different object entirely. A secondary English teacher's marginal comment is not a flag for an error. It is a reading: "this argument is doing something the writer may not see yet." It is a dialogue about voice, structure, evidence, and audience — the things writing actually is.
[P1.5] When an AI becomes the main source of that feedback, three harms follow.
[P1.6] First, AI rewards AI-flavored prose. It systematically favors a generic, hedged, register-flattening academic style, and pushes back against unusual structure or risky claims. Students learn to write what scores well against the model, not what argues well to a reader.
[P1.7] Second, dialogic mentorship disappears. Effective writing instruction is iterative: a student drafts, a teacher responds, the student responds to the response. AI delivers a verdict, not a conversation. Students stop developing the metacognitive habits of revision.
[P1.8] Third, the developmental purpose of secondary writing is identity formation as a writer. The "main" source of feedback shapes what students think writing is for. If the main voice answering their work is a model trained to optimize for plausibility, they inherit that purpose.
[P1.9] The proposition is not a compromise. It is the only consistent position: deploy AI where its strengths match the task, refuse to deploy it where doing so would distort the skill itself.

The PRO’s insistence that only human feedback can support writer identity formation ignores that many secondary students, especially multilingual learners or those with learning disabilities, get more targeted, affirming feedback from well-configured AI than from overworked teachers who can only leave scribbled marginal notes on a fraction of their papers.
[C1.4] The proposition’s arbitrary split prioritizes a vague ideal of “human-only” writing instruction over actual student outcomes, wasting AI’s potential to democratize access to both mastery support and high-quality writing feedback.
