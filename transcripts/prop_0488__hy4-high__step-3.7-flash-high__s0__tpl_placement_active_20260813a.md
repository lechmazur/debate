[P1.1] Resilience is not maximum output in a calm year; it is the ability to keep planting, adapting, and harvesting through pest shocks, weather swings, and input disruptions. A handful of integrated firms in global seed and agrochemical markets creates a common-mode risk: the same germplasm, trait packages, licensing rules, dealer networks, and chemical dependencies spread across farms. Government-mandated breakup is structural insurance for the food supply.
[P1.2] Concentration makes fragility profitable. An integrated firm gains when farmers buy a bundle: proprietary seed, the herbicide it tolerates, treatment packages, and contracts. That may raise efficiency in normal years, but it pulls breeding toward blockbuster platforms that fit a global chemical portfolio, not toward many locally adapted varieties or independent seed houses. When a pathogen breaks resistance, a weed adapts, or a port or plant is disrupted, the danger is not one bad product; it is whole regions tied to the same narrow playbook with too few alternatives.
[P1.3] Breaking these firms up changes the architecture of choice. Separate seed from agrochemicals, split regional and crop portfolios, distribute essential germplasm and trait libraries among independent firms, and end exclusive tying. Then chemical firms must court many seed firms; seed firms can choose among many protection suppliers; regional spinouts have reason to breed for local drought, soils, pests, and diets. Multiple breeding pipelines and input strategies give farmers substitutes before a shock, not after harvests fail.
[P1.4] The defense of concentration will invoke research scale. But scale guided by a few boards is the vulnerability: bigger budgets do not replace independent judgments. Conduct rules can police a license term, but cannot resurrect a lost breeding program, a local seed network, or a second supplier after the market has folded into one portfolio. Structural separation can preserve labs and research assets while dispersing control.
[P1.5] The choice is slightly cheaper coordination in stable years versus more ways for the food system to survive a bad one.

[C1.1] PRO’s core error is conflating corporate concentration with food system fragility. The integrated seed-agrochemical model exists because developing a single drought-tolerant, pest-resistant crop trait takes 10–15 years and over $100 million in R&D, a cost only large, coordinated firms can absorb. Breaking these firms up will not eliminate risk—it will dismantle the R&D pipelines that deliver the very tools farmers need to adapt to shocks, from drought to novel pest outbreaks. PRO claims breakup will spur local, context-specific breeding, but local seed systems already operate, and they rely on licensing germplasm and traits from the very firms PRO targets.
[C1.2] Splitting these companies will not magically fund small local breeders; it will strand shared trait libraries behind fragmented, undercapitalized entities that cannot afford to maintain or improve them. Smallholder farmers in Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, who rely on affordable improved hybrid seed from global firms, will be the first to lose access when R&D budgets are slashed to cover duplicated administrative and regulatory costs across dozens of tiny new companies. PRO also ignores that large integrated firms are the only players with the supply chain resilience to weather global disruptions.
[C1.3] When COVID-19 shut down ports or the Ukraine war cut fertilizer exports, these firms had the global logistics networks and inventory buffers to reroute inputs to farmers in crisis. A fragmented market of small, regional seed and chemical firms would lack that scale, meaning a single port closure or trade disruption could wipe out seed and input access for millions of farmers at once.

Breakup does not add genetic diversity; it destroys the coordinated, well-funded pipeline that delivers tested, resilient varieties to farmers when shocks hit. Conduct rules addressing tying and exclusive licensing can solve PRO’s stated access concerns without dismantling the R&D engine that smallholders rely on for affordable, adapted seed.
