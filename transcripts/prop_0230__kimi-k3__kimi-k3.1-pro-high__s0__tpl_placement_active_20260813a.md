[P1.1] Europe built its post-Cold War armies for expeditionary wars of choice, not for the territorial war that has returned to its doorstep. Ukraine's lesson is brutal: mass matters, reserves matter, and a society that has practiced mobilization survives the first year of attrition. Professional volunteer forces, however excellent, are small and cannot be regenerated once attrited. That is why the states closest to the threat — Finland, Sweden, the Baltics — maintain or have restored conscription, and why Germany is debating its return.
[P1.2] Front-line Europe is voting with its laws.
[P1.3] Readiness is the first pillar. A large trained reserve is the cheapest deterrence Europe can buy: a year of training plus periodic refreshers costs a fraction of procurement, yet multiplies wartime strength severalfold and signals resolve in a way no white paper can. Deterrence rests on what an adversary believes you can field in month six, not what parades on day one.
[P1.4] The second pillar is cohesion. European societies are fragmenting along class, regional, and migration lines, and almost no institution makes young citizens of different backgrounds live and work toward a common purpose. Universal service — military or civic, disaster relief, care work — builds exactly that. Switzerland's militia army has long glued together four language groups; Germany's old civilian service staffed hospitals and ambulances for decades.
[P1.5] Finally, fairness. Today the risks of defense fall on volunteers drawn disproportionately from poorer regions, while deployment decisions are made by elites whose children never serve. Universal service reconnects the whole society to the costs of security — and nations that share the burden choose war more wisely.
[P1.6] The objection that conscripts make poor soldiers misunderstands the model: professionals fight today's wars; the trained reserve guarantees a country is left to fight tomorrow's. National service is not nostalgia. It is the rational answer to a harder continent.

[C2.1] PRO’s flexibility splits their case in two. For readiness, service must be selective: “intake matched to need.” For cohesion and fairness, it must be universal: every class, every background, every family. A state cannot maximize both. Select the recruits the military needs and the banker’s son may never meet the farmer’s son; compel everyone and scarce instructors, equipment, and billets are consumed processing people the force does not need.
[C2.2] Those are not flexible options. They are competing models that sacrifice each other’s justification.
[C2.3] On readiness, reserves matter; compulsory national intake does not thereby become necessary. PRO says crews cannot be bought in month six, but neither can units, refreshers, officers, or equipment. Their argument establishes that preparedness must be built early, not that coercion is its cheapest form. Recruitment shortfalls in voluntary reserves show that reserves require funding, employer support, and attractive careers; they do not show that compelling teenagers fixes those weaknesses.
[C2.4] Finland’s system works, where it works, as an entire national defense structure—not as proof that the legal act of conscription alone generates capability.
[C2.5] “Article 5 makes Finland’s geography everyone’s” confuses shared risk with identical force design. Collective defense also permits specialization: states can contribute air, naval, logistics, or other capabilities rather than reproduce one territorial-mobilization model. The motion prescribes expansion across Europe; PRO still has not justified that leap.
[C2.6] On cohesion, invoking Switzerland’s history merely assumes what must be proved. A practice can coexist with national solidarity without causing it. And once intake is selective, the promised comprehensive mixing disappears. Nor does placing elite children alongside poorer citizens create democratic accountability: governments retain the same deployment powers, while influential families may still navigate selection and assignments.
[C2.7] Finally, “filled shifts that budgets left empty” is not an answer to the cheap-labor objection—it confirms it. Underfunded care needs stable staffing and proper pay, not a rotating supply of conscripts. Europe needs credible reserves and genuine solidarity. PRO has not shown that compulsory national service delivers either, especially when its military design defeats its social promises.

PRO’s demand for one total institution is a false dilemma: solidarity can be built through several inclusive institutions without commandeering a year of anyone’s life.
[C4.2] A2: Fill reserve ranks by making reserve service worth accepting: paid training and deployment, portable qualifications, predictable commitments, legal job protection, employer reimbursement, and focused recruitment of veterans and people with needed technical skills. [C4.4] Calling both tracks “obligation” conceals, rather than resolves, the trade-off.
