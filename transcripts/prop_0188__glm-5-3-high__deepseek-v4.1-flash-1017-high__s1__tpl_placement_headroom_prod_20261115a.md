[P1.1] Public money buys public knowledge, and knowledge that only flows when results are flattering is not knowledge at all. That is the core case for mandatory preregistration and outcome-blind publication commitments in publicly funded clinical and social-science research.
[P1.2] Start with the incentives. Researchers face enormous pressure to find something. Careers, grants, and publications reward positive findings; null results disappear into drawers. This publication bias doesn't merely leave gaps in the literature—it actively corrupts what we think we know.
[P1.3] If ten teams test a treatment and only the two with lucky results get published, the literature shows a therapy that works, when the honest record shows it probably doesn't. Patients enrolled in later trials, and eventually patients in clinics, pay the price of that distortion. The clinical trial registries created over the past two decades were a response to exactly this: studies that went unregistered went unpublished at dramatically higher rates, and registered studies that produced null results still often never appeared in journals.
[P1.4] Preregistration makes the selective disappearing of inconvenient studies visible and auditable.
[P1.5] Second, preregistration disciplines analysis. In flexible data, researchers can try many specifications—subgroups, outcome measures, statistical tests—and report the one that crossed the significance threshold. This garden of forking paths is not usually fraud; it's ordinary people rationalizing toward a publishable result. A preregistered analysis plan doesn't prevent exploratory work, it just honestly labels it as exploratory.
[P1.6] Third, the publication commitment. Preregistration without guaranteed publication is half a reform: if journals still cherry-pick exciting findings, file-drawer pressure simply migrates upstream. Committing to publish regardless of outcome—judged on the quality of the question and design, not the direction of results—removes the incentive at its root.
[P1.7] The counterargument will invoke scientific freedom and rigidity. But the motion governs publicly funded research, where accountability is owed to taxpayers and trial participants who take risks for the general good. Nobody is owed public money to generate privately filtered evidence. Good science is not improvised; it's planned.
[P1.8] We should demand the plan up front.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a useful tool for a universal rule, and then bolts on a publication guarantee that makes the rule worse. Preregistration can improve transparency in some confirmatory trials. It cannot bear the weight of a mandate across clinical and social science, and outcome-blind publication commitments create new distortions while pretending to solve old ones.
[C1.2] First, a registered plan is not a truthful result. Researchers can preregister a vague plan, amend it repeatedly, or write it after pilot work. The file then certifies procedure, not integrity, while giving bad studies a veneer of credibility. Mandatory registration does not stop p-hacking; it can launder it.
[C1.3] And when journals must publish regardless of outcome, the reward shifts from producing trustworthy evidence to producing a compliant paper. Guaranteed space is a weak substitute for rigorous gatekeeping, and it can crowd out better work in scarce journals.
[C1.4] Second, the mandate is badly matched to social science. Ethnography, historical work, qualitative interviewing, and exploratory theory-building cannot honestly specify hypotheses and analyses before the encounter with evidence. Forcing them into a clinical-trials template encourages ritual compliance: meaningless forms filled out to unlock funding. That is bureaucracy, not accountability.
[C1.5] Third, public funding justifies public access to results, not a single publication route. Taxpayers are owed timely reporting and usable data, which can be required through registries and repositories. They are not owed a promise that every funded study occupies journal space. The proposition confuses transparency with a particular workflow and publication model.
[C1.6] The real target is selective reporting. Require results reporting, audit protocols, and fund open data. Do not mandate a plan for every inquiry or guarantee publication before anyone knows whether the study was well executed. A mandate that certifies intentions and guarantees outlets will produce more paperwork and more mediocre papers, not more truth.
