[P1.1] Credit reports exist to predict future repayment behavior based on voluntary financial choices, but medical debt is the product of random, involuntary health shocks, not poor financial decision-making. Including it on credit reports creates a self-fulfilling cycle of harm that has no meaningful predictive value for future repayment risk.
[P1.2] Consider a typical case: a part-time retail worker with a 720 credit score, who has paid every rent, utility, and credit card bill on time for five years, develops a kidney stone requiring emergency care. Her insurance denies coverage for the majority of the $2200 bill due to a clerical error, and the provider reports the unpaid balance to credit bureaus after 120 days. Her score drops to 640, disqualifying her from a $10,000 auto loan she needs to get to her second job to pay the bill.
[P1.3] She is forced to take a $15,000 subprime loan at 18% APR, adding hundreds of dollars in monthly interest that makes it even harder to clear the original medical debt. The credit report penalty did not signal she was a bad repayment risk—it created the risk it claimed to predict.
[P1.4] Regulators have repeatedly found that the vast majority of small medical collections stem from billing errors, insurance disputes, or surprise out-of-network charges, not consumer refusal to pay. Unlike missed payments on voluntary loans, which reflect a borrower’s choice to prioritize other spending over debt obligations, medical debt arises when people are forced to choose between paying a random, often unexpected bill and covering rent, groceries, or childcare. A person who misses one medical payment after a car accident is statistically far more likely to resume on-time payments once the shock passes than a person who routinely overspends on discretionary purchases and misses credit card due dates.
[P1.5] Treating these two scenarios as equivalent in credit scoring erases the actual signal of repayment risk: the metric is broken, and medical debt has no place in it.
