[P1.1] Picture a grandmother who gets a call in a cloned voice from her 'grandson', sobbing that he's been arrested and needs bail. The synthetic profile that made the scam credible survived because the platform ignored repeated verified reports from her family and a consumer-protection group. By the time the account died, the money was gone and the scammer was untraceable. That is the harm this motion targets, and civil liability is the only fix that fits.
[P1.2] The proposition is narrow, so the usual defenses miss it. Platforms would not be punished for hosting user speech, nor forced to monitor everything AI produces. Liability attaches only when three things converge: the scam is AI-enabled impersonation, reports are repeated, and reports are verified. Platforms already verify trusted flaggers, copyright notices, and law-enforcement requests; synthetic-scam reports deserve the same pipeline.
[P1.3] At that point, the platform is not a passive conduit. It has knowledge, means, and a choice. Ignoring a known impersonation that it can delete, downrank, or label is its own culpable omission, not the fraudster's remote act.
[P1.4] Nor is suing the scammer a real alternative. The impersonator is often anonymous, overseas, and judgment-proof. Victims cannot subpoena a face or a voice. The platform is the solvent, controllable node that hosted the fake identity, amplified it, and kept it live.
[P1.5] Civil liability simply makes the platform internalize the losses it can prevent at low cost, instead of forcing grandmothers and small businesses to absorb them.
[P1.6] Voluntary moderation has failed because platforms profit from engagement and lack incentive to act fast. Damages change incentives. After notice, platforms can require provenance signals, disable reported accounts, add verification friction, or lock payout paths. This is not prior restraint or a speech crackdown; it is compensation and deterrence for ignoring a verified alarm.
[P1.7] Hold platforms liable when they ignore repeated verified reports, and the synthetic-scam economy loses its safest distribution channel.

[C1.1] The tragedy PRO opens with is real, but liability lands on the wrong actor. The platform did not clone the voice, demand bail money, or pocket it. Making hosts pay for criminals they did not hire and cannot catch turns every open platform into the insurer of global organized fraud. Victims wait years for a payout while the scammer who actually stole from them moves on untouched.
[C1.2] And 'repeated verified reports' sounds narrow until you try to run it. Verification of AI impersonation is notoriously uncertain. Detection tools misfire, human reviewers disagree, and context decides everything — a fan parody account, a consented AI-dubbed ad, a political satirist using a synthetic voice can look identical to a scam in a short clip or profile photo. PRO promises a copyright-style pipeline, but copyright has ownership papers; synthetic fraud has contested intent, evolving fakes, and no neutral referee who can certify truth in hours.
[C1.3] Faced with damages for guessing wrong one way, platforms will guess wrong the other way: delete on demand. That is what notice-based liability always produces. Risk-averse removal chills lawful speech and invites weaponization, where competitors, harassers, or scammers themselves file supposedly verified complaints to erase rivals, journalists, or warnings about their own schemes. A rule meant to kill impersonation becomes a tool for impersonators.
[C1.4] The burden also entrenches power. A tech giant can hire reviewers and lawyers to process floods of claims; a small forum, open-source host, or startup cannot. Threaten ruinous suits and only giants survive, while fraud simply hops to smaller, darker, offshore venues where victims have even less recourse.
[C1.5] Civil liability does not deliver cheap prevention. It outsources criminal policing to private firms, punishes the public square, and leaves the fraudster free. Stop the scammer, not the square where he stood.
