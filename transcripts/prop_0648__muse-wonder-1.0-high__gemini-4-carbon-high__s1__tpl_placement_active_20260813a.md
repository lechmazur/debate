[P1.1] A cyber war will not arrive as a tidy battle between uniformed hackers in secure bunkers. It will arrive as simultaneous attacks on power grids, hospitals, ports, banks, and local governments — almost all of them civilian systems, run by civilian engineers, on civilian networks. A defence built mainly around a small standing military unit is therefore mismatched to the threat in scale, location, and skill.
[P1.2] The decisive talent already sits outside the military, and most of it will not enlist. The best incident responders, cloud architects, malware analysts, and industrial-control specialists work in industry, where pay is higher, tools are current, and careers are not interrupted by postings, fitness tests, or rigid rank. A civilian reserve meets them where they are: periodic training, security vetting, and a legal duty to serve in crisis, without demanding they abandon the jobs that keep their skills sharp.
[P1.3] That structure also solves the surge problem. In peacetime, a country does not need thousands of full-time operators staring at quiet screens; in the first week of a crisis, it may need tens of thousands to triage malware, restore backups, patch hospitals, and defend municipalities at once. Standing units are either too small for that moment or ruinously expensive to keep at wartime size. Reserves provide mass on demand at a fraction of the cost, while a small professional cadre can coordinate, deconflict, and handle the most sensitive missions.
[P1.4] Finally, reserves stay technologically alive. Cyber tools turn over in months, not procurement cycles. A reservist who spends Monday defending a real bank network or a cloud platform brings fresher knowledge on Tuesday than a soldier rotated through generic postings and outdated ranges. Estonia’s voluntary Cyber Defence Unit points in this direction: specialists defend the systems they already understand, under state direction.
[P1.5] The choice is not between discipline and chaos; it is between a narrow, expensive priesthood and a vetted national reservoir. For the war that will actually be fought — broad, fast, and overwhelmingly civilian — the reservoir wins.
