[P1.1] The central question is not whether drugs are dangerous, but which policy produces fewer dead bodies and fewer victims. Punitive possession enforcement fails on both counts because it converts a health problem into a criminal-justice conveyor belt that actively raises the risk of overdose and later crime.
[P1.2] Consider the person found with a small amount for personal use. Under criminalization, they are handcuffed, jailed, and branded. That arrest can cost housing, a job, and custody of children. On release, tolerance has dropped while exposure to a fentanyl-adulterated supply has not; that gap is where fatal overdoses cluster.
[P1.3] Fear of police also suppresses bystander 911 calls, the single most effective immediate overdose intervention. Punishment does not make use safe—it makes it hidden, hurried, and lethal.
[P1.4] Decriminalization breaks that chain. Removing arrest for possession keeps users attached to jobs, families, and clinics. It lets them carry naloxone, access drug-checking and treatment without fearing that seeking help will trigger prosecution. Portugal’s post-2001 shift from arrest to health-focused dissuasion is the clearest natural experiment: overdose deaths and drug-related imprisonment fell substantially while problematic use did not spiral out of control compared with similar countries.
[P1.5] That outcome is exactly the mechanism this motion predicts: fewer deaths because use is managed, not merely punished.
[P1.6] The crime saving is just as concrete. Convictions for small possession manufacture records that shrink legal opportunity. A young person with a possession record is less employable and more likely to cycle through theft, survival economies, and deeper incarceration. Decriminalizing possession shrinks that pipeline, lets police redirect scarce resources toward violent traffickers, and reduces the prison churn that destabilizes neighborhoods.
[P1.7] The choice is not more drugs versus fewer drugs. It is a policy that multiplies harm through courts and cells, versus one that keeps people alive, employed, and reachable by care. On the two outcomes that matter—overdose deaths and downstream crime—decriminalization wins.

[C1.2] In a fentanyl-dominated street supply, that marginal increase is deadlier than in earlier eras because one bad dose can be fatal. Oregon is the recent caution: after personal-possession decriminalization, overdose deaths rose, open drug use became a visible crisis, and policymakers moved to restore criminal penalties. That directly contradicts the motion’s prediction.
[C1.3] The treatment argument also assumes a system that often does not exist. Decriminalization alone does not produce beds, counselors, or compulsory engagement. Portugal is repeatedly cited precisely because it did not simply end consequences: it kept civil sanctions, mandatory appearances, and substantial treatment infrastructure.
[C1.4] Strip those away and the result is not Portugal.
[C1.5] The crime claim is partly an accounting trick. Possession arrests obviously fall when possession is decriminalized, but the motion promises fewer downstream crimes: theft, burglary, robbery, and violence. Those are driven by addiction and open drug economies. A person with an untreated addiction still needs money for drugs, and visible open use invites disorder, turf disputes, and public nuisance that consume police resources.
[C1.6] Drug enforcement, including low-level possession encounters, is often the earliest point at which a person can be steered into supervision or treatment—not a perfect tool, but a real one.
[C1.7] The proposition wants to count fewer handcuffs as fewer crimes. The actual question is whether victims are safer and fewer people die. The fentanyl-era evidence and the missing treatment-accountability mechanism say no.
