[P1.1] Loneliness is not a mood; it is a measurable health exposure. Research going back decades links chronic loneliness to elevated risks of heart disease, stroke, dementia, and premature death, with effect sizes often compared to smoking many cigarettes a day. When a condition carries that kind of mortality signal, we do not tell people to handle it alone. We regulate it, fund it, and treat it as a population-level problem.
[P1.2] That is the standard we apply to tobacco, air pollution, and obesity, and loneliness belongs in the same category.
[P1.3] The private-coping model fails on its own terms. It assumes loneliness is an individual deficiency, so the fix is individual grit: join a club, download an app, be more outgoing. But loneliness is heavily driven by structural conditions, the design of cities and transport, the loss of third places, isolated housing for the elderly, the erosion of community institutions. You cannot self-help your way out of a built environment engineered for isolation.
[P1.4] Telling someone to cope privately with a social habitat that makes connection difficult is like telling a resident of a polluted city to breathe better.
[P1.5] The public-health framing also works where private coping demonstrably has not.
