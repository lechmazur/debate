[P1.1] The motion includes a crucial qualifier: "once immediate security concerns have passed." That qualifier does most of the defensive work. The proposition already concedes that genuinely active operations stay sealed. The real question is what happens to decades-old records about colonial administration, wartime detention, and secret-police surveillance once nobody alive can be endangered by disclosure.
[P1.2] The default today is often continued secrecy—and that default is wrong.
[P1.3] Families of the disappeared, of executed prisoners, of people erased from records by colonial regimes, carry a moral claim to truth that does not expire when a calendar year passes. A daughter who wants to know how her father died in police custody in 1972 is not asking for intelligence that threatens the state. She is asking for her own history. To deny her that is to extend the original violation into the present, making the archive an instrument of continuing harm rather than a record of past harm.
[P1.4] Historians and affected communities serve a broader public function. When states systematically abuse power, the archival record is often the only evidence that survives outside official mythology. Without broad access, the history of colonial violence, wartime atrocity, and secret-police terror gets written by the perpetrators or their successors. Opening archives is not a gift to scholars; it is the mechanism by which societies confront what was done in their name.
[P1.5] The structural problem with restrictive access is that it protects the wrong people. Indefinite secrecy shields torturers, collaborators, and officials who profited from silence. The information that genuinely endangers current operations is narrow and identifiable. Once that window closes, the only remaining beneficiaries of opacity are individuals and institutions dodging accountability.
[P1.6] The burden should fall on those who want old records sealed to justify why—not on citizens to prove they deserve their own history.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that once immediate security risks fade, old archives become harmless. That is dangerously false. Intelligence methods from the Cold War may still be in active use today; revealing the identities of long-dead informants can endanger their living descendants. Territorial disputes in former colonies remain volatile, and declassifying historical boundary documents or ethnic censuses can provide new fuel for current conflicts.
[C1.2] Secrecy does not expire simply because time passes. The passage of time does not magically erase the potential for harm.
[C1.3] Moreover, these archives are not just historical records—they are repositories of deeply personal, often coerced, information. Medical files, forced confessions, and informant lists violate the privacy of individuals who never consented to public exposure. In many cultures, the dignity of the dead is sacred, and publicizing their coerced confessions or medical secrets is a profound violation. The right to truth for some cannot override the right to dignity for others, especially when the affected cannot speak for themselves.
[C1.4] This is not a hypothetical concern: in South Africa, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission faced ethical dilemmas over publishing the names of informers, fearing vigilante justice.
[C1.5] Broad access does not guarantee justice. Without expert curation, raw archives become ammunition for partisan narratives. In Northern Ireland, the release of oral histories from the Troubles led to prosecutions that re-opened old wounds and chilled future truth-telling. History is not self-interpreting; dumping unfiltered records on the public risks replacing one distortion with another.
[C1.6] And the logistical reality is stark: redaction at the scale of colonial or wartime archives is a multi-decade, multi-billion-dollar endeavor. The result would be either a negligible trickle of sanitized documents or a chaotic deluge that harms more than it heals. Even if technically feasible, the process would divert vast public resources from current needs to serve a backward-looking agenda. The burden of proof should remain on those demanding disclosure to show that the benefits outweigh these enduring harms.
