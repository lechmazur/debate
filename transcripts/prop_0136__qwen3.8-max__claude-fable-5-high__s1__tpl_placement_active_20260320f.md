[P1.1] The central question is not whether governments want births to rise, but what actually changes a couple’s calculation about having another child. In East Asia, the decisive barriers are structural and ongoing: the price of a home large enough for a family, the cost and scarcity of reliable childcare, and the career penalty mothers still face. One-off baby bonuses do almost nothing to move those barriers. Against the lifetime cost of raising a child in a high-cost city, a single payment is noise.
[P1.2] A lump sum can help buy a crib or pay a deposit, but it does not make a two-bedroom apartment affordable in Seoul, Taipei, or Tokyo; it does not create a childcare slot; it does not protect a woman from being sidelined after maternity leave. Because the bonus is temporary, families rationally discount it against decades of rent, tuition, and lost income.
[P1.3] Housing and childcare reform is different because it alters the recurring budget constraint. If public housing, zoning, or mortgage support lets a young couple secure family-sized housing at a bearable cost, the decision to marry and have a child becomes realistic rather than aspirational. If subsidized daycare, after-school care, and paid leave are dependable, parents can combine work and children without gambling that one parent—usually the mother—must exit the labor force. These policies also address the gendered bottleneck that suppresses fertility in East Asian societies: when childcare is not privatized inside women’s unpaid labor, childbearing becomes less of a career trap.
[P1.4] Cash bonuses may nudge timing or produce symbolic publicity, but they are easily swallowed by landlords, tuition centers, or debt repayment. By contrast, affordability reforms compound: lower housing costs free monthly income, while reliable childcare keeps parents attached to work. Durable affordability measures raise the probability that a first child leads to a second. That is why they will do more.

Small Japanese towns that offered generous per-child payments saw birth rates rise sharply; Hungary's aggressive cash-and-loan package produced a measurable fertility recovery.
[C1.5] The empirical literature on child allowances consistently finds positive, if modest, effects—which is more than Korea's childcare buildout can claim.
[C1.6] There is also a credibility problem PRO cannot escape. [C1.7] The question is which raises birth rates more. One approach has failed spectacularly across the region; the other shows real, replicated effects. Judge on results.
