[P1.1] Governments should adopt sectoral bargaining because the alternative—setting minimums firm by firm or leaving them to individual bargaining—is structurally rigged. In fragmented industries like care, hospitality, cleaning, logistics and food processing, workers have little leverage, employers compete by cutting labour costs, and the public often ends up subsidising poverty wages. Sectoral bargaining changes the unit of negotiation: employers, unions and government set enforceable floors for pay, scheduling, safety and training across an industry, with room for regional or skill variation.
[P1.2] This is not command-and-control over every wage. It is a floor plus a process. Australia’s award system shows that broad industry minima can coexist with varied jobs, and several European systems extend collective agreements to non-members so coverage does not depend on union density. The mechanism is straightforward: when the whole sector moves together, no responsible employer is undercut for paying decently, and workers who cannot organise still gain protection.
[P1.3] Second, common standards are easier to enforce. Labour inspectorates, payroll systems and workers can monitor one transparent floor far better than thousands of bespoke contracts. Third, sectoral floors can reduce churn and fund training, which high-turnover industries chronically underprovide.
[P1.4] Opponents will warn about rigidity and job loss. That is a design objection, not a reason to reject the framework: floors can be phased, differentiated and reviewed. The real comparison is a status quo where monopsony power suppresses wages while taxpayers top up profits. If a job cannot be done without poverty pay, the problem is the business model, not the floor.
[P1.5] The decisive question is whether minimum standards should be set by fragmented, unequal firm-level bargaining or by a democratic industry-wide process. Sectoral bargaining is the only mechanism that covers the majority, stops undercutting, and makes decent pay a baseline rather than a privilege.

[C1.1] The central flaw in the proposition is that it treats an industry as a single employer. In reality, a sector contains giant incumbents and fragile startups, high-productivity regions and declining towns, stable careers and entry-level jobs that serve as first rungs. Sectoral bargaining imposes one negotiated wage structure across all of them. That is not simply a floor; it is a wage-setting cartel that favors the strong and harms the marginal.
[C1.2] When industry-wide pay and standards are set by councils of large employers and established unions, the predictable result is not just higher pay but higher barriers. Big firms can absorb mandates; small competitors cannot. A cleaning contractor with thin margins, a rural care provider, or a seasonal hospitality business may not be able to pay the negotiated rate without cutting hours, reducing hiring, or automating. The survivors gain market share.
[C1.3] Workers see fewer openings, less flexibility, and more consolidation. In the worst cases, work moves into informal or subcontracted arrangements, where enforcement is hardest.
[C1.4] The proposition claims this simplifies enforcement. But the more uniform the rule, the more pressure to create exemptions, regional differentials, and compliance certifications. That does not produce transparent governance; it produces a bureaucratic maze. It also shifts power to insiders: unions with members in declining occupations may protect incumbents, while gig workers, migrants, part-timers, and the unemployed have little voice.
[C1.5] The better answer is a high statutory minimum wage, indexed and regionally differentiated, plus targeted wage subsidies where low pay reflects low productivity. Combine that with stronger labor inspection, anti-monopsony enforcement, and portable benefits. Those tools raise pay without freezing industry structure or making employment contingent on a council’s estimate of what a sector can bear.
[C1.6] If the problem is poverty pay, aim directly at poverty pay. Do not replace a simple legal floor with industry-wide bargaining that risks pricing out the weakest workers and entrenching the largest firms.

[P2.1] C1.1's central premise is false: sectoral bargaining does not impose one wage on an entire industry. It sets industry-wide minima across job classifications, with regional and skill differentials. Australian awards and European sectoral agreements are schedules, not single rates. So the rural care provider and metro hospital need not be treated identically; what they share is a floor that stops a race to the bottom.
[P2.2] C1.2 says big firms absorb mandates while small firms cannot. But the status quo already favors big buyers and franchisors, who squeeze small contractors on price until poverty wages are the only way to survive. A common floor removes that undercutting. If a genuinely marginal firm cannot pay, the answer is phase-ins, small-employer support, or restructuring—not allowing it to externalize costs onto workers and taxpayers.
[P2.3] CON's high statutory minimum hits the same viability problem with less tailoring.
[P2.4] C1.3's informal-work worry is stronger under firm-level rules: sectoral bodies can extend portable benefits and joint liability down subcontracting chains. C1.4's enforcement objection cuts the other way too. A single statutory number cannot enforce scheduling, safety, training, or misclassification. Sectoral frameworks create public classifications and industry bodies, so a subcontracted cleaner can point to one standard rather than dozens of opaque contracts.
[P2.5] Exemptions are a design risk in any system; they do not justify leaving enforcement to firm-level discretion.
[P2.6] On insider power, sectoral extension covers non-members, so coverage does not depend on union density. Statutory minimums give workers no voice at all. The remedy for capture is inclusive representation—gig, migrant, part-time workers—not atomization.
[P2.7] The decisive comparison is not “flexible market versus rigid council.” It is a race to the bottom, patched by subsidies and inspections, versus an industry-wide floor that makes decent pay and basic standards the baseline. CON's alternative is a piecemeal version of the same idea, without the coordination that stops undercutting.
