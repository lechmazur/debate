[P1.1] The strongest argument for a legal cap is that the alternative is not neutral access—it is a market that profits from borrowers' desperation. A credit card rate of 30, 40, or higher is not a benign price signal to someone already short of cash; it is a mechanism that turns a temporary shock into years of payments. On a $3,000 balance, a 30% rate means roughly $900 a year in interest if the principal is not reduced. That is not inclusion.
[P1.2] It is a treadmill, and the borrower often discovers the cost only after the trap closes.
[P1.3] The motion concedes the lender's likely response: some higher-risk borrowers will lose access. That concession does not defeat the cap; it exposes the choice. We should ask what kind of access is being protected. If the only loan available is one whose payments are likely to cause default, the “access” is a statistical illusion.
[P1.4] Denying that loan can leave a borrower with no new debt but also no new spiral. The relevant comparison is not perfect credit versus no credit; it is expensive, dangerous credit versus less of it, plus space for safer alternatives.
[P1.5] Competition will not solve this segment. Borrowers in distress do not shop coolly; they take what keeps the lights on. Lenders know it, price for it, and collect. A ceiling also changes the bargaining table: lenders must compete on underwriting and terms, not merely on who can extract the most from the vulnerable.
[P1.6] A cap sets a social boundary: we do not let the price of money become predatory merely because someone is desperate. If genuine emergency credit is needed, that is a case for targeted public or nonprofit help, not for preserving a legal right to be exploited.

[P2.1] CON's case rests on a false symmetry: either borrowers get the card or they get nothing. But the real choice is between a loan whose price can double the balance and a smaller, safer credit footprint. Their transmission example [C1.3] helps us: a short bridge repaid in months is cheap in dollar terms and would survive any reasonable cap; the loans a cap targets are the ones that revolve for years at punishing rates. If a cap at a sane ceiling kills a product, that product was not a bridge—it was a trap with a friendly name.
[P2.2] Their "exclusion is a vacuum" point [C1.5] is overstated. Denied a card, some households will use worse options—but the answer is to extend the cap's logic to those products and fund alternatives, not to preserve one predatory option as a firewall. We do not allow lead paint because some families might otherwise use arsenic.
[P2.3] And CON's preferred fixes—disclosure, ability-to-repay, amortization—are not substitutes. They can coexist with a cap, but they fail at the moment of desperation: borrowers do not comparison-shop APR when the car is dead; they click accept. A legal ceiling is the only rule that protects the borrower who cannot walk away. Underwriting alone just moves the exclusion earlier; CON accepts that exclusion when lenders choose it, but calls it oppression when law chooses it.
[P2.4] The decisive point: CON defends access by pointing to the loan that keeps someone working, but ignores the many who get the same card for a temporary shock and spend years paying for it. A cap may cost some marginal borrowers a card. It protects the larger group from a debt spiral that is far harder to escape than a denied application. That is a trade worth making—and the motion asks us to make it.
