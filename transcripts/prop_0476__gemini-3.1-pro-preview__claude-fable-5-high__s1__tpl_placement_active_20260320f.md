[P1.1] Low Earth Orbit is rapidly transforming from a frontier into a crowded highway, driven by the exponential launch of commercial megaconstellations. We are hurtling toward the Kessler Syndrome—a cascading chain reaction of orbital collisions that could render near-Earth space unusable for generations. To prevent this, governments must require commercial satellite operators to post deorbit bonds.
[P1.2] Currently, space is treated as a free dumping ground. Commercial operators reap the profits of satellite connectivity but socialize the long-term risks of orbital debris. When a satellite reaches the end of its life, suffers a mechanical failure, or its parent company goes bankrupt, it often remains in orbit as a lethal, hypersonic projectile. Right now, there is no financial consequence for cluttering the commons.
[P1.3] Deorbit bonds solve this market failure by forcing operators to internalize the cost of their externalities. By requiring a financial deposit upfront—one strictly forfeited if the spacecraft is not safely retired—we fundamentally alter the economic calculus of space access.
[P1.4] First, bonds incentivize rigorous engineering. When a company’s capital is tied up in a bond, it gains a direct financial imperative to invest in highly reliable disposal mechanisms, such as backup propulsion or deployable drag sails. Operators will no longer be tempted to burn their last drop of fuel to squeeze out a few extra weeks of revenue, because failing to reserve power for a safe deorbit will immediately cost them their deposit.
[P1.5] Second, forfeited bonds provide a vital funding mechanism for cleanup. If a satellite unavoidably fails and becomes derelict, the forfeited funds can be directly channeled to the emerging active debris removal industry. This ensures the cost of capturing dead satellites is paid by the operator who left them there, rather than being passed on to the public or future spacefarers.
[P1.6] Commercial space enterprise cannot survive if the orbital environment is destroyed. Deorbit bonds provide the necessary accountability to guarantee that the companies profiting from space today do not foreclose humanity's access to it tomorrow.

[C1.1] A bond is a price tag, not a safeguard. The fundamental flaw in this proposal is that it confuses punishing failure with preventing it. When a satellite dies in orbit, forfeiting a deposit does not bring it down. The debris still circles the Earth at seventeen thousand miles per hour.
[C1.2] Bonds change who loses money; they do not change what happens in the sky. Worse, a forfeitable bond perversely legitimizes abandonment—it converts dereliction from a regulatory violation into a purchasable option. Pay the fee, leave the junk. That is a pollution license, not accountability.
[C1.3] Second, bonds are a tax on competition. Locking up millions in escrow per spacecraft is trivial for SpaceX or Amazon, with their constellations of thousands, and crushing for the startup launching its third satellite. The predictable result is an orbital oligopoly where the largest debris producers—megaconstellation operators—face proportionally the lightest burden, while the innovators who might build better disposal technology never get capital to fly. A policy designed to protect the commons would instead hand it to the incumbents most aggressively filling it.
[C1.4] Third, no global government exists to impose this. The motion asks individual governments to act, and individual mandates simply trigger flag-of-convenience licensing. Operators relocate to whichever jurisdiction skips the bond—exactly as shipping migrated to Panama and Liberia. The states that impose bonds lose their industries and their regulatory leverage; orbital risk stays the same or worsens, because satellites now answer to the most permissive regulator available.
[C1.5] Finally, this is a solution chasing a problem already being addressed by better tools: enforceable deorbit timelines as licensing conditions, design-for-demise standards, and liability insurance that scales with actual risk rather than a bureaucrat's guess at a bond figure. Set the bond too low and it's a rounding error; too high and you've strangled the industry. Regulators have no reliable way to price it correctly.
[C1.6] Demand compliance directly. Don't sell indulgences for orbital littering.
