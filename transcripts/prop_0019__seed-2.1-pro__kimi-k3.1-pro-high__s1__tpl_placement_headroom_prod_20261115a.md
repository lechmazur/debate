[P1.1] Antitrust law exists to protect consumers from the harms of suppressed price competition, not to punish specific methods of coordination. When competing landlords adopt the same shared algorithmic rent-setting tool, they produce the exact same market harm as direct price-fixing: artificially inflated rents, eliminated incentives to undercut competitors to fill vacancies, and no independent pricing discretion to push rates down toward competitive levels. That warrants the same antitrust scrutiny applied to explicit coordination. These tools do not operate as neutral, isolated pricing calculators.
[P1.2] Landlords feed real-time, non-public data about occupancy, rent concessions, turnover, and planned rate changes into a shared system, which then spits out a uniform recommended price for comparable units across a market. When dozens or hundreds of direct competitors in the same rental market all default to that shared recommendation, they do not need to meet in a room, trade texts, or make explicit agreements to lock in above-market rents. The algorithm acts as the common enforcer of a coordinated price floor, punishing any landlord that might otherwise cut rates by signaling every other market participant will hold prices high.
[P1.3] Consider a tight urban neighborhood where 70% of units are priced using the same shared tool: if the algorithm recommends a 7% hike when local occupancy hits 96%, no landlord will risk leaving money on the table by offering a discount, because they know their competitors will not undercut the recommended rate. There is no meaningful difference between that dynamic and a direct agreement to follow a jointly set price schedule. Scrutiny does not equal automatic liability; it simply means regulators will look past the thin technical layer of software to assess actual competitive harm, exactly as they do for overt price coordination between rival firms.

[C1.1] PRO’s central move is to assume the conspiracy, then point to the software as its proof. Sharing a vendor is not sharing an agreement. Landlords can use the same accountant, listing platform, or pricing model while making genuinely independent decisions. Antitrust therefore asks whether rivals agreed to restrain competition, what information actually passed between them, and how recommendations affected conduct.
[C1.2] The motion replaces those questions with a technological shortcut: common software becomes direct coordination.
[C1.3] That shortcut is not merely overinclusive; it abandons the distinction between tacit parallelism and collusion. Competitors in a transparent rental market often raise rents together because demand rises, or cut them when vacancies grow. Even identical recommendations would not establish assent, adherence, or communication. A landlord may reject a recommendation, offer concessions, or price a renovated unit differently.
[C1.4] Treating adoption alone like a price-fixing meeting would expose lawful independent conduct to the presumptions reserved for cartels. Where nonpublic competitor data is pooled and rivals commit to follow a common pricing strategy, enforcers already have evidence worth investigating. But that conclusion comes from the agreement and information exchange, not from the label “algorithm.”
[C1.5] PRO’s neighborhood hypothetical [P1.3] supplies the missing agreement by assertion: universal compliance, foreclosed discounting, and a punitive “enforcer” are stipulated, not demonstrated. Its fallback that scrutiny is not automatic liability cannot save the motion. Scrutiny “as if” direct coordination means starting from cartel-like suspicion rather than market evidence. The better rule is neither immunity nor algorithmic guilt: examine confidential-data flows, delegation of pricing authority, commitments among landlords, deviations, and actual effects.
[C1.6] Shared tools can also help small owners respond to vacancies and lower prices in weak markets. Condemning the common infrastructure instead of proving coordination would deter those efficiencies while missing the real target: provable collusion, whatever technology facilitates it.

That is not passive vendor use—it is a shared mechanism that eliminates the exact uncertainty that normally prevents parallel price hikes from sticking. No landlord has to guess if a rival will undercut them to fill a vacancy, because the algorithm tells them exactly what every other user is being told to charge.
[P2.2] The tacit parallelism defense [C1.3] fails for the same reason. Normal parallel pricing happens when each firm independently guesses where demand sits, risking lost occupancy if they guess too high. There is no independent guesswork when rivals feed confidential data on concessions, turnover, and planned rate changes into a common system to spit out a shared price target. Of course deviation is technically possible [C1.3], just as a cartel member can technically cheat on a price-fixing deal.
[P2.3] But the shared tool systematically erodes the reward for deviation: any landlord that cuts rates knows competitors using the same tool will immediately see market shifts and match, eliminating the chance to win tenants with lower prices. The claim that this scrutiny amounts to “algorithmic guilt” [C1.5] misrepresents how antitrust works. Scrutinizing these tools like direct coordination does not impose automatic liability, any more than investigating a ring of competitors swapping price lists skips evidence of actual harm. It simply refuses to let a thin layer of software shield a collusive price-setting arrangement from the same review applied to any other coordinated effort to raise rents.
[P2.4] Generic independent pricing tools for small owners remain entirely lawful [C1.6]; only the shared, competitor-fed hubs that operate as de facto cartel ringmasters draw targeted scrutiny.
