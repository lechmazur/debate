[P1.1] When an IT technician, a sales representative, or an office manager can excel at their job without a four-year diploma, demanding one at the hiring gate is both bad business and an unjust economic barrier. Nobody is arguing that hospitals should hire unlicensed surgeons. This debate is strictly about roles where a degree is not genuinely necessary for performance, yet remains an arbitrary filter.
[P1.2] First, unnecessary degree mandates sabotage employers. Through "degree inflation," companies have slapped bachelor’s requirements onto routine middle-skill roles simply as a convenient way to narrow applicant pools. The result is a self-inflicted talent shortage. By filtering out everyone without a four-year credential, employers automatically discard military veterans with hands-on leadership, self-taught coders with proven portfolios, and capable workers who learned on the job.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, firms pay higher wage premiums for a piece of paper while suffering higher turnover when over-credentialed hires grow dissatisfied with routine work.
[P1.4] Second, a college degree is a poor proxy for readiness when the curriculum isn't tied to the role. Defenders of the status quo claim a diploma signals grit or trainability. In reality, what an unrelated degree most reliably measures is whether an applicant had the financial cushion and life stability to spend four years out of the full-time workforce. Employers do not need this blunt, exclusionary guessing game.
[P1.5] They can use targeted skills assessments, work-sample tasks, relevant certifications, and structured interviews. Testing whether an applicant can actually do the work predicts performance far better than checking if they passed college lecture courses years ago.
[P1.6] Finally, superfluous degree requirements turn higher education into an expensive tollbooth for entry-level survival. Workers should not be forced into heavy debt just to pass an automated resume screen, and experienced talent should not be locked out of middle-class careers by an irrelevant credential. Employers should hire for capability, not pedigree.

[C1.1] The case against this motion does not require defending every degree requirement. It requires rejecting the motion’s central assumption: that “performance” can be reduced to the immediate tasks of a job. Hiring is a prediction under uncertainty. A sales representative may be able to make calls without a BA, but the employer is also betting on whether that person can later absorb product analytics, write client-facing proposals, manage territory data, and move into account leadership.
[C1.2] The degree does not guarantee any of that, but it is a low-cost, consistent signal that a candidate can complete long-term assignments, meet deadlines, and communicate at a baseline level. The proposition treats performance as a snapshot rather than a trajectory.
[C1.3] Second, the phrase “genuinely necessary for performance” is not a practical test. Almost no credential is strictly necessary in that narrow sense. The real question is whether a requirement improves expected performance enough to justify its use. That judgment varies by firm, role, and market.
[C1.4] A small sales team can waive a degree for an applicant with a strong pipeline record; a compliance-heavy office manager role may keep it because the manager will handle contracts and regulatory filings. A blanket demand that employers stop requiring degrees would strip away exactly that local judgment.
[C1.5] Third, the proposed substitutes are weaker than they sound. Work-sample tasks and structured interviews are useful, but they are costly to develop, administer, and validate. They often measure current, narrow competence rather than persistence, adaptability, or the willingness to finish unglamorous long-term projects. If such tests were always cheaper and more predictive, competitive firms would already favor them.
[C1.6] Many do not, because the degree remains a useful initial screen, not merely a badge of status bias.
[C1.7] Finally, flexibility already exists without the motion. Employers can make exceptions, accept certifications, or promote internally. The motion goes further: it says employers should stop requiring degrees. That overcorrection would make hiring more expensive and less informative, not more fair.
