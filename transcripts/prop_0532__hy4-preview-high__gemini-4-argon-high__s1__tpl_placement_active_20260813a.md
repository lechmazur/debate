[P1.1] The curb is not free; it is scarce public space, and keeping it mostly free subsidizes the most space-hungry users. Delivery trucks and ride-hail cars consume prime street space for loading, waiting, and cruising, then everyone else pays in the form of double-parked travel lanes, blocked bus stops, and riders swerving into traffic. A five-minute passenger pickup and a twenty-minute restaurant restock use the same curb, but they impose very different costs on the street. When space is unpriced, the result is not fairness but a race to occupy it first, while buses, cyclists, and pedestrians absorb delay and danger.
[P1.2] Dynamic pricing fixes the mismatch by making the price track time, place, and dwell. A short, high-turnover pickup stays cheap; a long occupancy or repeated circling becomes expensive. That signal pushes delivery fleets to consolidate loads, shift restocking to off-peak windows, use cargo bikes for the last leg, and keeps ride-hail drivers from idling in the travel lane while waiting for a fare. Cities that price scarce road space, from London to Singapore, show it works: charge the real cost of occupying space and behavior changes, traffic falls, and revenue appears for transit and safer streets.
[P1.3] Opponents will call this regressive, but free curb is already regressive in effect. The costs fall on bus riders, low-wage workers, and residents breathing exhaust, while platforms profit from free storage. Pricing can be designed with caps, small-business discounts, zero-emission rebates, and revenue dedicated to transit and street safety. The inequity is pretending the curb has no price, then letting private fleets monopolize it.
[P1.4] The real choice is managed scarcity versus unmanaged scarcity. Keeping curb mostly free guarantees congestion where demand is highest. Dynamic curb pricing tells operators the true cost of the space they take and lets cities prioritize people moving and loading over vehicles storing. That is why cities should allow it.

[C1.1] Dynamic curb pricing sounds neat on a whiteboard, but on a real street, it confuses the ability to pay with the need to stop. [P1.4] presents a false binary between dynamic tolls and an unmanaged free-for-all. Keeping the curb mostly free of user fees does not mean keeping it lawless; cities can ration space through dedicated loading zones, strict time limits, and enforcement rather than auctioning it to the highest bidder.
[C1.2] First, dynamic pricing misreads urban commerce. [P1.2] assumes surge prices will smoothly shift deliveries to off-peak hours or cargo bikes. In reality, curb demand is largely inelastic because recipients dictate the schedule. A neighborhood restaurant cannot accept fresh produce at midnight when the kitchen is unstaffed; heavy commercial supplies cannot fit on a cargo bike; a clinic patient or shift worker using ride-hail cannot wait two hours for curb rates to drop.
[C1.3] Because schedules are fixed, the vehicles still come. Dynamic pricing fails to clear the curb and instead functions as an inflationary tax passed onto local businesses, consumers, and gig drivers.
[C1.4] Second, pricing the curb by the minute worsens the street hazards [P1.1] describes. When pulling to the curb triggers a ticking toll, drivers evade it. Ride-hail drivers dump passengers directly in the unpriced travel lane or circle the block until the rider is on the pavement—increasing congestion and exhaust. Delivery trucks spill over into adjacent residential streets.
[C1.5] Finally, [P1.3]’s proposed discounts and caps are self-defeating: exemptions blunt the price signal, while unblunted prices let deep-pocketed platforms buy out the curb and squeeze out independent couriers and local shops. London and Singapore priced broad entry cordons to shift private commuters onto transit, not micro-tolled essential freight and passenger loading. Cities should govern the curb with physical design and clear rules, not turn every stop into a surge-priced transaction.
