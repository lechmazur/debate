[P1.1] Theatrical-first release is a distribution strategy, not a creative commandment. For most of cinema's history, it existed because theaters were the only venue capable of showing films. That constraint is gone. What remains is inertia—a business habit that gates access to art behind geography, physical ability, income, and scheduling luck.
[P1.2] The core case is simple: simultaneous release with robust home accessibility options expands who can participate in the cultural conversation when it happens, not weeks or months later. A wheelchair user in a town with one poorly accessible theater. A deaf viewer who wants reliable subtitles rather than the often-incomplete captions available at many cinemas. A parent who can't afford seventy dollars for tickets and concessions for a family of four.
[P1.3] An autistic viewer who needs to pause, adjust volume, or control the sensory environment. These aren't edge cases stitched together—they're millions of people systematically excluded from the default model.
[P1.4] The theatrical experience isn't destroyed by offering another option. The claim that simultaneous release kills theaters assumes audiences who want the big screen will abandon it the moment their living room becomes legal. That's an argument against the theater's own value, not for the window. If the communal spectacle is as irreplaceable as defenders claim, it survives choice.
[P1.5] If it survives only through coerced waiting, what's being protected isn't excellence but scarcity engineered as a business strategy.
[P1.6] The proposition doesn't say abolish theaters. It says stop treating theatrical-first as the default for almost every big title. More films get concurrent release, more audiences access art at the moment of cultural relevance, and theaters compete on genuine merit rather than artificially restricted alternatives.
[P1.7] Accessibility isn't charity. It's who gets to be part of the conversation.
