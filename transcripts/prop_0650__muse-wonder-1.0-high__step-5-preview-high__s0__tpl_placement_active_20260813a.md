[P1.1] Continuous glucose monitors for non-diabetics should be judged by what they prevent, not by the discomfort of seeing the truth. Metabolic disease develops silently for years, and our current system usually notices it only after fasting glucose or A1c has already crossed a disease threshold. A sensor worn for weeks makes that hidden phase visible: which breakfast drives a prolonged spike, how poor sleep worsens the next day's response, how a short walk after dinner blunts a rise that willpower lectures never made concrete.
[P1.2] That visibility is the public-health mechanism. Generic advice to eat less sugar and move more fails because it is generic. Real-time, personal feedback turns abstract risk into a learnable pattern. People discover that the same meal affects them differently, that pairing carbohydrates with protein and fiber matters, that meal order and timing matter, that consistent post-meal walking works for them.
[P1.3] Those are not pharmaceutical interventions. They are low-risk lifestyle adjustments, discovered faster and retained longer because the user can see cause and effect within hours instead of waiting three months for a lab result.
[P1.4] The scale of the payoff dwarfs the scale of the harm. Prediabetes and insulin resistance are extraordinarily common and frequently undiagnosed, and they feed into diabetes, cardiovascular disease, fatty liver, and dementia risk. Even modest population-wide improvements in post-meal glucose, weight, and activity — earlier detection for some, sustained habit change for many — would avert far more suffering and cost than they create. Late-stage metabolic disease is the real over-medicalization: lifelong drugs, injections, dialysis, amputations, and hospitalizations.
[P1.5] Anxiety and obsessive checking are real risks, but they are design and education problems, not reasons to withhold information. We solved analogous problems with home blood-pressure cuffs and scales by teaching ranges, trends over single readings, and when to seek care. With sensible non-diabetic ranges, time-limited use, and clear guidance not to chase flat lines, CGMs empower prevention. Keeping people blind until they are patients is the more harmful choice.

[C1.1] The motion is about widespread consumer adoption, not short, clinically supervised CGM use. At population scale, these devices turn ordinary meals into visible pass/fail tests. That is where the public-health damage begins.
[C1.2] Non-diabetic glucose is noisy. A spike after white rice can reflect sleep, stress, illness, exercise, hormone status, sensor placement, or simply what was eaten an hour earlier. A user cannot tell a benign curve from a warning sign, so the device invites two opposite errors: chasing harmless numbers and being reassured by deceptively stable ones. It substitutes a fashionable proxy—post-meal glucose—for the outcomes that matter: sustained diet quality, activity, sleep, blood pressure, weight, tobacco exposure, and real access to care.
[C1.3] The harms are not edge cases. Continuous numeric feedback rewards monitoring and perfectionism. For people with anxiety, disordered eating, or body-image distress—large groups in any widespread rollout—it can legitimize restriction, compulsive checking, and carbohydrate fear. It also creates pressure to act: cut fruit, skip meals, buy supplements, seek tests, request medication for a number that was never a diagnosis.
[C1.4] Widespread use would likely increase clinician visits and low-value interventions while consuming money and attention that prevention budgets could spend on food environments, preventive services, and metabolic screening that already works.
[C1.5] PRO’s home-blood-pressure analogy fails because blood pressure has clear treatment thresholds and an established measurement context. Consumer glucose does not. More information is not automatically better prevention; it is only useful if people can interpret it and act on it safely. At scale, most cannot.
[C1.6] Yes, late metabolic disease is terrible. But the answer is proven, equitable prevention—not distributing continuous monitors to healthy people and hoping they interpret the noise correctly. Widespread adoption will generate more anxiety, more over-medicalization, and more distorted behavior than durable health.

[P4.1] A1: A user does not need to isolate every cause from one spike; they need repeated, within-person comparisons. Eat the same breakfast after a normal night and after a poor night, repeat a dinner with and without a walk, note illness or a sensor pressed during sleep. When only the meal changes, the curve tends to move with the meal; when sleep changes, the baseline for the day shifts. Imperfect attribution is enough, because these are lifestyle experiments, not diagnoses.
[P4.2] A2: Clinicians gate the step from pattern to medicine: confirmatory fasting glucose or A1c, repeat testing, lifestyle referral, or discharge with reassurance. Those laboratory tests do have diagnostic thresholds, even if a consumer CGM trace alone does not. The sensor is therefore a prompt to test properly, not a prescription pad. Reassurance backed by a normal lab is triage, not over-medicalization; it is precisely how a noisy prompt is prevented from becoming a label or drug.
[P4.3] CON's case requires three leaps: confusion persists despite repetition, confusion produces harmful rather than ordinary healthy responses, and clinicians then amplify rather than filter it. Each answer breaks a leap. Repetition makes relative patterns learnable; the first responses — walk, fiber, protein, meal order, earlier sleep — remain useful even if the original spike had multiple causes; and confirmatory testing separates concern from disease.
[P4.4] That leaves weighing. Transient worry and an occasional reassurance visit are real costs, but they are bounded and correctable with ranges, time limits, and warnings for people prone to food anxiety. Undetected insulin resistance is neither bounded nor easily reversed once it becomes diabetes with its drugs, complications, and daily self-monitoring. CON would avoid the smaller, manageable medicalization of a screening prompt by accepting the far larger medicalization of late disease.
[P4.5] On its own demanded comparison — benefit at population scale versus harm at population scale — visibility still wins.
