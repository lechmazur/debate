[P1.1] Sugar-sweetened beverages are an unusually apt target for a serious consumption tax. They are not essential, they deliver large amounts of liquid sugar with little nutritional value, and their regular consumption is strongly linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and dental disease. The core question is not whether a tax costs money—it obviously does—but whether the state should use one of its few scalable policy levers to reduce a harmful habit, especially when the health damage is concentrated among the households the opposition will claim to protect.
[P1.2] A meaningful tax works because price changes behavior. When a tax is large enough to show up on the shelf, consumers trade down: fewer two-liter sodas, fewer daily energy drinks, more water or untaxed alternatives. Experience from places like Mexico and several city-level taxes points in the same direction: even moderate taxes can reduce purchases, and stronger taxes can do more.
[P1.3] That matters because sugary drinks are heavily marketed to lower-income neighborhoods and are often among the cheapest calories available. If the price signal is too weak, it is absorbed by distributors or ignored; if it is meaningful, it changes the default choice without banning anything.
[P1.4] The hardest point is regressivity, and we should meet it directly. Yes, lower-income households may pay more of their income in this tax if they continue buying sugary drinks. But the alternative is a world in which those same households continue to bear the heaviest medical and economic costs of diet-related disease: missed work, disability, dialysis, complications in pregnancy, and premature death. Those burdens are far more regressive than a tax on soda.
[P1.5] Moreover, the revenue can be directed toward the very communities affected—school meals, water access, food subsidies, or clinical prevention. The proposition is justified because the purpose is not to raise money fairly; it is to reduce a preventable harm, and the health gains matter most where the burden is worst.

[C1.1] The proposition explicitly concedes the central problem: this tax takes more from the people who have the least. That concession should end the debate, not open it. A policy designed to reduce consumption by making a product too expensive for its primary consumers is, by definition, a policy that punishes those consumers for not changing fast enough. The people who keep buying soda after the tax are the ones who either cannot find alternatives or whose habits are most entrenched—and they are precisely the households PRO claims to protect.
[C1.2] The substitution problem undermines the entire mechanism. When soda becomes expensive, low-income consumers do not magically switch to sparkling water and fresh produce. They switch to cheaper store brands, other sweetened drinks outside the tax's scope, or cheaper food overall that may be equally calorie-dense. The tax revenue becomes a transfer from the poor to the treasury, justified by a health improvement that may never materialize in the magnitude promised.
[C1.3] The revenue recycling promise is politically fragile. Earmarked revenue streams get redirected in every budget cycle. What begins as funding for school meals or water access becomes general revenue within a few years, while the regressive tax remains permanently embedded. We are asked to trade a certain, immediate, recurring burden on poor households for a speculative, contingent, politically vulnerable benefit.
[C1.4] The false binary between regressive taxation and diet-related disease ignores the full policy toolkit. Subsidizing healthy alternatives, regulating marketing to children, improving food access in underserved neighborhoods, and investing in preventive care all reduce harm without extracting money from the households already struggling. PRO's argument depends on the assumption that we must choose between taxing the poor and letting them get sick. We do not.
[C1.5] A tax that disproportionately burdens lower-income households to achieve a behavioral shift that may not materialize, funded by a revenue promise that may not hold, is not a policy worth defending.
