[C1.2] AMR is driven by antibiotic consumption across multiple sectors: human clinical misuse, therapeutic veterinary use, environmental contamination, and global trade in animals and food. The resistant pathogens of greatest human concern, including livestock-associated MRSA, emerged primarily from therapeutic dosing and human medicine, not prophylactic dosing of healthy animals. Denmark's own experience showed that aggressive reductions in livestock antibiotic use produced mixed AMR outcomes — some target bacteria declined, others did not. The lever the motion proposes is small relative to the problem it claims to solve.
[C1.3] PRO's "true cost of production" framing assumes the alternative is clean, low-density farming. In most of the world, the realistic alternative is less protein on the table, not better barns. Smallholders cannot fund the biosecurity overhaul that made Denmark's transition work.
[C1.4] There is a cost PRO's framing quietly erases: animal welfare. If healthy animals can no longer receive preventive antibiotics, more of them get sick. That means more disease, higher mortality, and ironically more therapeutic antibiotic courses once illness strikes, since treating sick animals is the larger share of veterinary antibiotic use. We could end up dosing more antibiotics into herds while worsening animal suffering.
[C1.5] Denmark and the Netherlands succeeded because restrictions were paired with heavy investment in biosecurity, veterinary oversight, and farmer compensation. That infrastructure does not exist in much of the world. A uniform ban lands very differently in a climate-controlled European barn than in a smallholder operation where clean water and basic vaccines are already scarce. Higher meat prices fall hardest on the poorest consumers, while the supposed AMR gains remain diffuse and contested.
[C1.6] The right response to AMR is targeted, evidence-based intervention across all sectors, not a blunt ban that imposes certain harm to chase uncertain benefit.

Tonnage is the wrong metric for resistance selection. Therapeutic courses typically deploy higher-potency, more critically important human drugs, applied to animals already shedding pathogens at high concentrations. Mass prophylaxis often uses older, cheaper, less-critical compounds precisely because they are dosed continuously at sub-therapeutic levels.
[C2.2] A kilogram of chlortetracycline in feed generates fundamentally different evolutionary pressure than a kilogram of a last-line drug injected into a sick animal. Conflating mass with risk distorts the picture.
[C2.3] PRO insists no therapeutic rebound will occur [P2.3, P2.4] and treats this as settled science. It is not. Denmark's celebrated reductions came bundled with massive parallel investments in biosecurity, ventilation, vaccination programs, and structured culling protocols. The lower tonnage reflects what replaced prophylaxis, not what happens automatically.
[C2.4] In jurisdictions lacking that infrastructure — most of the world — removing prevention without substitute capacity yields more disease, more therapeutic courses, and worse animal welfare. The evidence PRO actually cites undermines their own conclusion.
[C2.5] The characterization of prophylaxis as "the operational backbone of corporate feedlots" [P2.5] misreads global livestock geography. While industrial confinement dominates OECD production, smallholders across South and Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America rely on affordable preventive tools precisely because they lack climate-controlled housing, diagnostic labs, and reliable veterinary networks. A uniform ban strips these producers of their primary disease-prevention option while doing little to slow resistance driven by therapeutic misuse in hospitals and farms alike.
[C2.6] Finally, the closing frame [P2.6] asks us to sacrifice cheap protein for the future of medicine. But this hides the asymmetry: the AMR benefit of this particular ban is diffuse, slow, and shared across sectors, while the price and yield costs are immediate, measurable, and concentrated on the world's poorest consumers. Stewardship matters. This blunt instrument is not how we get there.
