[P1.1] The core of this debate comes down to which tool delivers more child poverty reduction per dollar spent, and monthly child tax credits (CTCs) outperform childcare subsidies on three decisive counts. First, childcare subsidies have a baked-in coverage gap: they only help families who purchase formal, licensed childcare, excluding the 40% of low-income families that rely on unpaid grandparent care, family care, or have infants not yet enrolled in formal programs. A single parent with a newborn who stays home to provide care gets zero childcare subsidy, but receives the full monthly CTC to cover rent, food, and utilities, directly lifting their household above the poverty line.
[P1.2] Second, childcare subsidies only reduce poverty if parents increase earnings enough to cross the threshold after covering care costs, a conditional effect that fails for families facing disability, local job shortages, or care responsibilities for other relatives. For these households, subsidies are useless, while CTCs provide unconditional cash that counts directly toward the official poverty measure. The 2021 expanded monthly CTC cut child poverty by almost 50% in its first full year of rollout—a faster, larger reduction than any equivalent childcare subsidy program has ever recorded.
[P1.3] Third, equivalent CTC spending has far lower administrative overhead: the existing tax infrastructure delivers funds directly to families with minimal red tape, while childcare subsidies require complex state-level provider eligibility checks and administration that eat into funding that could otherwise reach low-income kids.

[C1.1] PRO's central trick is that it treats cash as “direct” because it is counted by the official poverty measure [P1.2]. That is accounting, not cost-effectiveness. A low-wage parent paying hundreds a month for care is poor after that unavoidable cost; a subsidy that removes the cost, stabilizes work, and raises earnings can cut child poverty more per public dollar than a transfer that merely adds cash income. If the scoreboard credits only cash, the comparison is rigged before it begins.
[C1.2] Monthly CTCs are poorly targeted to the poverty gap. A broad refundable credit sends the same per-child amount to families far below the line, families barely below it, and many whose children would not be poor without it. Dollars to nonpoor households buy no poverty reduction; dollars to deep-poverty households may not change the headcount; dollars continue when need changes. Childcare subsidies can be income-tested, capped, and tied to the binding barrier: care costs that make paid work impossible or unstable.
[C1.3] A parent offered steady hours but unable to prepay daycare needs a guaranteed slot, not another check. The subsidy can unlock wages month after month, plus tax receipts and lower benefit use. CTC cash stops when the checks stop.
[C1.4] The “baked-in coverage gap” [P1.1] is not baked into expansion; it describes narrow existing programs. Expanded subsidies can cover infants, nonstandard hours, home-based or registered relative care, and care-desert supply. Hard cases outside paid work matter, but do not prove most dollars should be broad cash. PRO's pandemic claim [P1.2] is not apples-to-apples: a temporary income surge is compared with capped programs, and the effect disappears when checks disappear.
[C1.5] Finally, [P1.3] mistakes low paperwork for low waste. Monthly CTCs need real-time eligibility and reconciliation, while provider subsidies verify an enrolled child and a bill. Administrative savings cannot rescue spending sent to households with no childcare-cost barrier. The decisive comparison is durable child-poverty reduction per budget dollar; targeted subsidies win.
