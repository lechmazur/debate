[P1.1] Public honors are not neutral historical records—they are active endorsements. When a government places someone's name on a school, a street sign, or a holiday, it declares that person worthy of public celebration. The question is not whether colonizers, slaveholders, and dictators existed, but whether governments should continue elevating them as figures we collectively admire.
[P1.2] The continuity argument fails because it treats all history as equally worthy of preservation. We do not name schools after criminals or mark holidays for traitors simply because they existed. We select honorees based on values. When we discover that someone we honored built their legacy on genocide, slavery, or tyranny, maintaining that honor is not neutrality—it is an active choice to prioritize their reputation over the dignity of the communities they harmed.
[P1.3] Consider the practical effect on marginalized groups. A Black child walking into a school named after a slaveholder receives a clear message about whose history matters and whose pain is invisible. Indigenous communities forced to celebrate a holiday honoring the man who initiated their dispossession are not experiencing "historical continuity"—they are experiencing ongoing state-sanctioned erasure. These names are not abstract; they shape daily life and signal belonging.
[P1.4] Renaming does not erase history. We can teach the full truth about Columbus, Leopold II, or any historical figure without building monuments to their egos. Museums, textbooks, and honest curricula preserve memory far better than street signs that reduce complex legacies to unearned reverence.
[P1.5] Governments serve living citizens, not dead ones. Public spaces should reflect shared values—equality, justice, human dignity—not inherited deference to oppressors. When the two conflict, the living deserve priority. Continuity for its own sake is not a virtue; it is an excuse for refusing to reckon with moral failure.
