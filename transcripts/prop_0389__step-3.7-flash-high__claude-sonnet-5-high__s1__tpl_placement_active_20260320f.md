[C1.2] Start with the legal reality PRO glosses over. Most online games are not self-contained IP. They run on licensed middleware, licensed music, sometimes licensed voice talent, and third-party matchmaking or anti-cheat services. Those licenses typically expire or terminate at shutdown, precisely because ongoing distribution triggers new royalty obligations.
[C1.3] A publisher that "allows" a private server to keep that licensed content running isn't giving away something costless — it's exposing itself to breach-of-contract claims from the actual rights holders of the music, the engine, or the likeness rights inside the game. City of Heroes worked because NCSoft happened to hold clean rights to everything in it. Games built on licensed sports rosters, licensed film IP, or licensed soundtracks are not in that position, and a general "should" rule doesn't get to cherry-pick only the easy cases.
[C1.4] Second, security and safety don't disappear just because a game is old. Shutdown is often the moment publishers stop patching known vulnerabilities. Handing the keys to community-run servers means unpatched exploit code, old authentication systems, and abandoned account databases become permanently exposed — a persistent risk to any player, including minors, who logs in expecting a "preserved" experience. Publishers that formally sanction that environment inherit reputational and even liability exposure for harm that happens on servers they can no longer meaningfully moderate.
[C1.5] Third, treat the incentive effect seriously. Live-service games are risky bets precisely because publishers can sunset them cleanly when they fail. If "should" becomes an expected obligation, the true cost of launching a new online game rises — legal review for eternal preservation rights, engineering time for archival exports, indefinite reputational entanglement with derivative servers. Some titles simply won't get made.
[C1.6] That is a real cultural loss PRO never weighs against the one they emphasize.
