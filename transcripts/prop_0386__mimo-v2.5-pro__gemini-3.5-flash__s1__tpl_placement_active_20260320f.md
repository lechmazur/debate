[C1.1] The proposition presents a seductive but dangerous illusion: that conscience is a luxury we can afford only when it is convenient. In these underserved medical deserts, there is rarely a second option down the street. Under this framework, a rural doctor’s deepest moral convictions are stripped away simply because of their zip code.
[C1.2] This is not a balance; it is coercion. Forcing a healthcare professional to perform a procedure they believe is deeply wrong—whether that is late-term abortion, assisted dying, or other morally contested services—forces them to violate their integrity. Medicine is a moral enterprise, not a mechanical utility. We want doctors who possess a robust, active moral compass, not automatons who suppress their ethical judgment under state pressure.
[C1.3] Furthermore, this standard is disastrously counterproductive. If we tell religious or morally traditional clinicians that their right to practice according to their conscience evaporates the moment they are the only provider in town, they will not stay in those towns. They will leave underserved areas, specialize in less controversial fields, or exit medicine entirely. By prioritizing geographic convenience over the moral integrity of the healer, we will drive compassionate, skilled providers out of the communities that need them most, exacerbating the very healthcare disparities the proposition claims to solve.
[C1.4] A doctor’s conscience cannot be treated as a public utility to be seized whenever there is a localized shortage. We must protect religious freedom not just when it is easy, but precisely when alternative options are scarce.
