[P1.1] AI has turned romance fraud from an individual crime into a consumer-market failure. The ordinary fraud model asks: who lied, and can we prosecute or recover? That model is necessary but badly matched to a world where one operator can generate thousands of plausible suitors—synthetic photos, cloned voices, fluent translation, round-the-clock attentiveness—and where the scam is delivered through platforms whose design and paid features shape who gets contacted and how trust forms. A distinct consumer-protection problem is the right frame because the harms and remedies are upstream and systemic, not merely transactional.
[P1.2] The motion does not abolish ordinary fraud liability; it adds a category. Ordinary fraud focuses on the deceptive act and the lost money. A consumer-protection lens focuses on the market conditions that make deception scalable and repeatable: fake-profile production, verification failures, engagement algorithms that reward prolonged contact, paid boosts and messaging, intimate data collection, and payment flows that let victims be re-targeted. It also recognizes that romance scams cause distinctive non-financial injury—emotional dependency, shame, isolation—that suppresses reporting and makes “just be more skeptical” a cruel and ineffective policy.
[P1.3] Consider a widow matched with an overseas “engineer.” AI-generated voice notes and seamless translation sustain months of daily intimacy before a customs-fee request. Ordinary fraud sees one transfer and, often, an untraceable foreign suspect. Consumer protection sees a pipeline: synthetic identity, platform amplification, payment rail, repeat victimization. Only the consumer lens gives governments authority over the businesses and design choices that make the pipeline profitable.
[P1.4] The decisive question is not whether deception is involved—it is which framework can prevent mass synthetic-intimacy harms and provide redress when prosecution is impractical. Treating AI romance scams as ordinary fraud alone leaves regulators fighting the last crime after the money and trust are gone. A distinct consumer-protection approach targets the market before the next victim is groomed.

Governments should prosecute it as fraud and regulate synthetic content and platform accountability generally, not create a special romance aisle in consumer law.
