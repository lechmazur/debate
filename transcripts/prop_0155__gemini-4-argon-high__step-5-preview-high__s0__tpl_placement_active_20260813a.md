[P1.1] In a free society, suspicion must precede surveillance. Police walking down a street cannot force every passerby to show identification just in case someone is wanted. Real-time facial recognition does exactly that, silently and at scale. By scanning every face at a protest, street corner, or transit hub, live biometric tracking turns public life into a perpetual criminal lineup.
[P1.2] The law must therefore draw a bright line: ban real-time facial recognition, while permitting post-incident searches only with a judicial warrant.
[P1.3] Real-time surveillance destroys practical anonymity. When the state can automatically log who attends a political rally, visits a clinic, or enters a house of worship, freedoms of assembly and association wither. Worse, live alerts force split-second street encounters. Because algorithms still struggle with poor lighting, awkward angles, and demographic disparities, a real-time false match does not stay on a screen—it puts an innocent commuter at the barrel of a gun before any detective can verify the lead.
[P1.4] Yet a total ban would needlessly blind investigators after a serious crime occurs. If an armed robbery or kidnapping is caught on security footage, police have a compelling need to identify the perpetrator from that static image. Here, the warrant requirement is the crucial safeguard. Requiring probable cause before a neutral judge prevents casual fishing expeditions against activists or personal targets.
[P1.5] It forces police to demonstrate a concrete crime and a valid evidentiary basis before running a biometric search, and it ensures time to corroborate the computer's lead with independent evidence before making an arrest.
[P1.6] To reject this balance, the opposition must either defend a live biometric dragnet, strip away judicial oversight, or block detectives from solving violent crimes. We do not have to choose between a surveillance state and investigative paralysis. Protect the public square from automated mass tracking, and bind post-crime investigations to the constitutional discipline of a warrant.
