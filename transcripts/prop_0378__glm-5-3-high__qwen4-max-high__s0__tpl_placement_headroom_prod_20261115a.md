[P1.1] Adult friendship is collapsing quietly, and we have organized our entire society as if it were nobody's business. That is the proposition, and it survives on three facts.
[P1.2] First, the difficulty is real and structural, not a failure of personal effort. The classic sociological finding is that friendships form through repeated, unplanned interaction in shared settings — what researchers call proximity and repeated exposure. We used to get this for free: neighborhoods, congregations, bowling leagues, extended families nearby. Those settings have eroded through suburban design, longer commutes, later marriage, more mobility, and the move of social life onto screens that mimic connection while removing the physical scaffolding underneath it.
[P1.3] Surveys consistently show adults reporting fewer close friends than decades ago, with striking shares saying they have none at all. You cannot self-help your way out of a structure that no longer supplies the occasions for friendship.
[P1.4] Second, the costs are public, so the remedy cannot be private. Loneliness is associated in the research literature with elevated mortality risk comparable to well-known behavioral risk factors, worse cardiovascular and immune outcomes, and higher rates of depression. Surgeons general in more than one country have publicly labeled loneliness a health epidemic. A single isolated resident costs health systems and employers real money through absenteeism, turnover, and chronic disease.
[P1.5] When a problem generates diffuse public costs, "handle it yourself" is not a policy — it is a subsidy paid by everyone else.
[P1.6] Third, we already accept this logic elsewhere. We regulate parks, libraries, and public transit precisely because private life needs public infrastructure to function. Nobody says marriage is purely private and therefore family law should not exist. Friendship is the last major relational sphere we pretend requires no institutional support.
[P1.7] The proposition asks only that workplaces, cities, and public institutions treat friendship as a policy concern — designing third places, scheduling genuine social time, funding community infrastructure. Modest, tested tools for a serious public harm.
