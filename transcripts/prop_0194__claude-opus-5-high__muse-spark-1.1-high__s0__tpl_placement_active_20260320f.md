[P1.1] Democracies have a structural blind spot, and it is not stupidity or malice. It is arithmetic. A politician's horizon is the next election; a nuclear waste repository, a collapsing aquifer, a warming ocean, a pension system quietly hollowed out — these bills arrive after the voters who could punish anyone are gone. The people who will pay are not simply outvoted.
[P1.2] They are not in the room. So today's majority already exercises a silent, absolute veto over them: the veto of the fait accompli. The question is not whether an unelected constraint on present majorities should exist. It is whether the constraint runs one way only.
[P1.3] Advisory future-generations bodies have been tried, and their record is the strongest argument for the motion. Wales created a Future Generations Commissioner; Hungary once had an ombudsman for future generations. Where such offices lack teeth, they produce excellent reports that ministries thank them for and then file. Advice without leverage does not change a decision that is politically convenient today.
[P1.4] Binding power does — mostly not by being used, but by forcing officials to satisfy it upstream, the way constitutional review changes how legislation is drafted long before any court strikes anything down.
[P1.5] And democracies already do this. We give central bankers binding control of monetary policy precisely because we distrust our own short-term impulses. We let constitutional courts void popular statutes. We entrench treaty commitments and debt rules.
[P1.6] Nobody calls these the death of self-government; we call them the machinery by which self-government keeps its promises.
