[C1.1] The motion looks like a narrow transparency rule, but its practical effect is to make monitoring less trustworthy and to replace substantive fairness with compliance theater.
[C1.2] Mandating disclosure invites gaming. A monitoring system only works if it observes ordinary behavior rather than behavior engineered for the metric. Tell warehouse workers exactly which movements trigger “idle time,” or call-center staff how “resolution” is scored, and rational employees will stage activity, avoid difficult calls, and hit the disclosed target without improving work. Goodhart’s law applies: a disclosed metric stops measuring what it was supposed to measure.
[C1.3] PRO calls this accountability, but it would corrupt the data that lets algorithms reduce arbitrary manager favoritism.
[C1.4] Worker-level notice also promises more meaning than it delivers. Many scoring systems involve shifting combinations of signals; a generic notice about “data and conduct” can imply false precision or burden employees with details they cannot use. What workers actually need is narrower: clear, plain-language reasons after a consequential decision, access to underlying facts, and genuine human appeal. The proposed universal obligation expands beyond that and invites disputes over every productivity dashboard.
[C1.5] The regulatory side is worse. A small or mid-sized firm cannot produce audit-grade documentation for every scheduling optimizer, task timer, or performance dashboard. The likely response is not better systems: it is avoiding them and reverting to manager discretion, which is more opaque and more biased, or issuing generic notices that satisfy no one. For large employers, concentrating model details in regulator files increases trade-secret and cybersecurity exposure. “Proportionate and confidential” is a wish, not a workable statutory boundary; once any metric using an algorithm is in scope, definitions will be litigated and compliance costs will fall hardest on smaller employers.
[C1.6] The better path is outcome-based: prohibit discriminatory or unsafe uses, require meaningful explanation and appeal when algorithmic decisions hurt someone, and let regulators investigate actual harms. Accountability should mean a worker can contest a decision, not that every scoring formula becomes a target.
