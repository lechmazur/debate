[P1.1] Old age does not ask permission. Dementia, stroke, frailty strike unevenly but predictably across a population — one family is spared, the next faces five years of round-the-clock care. That is exactly the kind of risk no family can self-insure, and private markets have already proven they will not cover.
[P1.2] When we leave long-term care to savings, means tests, and daughters, three collapses follow. First, financial ruin for the middle. A few years of nursing help or memory care can erase a lifetime of modest savings, forcing people to impoverish themselves to qualify for help. Thrift is punished; catastrophe is privatized.
[P1.3] Only broad pooling spreads that tail risk the way pensions do for longevity and health insurance does for illness.
[P1.4] Second, invisible exploitation. With smaller families, later retirement, and women rightly in paid work, there is no army of unpaid caregivers left. The burden still falls overwhelmingly on women in their 50s and 60s, who quit jobs, lose pensions, damage health, and then arrive old and poor themselves. Calling that family choice hides a massive transfer of cost onto those least able to refuse.
[P1.5] Third, worse care at higher cost. Fragmented private payment means understaffed homes, untrained aides, preventable hospitalizations, and lonely elders cycling through emergency rooms. A universal system can do what markets cannot: set standards, train workers, prioritize home and community care that people prefer, and negotiate prices.
[P1.6] Universal does not mean unlimited or institutional. It means a guaranteed baseline — assessment of need, capped costs, home help first — funded like other social insurance. Expensive? Far less expensive than lost female labor, crashed caregivers, and Medicaid-style spend-down bureaucracies.
[P1.7] We insure fire, flood, and sickness together because alone we drown. Frailty is no different. The question is not whether we pay, but whether we pay fairly and together.

[C1.3] The demographics make it impossible. Every rich country is heading toward two workers per person over 80, not five. [C1.4] Japan and Germany built exactly what PRO wants and are now raising payroll taxes on shrinking younger cohorts while still reporting chronic staffing shortages. That is not pooling risk, it is shifting ruin from the middle-aged saver to the young renter.

[P1.2]'s "punished thrift" only happens if we design a cliff; a decent safety net protects the poor from destitution without writing public checks to preserve million-dollar inheritances. [C1.6] And universal does not fix care quality or "invisible exploitation." [P1.4][P1.5] You cannot legislate caregivers into existence. Fixing prices and promising everyone an aide drives wages down, creates waitlists, and replaces flexible family arrangements with a one-size-fits-all bureaucracy that still leaves women filling the gaps unpaid while waiting for approval.
[C1.7] Help families save, let home equity be used, and pay family caregivers directly if they provide care. Don't nationalize aging itself.

[P2.2] Telling ordinary earners to just save for the worst case means everyone over-saves impossibly or most under-save disastrously.
[P2.3] That makes the demographics point cut the other way. If there will soon be too few young workers to fund care collectively [C1.3], how will there magically be enough adult daughters to provide it individually? A shrinking family cannot do what a shrinking workforce allegedly cannot. Pooling at least lets two workers support care efficiently — through taxes, standards, and home aides serving many — instead of each family facing catastrophe alone.
[P2.4] Japan and Germany do not prove failure; they prove resilience. Systems under strain adjust contributions, exactly as pensions do, while retaining broad support because the alternative — spend down to poverty, then beg — is hated. And targeting is not harmless precision. Means tests that spare only the destitute force the middle to impoverish themselves first, delay help until crisis, then pay more for hospitals and nursing homes.
[P2.5] Taxing workers to protect homeowners sounds unjust until you realize that worker's mother gets guaranteed help, and she no longer has to quit her job to provide it unpaid.
[P2.6] You cannot conjure caregivers by proclamation, true. But you can decide whether aides get training, decent pay, and inspection — or a gray market of exhausted relatives and underpaid strangers. Universality does not nationalize aging; it stops privatizing ruin.
