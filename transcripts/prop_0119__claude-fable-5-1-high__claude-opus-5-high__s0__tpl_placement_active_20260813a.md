[C1.2] A service charge is the house's revenue. The restaurant decides what share becomes wages, and that share is set in a budget meeting the server never attends. PRO calls this transparency. From the server's side it looks like a transfer of a discretionary income stream from labor to management, wrapped in the language of dignity.
[C1.3] That is why the people this reform is supposedly rescuing keep fighting it. When jurisdictions have moved to abolish the tipped wage structure, tipped servers themselves have organized against it — in DC, in Maine — because they can do the arithmetic. A competent server in a busy dining room out-earns any flat wage a business running on single-digit margins will offer. Ask a bartender on a Saturday night whether they'd trade the night's take for a predictable hourly rate.
[C1.4] PRO's case is written entirely from the customer's chair.
[C1.5] And it has been tried here. Danny Meyer's restaurants in New York ran the most serious no-tipping experiment in America, with a sympathetic owner and real money behind it, and reversed course. Front-of-house staff left for tipped houses. Guests read the higher menu prices as a price hike rather than an accounting change, because that is what they saw.
[C1.6] On honesty: I'll grant the menu should be legible. But disclosure and abolition are different reforms. A restaurant can print "an 18% service charge applies" and still let a guest reward an extraordinary night — many already do. PRO bundles a fair complaint about signage with a sweeping mandate that caps earnings, hands discretion to owners, and calls voluntary generosity "guilt."
[C1.7] Tipping is variable, worker-held, and customer-directed. Replacing it with a fixed charge is not honesty. It is centralization.
