[P1.1] Judge urban freeways by what they do to the city, not by cars per hour. Inside a city, a freeway is not a neutral pipe: it removes blocks from the tax base, cuts the street grid, concentrates noise and exhaust near homes, creates hostile crossings, and locks the public into maintaining bridges, walls, drains, and pavement. The metric should be net urban value: access and safety in exchange for the corridor’s health, fiscal, and land costs.
[P1.2] On that test, widening fails. New lanes make driving briefly easier, which pulls more trips onto the facility, supports sprawling trips, and reproduces the bottleneck at the next ramp. A wider freeway also widens the scar: more land consumed, longer crossings, more pollution exposure, more stormwater, and a larger liability as the structure ages. It answers a peak-hour complaint by buying a permanent urban cost.
[P1.3] Removal changes the equation. Replace a severing highway with a boulevard, restored cross streets, transit priority, safe walking and cycling, and local access; traffic does not simply pile up forever. Trips reroute, shift time or mode, shorten, or vanish as land uses change. That is not anti-mobility; it is a better network.
[P1.4] A six-lane trench that turns a half-mile walk into a detour can become housing, shops, trees, bus lanes, and reconnected corners. The city gains places, not just vehicle capacity.
[P1.5] Because the motion is comparative, the bias should be clear: when a city faces an urban expressway and an expansion proposal, removal should be the default; widening, the exception requiring strong proof. Widening mistakes temporary throughput for lasting accessibility. Removal does what expansion cannot: returns scarce urban land to multiple uses. CON will point to congestion, but congestion is the signal of scarce road space in a dense city.
[P1.6] Widening suppresses that signal by consuming more city; removal treats the city as the destination, not an obstacle to through traffic.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to prefer demolition over expansion, but it starts by grading the city with a rigged rubric. [P1.1] judges freeways by land value and noise while ignoring the primary function of any urban corridor: moving people and goods efficiently. A city is an engine of economic exchange, and that engine runs on reliable access. Measured by total regional mobility—the ability of a nurse or delivery driver to reach opportunity—widening often delivers more value per dollar than removal, which merely displaces congestion onto surface streets never designed for freeway volumes.
[C1.2] [P1.2] claims widening "reproduces the bottleneck," but that logic cuts both ways. Remove a freeway and trips do not vanish; they reroute onto arterials where stop-and-go traffic raises travel time, fuel burn, and localized emissions in the neighborhoods [P1.3] claims to protect. A boulevard cannot absorb a six-lane freeway's throughput without signalized intersections that choke at peak hours. The result is not a "better network" but a slower, more polluted one, with the burden falling on communities that rely on cars because transit is thin.
[C1.3] Removal also carries a fiscal mirage. Demolishing a viaduct or filling a trench costs billions and years of disruption—money that could fund transit, housing, or bridge repair. Widening an existing corridor, especially with targeted bottleneck fixes like auxiliary lanes, preserves sunk investment and adds capacity where demand is proven. [P1.5] wants removal as the "default," but a default of destruction ignores that most urban freeways are mid-life assets serving critical freight and emergency routes.
[C1.4] Removing them more often than widening them would strand drivers, choke commerce, and shrink the job radius for low-income workers who live beyond transit reach.
[C1.5] The choice is not between a scar and a garden, but between improving a vital artery and gambling on a costly experiment that often leaves surface streets worse than before. Cities should widen strategically and remove only at true end-of-life—not "more often" as policy.

Meanwhile, the housing promised on the old right-of-way [P1.4] too often arrives as high-end development that displaces the very residents removal was meant to help. Strategic widening at proven bottlenecks preserves the job radius for workers who cannot afford to live near the core, and it does so without betting the city's budget on a surface-street experiment.
[C2.3] On the ledger [P2.5], PRO treats future maintenance as a certain bill while ignoring that demolition is an immediate, certain, multibillion-dollar hit that destroys sunk infrastructure and generates massive waste. Removal is not a neutral reset; it is a gamble that surface streets can absorb what they structurally cannot, while the city takes on debt and years of construction disruption. The prudent default is to improve the artery that exists; removal must remain the rare exception at true end-of-life, not the norm the motion demands.
