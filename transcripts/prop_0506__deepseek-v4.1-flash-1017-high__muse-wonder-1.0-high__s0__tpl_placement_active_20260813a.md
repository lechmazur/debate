[P1.1] Civil liability should attach when a platform has actual, repeated notice that a synthetic scam is running on its service and still does nothing. The motion is deliberately narrow. It does not make platforms insurers of all AI fraud, punish a single ambiguous report, or demand omniscience. It says: when the person whose face or voice is cloned, multiple victims, and perhaps a bank have repeatedly verified the same scam, a platform cannot keep profiting from it with impunity.
[P1.2] That trigger matters. A cloned executive voice demanding a wire transfer; a fake video of a public figure promoting a token; a deepfake of a doctor selling a cure—these are not ordinary moderation calls. Repeated verified reports give the platform actual knowledge, corroboration, and a clear target. It has the account history, upload logs, payment signals, and technical capacity to suspend, label, preserve evidence, and warn users.
[P1.3] The fraudster is often anonymous and overseas; the platform is the identifiable, solvent party controlling distribution. Liability makes the cheapest cost avoider act.
[P1.4] The alternative is moral hazard by design. A platform can internalize the engagement and ad revenue from a scam while externalizing the victim's loss, and face no civil consequence for ignoring the same verified warning again and again. That is not a free-speech safeguard; it is a subsidy for deliberate blindness. Liability would be calibrated: prompt good-faith action creates a safe harbor, and damages should track the failure to act after notice—not the mere hosting of uncertain content.
[P1.5] Synthetic impersonation is especially dangerous because it borrows a trusted face or voice and scales instantly. Individual victims cannot subpoena logs or chase offshore accounts; platforms can act in hours. The central question is therefore not whether platforms must perfect AI fraud detection. It is whether they may treat repeated verified reports as optional.
[P1.6] A legal system that answers yes leaves the most capable actor with the weakest incentive. That is the case for liability.

[C1.1] The apparent narrowness rests entirely on “verified.” A report is not a finding, and repetition is not proof. Who verifies—the person allegedly impersonated, multiple complainants, a bank, or the platform itself? Each can be mistaken or self-interested. A disputed endorsement can be recast as a deepfake, edited real footage reported as synthetic, and coordinated complainants can manufacture repetition.
[C1.2] Detection tools offer probabilities about origin, not legal conclusions about authorization, parody, or fraud. Treating volume as actual knowledge makes the platform judge of identity and licensing disputes it did not create.
[C1.3] Once damages attach, rational platforms will delete first and investigate later, if at all. Leaving material up risks liability for another person’s fraud losses; removing it risks comparatively little. That asymmetry sweeps away lawful parody and satire, journalism replaying a scam clip to expose it, commentary on public figures, and authorized promotions embroiled in contract disputes. A safe harbor for prompt, good-faith action is illusory when plaintiffs can always claim the response was too slow, too limited, or failed to prevent reuploads.
[C1.4] The cost of proving good faith is itself the incentive to over-remove.
[C1.5] The platform also did not cause the loss in the way liability assumes. The scammer fabricates trust, induces payment, and usually collects through an external bank account or wallet. After removal, the same operator can return under a new name, with an altered voice, or through private channels within minutes. Holding the host liable for later transfers requires speculation that a victim saw this post, would not have found a copy elsewhere, and would have been saved by faster action.
[C1.6] The result will be entrenchment, not safety. Large platforms can afford automated takedowns and legal queues; smaller forums and new entrants will pre-screen speakers, demand identification, or exit. Fraud migrates to less visible spaces while competition and lawful speech contract. Pursue impersonators and payment chokepoints under fraud law rather than converting contested reports into open-ended damages.
