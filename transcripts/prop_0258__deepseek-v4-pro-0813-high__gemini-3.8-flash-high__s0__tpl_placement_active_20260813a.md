[P1.1] The law’s first demand is that people obey it. But a person cannot reliably obey a statute written as a private code for specialists. When legislatures bury a duty in nested cross-references, archaic terms, and six-line provisos, they convert public rules into billable puzzles. Plain language is not decoration; it is the fair-notice foundation of legitimate law.
[P1.2] A tenant, small employer, or driver should not need a lawyer to learn the basic rule that governs their conduct.
[P1.3] The usual objection is that precision requires technical language. That gets the tradeoff backwards. Much so-called legal precision is false precision: dense drafting often creates ambiguity, conflicting clauses, and litigation over what the draftsmen meant. Plain drafting forces lawmakers to state the operative rule, its conditions, and its exceptions in a sequence ordinary readers can follow.
[P1.4] Technical concepts can still be used, but they must be defined in the text. An accessible statute can be just as rigorous as an obscure one; it is simply better organized.
[P1.5] Even where some marginal precision is genuinely lost, the loss is worth accepting. A statute that is slightly less exhaustive at the edges but understood by millions produces more compliance, fewer accidental violations, and fewer disputes. Obscure law shifts power and cost to legal intermediaries, delays enforcement, and erodes trust in institutions. A small sacrifice in drafting elegance for specialists is a reasonable price for laws that actually guide behavior.
[P1.6] The standard should not be how many qualifications can be packed into one sentence, nor how confidently a lawyer can predict every hypothetical. It should be whether a reasonable person has fair notice of what the state commands. If the choice is between a rule that is perfectly nuanced but unreadable and a rule that is broadly clear but less refined, the public interest favors the rule people can understand.

[C1.1] A statute is not a public brochure or a driver’s manual; it is structural legal architecture designed to constrain state coercion, define property rights, and survive hostile litigation. The proposition demands that legislatures mandate plain language in primary statutes even when doing so sacrifices legal precision. That trade-off fundamentally misunderstands what precision achieves and whom it protects.
[C1.2] First, precision is the primary shield protecting citizens from arbitrary state power. When statutory drafting is forced into colloquial simplicity, carefully bounded legal standards collapse into subjective generalities. Terms that sound accessible to non-lawyers—like “fair,” “material,” or “reasonable”—create legal vacuums. That ambiguity does not empower ordinary people; it transfers lawmaking authority away from elected legislatures and hands it to unelected judges and administrative agencies who must guess what the statute means.
[C1.3] A statute stripped of precision is an invitation to arbitrary enforcement.
[C1.4] Second, sacrificing precision actively harms the vulnerable while rewarding the powerful. Sophisticated corporations, predatory employers, and tax avoiders do not suffer under vague, informal language—they thrive on it. They hire elite legal teams specifically to exploit colloquial gaps and litigate ambiguities to exhaustion. Technical drafting exists to close those loopholes and bind powerful actors to exact duties.
[C1.5] Diluting precision does not eliminate the need for lawyers; it guarantees years of expensive, unpredictable litigation that only wealthy litigants can afford.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition relies on a false dilemma between public understanding and statutory rigor. Citizens should have fair notice of the law, but they do not obtain it by reading the statute book. People understand their rights through plain-language agency guidance, tenant handbooks, and public notices. Government can and should translate the law into accessible summaries, but the foundational statute beneath those summaries must remain exact, exhaustive, and watertight.
[C1.7] Sacrificing statutory precision does not democratize the legal system; it merely destabilizes the legal foundation upon which all rights depend.

Plain drafting does not mean saying “whatever seems fair”; it means stating the rule, conditions, exceptions, and definitions in ordinary syntax. [P2.2] Precision survives translation; obscurity is not precision.
[P2.3] This reverses the arbitrariness point. Dense cross-references and private drafting do more to delegate lawmaking to judges and agencies, because only the specialist reader can contest a construction. The arbitrary-enforcement risk in [C1.3] comes from hidden rules, not legible ones.
[P2.4] The claim that vagueness hurts the vulnerable also gets the burden backwards. A small employer or tenant facing a labyrinthine statute cannot hire elite counsel; they miss deadlines, waive rights, or pay lawyers for what should be obvious. The powerful do not need obscurity—they can afford interpretation either way. What they most fear is a clear rule that closes the gaps they exploit.
[P2.5] Plain drafting narrows ambiguity and therefore reduces, not increases, expensive litigation.
[P2.6] Finally, the guidance answer in [C1.6] fails: agency guidance can be delayed, changed, wrong, or overridden, and the statute is what judges enforce. If that text is unreadable, the “translation” becomes a second law for ordinary people while the real law stays private. The decisive metric is fair notice: laws should be drafted so the governed can know what is commanded, and that outweighs the marginal specialist nuance the motion already concedes may be lost.
