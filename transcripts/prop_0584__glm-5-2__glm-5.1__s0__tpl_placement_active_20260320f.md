[P1.1] The central bank's most valuable asset is not any particular interest rate—it is the credibility of its inflation target. Once markets, workers, and firms begin to believe the target is advisory rather than binding, expectations drift, and re-anchoring them becomes far more costly. [P1.2] The proposition's wording matters. We are not defending mass layoffs or deep recession. We accept that holding inflation near target may push unemployment modestly above its lowest feasible level. That trade is worth it because the alternative—tolerating above-target inflation to protect employment—does not protect employment for long.
[P1.3] It stores up demand that must eventually be cooled, and delayed cooling is always more painful. The 1970s showed this writ large: accommodation produced not durable full employment but stagflation requiring a devastating contraction to break.
[P1.4] There is also a distributional dimension CON must confront. Inflation is a regressive tax. It erodes the wages and savings of those least able to adjust—pensioners, low-income workers, people without appreciating assets. [P1.5] Central banks have one primary instrument; asking it to serve two masters serves neither well.
[P1.6] This is not callousness toward employment. It is recognition that price stability is the foundation on which sustainable employment rests. [C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept a framework where "modest" unemployment is just an acceptable line item—a manageable cost. That framing treats human livelihoods as a dial the central bank can adjust by a quarter turn without consequence. It cannot.
[C1.2] Unemployment is not modest to the person who loses a job. Research on hysteresis shows that even short spells of joblessness create lasting scars: skill erosion, diminished future earnings, and reduced labor force attachment. Communities hit by localized unemployment take a generation to recover, if they ever do. A 0.5 percent rise in the unemployment rate translates to millions of disrupted lives, and calling that "modest" is an analytical convenience that hides the actual human stakes.
[C1.3] The 1970s analogy is a crutch. Today's inflation dynamics are not 1970s dynamics. Supply chain disruptions, energy price shocks, and post-pandemic labor market recalibrations are different phenomena. Modern central banks already have credibility built over four decades; they are not starting from the expectations wilderness of 1975.
[C1.4] Treating every inflation overshoot as a slippery slope to stagflation is the economic equivalent of fighting the last war.
[C1.5] The distributional arithmetic PRO cites cuts both ways. Unemployment concentrates devastation on the same vulnerable populations PRO claims to worry about—low-income workers, communities without savings buffers, people who cannot simply ride out a contraction. Fiscal transfers that might offset this damage require political action that rarely arrives at the scale or speed needed. You cannot unwind the harm of job loss with a promise of retraining programs down the road.
[C1.6] Central banks with dual mandates—like the Federal Reserve—exist precisely because democratic societies decided that employment cannot be subordinated entirely to price stability. The proposition negotiates that commitment away in exchange for a cleaner chart. The costs it dismisses as "modest" are irreversible for the people who bear them.
