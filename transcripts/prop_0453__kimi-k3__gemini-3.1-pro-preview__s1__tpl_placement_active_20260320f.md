[P1.2] First, housing inflation is not ordinary inflation. Shelter is the one necessity households cannot substitute away from, and it eats the largest share of income for exactly those least able to absorb it: renters, the young, the lower-paid. When rents jump, there is no cheaper brand to switch to. The damage compounds — delayed household formation, workers priced out of job-rich cities, falling fertility, and a hardening line between those who owned before the boom and everyone after.

[P1.4] Second, recessions are recoverable; housing inflation is not. Output lost in a downturn typically returns within a few years; a price level that ratchets up never ratchets back. Credibility spent on tolerating "just this one" category of inflation must be repurchased at higher rates and harsher pain.
[P1.5] We saw it again recently — dismissing post-pandemic price surges as transitory let rent increases bake in that tightening is still chasing.
[P1.6] Third, central banks are not bystanders. A decade of near-zero rates and bond buying poured fuel on housing demand. Having helped light the fire, they cannot claim the hose belongs to someone else.
[P1.7] Expect the reply that housing is a supply problem — zoning, construction — and that rate hikes strangle building. But supply reform takes a decade; expectations unanchor in months. [P1.8] A recession costs a year of growth. Unaffordable shelter costs a generation.
