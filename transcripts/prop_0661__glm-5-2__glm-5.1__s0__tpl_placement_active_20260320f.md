[P1.1] The case for mandatory disclosure rests on a simple principle: you cannot contest, consent to, or regulate what you cannot see. Algorithmic monitoring and performance-scoring systems now govern hiring, firing, discipline, and daily work conditions for millions of workers — yet most workers have no idea these systems exist, let alone how they operate or what they measure.
[P1.2] Consider a warehouse worker whose pace is tracked by algorithm, whose breaks are scored against a benchmark, and whose termination is triggered automatically when a score falls below a threshold. Under current practice, that worker may never learn that a scoring system made the decision, what factors it weighed, or whether the inputs were accurate. They cannot challenge a score they have never seen. They cannot correct data they do not know is being collected.
[P1.3] The right to due process in employment becomes meaningless when the decision-maker is a black box.
[P1.4] The same opacity defeats regulators. Agencies charged with enforcing wage law, anti-discrimination protections, and workplace safety rules cannot audit systems they cannot inspect. An algorithm that penalizes bathroom breaks implicates labor standards. A scoring model trained on historical hiring data may encode racial or gender bias.
[P1.5] Without disclosure requirements, enforcement depends on whistleblowers and accidents — on harm already done.
[P1.6] Disclosure is not a radical new mandate. It is the extension of a principle already operating throughout law: that decisions affecting your rights demand reasoning you can examine. Employers already must explain termination decisions in many jurisdictions. Financial institutions must disclose credit-scoring factors.
[P1.7] Algorithmic management should not be the one domain where power operates in secret.
[P1.8] What CON must overcome is a structural asymmetry: employers choose, deploy, and tune these systems; workers and regulators bear the consequences. Mandatory disclosure is the minimum correction — not a ban, not micromanagement, just visibility. The burden on employers is paperwork. The burden on workers of continued secrecy is their livelihood.

Goodhart's Law is not theoretical: call center agents who learn the scoring system game average handle time by hanging up on complex calls.
[C1.3] Warehouse workers who know the pace threshold sprint early and coast near the line. The score improves; the work does not. Disclosure actively degrades the systems it exposes.
[C1.4] Existing law already provides the recourse PRO claims is missing. Wrongful termination, discrimination, and retaliation claims do not require understanding the algorithm—they require showing the outcome was unlawful. A worker fired by algorithm has the same legal avenues as one fired by a manager. Regulators enforcing wage law or safety standards can subpoena systems when harm is alleged; they do not need standing access to every scoring formula in advance.
[C1.5] The due-process analogy fails. Credit-scoring disclosures work because the factors are few, stable, and standardized across institutions. Workplace algorithms are numerous, firm-specific, and constantly iterated. [C1.6] The real asymmetry is not secrecy versus transparency. It is that mandatory disclosure destroys competitive advantage, incentivizes metric gaming, and piles regulatory burden on employers—all to solve a problem that existing employment law already addresses.
