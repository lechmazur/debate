[P1.1] The central question is whether autonomous taxi networks become public infrastructure in practice or merely a premium convenience optimized for profitable corridors. Robotaxis do not operate in a vacuum: they use public streets, rely on public safety systems, and benefit from curb access, mapping data, and regulatory permission. In exchange, they should not be allowed to cherry-pick only high-margin districts while treating low-income and low-demand neighborhoods as service deserts.
[P1.2] The proposition asks for reasonably similar reliability, not identical dispatch density or equal profit per mile. It means a resident in a poorer neighborhood can expect a comparable chance of getting a ride within a reasonable window, not a system that reliably appears only downtown, near stadiums, or in affluent commercial zones. That standard is modest, enforceable, and consistent with how we regulate other networked transport services.
[P1.3] Without this rule, the predictable outcome is transport redlining. Conventional taxis and ride-hail markets already show how quickly drivers avoid areas perceived as less profitable or less safe. Autonomous fleets could make that exclusion more efficient, because algorithms can route around low-margin zones without visible individual refusals. The harm is severe because low-income households have less ability to absorb unreliable transport: missed shifts, medical appointments, childcare pickups, and job interviews are not inconveniences; they are material losses.
[P1.4] Reliable service in these neighborhoods also creates benefits for the whole system. More balanced demand reduces dead-heading back to rich cores, expands the customer base, and supports transit gaps where fixed routes are weak. Requirements can be implemented through operating permits, minimum availability metrics, coverage standards, and penalties tied to response-time parity. Fleets can rebalance vehicles, use pricing incentives, and coordinate with public agencies.
[P1.5] If we allow robotaxi networks to concentrate only where margins are highest, we entrench a two-tier mobility system. The proposition prevents that by making access, not just profitability, the governing standard.

[C1.1] The proposition treats robotaxis as if they were public transit, but they are capital-intensive businesses that must generate returns to survive. Forcing these fleets to maintain "reasonably similar reliability" in low-demand neighborhoods ignores the fundamental economics of autonomous transport. Robotaxis achieve viability through high utilization rates—vehicles must constantly move paying passengers to justify their enormous upfront costs, sensor suites, and remote monitoring. When regulators mandate service to areas with sparse demand, they create a structural drag: vehicles dead-head long distances to pick up occasional riders, then return empty, burning energy and capital without revenue while blocking streets and increasing congestion for everyone else.
[C1.2] Every minute a robotaxi spends traveling empty to serve a mandated zone is a minute it isn't serving actual customers or recharging for the next trip.
[C1.3] This isn't a minor inefficiency; it's a potential existential threat to the business model. If operators must cross-subsidize low-margin zones out of high-margin profits, they will either raise prices for everyone, reducing adoption and slowing the technology's maturation, or exit the market entirely. The result is worse than the status quo: instead of a robust premium service in viable corridors, we get no service at all, or a heavily subsidized system that merely replicates the underfunded public transit we already have.
[C1.4] A mandate that kills the goose laying the golden eggs serves no one, least of all the low-income residents who would lose access to any robotaxi service.
[C1.5] The proposition also conflates low demand with low income, ignoring that reliability requirements cannot override operational realities like road quality, charging infrastructure gaps, and elevated vandalism risks in certain areas. Forcing service into neighborhoods where autonomous vehicles face higher damage rates simply accelerates fleet degradation and raises insurance costs for the entire operation. If the goal is equitable mobility, the honest approach is direct subsidies or transit investment—not regulatory mandates that force private companies to operate against market signals. We should not sacrifice the efficiency that makes robotaxis transformative by pretending they are buses with better software.

[C2.1] My opponent reframes the mandate as a modest "reliability" standard, but this obscures the brutal arithmetic of fleet operations. When a robotaxi dead-heads ten miles to pick up a single passenger in a low-demand zone, then returns empty, it consumes roughly twenty minutes of capital, energy, and road space that could have served three or four actual rides in high-density corridors. Requiring "similar reliability" in sparse areas does not mean one extra car repositioning occasionally; it means maintaining a standing reserve of vehicles idling near neighborhoods where demand may never materialize.
[C2.2] That is not optimization—it is capital destruction.
[C2.3] The comparison to taxi regulation collapses under scrutiny. Traditional taxis are labor-intensive with low barriers to entry; a driver can absorb an unprofitable fare and move on. Robotaxis are capital-intensive machines requiring constant utilization to service debt and depreciation. When cities imposed taxi service obligations, they regulated an industry with thin margins and high driver turnover.
[C2.4] They did not regulate a technology whose entire value proposition rests on eliminating the human cost structure. You cannot impose bus-like obligations on a business model built to replace buses precisely because buses could not achieve profitability in low-density areas.
[C2.5] My opponent claims balanced networks reduce dead-heading, but this confuses directional imbalance with absolute scarcity. A vehicle moving from downtown to a suburb to pick up a return fare is still moving toward demand. A vehicle sent to patrol a low-demand neighborhood "just in case" is dead-heading by definition, burning resources while reducing availability for riders who actually called. The mandate does not create two-way demand; it forces supply to chase absence.
[C2.6] Finally, the subsidy point reverses the burden. If the goal is reliable service for low-income residents, pay for it directly through vouchers or transit subsidies rather than holding the entire fleet hostage to an unprofitable coverage requirement. A mandate that triggers fleet contraction or price spikes helps no one. The honest choice is between subsidizing access and destroying the service—not pretending regulation can conjure abundance from scarcity.
