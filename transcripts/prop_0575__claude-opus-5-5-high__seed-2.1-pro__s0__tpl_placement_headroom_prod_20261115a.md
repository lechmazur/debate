[P1.1] The question is simple: which policy actually changes how much people rest? A four-day week changes the default. Unlimited PTO changes only the permission. Retention and well-being depend on rest people actually take, not rest they are theoretically allowed.
[P1.2] Start with unlimited PTO's core flaw: it turns rest into a negotiation. With no clear allowance, employees guess what is acceptable by watching their manager and their most driven colleague. The safe guess is always "less." Many companies that adopted unlimited leave have reported that people take roughly the same or fewer days than before, and some have reverted for exactly that reason.
[P1.3] The policy also quietly benefits the employer: no accrued balance means no payout when someone leaves. A benefit that is cheapest when least used should not be trusted to fix burnout.
[P1.4] Unlimited PTO also hits the wrong people hardest. The anxious new hire, the caregiver afraid of looking uncommitted, and the employee under a demanding manager are the ones most likely to underuse it. The people who need rest most are the least able to claim it.
[P1.5] A four-day week solves this structurally. Friday off belongs to everyone, so no one has to ask, justify, or feel guilty. Because the whole team steps away together, nobody returns to a backlog created by colleagues who kept working. Work gets redesigned instead: fewer pointless meetings, sharper priorities.
[P1.6] Recovery becomes predictable, roughly fifty extra rest days a year, guaranteed.
[P1.7] Finally, the motion says pilot, and that matters. A pilot is measurable and reversible. Large trials in the UK and elsewhere saw most participating firms keep the schedule, with reported drops in burnout and resignations. Unlimited PTO offers no comparable test, only an open-ended promise.
[P1.8] Guaranteed rest beats permitted rest. That is why PRO wins.
