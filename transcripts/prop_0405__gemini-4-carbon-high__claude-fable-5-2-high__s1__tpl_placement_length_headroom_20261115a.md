[P1.1] When a shared e-bike or scooter rider strikes an elderly pedestrian on a sidewalk and speeds away, who pays the hospital bill? Under the current legal baseline, almost always the victim. Micromobility companies deploy thousands of heavy, motorized vehicles directly onto public walkways, collect per-minute fees that reward haste, and retreat behind ironclad user agreements shifting all blame onto riders. Because these shared vehicles lack visible license plates and casual riders rarely carry applicable liability insurance, injured pedestrians routinely face a total recovery dead end.
[P1.2] Giving fleet operators greater legal responsibility fixes a broken system that privatizes fleet profits while socializing physical harm.
[P1.3] Tort law exists to place responsibility on the party best positioned to prevent harm and spread risk. Unlike traditional bicycle shops or car rental agencies, shared micromobility companies exercise continuous, real-time digital control over their fleets. Through geofencing, sidewalk-detection sensors, and remote speed governors, operators possess practical tools to throttle speeds in restricted zones and curb sidewalk riding. Yet today, strict enforcement frustrates paying customers and cuts into ride volume.
[P1.4] As long as sidewalk collisions cost these companies nothing, safety technology remains a public-relations talking point rather than an operational priority. Legal liability aligns profit with prevention while ensuring commercial insurance covers the injuries that still occur.
[P1.5] Crucially, increasing responsibility on sidewalks and clearly restricted zones is targeted and fair. This does not blame companies for unpredictable traffic accidents in legitimate bike lanes; it applies solely where motorized transit is strictly forbidden and pedestrians reasonably expect sanctuary. Bystanders never signed a liability waiver just by walking out their front door. When companies profit by staging connected vehicles across dense urban walkways, requiring them to police off-limits zones and backstop pedestrian injuries is the bare minimum of accountability.

So "more" responsibility can only mean paying for a customer's deliberate lawbreaking, in precisely the places where the rule was clearest. The more obvious the rider's fault, the more the motion shifts it away.
[C1.3] That is backwards.
[C1.4] It also dulls the best deterrent we have. Only one person can see the pedestrian, the curb, and the sign: the one holding the handlebars. Tell riders that a corporate insurer absorbs the consequences and you have subsidized recklessness at the moment of decision.
[C1.5] PRO's answer is technology [P1.3], which overstates what the tools do. GPS drifts by several metres in dense streets, often the difference between a bike lane and the adjacent sidewalk. Sidewalk detection misfires. And abruptly cutting power on a moving vehicle can throw the rider or strand them in traffic.
[C1.6] Liability for outcomes companies cannot reliably control is not an incentive. It is a tax, paid through higher fares, shrunken service areas, or exit. Riders pushed back into cars are hardly good news for pedestrians.
[C1.7] Then consider the "recovery dead end" [P1.1]. A shared scooter is the least anonymous vehicle on the sidewalk. Each trip is tied to an account, a payment card, and a GPS trail. The private e-bike rider who hits someone truly vanishes.
[C1.8] The remedy for hit-and-runs is to make operators identify the rider and to require rider insurance through permits, so the rule-breaker is held to account rather than replaced.
[C1.9] Cities should enforce the rules, build lanes that give riders somewhere lawful to go, and pursue the person at fault. Responsibility should stay with the hands on the throttle.
