[P1.1] Keeping petrol cheap for everyone does not protect the poor; it buys fuel for every driver, including those least in need. The proposition replaces that indiscriminate giveaway with a better bargain: let prices reflect costs, and deliver direct support to households that cannot reasonably absorb the shock.
[P1.2] Universal fuel subsidies are badly aimed. Assistance arrives in proportion to fuel consumed, so affluent households with cars, large vehicles and frequent travel can capture far more than poor families using public transport. Meanwhile, the fiscal cost crowds out health, schools and income support—the services poorer citizens depend on. A fixed sum spent through a transfer can put more money in a vulnerable household’s hands than the same sum dispersed across every litre sold.
[P1.3] Suppressing prices does not abolish costs; it hides and multiplies them. When controlled prices fall below supply costs, governments accumulate arrears, suppliers lose incentives to invest, and shortages, queues, rationing or illicit resale become plausible outcomes. Subsidised fuel also encourages waste and, where prices differ across borders, leakage and smuggling. Consumers may celebrate a low posted price while paying through taxes, unreliable supply and deteriorating public services.
[P1.4] Targeted cash transfers break that chain. They protect purchasing power without giving every business and household an energy discount. Because cash is flexible, a family can spend it on transport, food or electricity according to its actual needs. Preserving the price signal also makes efficient vehicles, public transport and reduced waste worthwhile, rather than behaviour the state pays people to avoid.
[P1.5] This should be a managed phase-out, not an overnight experiment. Transfers should begin before prices rise, use multiple enrolment channels to reach informal workers and people without bank accounts, and be reviewed as living costs change. Savings can fund delivery and support for genuinely affected groups. Transparent adjustment beats permanent controls whose failures are harder to see.
[P1.6] The choice is not help or abandonment, but help measured by fuel consumption or by need. Only the latter protects vulnerable households without draining public finances and distorting energy markets.

[C1.1] The flaw is not the goal of protecting poor households. It is the substitution: dismantle a universal, automatic price protection and trust a filtered cash bureaucracy to catch everyone before fuel costs spread through the economy.
[C1.2] Fuel is not just a purchase by drivers; it is in bus fares, trucking, irrigation pumps, refrigerated medicines, generators, and the commute to work. A means-tested transfer can compensate a listed family, but not every fare, delivery, stall, or small firm carrying the shock. The proposition's “fixed sum” [P1.2] treats the injury as an income gap. It is also an economy-wide pass-through.
[C1.3] If cash is late, under-indexed, or tied to an old poverty list, families receive money with one hand and pay higher transport and food prices with the other.
[C1.4] Targeting is not an implementation footnote. Universal subsidies leak upward, but they do not exclude the informal worker, displaced family, rural household with stale records, or commuter barely above a cutoff. False inclusion wastes money; false exclusion removes mobility, food access, and work. The strongest feature of a broad price control is that it needs no one to prove they deserve protection: it reaches people at the pump, taxi rank, and market without documents, bank accounts, payment systems, appeal offices, or updated rolls.
[C1.5] The proposition says “transfers should begin before prices rise” [P1.5]. That assumes the very administrative speed and fiscal reliability usually weakest where fuel subsidies matter. Cash can be frozen, captured, or left unchanged in a budget crisis; liberalized fuel prices rise immediately and visibly. Compensation is a promise, the price hike is a fact.
[C1.6] Governments can reduce leakages, add targeted top-ups, and invest in transit. But the plan requires phasing out universal protection and replacing it. For a cost embedded in every necessity, universality is not merely waste; it is the delivery mechanism that follows the shock. Replacing that shock absorber with an eligibility test is not reform, but exposure.

[P2.1] The opposition compares an idealised price control with a badly run transfer scheme. That is not a fair comparison. A government too weak to pay cash reliably is also fiscally vulnerable when financing below-cost fuel; arrears, shortages, rationing and illicit resale are administrative failures too. A cheap posted price protects nobody if fuel is unavailable or the queue consumes a working day.
[P2.2] Their central mistake is to call fuel subsidies “automatic” protection. They automatically follow fuel purchases, not need or economy-wide harm. The informal worker without a car gets little benefit at the pump, while the affluent driver receives assistance on every litre. Nor does a subsidy guarantee lower bus fares, food prices or medicine deliveries; those depend on whether savings are actually passed through.
[P2.3] Cash lets the affected household meet whichever costs have risen, rather than hoping protection travels through several markets. Protecting households does not require insulating every business from fuel costs indefinitely.
[P2.4] [C1.4] also narrows “targeted” into a rigid means test using one stale poverty list. That is not required by the motion. Targeting can combine income information with geographic and categorical coverage, multiple enrolment routes and review; it need not create a benefits cliff for the commuter just above a cutoff. Most importantly, the phase-out is sequenced: payments and access should be operating before price changes, with adjustment staged rather than abrupt.
[P2.5] CON treats a safeguard stated in the proposition as though it were an oversight.
[P2.6] Their alternative—retain the universal subsidy and add targeted top-ups—keeps the regressive, fiscally hungry base and simply spends more to patch it. It still rewards higher consumption and leaves less room for the transfers and public services they want.
[P2.7] The clash is therefore not certainty versus bureaucracy. It is guaranteed misdirection versus a system whose coverage can be tested and corrected. Cash has exclusion risks; universal subsidies have guaranteed upward leakage and no mechanism to match support to need. A managed phase-out addresses a remediable delivery risk without preserving a structurally unjust one.

[P5.1] The decisive question is what “universal protection” guarantees. A fuel subsidy guarantees a discount on every litre sold—including to affluent drivers. It does not guarantee fuel at the pump, a lower bus fare, cheaper delivered food, or help calibrated to a vendor’s losses. CON’s shock absorber therefore follows consumption and hopes protection travels onward; it does not automatically follow people.
[P5.2] That distinction defeats the claim that exclusion is uniquely a cash problem. An unlisted household can be enrolled and paid; a household missed by fare and freight pass-through has no claim at all under CON’s system. While delivery failures can be tested and corrected, upward leakage and the diversion of money occur by design, every day the subsidy remains.
[P5.3] The phase-out answers timing directly: instalments clear before each price step, coverage can be broad without becoming consumption-based, and support recurs as living costs change. CON’s alternative keeps paying for rich consumption first, then demands more money for top-ups and audits.
[P5.4] So choose protection aimed at people, not a reassuring label that conceals its guarantees. Cash risks are governable; universal misdirection is permanent. Protect households before prices move, and stop calling every litre sold a measure of need.
