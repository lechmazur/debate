[P1.1] For over a century, African economies have remained trapped in an extractive architecture designed to feed northern industrial centers. Under this legacy, the continent exports unrefined cocoa, crude oil, bauxite, and critical minerals for pennies, only to import back finished chocolate, refined fuel, and consumer goods at steep markups. This paradigm bleeds domestic value, exports employment, and leaves national balance sheets hostage to volatile commodity cycles.
[P1.2] Prioritizing intra-African trade and manufacturing is the only structural antidote.
[P1.3] First, value capture drives sustainable development. Primary extraction produces enclave economies: capital-intensive mines and oilfields that generate foreign-held rents but negligible domestic jobs. Manufacturing, conversely, creates powerful supply-chain linkages, drives technology adoption, and generates the millions of productive jobs Africa’s surging youth demographic urgently requires. A continent cannot industrialize by selling its soil; it must process what it produces.
[P1.4] Second, trade partners dictate trade composition. Historically, commerce with distant wealthy nations has been overwhelmingly raw and extractive. In sharp contrast, intra-African trade is already far more diversified, leaning heavily toward manufactured items, processed food, textiles, and light machinery. Deepening continental integration—connecting a market of over one billion consumers—delivers the scale and regional value chains domestic producers need to become globally competitive.
[P1.5] This priority is not about isolationism; it is about self-determination. Continued dependence on sending unprocessed dirt and oil to former colonial powers cements economic subordination and terms-of-trade decline. By pivoting inward to build manufacturing capacity and continental supply networks, African nations can finally convert their vast resource wealth into shared, durable industrial power.
