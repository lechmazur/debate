[P1.1] For a woman with a heavy burden of mutated mitochondrial DNA, each pregnancy is a gamble she cannot control. She may watch her child develop normally for months, then develop Leigh syndrome or MELAS — seizures, strokes, muscle failure, blindness, and progressive brain damage, often ending in death in childhood. There is no cure, only care managing crises as organs fail.
[P1.2] Mitochondrial replacement therapy breaks that inheritance. Doctors transfer the parents' nuclear DNA — the DNA that determines appearance, traits, and identity — into a donor egg whose nucleus has been removed but whose healthy mitochondria remain. The child is genetically the parents' child, but with working instead of faulty cellular batteries.
[P1.3] That option matters because the alternatives do not solve the problem. Pre-implantation genetic testing works for some lower-risk cases, but mitochondrial load can shift unpredictably between egg, embryo, and tissues, so a reassuring test can still produce a severely affected child. Egg donation prevents disease, but ends the mother's genetic link. For the highest-risk families, replacement is the only way to have their own healthy child.
[P1.4] Public funding is therefore not a luxury subsidy; it is the condition for fair and safe access. These families are few, so budget impact is small, while lifetime care for a severely affected child — intensive care, ventilation, feeding support, repeated admissions, and round-the-clock care — is immense. Without public funding, only the wealthy can buy prevention, everyone else faces medical tourism to poorly regulated clinics or accepts preventable suffering. Funding inside a licensed public system allows strict eligibility, long-term follow-up, and use only for severe disease.
[P1.5] A mitochondrial donor is not a third parent any more than a blood donor is. Mitochondria carry only a tiny set of genes for energy production; they do not make the child someone else's. Refusing to fund this because it changes mitochondria would leave known, devastating disease in place to protect an abstract line that harms real children.
