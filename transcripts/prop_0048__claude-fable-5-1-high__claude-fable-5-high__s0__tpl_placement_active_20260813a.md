[P1.1] A nurse with four patients can notice the one whose breathing has quietly changed. A nurse with eight cannot. That is not a debate about management philosophy; it is arithmetic about attention, and every hospital administrator already knows it. The question is who gets to decide how many patients that nurse carries when the budget tightens.
[P1.2] Leaving it to administrators fails for a predictable reason: nursing is the largest controllable cost on a hospital ledger, while the harms of understaffing are invisible on that same ledger. A missed deterioration, a pressure ulcer, a medication error, a fall at 3 a.m. show up months later as a readmission, a lawsuit, or a death certificate that never says "understaffed." When the benefit is booked immediately and the cost is diffuse and delayed, discretion systematically drifts one way. We recognize this pattern everywhere else: pilot duty hours, building codes, fire exits.
[P1.3] We do not trust the party paying the bill to set the safety floor, because the incentive to shave it is structural, not a matter of bad character.
[P1.4] The evidence tracks the mechanism. California has mandated ratios for roughly two decades; comparative studies found nurses there carried fewer patients, reported lower burnout, and hospitals with better ratios showed lower surgical mortality. Queensland in Australia legislated ratios more recently, and follow-up research pointed to fewer deaths and readmissions, with savings from avoided complications offsetting much of the wage cost. Predicted mass closures did not materialize.
[P1.5] Mandates also fix the shortage they are accused of worsening. Nurses do not leave the profession because ratios are too strict; they leave because they were given twelve patients and told to cope. Guaranteed floors are why California pulled nurses back into bedside work.
[P1.6] Finally, a floor is not a ceiling. Administrators keep every bit of discretion above the line. The law only removes the option that harms patients without their consent. That is precisely what law is for.

[C1.1] A law can mandate a ratio. It cannot mandate a nurse into existence. That gap is where this proposal breaks, and where patients get hurt.
[C1.2] Start with the arithmetic PRO invokes. A fixed statutory ratio treats a stable patient awaiting discharge and a crashing post-operative patient as identical units. Real safety depends on acuity, skill mix, ward layout, and which shift it is — variables a legislature cannot see and a statute frozen for a decade cannot track. Hospital-level staffing tools, acuity-based systems, and empowered nurse-led staffing committees adjust in real time.
[C1.3] A national number does not. The alternative to mandates is not "administrator whim"; it is regulating outcomes, transparency, and safe-staffing processes while leaving the allocation to the people who can see the patients.
[C1.4] Now the shortage. When nurses are scarce — and nearly everywhere they are — a mandated ratio does not conjure staff; it forces closures. A rural hospital that cannot recruit to the legal number must shut beds, divert ambulances, or close the maternity ward entirely. The patient boarding twelve hours in an emergency corridor because the ward upstairs is "legally full" receives a nurse-to-patient ratio of zero.
[C1.5] That harm never appears in ratio studies, because the studies count staffed beds, not the people turned away from them.
[C1.6] Third, the substitution effect. Ratios don't print money; they redirect it. After California's mandate, hospitals widely offset costs by trimming aides, technicians, and support staff — so nurses met the ratio while absorbing tasks others once did. Compliance on paper, degraded care in practice.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO's own logic cuts back: if floors are safe, floors become defaults. Administrators facing a legal number staff *to* it, even on wards that previously ran richer. The floor becomes the ceiling.
[C1.8] We all want more nurses at more bedsides. The question is whether a rigid national number delivers that, or delivers closed wards, hallway medicine, and box-checking. It delivers the latter.
