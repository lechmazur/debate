[P1.1] The default should follow power: firms that build intimate profiles from millions of people must justify that practice, not force millions of people to discover and escape it. “Presumptively unlawful” is not an absolute ban. It permits large-scale harvesting for targeted advertising where a company proves explicit, informed opt-in consent; otherwise, courts and regulators should stop it.
[P1.2] First, the existing notice-and-opt-out model reverses the burden of harm. Collection occurs silently across sites, apps, devices, and data brokers. A person may see one advertisement, while the underlying dossier—movements, purchases, health concerns, relationships, political interests—persists, combines with other data, and supports future inferences. No individual can realistically negotiate with every participant in that chain.
[P1.3] Burying permission in a policy, or offering opt-out only after collection, treats exposure as consent. Explicit opt-in puts the burden where information and control actually reside.
[P1.4] Second, the harms exceed any single transaction. Profiles can enable manipulative targeting, exclusion from opportunities, discriminatory audience selection, chilling effects on sensitive browsing, and lasting damage when data leak. They also create externalities: one person’s data can reveal information about associates, while ubiquitous tracking makes avoidance costly even for people who never agreed. A regime concerned only with whether each user clicked “accept” misses this collective dimension.
[P1.5] Presumptive unlawfulness supplies protection before diffuse, difficult-to-prove injuries become irreversible.
[P1.6] Third, the motion targets a business method, not advertising itself. Contextual advertising can match an ad to the page being viewed; services can use voluntarily supplied preferences; measurement can be designed without assembling person-level dossiers. If behavioral profiling truly delivers enormous value, firms can explain that value and ask people to choose it. Consent obtained through deception, blanket bundling, or interfaces engineered to exhaust refusal should not qualify.
[P1.7] Opponents will invoke free services and innovation. But “free” cannot mean paying with unbounded surveillance whose scope users cannot observe. Nor should convenience grant firms a property-like claim over personal behavior. The fair rule is simple: those profiting from large-scale profiling must demonstrate genuine permission.
[P1.8] Without it, the surveillance—not the regulator’s intervention—is what lacks justification.

In contrast, the open web—independent publishers, local newsrooms, and small app creators—depends on distributed ad networks to fund their content.
[C1.3] PRO suggests contextual advertising easily fills the void [P1.6], but contextual ads generate only a fraction of the revenue needed to sustain specialized content and investigative reporting. Imposing presumptive illegality suffocates independent media while funneling advertiser dollars directly into dominant platforms.
[C1.4] Second, PRO trivializes the economic model that democratized the internet. Universal, free access to news, communication tools, and software is an egalitarian achievement funded by targeted advertising. When data collection is treated as inherently illegitimate without friction-heavy consent, the inevitable alternative is not a utopian ad-free web, but paywalls. This creates a two-tiered internet: privacy and quality information for those who can afford monthly subscriptions, and locked-out services or endless consent-banner fatigue for everyone else.
[C1.5] Finally, presumptive unlawfulness is the wrong legal instrument. We do not declare entire industries presumptively illegal to address bad practices within them. The solution to data abuses is targeted, harm-based regulation: strict protections for sensitive categories like health, financial, and children’s data; transparent auditing; aggressive enforcement against deceptive dark patterns; and robust opt-out rights.
[C1.6] Targeted advertising is ordinary commercial activity matching users with relevant products. Regulate abuses aggressively, but do not dismantle the open digital economy by treating its foundational funding model as an inherent legal wrong.
