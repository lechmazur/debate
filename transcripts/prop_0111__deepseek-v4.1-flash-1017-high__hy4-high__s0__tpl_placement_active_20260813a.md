[P1.1] Primary care is not a luxury add-on; it is the front door of medicine. A government guarantee there is the minimum condition for a health system that treats people before small problems become emergencies. The proposition is deliberately modest: guarantee universal primary care, while leaving elective and supplementary services to private markets. That is the right dividing line.
[P1.2] Primary care markets fail in a predictable way. The people who most need prevention—people with uncontrolled blood pressure, early diabetes, untreated depression, or a child due for vaccination—are often the least able to pay and the least able to judge what care they need. Insurance churns with employment. Asymptomatic conditions produce no shopping decision.
[P1.3] Vaccination protects others, not just the patient. So a purely private front door rations care by ability to pay and then shifts the bill downstream in the form of emergency visits, avoidable hospitalizations, and lost work.
[P1.4] A guarantee does not require government-run clinics for everyone. It can be publicly financed and privately delivered: community clinics, independent doctors, nonprofit teams, capitated networks. What it requires is that no one is turned away from basic prevention, chronic-disease management, and routine treatment because of cost. That is compatible with private elective markets—cosmetic surgery, premium rooms, supplementary therapies—continuing to operate and innovate.
[P1.5] The central clash is simple. Opponents will warn about cost and rationing, but every system rations; the question is whether the rationing mechanism is waiting lists and clinical need or a credit card. Universal primary care is comparatively cheap, reduces expensive downstream care, and creates a baseline of shared health. Leaving that baseline to private markets does not preserve freedom; it preserves the freedom to be sick and bankrupt.
[P1.6] Guarantee the front door, and let the private market handle the extras.

[C1.1] Primary care is the front door, PRO says [P1.1]. That is why a universal guarantee is too large a promise. Whoever guarantees the front door ends up controlling the house: referral rules, records, standard treatments, appointment counts, payment rates, and staffing terms. Private cosmetic surgery, premium rooms, and “supplementary therapies” [P1.4] are thin consolation if diagnosis and follow-up sit behind a capped public gate.
[C1.2] A guarantee is not charity; it is a state-backed claim that “no one is turned away” from routine care because of cost [P1.4]. Zero price does not abolish scarcity; it moves scarcity into queues, staffing caps, capitation incentives, and benefit definitions. The affluent can wait, rearrange work, and navigate forms; an hourly worker with possible diabetes or depression often cannot. The silent early problems PRO names are precisely the ones easiest to miss in overloaded gatekeeping.
[C1.3] A credit card is a bad rationing device, but a waiting list with no affordable exit is not automatically humane.
[C1.4] Nor should we accept the downstream-savings assumption [P1.5]. Primary care is not a coupon that always reduces hospital spending. More checkups produce more tests, prescriptions, referrals, monitoring, and worry-driven visits. Some prevention saves money; much good care costs more because it finds need.
[C1.5] A universal entitlement spends scarce clinician time on everyone, including low-risk households, instead of concentrating public money on vaccination, high-risk chronic disease, maternal care, and catastrophic protection.
[C1.6] The “modest” boundary will not stay modest. Is nutrition counselling routine or supplementary? Is remote blood-pressure monitoring basic? Is counselling for mild depression primary care?
[C1.7] Every profession and patient group will lobby to be inside the guarantee, while finance ministries quietly narrow it through guidelines. The result is a political definition of enough care.
[C1.8] The choice is not care versus abandonment, but plural, adjustable arrangements with targeted subsidies versus one universal promise rationed at the very point PRO calls indispensable. That promise should not be made.
