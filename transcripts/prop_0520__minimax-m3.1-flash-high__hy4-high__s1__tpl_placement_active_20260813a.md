[P1.1] The central policy choice is whether to spend scarce administrative capacity verifying immigrants’ professional competence, or whether to make them pass a general test of local labor-market demand. Credential recognition does the first; labor-market testing does the second. If the goal is to admit qualified professionals efficiently, those are not equally useful tools.
[P1.2] A foreign-trained engineer, nurse, physician, or pharmacist already possesses evidence of technical training. Recognition asks the relevant question: can this qualification be verified, and what limited supervision or bridging is needed? Labor-market testing asks whether there are enough jobs locally for that worker, regardless of whether the worker can safely practice. That is a poor proxy for professional quality.
[P1.3] A country facing shortages in healthcare or infrastructure can suppress precisely the expertise it needs, while a recession can make established professionals wait for favorable demand before being allowed to contribute.
[P1.4] The problem is also one of timing. Every month spent resolving unfamiliar documentation, navigating local procedures, or re-taking examinations is a month in which a qualified immigrant is underemployed, dependent, or pushed into less regulated work. Faster recognition would therefore increase productive capacity and tax revenue while reducing the incentive to exploit vulnerable workers. It also gives employers a clearer signal of competence than vague labor-market claims do.
[P1.5] Properly designed recognition is not a blank check. Governments can require primary-source verification, practical assessments, language requirements where they are genuinely necessary, and supervised practice. These safeguards directly address public safety. In contrast, tightening labor-market tests creates delays and opportunities for arbitrary exclusion without necessarily establishing that a practitioner is competent.
[P1.6] The priority should be recognition with credible safeguards, not tighter labor-market testing. The former converts verifiable qualifications into public capacity. The latter risks turning immigration policy into a blunt instrument for protecting particular jobs, while making the economy miss skills that employers and communities urgently need.

[P2.1] The opponent’s central move is to treat a vacancy as the first legitimate question and competence as a secondary licensing question. That confuses a necessary condition with the best policy priority. A foreign-trained physician can be fully competent and still lack a job; conversely, an employer can sponsor a vacancy that turns out to be sham or exploitative. Labor-market testing measures whether an employer is willing to hire, not whether the immigrant can perform safely or whether the public interest is being served.
[P2.2] The claim that recognition “is not a job” is correct but does not establish that labor-market testing should take priority. Credential recognition cannot create employment by itself, and neither can a vacancy test. Recognition, however, identifies a productive worker whom employers can deploy once a suitable position arises. A policy that requires every candidate to pass through employer demand first makes foreign professionals dependent on sponsoring firms and vulnerable to recruitment abuse.
[P2.3] The worker’s verified qualification, rather than an employer’s application, becomes the stable basis for admission.
[P2.4] The opponent also presents labor-market testing as more administrable than reliable recognition. That is backwards at the point where mistakes matter. Vacancy records, wage levels, and employer histories can be fabricated or stale. By contrast, primary-source documents, practical assessments, language checks, and supervised practice directly test competence.
[P2.5] Recognition can be made faster without becoming a blank check: high-risk fields can retain staged assessment and supervision, while clearly comparable credentials receive rapid preliminary decisions. “Faster” means reducing avoidable duplication, not eliminating verification.
[P2.6] Consider a qualified foreign engineer. Labor-market testing may make recognition conditional on one sponsor’s hiring decision, even when a public infrastructure program could productively use that expertise. Recognition with safeguards gives that engineer a portable, verifiable qualification while regulators determine any required adaptation to local standards.
[P2.7] Thus, labor-market testing can complement responsible recognition, but it cannot substitute for it. Prioritizing recognition addresses the actual scarce asset—the ability to practice—and prevents immigration policy from being filtered through a narrow, potentially manipulated vacancy process.
