[P1.1] A cracked screen or a dead battery should not force an Indian family to buy a new phone. Today it often does, not because repair is technically impossible, but because manufacturers withhold the parts, tools, and documentation independent technicians need. A national right-to-repair law requiring that access for consumer electronics would treat a purchased device as property, not a license until the next model.
[P1.2] India already repairs. Neighborhood mobile markets and laptop benches keep devices alive because replacement is expensive. That economy is being designed out of existence: glued assemblies, parts pairing, proprietary fasteners, and manuals locked behind authorized networks that never reach most towns. Households pay twice.
[P1.3] Independent shops lose work. Still-usable electronics become waste. E-waste grows when a two-year-old laptop is written off for a replaceable board. So does household strain, in a market where a phone is not a gadget but infrastructure.
[P1.4] The law has to be national. Consumer electronics cross state lines; fragmented rules would protect manufacturers, not buyers. The duty is narrow: spare parts on fair terms, diagnostic tools, and the documentation needed to service what was sold. It does not require dumping source code or unlocking stolen devices.
[P1.5] Safety and cybersecurity are reasons to write standards and traceability into the statute, not reasons to seal the market.
[P1.6] Without the law, firms profit by shortening product life and rationing repair. With it, ownership includes the right to maintain. Independent repair is skilled work that already exists; the law would keep it viable rather than funneling every failure through a distant authorized counter. In a country where a smartphone is a bank, a classroom, and a workplace, that is not a luxury.
[P1.7] It is whether a working device stays in the owner's hands.

[C1.1] A cracked screen is a real frustration. Forcing every maker of consumer electronics to supply parts, diagnostic tools, and service documentation is the wrong remedy. That is not a modest property right. It is a compulsory opening of proprietary systems to an aftermarket the state cannot police.
[C1.2] Consumer electronics fail in ways furniture does not. Lithium cells puncture and burn. Secure elements hold payments, biometrics, and work logins. Parts pairing and locked diagnostics exist because a swapped board can brick a phone, defeat anti-theft, or put a stolen handset back into service as a bank.
[C1.3] Those tools will not remain inside honest shops. They circulate. A statute cannot promise traceability while multiplying the means to defeat it.
[C1.4] India already repairs. The binding constraint is often not a missing manual. It is whether a genuine part, moving through a global supply chain, is worth fitting to an aging device. A duty to supply parts on fair terms does not stock every town.
[C1.5] It stocks disputes: price challenges, documentation audits, fights over what fair means. Nor does the law make a bad bargain into a good one. Labor plus a genuine battery often exceeds what the phone is still worth, so households still replace. What changes is that every buyer pays the compliance cost.
[C1.6] Makers will not swallow that quietly. Prices rise, and thinner model ranges follow. Calibration data and diagnostic secrets are how firms stop counterfeit boards from looking official. Forced disclosure blurs that line in a market already thick with fakes, then leaves manufacturers answering for repairs they never performed.
[C1.7] Targeted safety rules, and competition cases against real foreclosure, are legitimate. A blanket national right to parts, tools, and documentation is not. It weakens security, spreads unaccountable repair, and taxes ownership in the name of saving it. A phone stays useful because it remains safe and supported, not because its service manual has been made public property.
