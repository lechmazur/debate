[P1.1] Persuasion has always been optimized, but generative AI changes the target and the scale. Today a campaign no longer has to broadcast one message to millions; it can test thousands of emotionally tuned variants, tailor them to micro-groups, and suppress versions that fail. The user sees a message that feels native, local, and personally persuasive, with no reliable signal that the sender was an algorithm or that the appeal was selected because it worked on people like them. That opacity is the harm.
[P1.2] Labeling only AI-generated content leaves the core gap. A synthetic image or paragraph may be plainly generated yet not part of a targeted campaign. Conversely, a persuasion campaign can rely on ordinary human-written text, but use AI to identify which voter, patient, or consumer is most susceptible to which fear and then automate delivery. The persuasive event is the campaign-user pairing, not the mere artifact.
[P1.3] The duty should therefore attach where platforms already operate: when a major platform's systems materially use AI to select, sequence, or optimize persuasive content for specific users or micro-groups. That is not regulation of thought. It is disclosure of a commercial or political technique users cannot infer from the post itself.
[P1.4] The reasons are practical. Microtargeting defeats outside scrutiny because different audiences receive different messages. Labels let researchers, journalists, and ordinary users see when they are inside an experiment. They give platforms a compliance incentive to log optimization decisions and preserve records.
[P1.5] And they protect autonomy without banning anything: the audience remains free to read, share, or dismiss, but knows they were targeted.
[P1.6] Any burden claim fails on scope. The motion covers major platforms, not individuals, and persuasion campaigns, not private conversation. Those actors possess the optimization models and delivery data needed to attach a label. Requiring disclosure there is less intrusive than leaving them free to manufacture invisible influence at population scale.

[P2.2] So the label would not be a warning on everything; it would be a narrow signal on content engineered as persuasion.
[P2.3] The same distinction answers the “predator with crude filters” and “stigmatized civic group” worries. A label is not a verdict; it is a provenance tag. A public-screening message optimized for a vulnerable micro-group deserves the same disclosure as a manipulative one because users need the same basic fact: this appeal was selected for someone like me. CON’s alternative—no tag for tailored civic speech—does not protect citizens; it lets the predator hide behind the legitimacy of the nurse.
[P2.4] Uniform disclosure neutralizes that camouflage.
[P2.5] CON also claims labels manufacture transparency while real scrutiny needs auditable limits. But auditors cannot investigate optimization they cannot see. Microtargeted campaigns differ per user, so researchers, journalists, and regulators can only sample the population of persuasive events if the platform marks them. [P2.6] Finally, evasion is overstated. If a major platform’s data, delivery, and billing systems route the message, the campaign is occurring on the platform regardless of whether optimization is outsourced or dressed in human review. The duty attaches where the targeting infrastructure lives.
[P2.7] The choice is not between a badge and real accountability. It is between invisible optimization and the minimal visibility needed to govern it.
