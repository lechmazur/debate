[P1.1] Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease, yet insurers routinely treat it as the one diagnosis where effective medicine is optional. A patient can get coverage for diabetes drugs, blood pressure pills, a CPAP machine, and eventually a knee replacement or cardiac care — but be denied an FDA-authorized anti-obesity medication that treats a root driver of all of them. That is rationing by stigma, not medical judgment.
[P1.2] Newer anti-obesity medications produce substantial, sustained weight loss for many patients when diet and exercise alone have failed, and that weight loss translates into what insurers claim to want: lower blood sugar and fewer progressions to diabetes, reduced blood pressure, improvement in sleep apnea and fatty liver, and less strain on joints. Denying coverage does not make those costs disappear. It defers them, enlarges them, and shifts them to emergency care, surgery, disability, and Medicare. Limiting the mandate to clinically indicated, FDA-labeled use targets treatment where benefit is greatest, not cosmetic use.
[P1.3] Voluntary coverage has failed for a structural reason. Any single insurer that covers generously fears attracting higher-cost enrollees and paying today for savings that another insurer, or Medicare, captures years later after the member switches plans. So plans exclude, cap, or restrict access, even when treatment is cost-effective over a realistic horizon. A universal requirement solves that collective-action problem, spreads risk across the pool, and ends the arbitrary line where the same drug is covered after diabetes develops but not before.
[P1.4] This is not a blank check, because the proposition builds in the control mechanism: value-based pricing and outcomes-based contracts. Insurers and manufacturers can tie payment to real results — rebates if patients do not achieve or maintain meaningful weight loss, lower net prices for lower response, and renegotiation as competition expands. That aligns price with value instead of letting list prices dictate access. Require coverage, pay for outcomes, and treat obesity like the disease insurers already pay far more to manage downstream.

[C1.1] Insurers denying these drugs are not practicing stigma. They are doing arithmetic.
[C1.2] Obesity is common — roughly two in five American adults — and FDA labels for new anti-obesity drugs reach far down into overweight with a comorbidity. “Clinically indicated” therefore does not mean a narrow few. It means tens of millions of potential claimants. At hundreds of dollars per month net, even after rebates, multiplied by years and often lifelong use because stopping typically brings regain, the math is brutal. Certain, immediate pharmacy spending on a mass scale to chase possible, distant medical savings.
[C1.3] That timing mismatch destroys the prevention-pays argument. Treating diabetes or hypertension after diagnosis costs much less per year than putting the entire at-risk population on expensive biologics indefinitely. Many people with obesity will never have a heart attack or need a knee replacement; every person put on these drugs will generate a bill every month. Shifting costs earlier does not reduce them — it multiplies them, and commercial plans that pay now will rarely be the Medicare plan that might benefit decades later.
[C1.4] Nor does the phrase “outcomes-based contracts” fix it. Weight is not like clearing an infection. It fluctuates with adherence, illness, life disruption, switching jobs and plans. Building the apparatus to track pounds lost, attribute blame, claw back rebates, and police continuation takes years while money flows out the door.
[C1.5] Similar experiments with other high-cost drugs have been narrow, short-lived, and administratively heavy because manufacturers will not underwrite population behavior and insurers cannot cheaply monitor it.
[C1.6] Worst, a mandate surrenders the only discipline that lowers drug prices: the credible threat to walk away. Lock in coverage by law and manufacturers have no reason to offer real discounts, while employers and workers have no escape from higher premiums, higher deductibles, and coverage cut elsewhere. That harms precisely the low-wage families forced to subsidize a therapy they may never use.

[P2.1] That arithmetic treats “clinically indicated” as if tens of millions automatically start and stay on therapy for life. It does not. A clinician must document labeled need and risk; insurers can still apply medical-necessity review and prefer one product over another. Patients still discontinue for side effects or preference.
[P2.2] Eligibility on paper is not utilization in practice for statins or antidepressants either — and conflating them invents a mass entitlement the proposition does not create.
[P2.3] Regain after stopping proves chronicity, not futility. Blood pressure rebounds without antihypertensives and glucose rises without diabetes drugs, yet no one calls those treatments failures. The question is benefit while treated, concentrated in severe obesity, prediabetes, sleep apnea, and high cardiovascular risk. Paying only after diabetes, joint damage, or apnea is established is not thrift; it is waiting until an ignorable patient becomes an expensive one.
[P2.4] The insistence that prevention repay itself within one plan's short enrollment window is imposed almost nowhere else. Statins and hypertension treatment are covered to buy health and avert catastrophe, though many treated patients would never have had the event. A universal mandate answers the churn objection: when every plan must cover, none is punished for treating early, and risk is pooled rather than dumped on the insurer holding the patient when complications arrive.
[P2.5] A mandate to cover is not a mandate to pay list price. It removes exclusion — the tactic that has yielded high cash prices and no access — while preserving negotiation over net price, preferred products, and rebates. Outcomes contracts need not police every pound: use continued dispensing and clinician-recorded response at six or twelve months, with larger rebates for non-response. Imperfect shared risk beats certain denial.
[P2.6] Premiums already contain obesity, in diabetes care, surgeries, and apnea devices. Exclusion does not shield low-wage workers; it shields them from the pharmacy counter and exposes them at the hospital door.

[P4.1] A1: An insurer could deny a specific request when labeled criteria are not met — BMI and comorbidity thresholds, contraindication or duplicate therapy, inadequate documentation, or no meaningful response to justify continuation. It could also steer patients to a preferred product. What it could not do is categorically exclude the class when a clinician documents indicated need. That is coverage for appropriate treatment, not guaranteed payment for any brand, dose, or refill forever.
[P4.2] A2: Low-wage workers are not being asked to pay list price for savings only Medicare sees decades later. With value-based pricing, they pay a pooled, negotiated price for benefits that start during working life: glucose control before diabetes, lower blood pressure, improved sleep apnea, less joint pain, and lower disability risk. They already pay for obesity through premiums funding diabetes care, surgery, and apnea devices. Exclusion is more regressive: wealthier patients can pay cash for prevention, low-wage patients must wait until complications qualify.
[P4.3] First, scale is controlled by clinical gates and continuation review — what the opposition dismissed in [C2.1] as paperwork is precisely how insurers target expensive chronic care instead of funding automatic mass treatment.
[P4.4] Second, the leverage argument has it backwards. The threat to exclude the whole class has produced denial and high cash prices, not affordable access. A mandate to cover the class preserves leverage inside it: manufacturers must compete to be the preferred product and accept rebates for non-response, or lose the market to a rival. That is a credible threat insurers can actually use without abandoning patients.
[P4.5] Finally, churn does not make treatment worthless. Preventing diabetes, apnea, and disability in the next few years helps current employers and workers even if a plan changes, and universality stops any one plan being punished for doing so. The choice is not pay now or pay nothing. It is pay negotiated prices for measured response in high-risk patients, or keep paying unnegotiated costs when preventable disease arrives.

[P5.1] The decisive question is not whether obesity will be paid for. It already is — in diabetes treatment, apnea devices, joint surgery, cardiac care, and disability. The question is whether insurers may refuse the earlier, targeted treatment and reserve payment for the costlier damage.
[P5.2] That refusal is what the opposition defends. Their case assumes exclusion is discipline. But exclusion has not produced affordable access; it has produced denial for patients who cannot pay cash and unconditional payment later when complications qualify. That is the worst bargain: no price leverage at the prevention stage, no choice at the crisis stage.
[P5.3] The mandate reverses it. Cover the class when labeled, clinical need is documented. Steer to a preferred product. Continue payment only with meaningful response.
[P5.4] Extract rebates when treatment fails. That does not guarantee every prescription forever; it guarantees that price and continuation are tied to value instead of a blanket no.
[P5.5] Workers do not escape obesity costs by being denied medicine. They inherit them as premiums for downstream care, while only wealthier patients can buy prevention outright.
[P5.6] Pay conditionally, early, and at a negotiated price — or pay unconditionally, late, at the hospital price. That is why the mandate wins.
