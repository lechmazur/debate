[P1.1] A free society is not defined by how safe its citizens feel but by how free they actually are. Security can be delivered by any regime—a dictatorship, an occupation force, a warden. Privacy is different. Privacy is the line that separates a citizen from a subject, and when a society erodes it in the name of safety, it doesn't become more secure; it becomes less free.
[P1.2] The motion asks which matters more *in a free society*, and that qualifier is dispositive, because privacy is what makes the society free in the first place.
[P1.3] Consider what security without privacy looks like. The Stasi didn't just protect East Germans from crime—they catalogued their letters, bugged their apartments, and turned neighbor against neighbor. China's surveillance apparatus delivers genuinely low street crime. Singapore's order is enviable on paper.
[P1.4] But we don't call those societies free, because the absence of a private sphere means the absence of self-government. When the state can watch everything, dissent becomes evidence, association becomes suspicion, and conformity becomes survival. Security in that world is real. Freedom is not.
[P1.5] Privacy is not a luxury good purchased with the surplus of safety. It is the precondition for democratic life. People need unmonitored space to think, to disagree, to organize, to be wrong before they are right. Without that space, voting becomes theater and rights become permissions the state grants and withdraws at will.
[P1.6] Security protects life. Privacy protects political life—the life of a person who governs themselves rather than being governed. A society that trades the latter for the former has accepted the terms of a prison and called it safety. In a free society, privacy matters more because without it, the adjective no longer holds.
[P1.7] The society we are securing is no longer free, and securing an unfree society is not what this motion asks us to defend.
