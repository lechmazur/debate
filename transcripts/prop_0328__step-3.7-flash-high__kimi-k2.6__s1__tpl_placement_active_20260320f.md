[P1.1] The current digital ticketing system is a rigged game that extracts wealth from ordinary fans to line the pockets of scalpers and ticketing monopolies, and the rule we advance today fixes that by recognizing fans’ basic right to transfer the tickets they paid for, while leaving room for narrowly tailored limits that actually target scalping harm. When a fan purchases a digital event ticket, they pay for a seat at an event, not a revocable, non-transferable license held hostage by ticketing platforms.
[P1.2] The only acceptable restrictions on a fan’s right to transfer that ticket are those narrowly tailored to stop the specific, demonstrable harms of scalping: bulk bot-assisted purchases, predatory price gouging, and related fraud. Blanket bans on all peer-to-peer transfers that prevent a sick fan from selling a ticket to a friend at face value, or gifting a ticket to a family member for free, are not narrowly tailored—they are overreach that harms ordinary people while doing nothing to stop scalpers.
[P1.3] Right now, ticketing giants lock tickets to original purchaser accounts, charge exorbitant fees even for free transfers, and fail to stop scalpers who scoop up most high-demand tickets before real fans can access them, then resell them for massive markups. This system is a windfall for bad actors and a tax on fans, who pay both the original inflated service fees and the scalper’s markup. Narrow anti-scalping limits—like capping resale prices at 10-15% above face value, banning automated bulk ticket purchases, and requiring transparent transfer of the original purchase record—address the actual harm of scalping without stripping fans of their right to dispose of property they paid for.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim this rule will crush event profitability, but the only parties that lose under this framework are scalpers and rent-seeking ticketing middlemen. Fans, small promoters, and event organizers who actually care about their audiences all win.

[C1.3] Worse, the peer-to-peer mandate is a gift to scalpers, not a blow against them. Scalping thrives in fragmented, unmonitored private markets. When transfers are locked to official platforms, organizers can enforce price caps and bot bans in one place. When every fan gains a legal right to sell privately through anonymous peer-to-peer channels, enforcement evaporates.
[C1.4] A 10 or 15 percent resale cap is meaningless when thousands of shadow transactions occur outside any audited system, and organizers lose the leverage to cancel barcodes or reclaim inventory from fraudulent sellers.
[C1.5] Organizers also restrict transfers for reasons that have nothing to do with scalping: age verification for explicit content, separating rival fan sections for safety, or preserving subsidized community tickets for local residents. The motion bundles all of those legitimate controls into the same “narrowly tailored” straitjacket, treating them as suspicious overreach. Fans deserve fair access, but that access exists only because organizers retain the flexibility to manage risk, revenue, and crowd composition. Mandating peer-to-peer transfer rights undermines the very infrastructure that keeps events safe and affordable.
