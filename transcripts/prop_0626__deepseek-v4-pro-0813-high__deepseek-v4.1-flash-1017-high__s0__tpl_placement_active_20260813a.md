[P1.1] The core problem is not the weapon’s accuracy; it is the absence of a responsible human decision-maker. A fully autonomous system that selects and engages targets without meaningful human control removes the essential link between lethal force and moral and legal judgment. The proposition is narrow: it does not ban precision-guided munitions, human-supervised strikes, or defensive countermeasures. It bans only the delegation of the kill decision itself to a machine.
[P1.2] First, such delegation creates an accountability void. If an autonomous system strikes the wrong house or misidentifies a group of civilians, who is responsible? Not the machine, which cannot answer or bear guilt. Not the manufacturer, whose product operated after deployment.
[P1.3] Not the commander, who did not select the target. The chain of responsibility dissolves precisely where it matters most—after a wrongful killing. A ban keeps a human being answerable for lethal decisions.
[P1.4] Second, lawful targeting requires contextual judgment that cannot be reduced to code. Distinction and proportionality demand more than classification. A person carrying a rifle may be a soldier, a hunter, or a child with a toy. A convoy may be military or a wedding party.
[P1.5] A building may be a command post or a crowded school. Machines can recognize patterns, but they cannot weigh surrounding circumstances the way a commander must under international law. Fully autonomous systems turn legal judgment into statistical inference, which is not compliance.
[P1.6] Third, the failure mode is uniquely dangerous. Sensor confusion, spoofing, or an unforeseen environment can produce mass harm rapidly and irreversibly. There is no human pause, no last check before engagement, no ability to notice that something is wrong. That raises the risk of civilian massacres and escalatory accidents between states.
[P1.7] The alternative is not to reject autonomy entirely. It is to require meaningful human control before lethal force. That is a clear, enforceable line. States should draw it.

[C1.1] The case for a ban rests on an idealised human and an undefined machine. “Meaningful human control” has no settled legal meaning. Does it require a finger on a trigger, a veto within seconds, or a commander approving a targeting category? A ban built on that phrase is not a clear line; it is a litigation trap. States will disagree about scope, comply selectively, and the worst actors will ignore it.
[C1.2] That is not arms control; it is abandonment of the very standards we need.
[C1.3] Second, accountability does not vanish when a machine executes. It moves earlier. The commander who deploys, the officer who certifies rules of engagement, the procurement official who accepts testing, and the state that authorizes use can all be answerable. Domestic and international law already punish negligent deployment and unlawful orders.
[C1.4] A human need not pull a trigger to bear responsibility for a foreseeable killing. A ban, by contrast, lets responsibility diffuse into treaty ambiguity.
[C1.5] Third, human judgment is not a magic safeguard. Humans get tired, frightened, vengeful, and prone to confirmation bias. Autonomous systems can be constrained by explicit target categories, positive identification requirements, geofences, and abort criteria; they can be tested and audited in ways a stressed crew cannot. That does not make them moral agents.
[C1.6] It means the right question is whether the system’s use is lawful and supervised, not whether a human hand is physically present.
[C1.7] Finally, prohibition is the wrong tool because it freezes out the safest developers while pushing capability to opaque ones. The realistic choice is not ban or wild west. It is binding rules: certification, data logging, human authorization for categories of force, and liability up the chain. States should regulate autonomous lethality, not pretend a vague ban makes it disappear.
