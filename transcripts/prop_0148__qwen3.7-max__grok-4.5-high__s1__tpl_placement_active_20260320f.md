[P1.1] The transition to adulthood cannot happen overnight at eighteen. It requires a scaffolded expansion of autonomy. Indefinite child-style monitoring creates a developmental cliff, leaving young adults fundamentally unprepared for the real world. We support a growing right to privacy because it is essential for psychological development, practical life skills, and healthy family dynamics.
[P1.2] First, teenagers need private spaces to form identities, navigate peer relationships, and make low-stakes mistakes. When parents track every text and GPS location, teens do not learn internal risk assessment; they learn evasion. Constant surveillance teaches them to hide their lives rather than manage them. A growing right to privacy lets them practice decision-making where stakes are manageable, building the internal compass they need when parental oversight is permanently gone.
[P1.3] Second, a "growing right" is not a demand for absolute freedom for young adolescents. It means progressive, age-appropriate trust. A thirteen-year-old might have private thoughts but guided digital habits; a sixteen-year-old should have private peer communications and freedom to navigate their community without a live tracker. This scaffolding mirrors their expanding cognitive capacities.
[P1.4] Finally, indefinite monitoring damages the parent-teen relationship. It replaces open dialogue with a warden-inmate dynamic. When teenagers feel trusted with incremental privacy, they are far more likely to voluntarily come to parents with genuine, high-stakes problems. When relentlessly surveilled, they simply become better at keeping secrets.
[P1.5] We must choose between raising compliant dependents who fall apart when tracking apps are disabled at eighteen, and raising capable adults who practiced independence under a gradually loosening tether. The growing right to privacy is that essential practice.
