[P1.1] Personality is not public property. A person's voice, cadence, private jokes, the way they comforted you at 2 a.m.—those are not family heirlooms to be uploaded. They are the last remaining pieces of a self that can no longer say no. Requiring prior consent from the deceased or their estate before an AI grief chatbot simulates those intimate traits is the minimum respect we owe the dead.
[P1.2] Without that gate, anyone with enough texts and a credit card can resurrect a performance of the person. The simulation will laugh at the wrong things, forgive what they would never forgive, or become a subscription product that outlives the family it was built to soothe. That is not mourning. It is ventriloquism.
[P1.3] Ordinary remembrance needs no license. A photograph or a story is one thing. An interactive replica that answers in their voice and offers reserved intimacy is another. That replica replaces their silence with a performance they never approved.
[P1.4] We already treat voice, likeness, and unpublished writings as protectable. Estates control the memoir, the unreleased album, the digital archive. Intimate personality simulation is more invasive than any of those, because it talks back. It can be prompted into confidences the living person never offered.
[P1.5] Consent is how we decide who may put words in a dead mouth.
[P1.6] The alternative is a market in the unconsented dead: an ex reconstructing the partner who stayed, companies mining leaked voice notes, heirs stuck with a stranger in a parent's voice. Grief does not confer ownership of another person's inner life. If the deceased never authorized that use, or their estate refuses it, that chatbot should not exist.
[P1.7] Memory is free. Recreating the living texture of a specific human being is not.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like respect for the dead. In practice, it hands a veto over private grief to whoever happens to control an estate—and that is where it collapses.
[C1.2] Start with who actually uses these tools. A widow feeds her husband's voicemails and twenty years of texts into a chatbot so she can hear "goodnight" one more time. Those messages were sent *to her*. They are her property, her memories, her correspondence.
[C1.3] Under this motion, she must petition the estate—perhaps an estranged adult child from a first marriage, perhaps a hostile executor—for permission to process her own grief with her own data. Grief does not confer ownership of another's inner life, PRO says. Fine. But neither does inheriting a house confer ownership of someone else's mourning.
[C1.4] Second, "prior consent from the deceased" is a null set dressed up as a standard. Almost nobody leaves an AI directive. So the motion's real content is estate control—and estates are legal-financial constructs, frequently captured by the least intimate relative or, for public figures, by corporations. The people who knew the deceased best often have no legal standing at all.
[C1.5] PRO's gate doesn't protect the dead person's wishes; it empowers whoever won the probate fight.
[C1.6] Third, the motion cannot distinguish what actually needs distinguishing. There is a real difference between a company selling subscriptions to a dead celebrity's voice and a daughter privately simulating a conversation she never got to finish. Commercial exploitation is already addressable through publicity rights, contract, and fraud law—targeted tools for the real abuses in [P1.6]. A blanket consent requirement instead criminalizes the most sympathetic case: private, therapeutic, non-commercial remembrance.
[C1.7] Finally, grief practices have always been the mourner's domain. We do not license eulogies, séances, letters to the dead, or impressions of Dad at the dinner table. The chatbot is a new instrument for an ancient practice. The burden of veto should fall on demonstrated harm—not on love seeking permission.
