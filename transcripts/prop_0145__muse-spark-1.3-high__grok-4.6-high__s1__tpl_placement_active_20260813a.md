[P1.1] Good parenting is not maximum watching. It is raising a teenager who stays safe when nobody is watching.
[P1.2] Adolescence is rehearsal for adult life. A teenager has to learn to set boundaries, read people, handle temptation, and ask for help. That learning requires some private space to think, talk to friends, make small mistakes, and correct them. A home where every location ping is logged and every message can be read removes that space.
[P1.3] It keeps the child compliant while the parents are looking, but less capable when they are not.
[P1.4] Default surveillance also breaks the very trust that keeps teenagers safe. A teenager who assumes mom and dad read everything does not become more open. She becomes more secretive. She moves difficult conversations off the monitored phone, deletes drafts, speaks in code.
[P1.5] The teen facing bullying, unwanted sexual pressure, or dark thoughts is exactly the teen who needs a parent to confide in — and exactly the teen most chilled by knowing privacy is impossible.
[P1.6] Nor does always-on tracking buy the safety it promises. A dot on a map tells you where a phone is, not whether the driver is sober, whether consent is respected, whether a joke is cruelty. [P1.7] Control escalates, closeness collapses, and real warning signs hide better.
[P1.8] The motion draws the right line. It does not forbid intervention. [P1.9] Trust with verification — curfews, check-in texts, knowing friends and plans, frank talks about predators, drugs, and driving — builds judgment that travels with the child. Surveillance as default builds dependence that ends at eighteen. Parents should parent for that birthday, not for today's dashboard.
