[C1.2] Notice what the motion actually says: permission for AI performances in the "style *or* likeness" of dead artists. That word "style" is fatal. Style has never been property, and for good reason: it is the shared vocabulary of an art form. Under this rule, a producer using AI tools to craft a Sinatra-esque croon or a Hendrix-flavored guitar tone needs a license from a lawyer in Los Angeles.
[C1.3] Human tribute acts remain free; the same homage made with newer tools suddenly requires a permission slip. That is not protecting artists — it is taxing technology.
[C1.4] And who grants this permission? Not the artist, who is dead and expressed no view. Estates are frequently distant relatives, trusts, or investment funds that bought the catalog outright. The proposition dresses up dynastic gatekeeping as consent.
[C1.5] Worse, it has no expiry: does a filmmaker need clearance from whoever claims Caruso? Mozart? A permission regime with no time limit is a perpetual veto over the cultural commons — stronger than copyright itself, which at least expires.
[C1.6] The genuine harms here are deception and false endorsement: audiences believing the dead artist actually made or approved something. Those harms have targeted remedies — mandatory labeling of synthetic performances, fraud and false-endorsement law, publicity rights where the deception is real. Disclosure fixes the confusion problem without giving estates a chokehold on style itself.
[C1.7] So the clash is simple: a narrow rule against deceiving audiences, versus a sweeping ownership claim over how art sounds. The first protects people. The second protects rent collectors — and starves the living artists who build on the dead.
