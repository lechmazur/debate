[P1.1] The United States needs to roughly triple its transmission capacity over the next two decades to meet climate targets, connect remote renewable resources to population centers, and keep the grid reliable. Local veto power is the single greatest obstacle standing between us and that buildout. Every year of delay measured in permitting battles means more fossil generation running longer, higher electricity costs for consumers, and emissions we cannot afford.
[P1.2] Consider what actually happens under the status quo. A transmission line that crosses multiple jurisdictions can face a decade or more of litigation, environmental review, and local board opposition. Projects like the Champlain Hudson Power Express or Clean Line Energy's various corridors have stalled for years—not because of engineering problems, but because a single municipality or a small group of landowners can freeze a project that serves millions. This is not democratic participation; it is a structural veto that distorts priorities toward parochial concerns at the expense of national ones.
[P1.3] Reform does not mean silencing communities. It means replacing absolute veto power with meaningful but bounded input—compensation for landowners, route adjustments, mitigation requirements. What is not manageable is the compounding damage of delay. Every gigawatt of wind or solar sitting idle because it cannot reach the grid is a concrete, measurable failure.
[P1.5] Climate timelines do not bend to local preference. The question is whether we let fragmented veto power dictate national infrastructure, or whether we build the grid fast enough to matter. The answer has to be speed.
