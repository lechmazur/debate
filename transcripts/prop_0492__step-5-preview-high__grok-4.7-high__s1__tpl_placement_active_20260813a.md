[P1.1] Automation is not a costless miracle. When a company replaces a worker with robotics, it captures the productivity gain while shifting the burden—lost wages, declining neighborhoods, and eroded tax bases—onto the public. An automation tax corrects this imbalance. It treats job displacement not as an inevitable force of nature, but as a business decision with measurable social consequences.
[P1.2] The revenue directly funds retraining and adjustment support, creating a compact between innovation and fairness. Consider a factory that automates its assembly line. The company gains efficiency; the displaced worker bears the risk. Without intervention, those employees often land in lower-wage service jobs or drop out of the labor force entirely, while the surrounding community loses the tax revenue that once supported schools and roads.
[P1.3] Retraining programs financed by the tax give workers a genuine path forward, while transition assistance stabilizes local economies during the shift.
[P1.4] Critics will claim this tax stifles progress. But the proposal does not ban robotics or freeze technology. It modestly prices the social cost of displacement, ensuring automation proceeds at a pace society can absorb. Innovation funded entirely by displaced workers' misery is neither sustainable nor morally defensible.
[P1.5] Moreover, by preserving consumer purchasing power, the tax protects the very demand that businesses rely upon.
[P1.6] This is not punishment; it is fair compensation. Companies automate using infrastructure, legal systems, and an educated workforce sustained by taxpayers. When they remove human contributors from that system, they should contribute to the repair. The automation tax converts a private windfall into a public investment, making technological progress durable rather than divisive.
[P1.7] Every dollar collected returns to the very people whose labor built the system being automated. If we refuse to pay this modest price, we do not accelerate innovation—we simply write a blank check to capital and present the bill to everyone else.

[C1.1] An automation tax does not charge firms for harm. It charges them for buying machines, and that distinction collapses the proposal.
[C1.2] Displacement is not a line on a tax return. A welder replaced by a robot looks easy to levy. A vision system that lets three workers do five jobs, software that retires a clerical task, a cobot that makes a dangerous shift survivable—these are how productivity actually arrives. No statute can police that line without taxing tools that raise output per hour, which is how real wages rise.
[C1.3] Firms will not offer clean classifications. They will delay purchases, lease instead of own, push tasks abroad, or hire fewer people so “replacement” never triggers. The tax hits the visible factory and misses the substitution that changes employment.
[C1.4] Capital moves; local payrolls do not. A levy on robotics raises the cost of staying and modernizing relative to producing where no such tax exists. The factory in the other case does not keep its wage bill and write a polite check for retraining. It automates under foreign ownership, or it closes.
[C1.5] Either way the town loses the employer, and the revenue meant to cushion the shock never shows up.
[C1.6] The earmark does not rescue the design. Retraining fails when skills, place, and employer demand do not match—not because the money lacked a robot label. Tying support to a narrow, gameable base makes the cushion weakest when investment pauses. Wage insurance and portable benefits, paid from ordinary revenue, can ease transitions without punishing the capital deepening that creates the next job.
[C1.7] Slowing productivity to “preserve purchasing power” is an accounting trick. Cheaper goods and higher output are purchasing power. A tax that makes automation slower shrinks those gains, then spends the proceeds on programs that cannot restore the work it discouraged. Letting firms adopt better tools is not a blank check to capital.
[C1.8] It is how the bill gets paid.

[P2.1] CON’s case rests on a false choice: price displacement or ban progress. An automation tax does neither. It makes firms pay for a social cost they currently dump on the public. When a factory automates and leaves a town with no wages, no tax base, and no schools, that is a real externality.
[P2.2] The difficulty of measuring it exactly is not an argument for pretending it doesn’t exist. We tax pollution despite hard measurement problems, because the cost of doing nothing is worse.
[P2.3] The capital flight threat is overstated. Many firms automate to stay local—logistics, food processing, energy—where market access, infrastructure, and skilled labor pools are fixed. A modest levy does not erase those advantages. If a company’s only plan is to eliminate every worker and hollow out the community, the tax is precisely what stops them from treating public investment as a free input.
[P2.4] And if they would leave over a small tax, they were never committed to the location.
[P2.5] The “gaming” objection proves too much. Leasing, delaying, and offshoring substitution already happen without any tax. A well-designed automation levy can be based on the labor cost avoided, not the machine itself, making it harder to evade by merely changing the ownership structure of a robot. If a cobot keeps workers on the line and raises output, the tax does not trigger.
[P2.6] The opponent wants us to ignore displacement because the tax code is imperfect; the right response is to fix the code, not abandon the principle.
[P2.7] Finally, wage insurance and portable benefits are complements, not substitutes. They lack the dedicated revenue stream that ties automation’s beneficiaries to the people it harms. The opponent’s plan leaves the burden on general taxpayers—the very people who already lost the jobs. PRO makes the disruptor pay for the disruption.
[P2.8] That is not punishment; it is accountability.
