[P1.1] Third places are social infrastructure, not sentimental extras. When the only acceptable places are home, work, or somewhere you must keep buying, teens, seniors, caregivers, and newcomers lose the casual, repeated contact that builds trust and mutual aid. That decline is a policy outcome. Single-use zoning made walkable mixed-use streets scarce; parking rules pushed daily life into cars; commercial rents favored high-turnover chains; library and recreation hours were cut.
[P1.2] The result is more isolation, thinner civic life, and fewer free places to simply be.
[P1.3] The market will not reverse this on its own. Private third places charge for access, cluster where incomes are high, and close or limit lingering when profit demands. But the main benefits are diffuse and non-commercial: reduced loneliness, informal watching over kids, cross-class and cross-generational contact, local organizing, and democratic conversation. Those benefits do not appear on a cafe’s receipts, so private supply will systematically fall short.
[P1.4] That is a standard public-goods problem. Governments already treat parks, libraries, and public squares as public goods; extending that logic to revive third places is not radical.
[P1.5] Nor is the status quo neutral. Zoning, street design, and public budgets already shape where people can gather. Refusing to act simply subsidizes isolation. Active funding should mean longer library and recreation-center hours, small grants for low-cost community spaces, and public seating where people can stay without purchase.
[P1.6] Zoning should allow corner stores, community rooms, and mixed-use gathering spots within walking distance of homes. A bench, a community room, or a walkable corner store gives teenagers somewhere to go after school and isolated adults a reason to leave home. A neighborhood that zones such places out pays later in loneliness, mistrust, and weaker resilience.
[P1.7] The choice is not whether people should use third places; it is whether they can. Governments should make that choice real where the market and old zoning have taken it away.

[C1.4] Second, look at PRO's own list of causes: single-use zoning, parking minimums, restrictive licensing. Every one is a government rule. Government space arrives with neutrality mandates, posted rules, no alcohol, and a 6 p.m. closing. That is why cities are full of windswept plazas and community centers that are empty except for the Tuesday zumba class, while the unsubsidized taqueria next door is packed.
[C1.7] Conviviality is an emergent property; funding it is like funding friendship.
