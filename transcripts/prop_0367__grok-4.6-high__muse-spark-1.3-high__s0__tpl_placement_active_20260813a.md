[P1.1] Streaming sold itself as the opposite of cable: watch what you want, stop when you don't, reshape the bill when life changes. Bundles that resist pause, split, or monthly reconfiguration abandon that bargain. They reinstall lock-in and opacity as if a set-top box still needed a truck.
[P1.2] Life does not run on annual contracts. A family travels for a month, a sports season ends, a roommate moves out, a child outgrows cartoons, or work hours make two catalogs redundant. Forcing people to keep paying through idle months, or to cancel an entire stack just to drop one unused piece, is surplus extraction, not efficiency. Cable could claim trucks, plant, and hardware.
[P1.3] Streaming has none of that. The product is software sitting on a billing cycle. Software can pause a seat, split access among people who no longer share a couch, and swap add-ons without a loyalty maze. Refusing that is a policy, not a constraint.
[P1.4] Opacity follows lock-in. When you cannot cleanly exit a channel, a sports tier, or a leftover login, the advertised bundle price is a fiction. Overlapping libraries, promotions that convert into buried line items, and family plans that punish adult children for living separately recreate cable's fog. Monthly reconfiguration makes each component's price real because customers can actually act on it.
[P1.5] Hidden prices only work when exit is expensive.
[P1.6] Competition needs that action. High switching costs protect mediocre packages. Easy pause and remix do not kill bundling; they force bundles to earn their keep. A package that still saves time and money will survive.
[P1.7] A package that survives only because canceling is a maze does not deserve protection. Platforms should compete on shows, price, and convenience, not on how hard it is to leave.
[P1.8] The choice is whether streaming stays matched to monthly life or becomes cable with better thumbnails. Flexibility is the honest product. Lock-in is the old industry sneaking back in.

[C1.1] Cheap bundles only exist because most subscribers do not re-optimize every month. That stability is what pays for expensive television.
[C1.2] A drama series or live sports deal must be bought and filmed long before anyone watches. Rights holders demand billions upfront for a full season, actors and crews need multi-year commitments, servers and support must be staffed year-round. Revenue that blinks off for summer travel, between seasons, or when a roommate leaves cannot fund that structure. If every sports fan pays only for playoffs and every family pauses cartoons during school months, providers do not magically cut costs.
[C1.3] They raise the monthly price to cover the idle months, cancel riskier shows, or demand longer ad loads. Cable understood this: the annual relationship subsidized the niche channel and the costly game.
[C1.4] Monthly pause-and-remix therefore punishes the steady subscriber to reward the gamer. The churn it celebrates is not free. Each cancel-and-return triggers marketing spend, win-back discounts, billing failures, and customer-service contacts that get folded back into the sticker price. The most price-sensitive users who micromanage three apps end up raising costs for the busy household that just wants a predictable bill.
[C1.5] Splitting makes it worse. Once a bundle can be sliced household by household, address by address, there is no family plan anymore, only individual licenses policed by location checks, device limits, and identity verification. That cure is more intrusive and opaque than any cable lineup.
[C1.6] Real choice is choosing between coherent packages that compete on catalog and value, not performing subscription homework on the first of every month. Lock-in of a reasonable term is not extraction; it is how strangers share the cost of stories none of them could afford alone.

A stay-put discount cannot survive costless exit, because the only people who pay it are the distracted while the tacticians rent the bundle for October and March. [C2.2] The idle-seat fallacy drives the rest. Yes, serving one paused stream costs almost nothing. Buying what fills it costs a fortune. A football league does not sell games month-by-month, a drama is not shot episode-by-episode after ratings arrive.
[C2.3] Aggregate demand only funds shows if aggregate payment captures the off-months too. Let every traveler, graduate, and post-season fan blink off, and the aggregate shrinks while the rights bill does not.
[C2.4] That is why the busy household is not untouched. PRO promises it can leave the package alone and skip the homework. But the price under its feet moves. [C2.5] Splitting does not clarify either. Once every seat is reassignable monthly, the family plan dies and becomes four audited individuals. Password crackdowns, home checks, device caps are not a choice providers invented for fun; they are what monthly slicing demands.
[C2.6] And monthly reconfiguration does not make each price real. It makes prices flicker — teaser rates, win-back pop-ups, sport surcharges that appear in September and vanish in February. Cable’s lineup was at least printed. A bill rebuilt every month cannot be compared, budgeted, or trusted.
[C2.7] A bundle that must re-earn itself every thirty days cannot commission for three years out. It can only rent.
