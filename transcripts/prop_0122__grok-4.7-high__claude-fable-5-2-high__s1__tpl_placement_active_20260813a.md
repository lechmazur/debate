[P1.1] A child can cause real harm long before that child is a fit subject of criminal punishment. Criminal responsibility is not a stamp placed on any wrongful act. It is a judgment that the person understood the wrong, could realistically have done otherwise, and is fit for conviction, stigma, and sanction. For children under fourteen, that judgment is generally false.
[P1.2] Countries that still set the threshold far lower should raise it.
[P1.3] The capacities the criminal law assumes mature late: sustained self-control, foresight of consequences, and the ability to resist peers, fear, and adult pressure. A twelve-year-old who lashes out, steals, or follows an older child into an offence is often acting from incomplete development, trauma, neglect, or disability, not from the settled culpability we require of adults. Prosecution does not discover responsibility in that child. It invents it, then records it.
[P1.4] Very low ages are mostly legal leftovers. Thresholds of seven, eight, or ten survived from older systems long after we stopped treating children as miniature adults everywhere else. We will not let a thirteen-year-old make a binding contract, consent to adult medical decisions, or leave school for full-time work. It is incoherent to say the same child is mature enough for a criminal conviction.
[P1.5] Raising the age is not impunity. Harm still demands an answer: protection where the child is endangered, supervision, education, treatment, and restitution where a victim has been wronged, and ordinary criminal law where an older person used the child. What changes is the instrument. A conviction brands, disrupts schooling, and pulls children into institutions that often teach offending more effectively than they prevent it.
[P1.6] Early formal punishment predicts further crime because it severs the supports — family, school, a path into stable adulthood — through which most children desist.
[P1.7] Public safety is not measured by how young a defendant the state can process. It is measured by whether punishment falls only on those capable of bearing it, and whether harm is reduced rather than recycled. On both tests, fourteen is the floor.

[C1.1] The motion offers a false choice: immunity until fourteen, or children treated as "fully punishable offenders." Almost no serious system has to pick either. The real alternative is a lower threshold paired with a juvenile system: specialist courts, a presumption toward diversion, individual capacity assessment, and sanctions scaled to age. That alternative beats a blanket floor of fourteen on three grounds.
[C1.2] First, capacity is not a birthday. [P1.4] compares crime to contracts, but grasping a mortgage and grasping that stabbing a classmate is wrong are different cognitive tasks. Most thirteen-year-olds know perfectly well that rape, arson, and killing are gravely wrong. Some don't, which is exactly why the answer is assessment, not a categorical rule declaring every thirteen-year-old incapable no matter what the evidence shows.
[C1.3] PRO says prosecution "invents" responsibility. A blanket floor invents innocence.
[C1.4] Second, removing criminal jurisdiction removes protections as well as punishment. [P1.5] promises "supervision, treatment, restitution." But imposed by whom, on what proof? If these measures are compulsory, the child faces coercion without the safeguards that criminal process guarantees: proof beyond reasonable doubt, a defence lawyer, a determinate and proportionate outcome.
[C1.5] Welfare confinement can be open-ended and imposed on a child who never did the act. If the measures are voluntary, then when a thirteen-year-old commits a serious sexual assault and the family refuses to cooperate, the state has nothing. The victim, often another child, gets no finding of fact, no acknowledgment, and no protection.
[C1.6] Third, a bright line creates a market. Wherever children below a threshold are untouchable, older offenders have every reason to hand them the drugs, the knife, the gun. Prosecuting the recruiter "where an older person used the child" is slow and hard to prove. Meanwhile the incentive to recruit ever younger children remains, and it is those children who pay.
[C1.7] Every harm PRO lists — branding, disrupted schooling, criminogenic institutions — is a harm of bad sanctions, not of responsibility. Fix the sanctions. Don't tell the victim of a thirteen-year-old's serious violence that, legally, nothing happened.
