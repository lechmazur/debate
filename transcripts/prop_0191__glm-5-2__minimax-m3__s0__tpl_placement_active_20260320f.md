[P1.2] Drug metabolism is not uniform. Sex differences in pharmacokinetics are documented across numerous drug classes — women have metabolized certain sedatives more slowly, producing dosing recommendations that were wrong for half the population for years. Age changes hepatic and renal clearance dramatically. Genetic variation across ethnic groups affects enzymes like CYP2D6 and CYP2C19, which metabolize a large share of commonly prescribed drugs.
[P1.3] When trials enroll predominantly young, white, male subjects, the resulting label is a precision instrument aimed at the wrong target.
[P1.4] The alternative — approving faster and catching problems through post-market surveillance — sounds pragmatic but reverses the burden of risk. In a trial, participants give informed consent. In the market, patients receiving an approved drug assume it has been tested for someone like them. When it hasn't, adverse events aren't discovered in a controlled setting; they're discovered in emergency rooms and funeral homes.
[P1.5] The cost of that discovery falls on the people least represented in the original trial — which is to say, the people already most marginalized by the research enterprise.
[P1.6] The CON side will frame speed as a patient welfare issue: faster approvals mean faster access to treatment. But access to what?
