[P1.1] Tax exemptions for religious institutions are not a divine right; they are a civic bargain. Society grants these organizations immense financial advantages—relieving them of property and income taxes and making donations tax-deductible—because they traditionally provide vital charitable and spiritual public goods. When a religious organization pivots from providing spiritual guidance or sheltering the vulnerable to endorsing candidates and operating as a partisan political actor, it breaks that bargain. When a church acts like a Political Action Committee, it should be taxed like one.
[P1.2] First, tax exemption functions as a public subsidy. When a religious organization uses tax-free dollars to run partisan campaigns, the broader public is forced to underwrite that political speech. A taxpayer who fundamentally opposes a specific political candidate should not have their tax burden indirectly increased to subsidize a religious institution’s partisan campaign for that very candidate.
[P1.3] Second, allowing partisan religious organizations to keep their tax privileges creates a dangerous loophole in campaign finance. Traditional political campaigns and advocacy groups are subject to strict transparency, contribution limits, and taxation rules. If religious organizations can engage in overt partisan politics while retaining tax-deductible status, they become the ultimate dark-money vehicles. Wealthy donors could funnel unlimited, tax-deductible funds into a cooperative church to sway an election, hiding behind the veil of religious liberty to bypass campaign finance laws entirely.
[P1.4] Finally, removing this tax advantage does not infringe on religious freedom. Religious leaders and congregants remain entirely free to speak their minds, advocate for moral issues, and vote their conscience. This policy merely establishes that if a religious entity chooses to cross the line into overt partisan electioneering—coordinating with campaigns or explicitly endorsing candidates—it must operate under the same tax rules as any other political apparatus.
[P1.5] We cannot allow partisan organizations to masquerade as charities to evade taxes and democratic transparency. If an institution wants to wield its institutional power in the arena of partisan politics, it must pay the standard price of admission.

[C1.1] The central problem with this proposition is that it hands the state a vague and dangerous line-drawing power over religious speech. The moment tax authorities decide whether a sermon, voter guide, or moral appeal is “overtly partisan,” they must interrogate theology, intent, and emphasis. That is not neutral administration. It invites intrusive investigations and selective enforcement.
[C1.2] A church that calls abortion, poverty, immigration, or war a moral crisis may be accused of covert partisanship because its teaching happens to align with one party’s platform. The rule chills exactly the kind of moral witness that religious bodies have offered for centuries, including abolition, civil rights, and labor reform.
[C1.3] Second, the penalty is radically disproportionate to the harm. The proposition would not merely tax a campaign advertisement; it threatens the tax advantages of the whole institution. If a congregation of a few hundred members endorses a candidate in a bulletin, it could face property taxes that force it to close a food pantry or shelter. The people most harmed are not wealthy operatives but ordinary congregants and the poor served by religious charities.
[C1.4] If the concern is electioneering, the sensible response is to regulate the political activity itself—require disclosure, treat express advocacy as campaign speech, or tax a separate political committee—not to strip a house of worship and its charitable ministries of their civic standing.
[C1.5] Third, PRO’s civic bargain argument proves too much. Religious institutions receive tax advantages not because they promise political silence, but because they form conscience, sustain communities, and deliver services the state cannot replicate. A better test is whether the organization is primarily religious and charitable, not whether a public statement is politically uncomfortable. Where actual coordination or illegal contributions occur, use campaign-finance law.
[C1.6] Taxation does not create transparency; disclosure does. But this motion would create a broader regime of suspicion and state entanglement with religion. That is a worse bargain than the one it claims to defend.
