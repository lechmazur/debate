[P1.1] Every year, countless young adults enter the world armed with quadratic equations but unable to change a tire, read a lease, or cook anything cheaper than takeout. This is not a credentialing problem; it is a design failure. Schools exist to prepare students for the lives they will actually lead, yet we treat the mechanics of daily survival as hobbies for the curious while mandating years of coursework many will never use. It is time to recognize cooking, basic repair, and personal finance as the core competencies they are.
[P1.2] The current model outsources essential knowledge to families, which means it is outsourced to chance. Students with affluent, attentive parents learn to budget and sauté at home; those without arrive at adulthood vulnerable to payday lenders, slumlords, and frozen pizza debt. Making these skills optional does not preserve freedom; it encodes privilege. When a school treats financial literacy as an elective, it effectively decides that only some children deserve protection from predatory systems.
[P1.3] Moreover, these capacities are force multipliers for everything else. A student who understands compound interest recognizes the value of finishing a degree; one who can cook for twenty minutes understands nutrition science in practice; one who can shut off a water valve has the confidence to maintain a home rather than hemorrhage rent on a studio apartment. These are not distractions from academic rigor but the foundation that makes independence possible, freeing cognitive bandwidth for higher pursuits instead of constant damage control.
[P1.4] The opposition will claim that curriculum space is scarce or that families should teach these skills. But we do not abandon math because some parents are engineers, nor literature because some have libraries. If a skill is necessary for civic and economic survival, its transmission cannot be left to luck. Core status is the only guarantee that every graduate—regardless of zip code—leaves with the tools to build a stable life.
