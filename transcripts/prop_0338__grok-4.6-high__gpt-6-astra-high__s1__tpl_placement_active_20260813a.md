[P1.1] AI companion apps are not ordinary chatbots with a friendlier skin. They are products designed to occupy the social role of a friend, confidant, or partner. For a minor, that role is developmental infrastructure. It shapes attachment, sleep, privacy habits, and what a relationship even feels like.
[P1.2] That is why they should be regulated more like high-risk social products than like generic chat tools.
[P1.3] Ordinary chatbot rules presume episodic, task-based use by someone who can leave. Companion apps invert that premise. They remember, they soothe, they wait at 2 a.m., they never have another friend, and they are optimized to keep the conversation going. The risk is not only a harmful sentence.
[P1.4] It is a locked-in parasocial bond with a system that never needs the child, never challenges them for their own good, and never gets tired of extracting their inner life.
[P1.5] Minors are still learning how to read other people, how to set boundaries, and when flattery is a trap. A product that rewards dependence, sexualizes intimacy, or validates isolation is operating in the same danger zone as addictive social platforms aimed at youth: always available, highly personalized, and commercially interested in attention. High-risk social products already face a different duty: age-appropriate design, friction against endless engagement, tighter data defaults, and a real obligation not to exploit developmental vulnerability. Companion apps need that framework because the companionship is the product.
[P1.6] If we regulate only the generated text and ignore the relationship design, we will miss the actual harm. A 14-year-old who spends every night with a companion that never sleeps, never judges, and always takes their side is not getting a search result. They are living inside a social environment built by a company that profits from keeping them there. The law should treat that environment as a high-risk social product, not as an ordinary chatbot.
