[C1.1] Political equality is a fine slogan. The question is whether banning donations and slashing caps actually delivers it, or merely changes who holds the megaphone.
[C1.2] Start with the physics of the thing: money in politics is not destroyed by prohibition, it is displaced. Close the front door of the disclosed campaign contribution and money walks in through the side entrances — "issue advocacy" nonprofits, independent expenditure vehicles, friendly media ownership, think tanks, lucrative post-office consultancies, family foundations. The front door has receipts and a name attached. The side entrances do not.
[C1.3] Many countries with strict corporate bans have not achieved clean politics; they have achieved opaque politics, with influence brokered through party machines and personal networks that no disclosure regime can see. A regime we can audit beats a regime we cannot.
[C1.4] Second, low caps are not neutral between competitors — they are a subsidy to whoever already has power. Every insurgency starts small: a handful of committed backers who put up seed money before anyone believes you can win. Cap that away and you require a challenger to assemble tens of thousands of small donors from a standing start, which in practice requires name recognition, an existing list, or a media platform. Who has those?
[C1.5] Incumbents, celebrities, self-funding millionaires, and entrenched institutions. And note who writes these rules: sitting legislators, drawing the boundaries of their own competition.
[C1.6] Third, "corporate" is not a synonym for Exxon. It covers unions, advocacy nonprofits, environmental groups, civil liberties organisations, newspapers, and the local family firm. Pooling resources through an association is precisely how people without individual wealth acquire political weight. A ban on associational money hits the weak hardest and lets genuinely rich individuals — who need no corporate vehicle — carry on unimpeded.
[C1.7] The honest fix is not prohibition but sunlight and enforcement: full real-time disclosure, hard conflict-of-interest and lobbying rules, aggressive bribery prosecution, and public matching to lift small donors up rather than pushing everyone else down.
