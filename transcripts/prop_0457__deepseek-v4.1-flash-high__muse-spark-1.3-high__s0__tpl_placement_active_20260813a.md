[P1.1] Climate policy is now infrastructure policy, and infrastructure policy is permitting policy. A target is not a ton of carbon; a solar farm, wind project, or transmission line is. When governments let every project be studied, relitigated, and stalled for years by any local objection, they quietly choose the status quo—and the status quo is the most destructive environmental policy of all.
[P1.2] The proposition does not abolish environmental law. It streamlines it: shot clocks, one lead agency, consolidated judicial review, categorical exclusions for low-impact projects, and limits on late-stage injunctions. Those tools make good projects possible without pretending every kilowatt needs a decade of paperwork.
[P1.3] Local review has a structural bias. It counts local disruption and discounts global benefit. A transmission line that unlocks clean power for millions may cross a county that receives few direct benefits and organized opposition. If that county can veto or sue indefinitely, the decarbonization that helps everyone—including that county—does not happen.
[P1.4] That is not healthy democracy; it is decentralization producing paralysis.
[P1.5] CON will say litigation catches mistakes. Sometimes it does. But delay is also a harm. Every year a gas plant runs while a clean line is tied up is another year of pollution, asthma, and climate damage.
[P1.6] The question is not whether review has value. It is whether the current level of review is worth its cost. It is not.
[P1.7] Significant streamlining still allows emergency stops for demonstrable, serious harms and retains core substantive standards. What it removes is procedural weaponization: duplicative reviews and lawsuits whose main function is delay.
[P1.8] So the real comparison is this: perfect local process with insufficient clean energy, or imperfect but faster build-out that actually cuts emissions. Climate change is cumulative. Speed is not a technical detail; it is the policy.
