[P1.1] Bodily autonomy is the foundational prerequisite for all moral agency. Before a person can pursue any conception of a good life, they must have sovereign control over their own physical vessel. In personal medical choices, autonomy must carry more moral weight than appeals to dignity because autonomy is an objective, individual right, whereas dignity is a subjective, socially constructed concept easily weaponized by third parties.
[P1.2] When autonomy and dignity conflict in medicine, prioritizing dignity inevitably results in medical paternalism. Consider a terminally ill patient who chooses to refuse artificial nutrition, preferring to pass naturally. The state often intervenes, arguing that starving is "undignified," and forces a feeding tube. Here, the appeal to dignity is not protecting the patient; it is overriding their explicit wishes to satisfy society’s discomfort with bodily decline.
[P1.3] This coercive use of dignity strips the patient of their final agency. By prioritizing an external standard of dignity over bodily autonomy, the state commits a profound moral violation: it turns the patient’s body into a project for public sensibilities.
[P1.4] Furthermore, dignity is inherently pluralistic and subjective. What one person considers a dignified medical intervention—such as aggressive life support, experimental surgeries, or assisted dying—another might view as profoundly degrading. Because dignity lacks a universal, objective metric, it cannot serve as the supreme moral arbiter in medical ethics without imposing one group's values on another. Autonomy, conversely, provides a clear, actionable boundary: the individual is the sole author of their physical limits.
[P1.5] If we allow abstract appeals to dignity to supersede bodily autonomy, we strip individuals of the fundamental right to define their own suffering, their own bodies, and their own ends. Autonomy must prevail because it is the only principle guaranteeing the patient remains the true author of their life, rather than a captive subject of someone else’s moral aesthetic.
