[P1.1] Donor-conceived adults hold a fundamental, inalienable right to know their genetic parentage, and that right cannot be traded away to prop up gamete donation supply. This is no trivial preference for casual family lore. It is tied to concrete, life-altering needs: accurate, complete family medical history that can catch hereditary cancer, heart disease, or rare genetic conditions before they become untreatable; resolution of the quiet, pervasive identity dissonance many donor-conceived people describe growing up, cut off from half their biological roots with no legal path to answers; and the basic ability to avoid accidental incest with unknown genetic siblings, a real risk as anonymous donor cohorts grow larger.
[P1.2] The supply argument frames donor-conceived people as secondary to the preferences of adults seeking to conceive and donors who want permanent, enforceable secrecy around their parentage. That framing is morally backwards. We abandoned permanently sealed adoption records, even when defenders claimed closed placements boosted adoption supply, because an adopted person’s claim to their own identity outranks the comfort of every other party to the arrangement. We reject unregulated organ sales even when proponents argue open markets would drastically increase organ supply, because some trades exploit the people most harmed by the transaction too deeply to justify, no matter the benefit to others.
[P1.3] Even if ending anonymity permanently reduced donation volumes, a smaller, transparent system that treats the people born from donation as full, equal rights-bearers is vastly preferable to a larger system that treats their identity as a disposable contractual term. Donors and intended parents enter these arrangements by choice; donor-conceived people do not, and their most basic claims to knowledge about their own lives cannot be signed away before they are even born.

[P2.1] CON’s core warning that ending anonymity will push people to unregulated, unsafe markets is not an argument against the right—it is an argument for properly regulating donation to stop that evasion, just as we did not roll back open adoption records because some people attempted private, unregulated placements to avoid them. Surrendering a fundamental right out of fear some actors will break rules is a race to the bottom that leaves every donor-conceived person with fewer protections, exactly the outcome CON claims to fear.
[P2.2] CON’s proposed “narrow alternatives” are hollow. Static, one-time medical files cannot capture new diagnoses that emerge in a donor or their extended family decades after donation—those life-saving updates require an identifiable, reachable person. Voluntary sibling registries leave accidental incest risks intact for anyone not signed up, and mutual-consent-only systems leave donor-conceived people dependent on a donor’s choice to opt in, rather than holding a basic entitlement to facts about their own body and origins.
[P2.3] CON’s claim that parents will hide donor conception from their children if names are released gets incentives exactly backwards. Permanent, enforceable anonymity is what feeds the destructive myth that donor conception is a shameful secret to bury. When identity release is the universal, unremarkable legal default, there is no dramatic “surprise” arrival to fear, so parents have far less reason to lie.
[P2.4] CON’s analogies fail: organ donation does not create a lifelong genetic parent-child relationship, so that comparison collapses. The argument that a smaller system means some people are never born misframes the debate: we protect the rights of existing donor-conceived adults, not trade their interests against hypothetical future people barred forever from their own identity. No contract between adults can bind a child who never signed it.
