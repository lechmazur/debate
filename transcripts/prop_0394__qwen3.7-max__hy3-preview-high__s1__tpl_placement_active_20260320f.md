[P1.2] First, presumptive access leverages the curb-cut effect. The benefits extend far beyond students with documented disabilities. Denying these tools to the broader student body just to avoid accommodating a disabled minority is inefficient.

[P1.7] We are not asking to dismantle rigorous education; we are asking to dismantle unnecessary barriers. The burden of proof should not rest on the student to prove they deserve to understand the lecture. It should rest on the institution to prove why a student shouldn't. Presumptive access aligns our institutional defaults with the actual purpose of learning.

[C1.1] A presumptive mandate to record and caption every lecture fundamentally alters the pedagogical relationship between instructor and student, shifting the focus from active engagement to passive consumption. [C1.2] Recordings and captions, while useful for review, often degrade the quality of the live educational experience. When students know a lecture is being recorded, "zoom fatigue" and physical absenteeism increase. The classroom becomes a studio, and the "back channel" of spontaneous question-and-answer sessions—the heart of seminars and advanced coursework—often withers. The motion’s assumption that "more access equals better learning" ignores the critical role of "acute presence" in cognitive retention.
[C1.3] Furthermore, the "curb-cut" argument fails to account for the significant resource diversion involved. Creating truly accessible materials—synchronized captions, verified transcripts, and searchable indexes—requires substantial institutional funds and IT support. These are resources that could otherwise be directed toward smaller class sizes or direct student support services. By making recording the "presumptive" baseline, we mandate a one-size-fits-all technological solution that may benefit a subset of students while providing diminishing returns for the majority.
[C1.4] Finally, the "strong pedagogical reason" safeguard is a procedural trap. In practice, "presumptive" policies become "mandatory" ones due to administrative inertia and the fear of bias complaints. If a professor believes a "flipped classroom" or a sensitive role-play exercise is essential, they should not have to navigate a bureaucratic hurdle to protect the integrity of their teaching. The proposition replaces academic judgment with a technological imperative, ultimately devaluing the human element of instruction that no recording can replicate.
