[P1.1] A juvenile record was never meant to be a life sentence. For a century, juvenile courts operated on a simple promise: we treat children differently because children change, and the point of intervention is to return them to ordinary life, not to brand them. That promise used to be kept by accident. Records sat in county basements, on index cards, in files nobody could find without a car and a reason.
[P1.2] Practical obscurity did the work that law only gestured at.
[P1.3] Digitization destroyed that. A single searchable database entry, a scraped court docket, a commercial background-check aggregator, and a 15-year-old's shoplifting charge follows a 25-year-old into every apartment application, licensing board, and hiring portal for the rest of her life. Nothing was decided to make that happen. No legislature voted for permanent exposure.
[P1.4] It is an artifact of storage technology. The proposition simply asks courts to restore, deliberately, the protection that the filing cabinet used to provide by default.
[P1.5] Notice what the word "presumptively" is doing. This is not abolition of access. It is a burden-allocation rule: at the moment a person legally becomes an adult, the default flips, and anyone who wants the file — a prosecutor, a journalist, a victim, a researcher, a court weighing a later sentence — must give a reason. That is how we already treat things we consider presumptively private: medical files, adoption records, grand jury material.
[P1.6] Access on justification, not access by scraping.
[P1.7] The alternative default is worse on every dimension that matters. It punishes people for conduct committed at an age when we deny them the vote, contracts, and the presumption of adult culpability. It falls hardest on the kids who were policed most heavily, converting unequal childhood enforcement into permanent unequal adulthood. And it undercuts the state's own investment: society pays for rehabilitation programs and then blocks the employment that makes rehabilitation stick.
[P1.8] The question is which default a court should adopt when it does not know who will ask or why. The answer is the one that assumes a twenty-five-year-old [clipped]

[C1.1] PRO's story is sympathetic: a 25-year-old haunted by a shoplifting charge from age 15. But the motion doesn't target that case. It seals every juvenile's digital record, for every offense, on a birthday. That is where it fails.
[C1.2] First, the trigger is arbitrary. Turning eighteen proves nothing about rehabilitation. Under this rule, a seventeen-year-old adjudicated for armed robbery or sexual assault has that record presumptively sealed months later, before any evidence he has changed. PRO invokes "children change," but a presumption keyed to a calendar ignores whether this child has.
[C1.3] The better rule is earned relief: sealing after a clean period, graded by offense severity, with the shoplifter cleared quickly and the violent offender cleared only after time shows a changed life. That protects PRO's sympathetic example without extending the same default to everyone.
[C1.4] Second, sealing court records doesn't reach the harm PRO describes. PRO's villain is "a commercial background-check aggregator" [P1.3]. Those companies already copied the data. A court's seal binds the court, not a private database scraped years earlier.
[C1.5] So the motion blinds the public, journalists, and researchers while the brokers keep their copies. The real fix is regulating how background checks may use juvenile information: limits on reporting, on employer inquiries, on licensing questions. That targets the harm without going dark.
[C1.6] Third, "access on justification" [P1.6] is hollow when you can't see what you're asking for. A journalist can't show a reason to open a file she doesn't know exists. Juvenile courts already operate with little public scrutiny, and that has enabled abuse. Pennsylvania's "kids for cash" scandal, where judges sent children to detention in exchange for kickbacks, is the warning.
[C1.7] Worse, PRO's equity argument cuts against PRO. Proving that some kids were "policed most heavily" [P1.7] requires records researchers and advocates can examine. Seal everything by default, and the evidence of discriminatory enforcement gets sealed too.
[C1.8] So the choice is between a blanket rule that hides the system while missing the brokers, and targeted relief that frees the rehabilitated without blinding everyone else. Reject the blanket [clipped]
