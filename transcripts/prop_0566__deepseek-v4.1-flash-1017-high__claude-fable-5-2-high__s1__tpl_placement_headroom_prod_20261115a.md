[P1.1] Selective universities ration a scarce opportunity. The question is what should decide who gets it. Legacy and donor preferences answer with the wrong facts: who your parents are, and how much your family has given. That is inherited privilege dressed up as institutional loyalty.
[P1.2] Sharp limits are the minimum correction.
[P1.3] These preferences are not harmless traditions; they compound advantages applicants already have. A student with alumni parents arrives with tutoring, polished essays, and a school that knows how to work the system. Donor preference is worse: it can turn a seat into a transaction, making the child of a giver a special case before any file is read. At selective campuses, a handful of such choices crowd out first-generation and immigrant students who had to earn their polish without a family coach.
[P1.4] The proposition does not require pretending money is irrelevant to universities. Donations can fund scholarships, labs, and faculty; everyone can applaud that. But there is no need to pay donors in admissions decisions. Name a building, endow a chair, publish transparent giving priorities—do not sell a freshman seat.
[P1.5] If a gift is conditional on admitting a particular student, that is not philanthropy; it is a purchase.
[P1.6] Nor must sharp limits erase context. Applicants' own obstacles, achievements, and talents remain fair game; holistic review can weigh a job, a hardship, or a distinctive voice. What disappears is the ancestry-and-wealth thumb on the scale.
[P1.7] This also protects institutional credibility. When students suspect that a mediocre legacy got in because a parent wrote a check, every admitted student's accomplishment is stained. Public universities, funded by taxpayers, have a strong duty to treat applicants as equals. Private ones owe their communities a defensible answer about why birth and bank balance beat merit.
