[P1.1] Left to their own devices, legislatures treat public finances like a credit card with no limit and no repayment date. Election after election, the same pattern repeats: benefits expand in good times, emergencies justify fresh deficits in bad times, and the bill is deferred to future taxpayers who cannot vote today. That is why structural deficits accumulate even through long expansions. Constitutional balanced-budget provisions interrupt this logic.
[P1.2] By requiring planned spending to match revenues except when automatic stabilizers activate, they convert fiscal restraint from a temporary political promise into a durable institutional constraint that binds successor governments.
[P1.3] Automatic stabilizers—unemployment insurance, progressive tax collections, means-tested transfers—already expand and contract with the economy without new votes. Explicit exceptions for them prevent the rule from forcing austerity precisely when private demand collapses. The result is not rigid procyclical policy but a framework that cushions shocks while still disciplining the discretionary choices that generate chronic shortfalls.
[P1.4] Discretion alone has failed across rich and poor countries: large structural imbalances, interest payments crowding out productive investment, and vulnerability to sudden financing stops. Purely rigid balance would risk amplifying cycles. The proposition pairs constitutional discipline with automatic flexibility. That combination is superior to hoping that tomorrow’s majority will simply be thriftier than today’s.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like a neat compromise—constitutional discipline paired with automatic flexibility. But that promise rests on a fantasy: that you can write a fiscal rule rigid enough to bind politicians yet flexible enough to handle real crises. In practice, you get neither.
[C1.2] Start with the exception. Automatic stabilizers—unemployment insurance, progressive tax elasticities, means-tested transfers—are narrow instruments. They absorb perhaps one to three percent of GDP during a typical recession. But the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID pandemic each required discretionary fiscal responses an order of magnitude larger: bank recapitalizations, payroll support, infrastructure stimulus, emergency lending guarantees.
[C1.3] None of those fit neatly into "automatic stabilizer" categories. A constitutional balanced-budget requirement, even with the PRO's exception, would have forced governments to either delay these responses until the damage was catastrophic or override the constitution itself—undermining constitutional authority precisely when it matters most.
[C1.4] Then consider enforcement. A constitutional fiscal rule requires someone to adjudicate it. If courts enforce it, judges become fiscal policymakers—ordering spending cuts or tax hikes mid-recession, with no democratic mandate and no economic expertise. If the rule is enforced only by political norms, it offers nothing beyond legislative discretion.
[C1.5] The PRO side cannot have it both ways.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, fiscal discipline has been achieved without constitutional mandates. Countries with strong political consensus and credible medium-term frameworks have sustained surpluses through ordinary legislation. The problem PRO identifies—chronic structural deficits—is a problem of political incentives, and constitutions do not rewrite incentives; they shift the battlefield.
[C1.7] The real choice is not "constitutional rule versus fiscal chaos." It is rigid constitutional constraint that risks pro-cyclical austerity and judicial overreach versus democratic fiscal governance improvable through fiscal councils, independent budget offices, and transparent medium-term frameworks—without entrenching a rule future generations cannot adapt to circumstances we cannot foresee.
