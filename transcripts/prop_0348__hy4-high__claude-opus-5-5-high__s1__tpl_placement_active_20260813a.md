[P1.1] Routine care already has a front door: a morning phone line, a portal message, or a worried search engine at midnight. The choice is not AI versus clinicians; it is a governed, approved front door versus an improvised one. Health systems should choose the governed one.
[P1.2] An approved AI symptom-checking and triage tool can do the boring work that makes care safer: collect a structured history, ask red-flag questions, identify urgency, explain what can wait, and pass a concise summary to a clinician. Someone with fever and sore throat should learn whether home care, a routine appointment, urgent assessment, or emergency care fits, and have that reasoning attached to the record. This does not automate diagnosis; it organizes first contact so human attention starts from better information.
[P1.3] The access case is decisive. Discouragement assumes patients will do without help. They will search, use unvetted chatbots, crowd-source symptoms, delay until a problem worsens, or go to emergency departments because that door feels open. Those routes are less transparent, less auditable, and often worse for clinicians.
[P1.4] Approved tools can be monitored, updated, limited to reviewed guidance, checked for bias, and built to escalate to a nurse or doctor when uncertainty or danger appears.
[P1.5] Safety objections therefore misfire. The dangerous version is an isolated consumer bot implying that it knows the diagnosis. The safer version is a health system saying: here is the reviewed tool, here are its limits, here are escalation thresholds, and here is how information reaches a clinician. Discouragement removes the system from the precise moment when patients decide what to do next, while preserving gatekeeping that rewards health literacy, free time, and confidence on the phone.
[P1.6] So the central burden is plain: discouragement must produce safer, fairer, more timely routine care than supervised approved use. A closed door cannot do that. A visible, auditable, escalation-ready front door can.
