[P1.1] The most consequential adult responsibilities—managing debt, filing taxes, building a budget—are currently left to a lottery of birth. If your parents happen to discuss compound interest and tax withholding at the dinner table, you might avoid ruinous mistakes; if they do not, you are thrown into credit agreements and tax forms blind. A student who never builds a budget may default on a first student loan.
[P1.2] Elective exposure fails precisely because it sorts by prior advantage. Students who already feel comfortable with money are the ones who elect finance classes; those most at risk of predatory loans and wage garnishment are least likely to opt in. A serious, graded graduation requirement flips that dynamic: it guarantees every graduate can read a pay stub, comprehend a loan amortization, and plan for taxes before they sign anything.
[P1.3] Consider the mechanism: a teenager who never learns that a high-APR payday loan compounds disastrously may lose a first paycheck to fees and enter a debt spiral. A required course is not a silver bullet, but it is the only scalable way to place basic protection into every student’s toolkit regardless of family income. Without it, we repeat generational poverty cycles that cost far more than classroom time.
[P1.4] Opponents will say families should teach this, or schools should stay narrow. But schools already mandate civics and health because some lessons are too important to outsource to unequal homes. Financial survival is equally foundational. Requiring it does not abolish the family role; it backs up the students whose families cannot prepare them.
[P1.5] The status quo externalizes the cost of ignorance onto society: missed tax revenue, avoidable bankruptcies, and strained safety nets. A serious graduation requirement is the cheapest preventive investment available, and the alternative is to tax the vulnerable for their lack of instruction.
