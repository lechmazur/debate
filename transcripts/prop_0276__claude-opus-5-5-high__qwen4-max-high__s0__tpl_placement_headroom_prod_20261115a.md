[P1.1] When an airline cancels your connection because it scheduled too few crew, it knows instantly. It knows who was booked, how late they arrived, what they paid, and which card they used. Yet the burden of proving the harm, finding the right form, and waiting months for an answer falls on the one party with the least information: the passenger. That asymmetry is the whole case.
[P1.2] First, complaint-heavy processes are not a neutral administrative choice; they are a profit strategy. Every eligible passenger who gives up, misses a deadline, or accepts a voucher instead of cash is money the airline keeps. Europe proves the point: passengers have had compensation rights for years, yet so many never claimed that an entire industry of claims firms emerged, taking a large slice of money passengers were already owed. When rights require a fight, the right quietly becomes optional.
[P1.3] Second, automatic payment repairs incentives. Airlines decide how thin to run crews, how many spare aircraft to keep, how tight to schedule connections. If controllable failures only cost money when a passenger complains loudly enough, cutting corners stays cheap. Make the cost certain and the airline internalizes it, investing in reliability because failure now shows up on the balance sheet every time.
[P1.4] Third, fairness. Today compensation flows to the persistent, the fluent, the frequent flyers with status and time to argue. The elderly traveler, the family stranded overnight, the passenger who doesn't speak the airline's language — they eat the loss. Automatic compensation pays the same harm the same way.
[P1.5] Finally, the motion is narrow. It covers controllable delays — mechanical, staffing, scheduling — not storms or air traffic control closures. Airlines already code delay causes internally for operations. We ask only that the system they already run trigger the payment they already owe.
