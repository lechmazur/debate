[P1.1] Theatrical-first as the default locks out millions to protect an outdated distribution window. Studios should make simultaneous home release—with robust accessibility—common for more big titles because the movie itself, not the auditorium, is the product audiences actually need.
[P1.2] Home viewing with serious accessibility delivers what theaters still treat as optional extras. Working captions, high-quality audio description, adjustable speed and pause, private seating, and controlled lighting let deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, blind and low-vision audiences, autistic people, those with mobility limits, and people managing chronic conditions experience the film on equal footing. Simultaneous release with those options built in makes inclusion the baseline instead of an afterthought or a delayed rental.
[P1.3] The barriers are not only medical. Rural viewers drive hours. Parents balance childcare against fixed showtimes. Shift workers, low-income households calculating tickets plus parking plus food, and people still cautious about crowded rooms all get told the cultural conversation is not yet for them.
[P1.4] That is discrimination by logistics dressed up as tradition. Expanding simultaneous windows shrinks that exclusion without erasing theatrical spectacle for those who seek it.
[P1.5] The motion does not demand that every film abandon theaters. It demands that exclusive theatrical priority stop being the automatic rule for almost every tentpole. When studios treat home as second-class, they shrink the legitimate audience that can join the moment of release, rewatch for comprehension, or share the work without logistical gymnastic. Day-and-date with real accessibility options widens who participates, widens who can pay, and stops confusing artificial scarcity with artistic value.
[P1.6] Theaters can still sell the big-screen event. They simply should not remain the gatekeeper of default access.
