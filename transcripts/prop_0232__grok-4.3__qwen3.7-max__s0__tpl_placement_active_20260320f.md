[P1.1] Legalizing cannabis nationally removes the artificial price premium that currently funds cartels. When governments license production and set competitive retail prices, the black-market markup disappears. Growers and distributors no longer need armed protection or corrupt officials to move product, so the same volume of cannabis generates far less revenue for criminal organizations.
[P1.2] Cartels treat cannabis as a low-risk, high-volume earner that subsidizes cocaine and fentanyl operations. Once legal supply satisfies demand, that cross-subsidy shrinks. Enforcement resources can then focus on harder drugs and violent crimes instead of chasing every cannabis shipment. The result is not the elimination of all organized crime, but the targeted erosion of its most stable profit center.
[P1.3] National regulation also prevents the patchwork problem that lets traffickers exploit differing state rules. A single set of licensing, taxation, and age controls closes internal borders to illegal cannabis flows and gives police a legal market they can monitor rather than an invisible one they must disrupt. Countries that keep prohibition in place continue to hand cartels both the profits and the recruitment narrative that prohibition is a failed external imposition.
[P1.4] The mechanism is straightforward: move the transaction into daylight under rules the state can enforce, and the groups that thrived only in darkness lose ground.

[P2.2] The claim that cartels will simply undercut legal prices ignores the effect of national regulation on supply scale and enforcement. When production is licensed at volume, compliant operators can match or beat black-market costs on the domestic market without the overhead of constant armed protection. Cross-border smuggling becomes riskier and less profitable once legal channels exist inside the country, because police can inspect documented shipments rather than chase invisible ones.
[P2.3] State-capacity concerns cut the opposite way. Prohibition already places the entire trade outside official view, leaving regulators with no records to audit and no licenses to revoke when front companies appear. A regulated market creates taxable entities that must submit to inspections and banking oversight; corrupt officials or criminal infiltrators can still be prosecuted under the same licensing rules that do not exist when everything remains illegal.
[P2.4] The decisive difference lies in where violence occurs. Under prohibition, groups fight to control production and routes. Under national legalization, the fight shifts to protecting legal supply chains that the state can monitor and disrupt with ordinary commercial law instead of perpetual military-style interdiction.
