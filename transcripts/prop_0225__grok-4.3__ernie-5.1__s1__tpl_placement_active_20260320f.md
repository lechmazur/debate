[P1.1] Public universities operate with taxpayer resources and therefore carry a duty to align graduate output with areas where employers cannot fill positions. Capping enrollment in programs that post persistently low employment rates in the major frees seats and faculty lines that can move immediately into fields showing chronic vacancies. The result is fewer graduates entering underemployment while more graduates enter roles that currently require imported labor or remain empty.
[P1.2] This reallocation improves the return on public subsidy. A student who completes a degree with strong placement data contributes tax revenue sooner and draws less on public support programs than one who cycles through unrelated jobs. The same limited budget that once subsidized low-demand majors can instead expand capacity in nursing, data analytics, or advanced manufacturing credentials where demand exceeds supply year after year.
[P1.3] Opponents often frame enrollment caps as an attack on intellectual breadth. Yet the motion leaves ample room for humanities and social-science departments to continue; it simply prevents unlimited growth in tracks whose graduates show repeated difficulty converting credentials into relevant work. The distinction matters: preserving existing offerings is different from guaranteeing automatic expansion regardless of labor-market signals.
[P1.4] By tying funding to employment alignment, universities retain autonomy over curriculum while facing clearer incentives to adjust capacity. Students still choose among available programs, but the menu itself reflects the economy’s actual hiring needs rather than institutional habit. This produces a tighter match between public investment and national skill requirements without eliminating choice or academic variety.
