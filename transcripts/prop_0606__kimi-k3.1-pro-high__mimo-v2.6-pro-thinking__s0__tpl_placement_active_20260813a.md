This motion creates a defined escape valve: when an essential medicine crosses a predetermined affordability threshold, access becomes grounds for compulsory licensing. [C1.1] Access to essential medicines matters too much to be entrusted to a rule that fires mechanically and delivers nothing by itself.
[C1.2] Begin with the word doing the most damage: "whenever." The motion does not authorize governments to consider compulsory licensing; it commands them the instant an arbitrary number is crossed. But affordability is not one number. It depends on household income and insurance coverage, on whether a cheaper therapeutic alternative exists, on the size of the patient cohort, on the dose and duration, and on total budget impact.
[C1.3] A single fixed threshold either misses all of that context or swallows it — and it converts every pricing decision into a fight over where a minister drew the line, rather than a judgment about actual patient harm.
[C1.4] Second, a compulsory license is a permission slip, not a supply chain. It confers no factory, no formulation know-how, no active ingredient, no regulatory dossier, and no quality assurance. Most countries facing affordability crises are importers with little or no domestic manufacturing able to step in within a clinically relevant time. Under the TRIPS system, importing under a compulsory license is itself cumbersome.
[C1.5] So the mandated trigger reshapes bargaining leverage while risking not one additional course of treatment reaching a patient.
[C1.6] Third, the predictable response is worse than proponents admit. Faced with a standing trigger, manufacturers rationally withdraw launches, price every product at the threshold rather than below it, protect molecules through trade secrecy instead of patents, or delay entry in trigger-prone markets. The people harmed are patients in middle-income countries waiting for the next essential therapy — not the executives in wealthy markets where the returns remain. Innovation incentives live on expected returns across a portfolio; a rule that reliably seizes the upside also reliably shifts capital elsewhere.
[C1.7] Finally, the motion forecloses the tools that actually work: tiered pricing, negotiated and voluntary licenses — which delivered affordable HIV antiretrovirals without any mandate — pooled procurement, and compulsory licensing used surgically as a genuine last resort. This motion converts last resort into first response. [clipped]

[P2.1] The opposition’s central error is to confuse a legal ground with a self-executing factory order. “Whenever” means that once a country’s announced affordability line is crossed, access cannot be dismissed as irrelevant to patent enforcement; it does not mean a license appears without remuneration, regulatory review, or a viable supplier. The motion removes discretion to deny that ground, not the practical need for implementation.
[P2.2] Nor must the threshold be “one arbitrary number.” A defined rule can distinguish household and public-payer affordability, account for insurance and therapeutic alternatives, and be revised transparently. CON demands contextual judgment, then offers discretionary negotiation—the setting where lobbying, unequal bargaining power, and delay thrive. A published threshold prevents context from becoming an indefinite excuse.
[P2.3] A license is indeed not a supply chain. But where patent exclusivity is the legal barrier, every alternative supply chain begins by removing it. Generic production, regional procurement, importation, or a voluntary agreement can then deliver treatment; without that legal opening, even a willing supplier may be stopped. The “permission slip” argument proves implementation matters, not that patients should await the patent holder’s consent.
[P2.4] Their most revealing prediction is that firms might price at the threshold. If that threshold genuinely measures affordability, this is compliance and access, not injury. Launch delays and capital shifts are risks to address through sensible thresholds, remuneration, and implementation—not reasons to give monopoly pricing a veto. Portfolio-wide incentives also undermine predictions of collapse: this rule concerns essential medicines only after excessive pricing, while ordinary markets and royalty income remain.
[P2.5] Nothing in the motion bans tiered pricing, pooled procurement, or voluntary licenses. It prevents them from becoming a permanent waiting room. CON calls compulsory licensing a last resort but supplies no enforceable endpoint when negotiations fail and patients cannot pay. On their side, crossing the affordability line triggers more discussion; on ours, it triggers a genuine legal route to competition.
[P2.6] Possible supply frictions cannot justify preserving the exclusion this rule is designed to end.

[P4.2] A2: The government sets the methodology publicly and in advance, with transparent revision rather than case-by-case bargaining. [P4.3] This resolves CON’s alleged dilemma. The motion mandates recognition of the ground and a licensing route; it does not suspend drug regulation or conjure manufacturing. [P4.5] Finally, automaticity strengthens rather than destroys voluntary dealing. A firm can avoid the trigger through genuinely affordable pricing or negotiated licenses, retaining ordinary market control; refusal no longer means indefinite delay. Pricing at an affordability-based threshold is not injury, and a multi-part threshold need not impose one price on everyone.
