[P1.1] Renting a home is not a minor purchase; it is a year-long commitment to a specific building. Yet landlords currently sell that commitment while keeping the most decision-relevant facts about daily life hidden. The proposition is modest: if a building has recurring short-term-rental party complaints, severe noise problems, or chronic package theft, say so before signature. Not every slammed door.
[P1.2] Not one noisy weekend. Recurring, severe, chronic.
[P1.3] Those conditions are material because they strike at what tenants are actually paying for: quiet enjoyment, sleep, safety, and the ability to receive essentials. A tenant touring at 2 p.m. cannot see the Friday-night Airbnb party, the hallway that turns into a nightclub, or the package room emptied weekly. The landlord knows because complaints, security logs, and tenant turnover generate the record. The lease is signed under asymmetric information.
[P1.4] Disclosure corrects that asymmetry at almost no cost.
[P1.5] This is also fair risk allocation. If a landlord wants to rent a unit in a building with chronic package theft, fine—but the tenant deserves to know before being locked into a year of replacements and security worries. With disclosure, the tenant can choose another building, negotiate rent, or insist on a package locker. Without it, the landlord externalizes a known defect onto the person with the least ability to discover it.
[P1.6] And disclosure creates accountability. A landlord who must reveal recurring party complaints has an incentive to address them; a landlord who can bury them has an incentive to keep collecting rent and let the next tenant discover the problem. The proposition does not guarantee a perfect building. It guarantees that the tenant is not the last to know.
[P1.7] That is the standard: material, known, recurring conditions should be disclosed before a binding commitment. Vote PRO.
