[P1.1] A statue in a courthouse square is not a neutral history lesson. It is a government-endorsed act of honor, maintained with public resources and placed where citizens are expected to gather, protest, or seek justice. The question is therefore not whether slavery, colonial domination, or mass repression should be remembered. It is whether leaders primarily associated with those practices should continue receiving civic veneration.
[P1.2] They should not.
[P1.3] First, civic honor is a present-tense decision. A community may inherit a monument, but keeping it prominently displayed is an ongoing choice about whose achievements define public life and whose suffering can be treated as incidental. A pedestal does not merely say, “This person existed.” Its scale, setting, and celebratory presentation say, “This person deserves admiration.” When the person’s central public legacy is enslaving people, conquering them, or crushing them, that message conflicts with equal citizenship. Descendants of the victims should not have to pass state-sponsored tributes to their oppressors on the way into institutions that claim to serve everyone.
[P1.4] Second, relocation protects history better than either destruction or decontextualized display. Museums and contextualized sites can preserve the object, explain who commissioned it, identify the interests it served, and examine both the honored leader and the people excluded from that honor. A lone statue usually supplies a name and an heroic pose; serious interpretation can supply the political struggle, propaganda, and human costs behind it. Removing a monument from a place of honor is therefore not erasing the past.
[P1.5] It is refusing to let the past’s winners dictate the terms on which everyone else must remember it.
[P1.6] Opposition will likely invoke a slippery slope: today enslavers, tomorrow every flawed figure. [C1.1] A public square is inherited, not designed. This motion treats that inheritance as a standing endorsement we must either ratify or repudiate. I reject premise and mechanism alike.
[C1.2] Start with the limiting principle: "primarily associated" with slavery, colonial domination, or mass repression. That net catches everyone who governed before 1900—Washington, Jefferson, Churchill, every leader of a colonial-era state. Who applies it? Not calm committees but the loudest crowd in the square.
[C1.3] When the principle met the street in 2020, it did not stop at Confederates: Lincoln and Roosevelt were toppled in Portland, Grant—who broke the Confederacy—was pulled down in San Francisco, and Churchill's statue in London was boarded up. The "demanding limit" is parchment; the practice is set by whoever is angriest that year.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's core claim—that a statue is present-tense endorsement of the person—flattens how commemoration works. Churchill in Parliament Square honors the salvation of democracy in 1940, not colonial policy; Washington honors the founding, not Mount Vernon's slave quarters. We honor particular deeds by flawed people. If monuments implied endorsement of everything their subjects did, no one could be commemorated.
[C1.5] Third, relocation is not benign curation. A monument's meaning lives in its site—who raised it, when, why, in whose square. In a museum it becomes an artifact cut from the civic story it embodies; often it simply languishes in storage. That is erasure by logistics.
[C1.6] There is a better tool PRO ignores: contextualize in place. Explanatory plaques, counter-monuments to the enslaved and colonized, added inscriptions. Addition preserves the layered evidence—the man, the myth, the victims—and answers the descendant's grievance with more history, not less.
[C1.7] The choice is not honor versus memory. It is addition versus subtraction: a public square that keeps its history and argues with it, or one curated into moral safety by whoever wins the latest purge.
