[P1.1] When a city hands a team hundreds of millions of dollars, it is not making a donation. It is making an investment in a shared civic asset. The question is simple: once the public has paid, should the owner alone decide to walk away with what the public helped build? The answer is no.
[P1.2] First, public money creates public stakes. Taxpayers finance stadiums through bonds that run for decades. Owners can leave in a few years. St. Louis kept paying for a dome after the Rams left for Los Angeles.
[P1.3] Oakland was still carrying debt from renovating its stadium for the Raiders when they departed for Las Vegas. Seattle watched the SuperSonics vanish to Oklahoma City. In every case, the community kept the bill and lost the team. A binding approval process simply ensures that the people holding the debt get a vote on whether the deal ends.
[P1.4] Second, the threat of relocation is the engine of the subsidy racket. Owners extract new stadiums by hinting they might move, and cities pay because they have no leverage. Require community approval for subsidized teams, and that threat loses most of its force. This proposal doesn't just protect cities after a move; it disciplines every negotiation before one.
[P1.5] Third, this is basic contract fairness, not confiscation. Nothing forces an owner to accept public money. Teams that build privately remain free to move. But a team that accepts subsidies has accepted a partner, and partners don't get dissolved unilaterally.
[P1.6] Nor is this a permanent cage: a community can approve a move in exchange for repaying the subsidy, covering outstanding debt, or leaving the name and history behind, as Cleveland retained the Browns' identity.
[P1.7] The status quo lets owners privatize the gains and socialize the losses. Binding approval restores the bargain the public thought it was making. That accountability is the standard CON must defeat.

[C1.1] Binding community approval for relocation of subsidized pro sports teams sounds like fair accountability, but it replaces clear, enforceable contract terms with an unaccountable, open-ended veto that hurts taxpayers and fans far more than it helps. First, public subsidy deals already come with binding lease terms, debt repayment clauses, and relocation penalties negotiated up front. When the Rams left St. Louis, the city secured a settlement covering outstanding debt and damages precisely because those contract rights existed, no popular vote required.
[C1.2] A separate, mandatory plebiscite adds no clear protection; it replaces predictable, written bargains with the whims of low-turnout, emotionally charged elections swayed by last-minute ad campaigns, unrelated grievances, or grandstanding politicians with no stake in upholding the original deal’s fine print. Second, the rule creates perverse incentives on both sides. City officials would have zero pressure to deliver on promised stadium upkeep, surrounding development, transit access, or public safety commitments if they know a team cannot leave without voter approval, since voters will almost never reject the chance to keep a popular local asset no matter how badly the city mismanages its end.
[C1.3] For owners, the permanent risk of a populist veto will make public subsidy deals far less attractive, pushing teams to build fully private venues with higher ticket prices, fewer community programs, and no required public payback, rather than accept a string attached to a one-time public investment that often pays for itself in tourism and tax revenue within a decade.
[C1.4] Third, the rule traps teams in markets where economic conditions or fan support have collapsed, with no guarantee voters will accept full repayment of public funds if it means losing the team. That is not fair bargaining—it is a one-sided mandate breaking the mutuality at the heart of any legitimate deal.
