[P1.1] The line this debate should turn on is simple: detention may be a tool while removal is real; once removal is not reasonably foreseeable, it becomes a cage with no exit and no purpose. That is the evil the motion targets.
[P1.2] Immigration detention is civil. It is not a criminal sentence, and it is not imposed after a trial with the safeguards we reserve for punishment. If a government cannot remove someone, and no court has found them dangerous under a criminal standard, what exactly is indefinite detention accomplishing? It does not make a removal happen.
[P1.3] It punishes the accident of nationality—the person whose country refuses repatriation or whose papers cannot be obtained. A system that can hold someone for years without a removal date, a dangerousness finding, or meaningful review has stopped enforcing immigration law and started warehousing human beings.
[P1.4] Presumptive release plus case management is the better default because it matches the actual problem. Most immigration violations are about appearance and compliance, not violence. Case managers, reporting requirements, housing support, and court reminders address those risks directly. Detention does not make people more likely to be removed; it often makes cooperation harder by cutting them off from lawyers, family, and medical care.
[P1.5] Release with conditions can be tightened for those who abscond. The state keeps leverage without making liberty the exception.
[P1.6] The fiscal comparison matters too. Indefinite detention consumes enormous per-person resources for people who cannot be removed, while case management can supervise far more people at lower cost and with better outcomes. That is not softness; it is triage.
[P1.7] If the state can show a concrete danger or a real removal prospect, it can act. What it cannot do is call endless confinement “immigration policy.” The proposition replaces a failed default of confinement with a workable default of liberty.
