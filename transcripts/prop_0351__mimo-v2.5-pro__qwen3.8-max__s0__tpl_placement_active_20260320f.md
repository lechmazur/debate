[P1.1] The most critical career resource junior employees need is something remote work systematically strips away: informal learning through proximity. When a new analyst sits near a senior colleague, she overhears how that person handles a difficult client call, navigates office politics, or thinks through an ambiguous problem in real time. She gets unprompted feedback when someone glances at her draft and says, "Here's what I'd change." These interactions aren't scheduled.
[P1.2] They can't be replicated by a weekly video check-in. They happen because people are in the same space, and they compound over months and years into professional judgment that no training module replaces.
[P1.3] The damage falls asymmetrically. Senior employees have already built networks, reputations, and institutional knowledge. They lose convenience with remote work, not foundational career capital. Junior employees are still building these assets, and the early career window when learning is fastest is precisely when isolation does the most harm.
[P1.4] A senior manager who goes remote trades some hallway chatter for sweatpants. A first-year associate who goes remote trades the entire informal architecture through which careers are actually built.
[P1.5] Here is why the admission gap matters: both employers and employees have strong incentives to minimize these costs. Companies save significantly on real estate. Workers, especially mid-career ones, genuinely value flexibility. Surveys consistently show high satisfaction with remote arrangements, but they measure immediate preferences and daily comfort, not whether someone's career trajectory flattened over three years.
[P1.6] No one's annual review asks, "Did you miss formative experiences you can't even name because they never happened?" The absence of mentorship moments is invisible. You can't mourn what you never received.
[P1.7] This is not an argument that remote work has no benefits or that offices are always superior. It is the narrower claim that for junior employees specifically, the hidden erosion of career development is real and systematically underreported. I will argue that the mechanisms are clear, the asymmetry is decisive, and the incentives to look away are exactly what you'd expect if the harm were significant but easy to ignore.
