[P1.1] Public-facing digital services are no longer optional extras. They are how people pay bills, claim benefits, book care, and apply for work. When those services repeatedly fail basic accessibility—unlabeled buttons, no keyboard path, contrast too poor to read—the effect is not a UX nitpick. It is a locked door on civic and economic life.
[P1.2] The standards at issue are not exotic. They are the digital equivalent of a ramp and a readable sign: text alternatives, logical focus, captions, operable forms. This motion is not about perfection or a first honest mistake. After notice and time to fix known, basic failures, continued noncompliance is a choice.
[P1.3] Markets will not close that gap. Disabled users are too small a slice of traffic to win the roadmap. Teams ship what they measure, and exclusion is currently free. Firms know the playbook and still leave broken flows live because the harm falls on disabled people, not the product budget.
[P1.4] Meaningful fines change that math. Token slaps get written off. Real money makes compliance the cheapest path.
[P1.5] Guidance without teeth is ignored. Individual lawsuits are slow, costly, and random. Predictable fines for repeated basic breaches are how we already treat inaccessible buildings and unsafe workplaces. If a shop blocked wheelchair users week after week after being told, nobody would call another warning sufficient.
[P1.6] The internet is now the public square. Digital public doors deserve the same rule.
[P1.7] Without real penalties we accept a two-speed society: one internet that works if you can see, hear, and use a mouse, and another that quietly locks everyone else out. Fines do not punish innovation. They punish the decision to keep people out after you already know how to let them in.

[C1.1] Accessibility matters. The question is not whether disabled users deserve full access—of course they do—but whether "meaningful fines" are the mechanism that gets them there. They are not. They are a blunt instrument that punishes the wrong actors, entrenches the wrong incentives, and often makes the digital world less accessible, not more.
[C1.2] Start with who actually gets fined. Large tech platforms can absorb any penalty and treat it as a rounding error, exactly as PRO concedes when they say token fines get "written off." So to actually bite, fines must be large. But the services most likely to get caught by "repeated failure" audits are not just Amazon and government portals—they're small clinics, local nonprofits, regional credit unions, and small e-commerce shops running third-party platforms they don't fully control.
[C1.3] WCAG compliance is technically demanding, standards keep evolving, and full conformance is genuinely hard even for well-resourced teams. A punitive fine regime doesn't sort good-faith strugglers from bad-faith foot-draggers—it sorts by legal budget. That is the opposite of justice for disabled users, because those same small organizations often serve local, underserved communities that have nowhere else to go if the service shuts down rather than pay.
[C1.4] Second, fines create a compliance-theater incentive, not an accessibility incentive. Once the goal becomes "avoid the penalty," organizations spend money on legal defensibility—audits, checkbox certifications, boilerplate accessibility statements—rather than on actually testing with disabled users and fixing real friction. We've seen this pattern with cookie-consent and privacy law: firms optimize for the letter of compliance, not the lived experience the law was meant to protect.
[C1.5] Third, fines are reactive and slow. They require detection, notice, repeated failure, enforcement action—years pass before money changes hands, and the disabled user in front of a broken form today gets nothing from that eventual settlement.
[C1.6] PRO's real target—continued, in-your-face negligence—is better solved by procurement leverage, mandatory remediation timelines, and public accessibility scorecards than by fines that mostly reward big players and punish the small.
