[P1.1] The opposition's case rests on a zero-sum fantasy: that every dollar spent resurrecting a mammoth is stolen from the last Sumatran rhino. That framing is not just pessimistic—it is factually wrong. De-extinction is not a rival to conservation; it is a branch of it. The reproductive technologies being developed for mammoths—stem cell techniques, artificial wombs, embryo transfer, cryopreservation of diverse genomes—are exactly the toolkit endangered species need.
[P1.2] A cloned black-footed ferret, genetic rescue for the kakapo, an embryo bank for the northern white rhino: these are de-extinction's byproducts serving the living.
[P1.3] Consider the mammoth on its own terms. The goal is not a zoo curiosity but a cold-adapted elephant that can rewild the Arctic tundra, compacting snow and preserving permafrost that releases carbon as it thaws. Whether or not one fully credits that, the mechanism is plausible and the stakes are planetary. De-extinction research is climate research.
[P1.4] Then there is the thylacine. Australia holds one of the worst mammal extinction records on Earth, driven largely by introduced predators and habitat loss. A marsupial predator's return would force investment in predator-free landscape management, fenced sanctuaries, and genetic biobanking—infrastructure that shelters living species today. Revival is a Trojan horse for conservation capacity.
[P1.5] Finally, the moral ledger. Humans drove these animals extinct. The thylacine was hunted to death under government bounties; the mammoth perished amid a changing world we now dominate. Refusing even to try is not prudence; it is surrender dressed as fiscal discipline.
[P1.6] And the money objection collapses on inspection: de-extinction is overwhelmingly private capital chasing ambitious science, while government funding is modest. Public support legitimizes and steers it. Denying that partnership protects no living animal. Funding it multiplies the tools, the habitats, and the public will to save them.

[C1.1] The motion does not hide the trade-off; it asks governments to fund de-extinction even when it diverts money and attention from protecting species alive today. That is the case. Conservation budgets are finite and political attention is scarcer still. A mammoth or thylacine program means fewer rangers, fewer secured habitats, and fewer disease-management teams for species that can still be saved.
[C1.2] Calling this a “zero-sum fantasy” ignores that the money was already scarce.
[C1.3] The technological-spillover case points the wrong way. If governments want genetic rescue for the black-footed ferret, biobanking for the northern white rhino, or assisted reproduction for the kakapo, they can fund those directly. Those tools do not require decades of artificial-womb research on an elephant engineered for a vanished world. De-extinction is the most expensive and least certain route to those tools, while extinctions are happening on annual-budget timelines, not speculative breakthroughs.
[C1.4] The climate-rewilding claim is also weak. Altering permafrost at meaningful scale would require enormous, self-sustaining mammoth populations over huge tundra, with unknown survival, welfare, and ecosystem effects. That is not a serious climate plan; protecting intact tundra and cutting emissions are. If public money is meant for climate, it should not be hidden inside a resurrection project.
[C1.5] The thylacine shows the attention problem. Australia already knows what living species need: invasive predator control, predator-free fencing, and biobanking. Numbats, bilbies, and potoroos need those investments now. A thylacine project can absorb attention rather than attract more.
[C1.6] A charismatic revival may generate headlines; it does not guarantee a dollar for the unglamorous species fading in the same landscapes.
[C1.7] Finally, the moral argument is backward. If humans owe a debt for past extinctions, the first payment is preventing new ones. Resurrecting a mammoth or thylacine does not compensate the vaquita, the Sumatran rhino, or any living relative. Government duty runs to the species we can still save, not to symbolic proxies for past wrongs.
