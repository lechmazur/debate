[P1.1] Every year a transmission line sits in review, the coal plant it would retire keeps burning. That is the fact this debate turns on. Permitting delay is not a neutral pause while we gather information; it is an active decision to keep the dirtiest infrastructure running longer. A review process that treats a wind farm as the threat and the status quo as harmless has its environmental priorities exactly backwards.
[P1.2] Look at where the bottleneck actually sits. In the United States, hundreds of gigawatts of solar, wind, and storage are stuck in interconnection queues, more capacity than the entire existing fossil fleet, waiting years for approvals. Major interstate lines like SunZia took well over a decade from proposal to construction. Environmental impact statements routinely run thousands of pages and several years, and after all that, a single lawsuit can reopen everything.
[P1.3] Meanwhile an existing gas plant renewing its operations faces nothing comparable. The asymmetry is the problem: our rules impose maximum friction on the cure and minimal friction on the disease.
[P1.4] Notice too who actually uses these litigation pathways. Not, mostly, vulnerable frontline communities, who rarely have the lawyers. Reforming that is not anti-environmental; it is refusing to let the word "environment" be captured by whoever can afford the longest lawsuit.
[P1.5] The motion asks for streamlining, not abolition: firm deadlines, standardized programmatic reviews, categorical exclusions for low-impact sites like existing corridors and brownfields, and a short statute of limitations on challenges. Some marginal local review is traded for decades of avoided emissions, cleaner air, and cheaper power.
[P1.6] The choice is not "review versus no review." It is careful delay that guarantees harm, or decisive action that mostly prevents it.
