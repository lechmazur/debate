[P1.1] Writing to the brain is not another app or wearable. A phone can show you the wrong information; a stimulation headset can make you a different thinker, feeler, and decider. Electrical or magnetic pulses, focused ultrasound, even crude currents change firing thresholds, plasticity, mood and seizure risk. A miscalibrated earbud annoys you.
[P1.2] A miscalibrated brain stimulator can trigger a seizure, entrench anxiety or mania, disrupt sleep and memory, or quietly reshape habits without you noticing until the damage is wired in.
[P1.3] The ordinary consumer market is structurally unfit to manage that power. Consumer tech wins by shipping fast, cutting costs, pushing engagement updates, and shifting risk onto users through clickwrap consent no one reads. That model fails catastrophically for brain intervention, where safe dosing depends on individual anatomy, psychiatric history, medications, age, and cumulative exposure — facts a checkout page cannot screen. Companies will be tempted to sell stronger 'focus boosts,' 'calm modes,' or sexual and gaming enhancements, then fix harms with the next firmware patch.
[P1.4] Worse, write-capable devices are hackable levers into thought itself. Consumer gadgets have notoriously weak security, abandoned software support, and data-hungry business models. A compromised stimulator is not a stolen password; it is forced mood alteration, impaired judgment behind the wheel, or covert conditioning for ads. And even without hacking, normalization invites coercion: employers expecting sharper workers, schools expecting calmer pupils, militaries and marketers exploiting the shortcut to the nervous system.
[P1.5] Restricting these tools to tightly regulated medical use does not end innovation; it routes it through prescriptions, clinician screening, adverse-event reporting, and proof of long-term safety. Treat brain-writing like implantable stimulators and psychoactive drugs, not like headphones. No one's right to a thrill or productivity hack outweighs everyone's right not to have their neural circuitry damaged, addicted, or remotely controlled by a mass-market product.
