[P1.1] Right now, when you buy a listed "authentic" designer jacket on any major resale site, you the buyer have to prove it's fake. You pay for third party authentication, ship the item back at your cost, fight through weeks of support tickets, and half the time you still lose. That is not a functioning market.
[P1.2] These platforms already take 15 to 25 percent commission on every single sale. They are not neutral pipelines. They choose which items get featured, set the listing rules, process every payment, and advertise heavily that shoppers can trust their site. It is absurd that they capture all the upside of every transaction, but walk away from the single biggest risk that makes people hesitant to use resale at all.
[P1.3] Worse, the current system actively rewards counterfeiters. A seller can list ten fakes, get caught once, and simply make a new account. Buyers bear 100% of the cost of catching fraud, so almost no one bothers. By rough industry estimates, counterfeits now make up roughly one fifth of all listed designer items on major resale platforms.
[P1.4] This is not asking for charity. This is aligning risk with reward. When the platform absorbs counterfeit risk, they will finally have incentive to authenticate properly instead of running cheap, superficial checks that miss most fakes. Buyer trust will rise.
[P1.5] Transaction volumes will grow. Everyone wins except the people selling fakes. The only parties defending the status quo are platforms that would rather offload costs onto users than build a reliable market.

[C1.1] Counterfeits are a serious problem, but the proposition treats authentication as certainty and platform insurance as a free solution. Neither is true. Experts can disagree, especially over vintage goods, replaced components, and items without reliable serial records. Requiring platforms to authenticate more listings and absorb more losses therefore creates a costly guarantee built on imperfect judgments—not a reliable market.
[C1.2] PRO says commissions prove platforms can carry the risk. They prove no such thing. A commission pays for payments, listings, support, and logistics; it is not an unlimited insurance fund. Once platforms must price in expensive inspections, refunds, fraudulent return claims, and buyers potentially switching genuine items for fakes, the likely responses are higher fees, tighter seller requirements, slower payouts, or withdrawal from risky categories.
[C1.3] Those costs fall on honest buyers and sellers, while casual resale becomes less attractive than the very platforms PRO criticizes.
[C1.4] Nor does shifting the loss automatically stop counterfeiters. PRO’s own example—a seller caught once who simply opens another account—identifies failures in seller verification and enforcement. Reimbursing that seller’s victim does not prevent the next account or the next fake. It may instead invite collusion between sellers and buyers when the platform, rather than either participant, pays.
[C1.5] Authentication incentives also cut both ways: a platform facing open-ended liability may reject legitimate goods or exclude small sellers merely to avoid uncertainty.
[C1.6] The sound principle is responsibility according to control and fault. Platforms should be liable when they expressly warrant an item, ignore warnings, or conduct negligent checks; deceptive sellers should remain primarily responsible. Beyond that, risk-based authentication, stronger seller identity controls, provenance requirements, and affordable dispute mechanisms target fraud without converting every listing into an insurance contract.
[C1.7] PRO offers an appealing slogan—make the intermediary pay—but never shows that platforms can detect counterfeits accurately enough, or police guarantee fraud cheaply enough, for the promise to work. The motion would socialize losses, raise barriers, and leave the actual counterfeiter’s business model intact.

If a platform stamps “verified authentic” on a listing, it should stand behind that warranty and answer for negligent checks. But the motion demands authentication of **more items** and absorption of **more counterfeit risk generally**. Responsibility for a specific promise is not a justification for making the platform insurer of inventory it may never have examined.
[C2.2] That distinction answers the commission argument. A fee charged for today’s service does not demonstrate capacity to fund tomorrow’s expanded guarantee. PRO says the costs already exist, so platforms should simply spread them. But spreading a bill is not eliminating it, and the new policy adds bills: more inspections, more disputed refunds, and more opportunities for item-switching or collusion.
[C2.3] Those costs must reappear through commissions, seller restrictions, rejected goods, or slower payouts. “Spread fairly” actually means honest low-risk buyers and sellers compulsorily subsidizing higher-risk transactions, whether they want that insurance or not.
[C2.4] Nor is fraudulent-return risk merely an “edge case” under this motion. Counterfeit disputes can concern the very identity and condition of the object: replaced components, switched items, or genuine goods returned as alleged fakes. Ordinary retail return systems do not dissolve those evidentiary problems. The more valuable and uncertain the goods, the more expensive open-ended guarantees become to administer.
[C2.5] Finally, [P2.7] undercuts itself. If throwaway accounts are the root problem, seller identity controls and enforcement attack that problem directly; compensating the latest victim does not prevent the next account. PRO’s claim that incentives “cannot be built any other way” ignores liability for express warranties, ignored warnings, and negligent checks—the precise failures PRO describes.
[C2.6] So PRO’s examples justify accountability where platforms are at fault, not blanket counterfeit-risk absorption. Their case identifies bad platform conduct, then prescribes a policy whose costs extend far beyond it. That overreach is why the motion fails.

Honest users finance both counterfeit losses and guarantee fraud, while repeat sellers still must be stopped through identity enforcement.

[C5.4] So the question is not whether platforms should face consequences. They should. It is whether indiscriminate insurance is necessary for accountability. It is not.
