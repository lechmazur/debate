[P1.1] Mitochondrial replacement therapy (MRT) is the only proven way to stop the transmission of devastating mitochondrial DNA disorders such as Leigh syndrome, MELAS, and Kearns‑Sayre syndrome. These diseases strike newborns with progressive organ failure, and the average affected child requires decades of intensive medical care—often costing the health system millions of dollars per patient. Incidence is roughly one in five thousand births, meaning that each year hundreds of families in a typical public‑health system face a life‑sentence of suffering that could be averted by a single, well‑studied procedure performed before conception.
[P1.2] The United Kingdom, the first nation to approve MRT under strict regulation, has already demonstrated that the technique can be performed safely. Preliminary data from dozens of births show normal development and no detectable increase in genetic abnormalities. The procedure replaces faulty mitochondria in the mother’s egg with healthy donor mitochondria, eliminating the disease‑causing mutation while preserving the child’s nuclear DNA—the same genetic make‑up that defines the parents.
[P1.3] From a public‑health perspective, funding MRT is not a luxury; it is a cost‑saving preventive measure. Preventing one severe mitochondrial case spares the system the lifetime expense of ventilators, cardiac surgery, neurologic care, and special‑education services. When we compare the upfront cost of a single IVF‑based MRT cycle—comparable to existing assisted‑reproduction funding—to the cumulative billions spent on chronic care for affected children, the economic case is decisive.
[P1.4] Therefore, the core of my case is simple: the state has a duty to fund interventions that reliably prevent catastrophic, lifelong disease, and MRT meets that standard with demonstrated safety, clear benefit, and a favorable cost‑benefit profile. It also aligns with the principle that health systems should prioritize prevention over treatment, ensuring that those who would otherwise bear an impossible burden receive the same chance as any other family. Any argument against funding must outweigh the moral imperative to stop families from watching their children die from a preventable genetic error.

[C1.2] First, MRT is largely a solution in search of a patient. The overwhelming majority of mitochondrial disease is caused by nuclear DNA mutations, which MRT cannot touch. Of the remaining mtDNA cases, most prospective mothers can already avoid transmission with preimplantation genetic diagnosis, egg donation, or adoption — all cheaper, all established, none requiring heritable germline modification. MRT's unique added value is a narrow sliver: women with very high heteroplasmy or homoplasmic mtDNA mutations who insist on a genetically related child.
[C1.3] My opponent's "hundreds of families per year" figure quietly collapses once you subtract the cases other interventions already handle.
[C1.4] Second, the safety record is thin, not "proven." A few dozen UK births followed for a few years is not a basis for a public funding commitment to a germline change inherited by every future daughter in the female line. [C1.5] Third, the economic case is sleight of hand. PRO compares one IVF cycle to "lifetime millions," but MRT often requires multiple cycles, donor recruitment, screening, and long‑term surveillance of offspring. Compared against PGD — the real alternative — the marginal cost per avoided case is enormous, and the marginal QALYs gained over existing options are small.
[C1.6] Finally, prioritising a boutique reproductive technology for a few dozen families, while public systems ration insulin, cancer drugs, and ICU beds, is not "prevention over treatment."
