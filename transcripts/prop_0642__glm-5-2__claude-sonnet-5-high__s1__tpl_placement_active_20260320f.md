[P1.1] Parking minimums don't respond to demand—they mandate it. When a city tells every new apartment, office, or shop to include a fixed number of off-street spaces regardless of context, it doesn't merely accommodate cars; it requires the infrastructure that makes driving the default and every other mode an afterthought.
[P1.2] The critical mechanism is that minimums are self-reinforcing. They force developers to bundle parking into every project, which depresses the visible cost of driving at the point of use, which generates more car trips, which then becomes the "evidence" that more parking is necessary. Breaking that loop is exactly what maximums do. A cap says: the market may provide parking when tenants demand it, but the city will no longer compel surplus spaces into every lot, subsidizing car storage at the expense of housing, commercial space, and walkability.
[P1.3] The objection that car-dependent regions can't handle this gets the causality backwards. Car-dependent regions are car-dependent in significant part because decades of minimum parking rules have made alternatives structurally impossible. Surface lots consume land that could hold housing or retail. Required parking raises construction costs, which raises rents, which pushes development outward, which increases driving distances, which deepens dependence.
[P1.4] Minimums aren't the solution to car dependency—they're its engine.
[P1.5] A maximum doesn't ban parking. It lets the market, not the zoning code, decide how much is enough. If a developer in a suburban region believes tenants will pay for spaces, they can build them—up to a ceiling that protects street-level vitality and leaves room for other land uses. That is a stricter and more honest check than today's regime, where minimums force parking even into projects where the developer would rather not provide it.
[P1.6] Cities should cap what they don't want overbuilt and let genuine demand do the rest. The proposition stands.
