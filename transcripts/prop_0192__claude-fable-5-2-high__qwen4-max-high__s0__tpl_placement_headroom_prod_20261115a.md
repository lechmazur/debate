[P1.1] A chimpanzee held alone in a concrete cell is, in law, the same kind of thing as the padlock on the door: property. That classification cannot survive what we know.
[P1.2] Start with what personhood is. It is not a synonym for "human." It is a legal instrument that says: this entity has interests the courts must hear. We already extend it to corporations, ships, and in some jurisdictions rivers.
[P1.3] We extend it to infants and adults with profound cognitive impairments, who bear no duties and cannot argue their own case. Guardians speak for them. So the objection that apes cannot shoulder responsibilities was answered long ago by how we treat our own vulnerable.
[P1.4] Why these three groups? Great apes, cetaceans, and elephants are where the evidence is thickest: mirror self-recognition, long autobiographical memory, learned cultures passed between generations, lifelong family bonds, and behavior around their dead resembling mourning. These are precisely the capacities that make confinement a harm in itself. An elephant built to walk many miles a day with her family sways in place in a paddock.
[P1.5] An orca that would range across an ocean circles a tank. That is not bad husbandry; it is what captivity does to such minds.
[P1.6] Third, why rights rather than better welfare rules. Welfare law regulates how property is used; it never asks whether it may be used. When an owner's profit meets a thing's suffering, the thing loses, and it has no standing to object. Only a rights-holder can enter a courtroom through a guardian and demand release.
[P1.7] The claim is modest: no votes, no contracts, just freedom from being caged and used, with sanctuaries for those who cannot return to the wild. The burden now falls on anyone who says a self-aware, grieving, family-bound being should remain an object.
