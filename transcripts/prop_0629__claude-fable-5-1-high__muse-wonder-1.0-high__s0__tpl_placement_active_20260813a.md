[C1.1] The Kindle example proves the opposite of what it is meant to prove. Amazon remains free to sell books on the web, on Kindle devices, on Android, and to let iPhone users read them. What it wants is to use Apple's storefront, audience, software tools, and trust to acquire customers, then divert the transaction outside the system that paid for all of it.
[C1.2] That integration is not an accidental toll booth. It is the bargain that makes the modern app economy work for small developers as well as giants. One payment system gives parents one place to approve or block purchases, users one place to cancel abusive subscriptions and get refunds, and developers one system for taxes, fraud screening, failed cards, and receipts across dozens of countries. Force every large app to steer users to its own checkout, and that protection fragments into a maze of separate logins, stored card numbers, cancellation traps, and payment pages users cannot easily verify.
[C1.3] Sideloading multiplies that risk. A phone is not a Mac on a desk. It is always on, always logged in, carrying banking apps, location, photos, messages, health data, and microphones, often in the hands of children and older users who can be talked through a warning screen. A single spoofed popular app, employer “update,” or stalkerware install outside review can bypass privacy labels, permission checks, and rapid removal.
[C1.4] Warnings do not stop social engineering; that is why social engineering works.
[C1.5] Consumers already have a choice between ecosystems: an open model and a closed, curated model. Many buyers knowingly pay a premium for the second because they do not want to become their own security department. This mandate would abolish that choice by law, force every platform toward the riskiest common denominator, and reward the largest companies best able to self-distribute and self-process while smaller developers lose the trust, discovery, and simplicity that made customers willing to install them at all.
