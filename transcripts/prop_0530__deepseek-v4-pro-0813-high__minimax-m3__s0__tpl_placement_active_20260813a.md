[P1.1] These places are not neutral repositories of data; they are open wounds. A mass grave is both a crime scene and a cemetery. A forced labor site is where a particular community’s people were consumed by violence, exhaustion, and contempt. When outside authorities decide unilaterally how to excavate, which remains to keep, what to display, and whose interpretation explains the site, they repeat the original structure of the atrocity: power over the bodies and stories of the dead belongs to someone else.
[P1.2] Descendant control is therefore not a courtesy. It is the minimum condition for not re-enacting the harm.
[P1.3] Substantial control should mean real participation in excavation decisions, meaningful consent over the treatment and reburial of remains, veto power over public display, and co-authority over interpretive framing. It does not mean banning experts or abandoning forensic standards; it means changing who ultimately decides.
[P1.4] The moral case is decisive. Descendants have the strongest interest in the dignity of the dead. They carry the intergenerational memory, the burial customs, the kinship obligations, and the trauma that outsiders can study but never inherit. When their authority is absent, remains are treated as specimens, soil is disturbed against ritual requirements, and atrocity becomes a museum exhibit curated by people whose families were not the ones destroyed.
[P1.5] There is also a practical case. Descendant knowledge improves the work itself. Oral histories, community memory, and local knowledge often locate graves, identify individuals, explain objects, and prevent misinterpretation. Excavation without that knowledge is not more scientific; it is more likely to be blind.
[P1.6] Finally, public explanation must be accountable. Without substantial descendant control, sites drift into voyeurism, nationalistic myth-making, or comfortable narratives that protect perpetrators. The right to explain one’s own dead is not a special privilege; it is the necessary counterpart to having survived. Justice begins when the people whose ancestors lie in the ground hold real authority over what happens to them next.

[C1.3] Second, descendant control can reproduce the original silencing rather than repair it. Within any affected community there are dissenters, survivors who carried different stories, families who want the truth told rather than ritually softened. [C1.4] They are not.
[C1.5] Third, forensic and historical standards exist for reasons the proposition quietly overrides. Mass graves contain evidence of who killed whom, by what method, on whose orders. [C1.6] The same structure that once silenced victims is now licensed to silence them again.
[C1.7] Finally, public memory is a public good. These sites teach societies what was done in their name. Substantial descendant control over explanation does not replace curated narrative with truth; it replaces one set of curators with another. The defensible standard is shared authority among descendants, experts, courts, and the broader public, with no single party holding a veto.
