[P1.1] States are justified because the question is not whether federal regulators are slow in the abstract; it is whether children should keep consuming dyes while evidence accumulates. Synthetic food dyes are not nutrients. They exist to make products look brighter, and many are consumed heavily by children. When the suspected harms involve behavior, attention, or sensitivity, waiting for a final federal ruling can mean years of exposure during critical developmental windows.
[P1.2] If a state has a reasonable basis to suspect harm, it may act to reduce a nonessential risk.
[P1.3] The evidence dispute is real, but it cuts in favor of precaution, not paralysis. Several studies and reviews have raised concerns that certain synthetic dyes may contribute to hyperactivity or other adverse effects in susceptible children, while mechanistic work suggests immune or neurobehavioral pathways. That does not prove every dye is unsafe, but it creates enough uncertainty to justify shifting the burden away from parents and pediatricians and toward manufacturers who can reformulate. States do not need perfect certainty when the product is cosmetic and the potential injury is developmental.
[P1.4] Federal uniformity is valuable, but it is not a reason to forbid state action when federal process lags. States routinely act as first responders on consumer safety, and their decisions can pressure slower institutions to revisit standards. A state ban can also be narrower than it sounds: targeted to particular dyes, adjustable as evidence evolves, and compatible with interstate commerce if producers choose one safer formula. Claims about patchwork compliance matter, but they are costs of doing business, not reasons to override a state’s health judgment.
[P1.5] The real alternative is not elegant national policy; it is continued exposure while committees deliberate. If the dyes are unnecessary and the harm is plausible, the state with the power to protect residents has the justification to act now.
