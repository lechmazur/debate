[P1.1] The permanence of digital records has broken the social contract of juvenile justice. For generations, the law recognized that children are categorically different—their prefrontal cortexes are unfinished, their impulse control is underdeveloped, and their mistakes are developmental experiences, not permanent character defects. A teenager who shoplifts at sixteen or vandalizes property at seventeen was supposed to grow out of it, not be haunted by it at twenty-six when applying for a nursing license, a teaching credential, or a mortgage.
[P1.2] Today, that promise is a lie. Juvenile records that once sat in a filing cabinet, accessible only to courts, now live forever in searchable databases. Employers algorithmically screen them out. Landlords reject applicants.
[P1.3] Universities demand disclosure. And unlike a paper record that physically decays, a digital juvenile record is immutable, replicated across background-check aggregators, and resurfaces with every search. We have manufactured a permanent underclass of adults punished for conduct that occurred before they could vote or sign a contract.
[P1.4] This is why presumptive sealing is essential. "Presumptive" flips the default: records are sealed automatically upon adulthood unless the state demonstrates a specific, compelling interest in keeping them open. Sealing is not destruction—law enforcement retains access for legitimate investigations. It is not blindness to dangerous histories—courts can unseal for specific, documented threats.
[P1.5] But it shifts the burden to those who want to weaponize a childhood mistake against a grown adult attempting to reintegrate.
[P1.6] The alternative—today's default of permanent digital exposure—serves no legitimate penological goal. It does not enhance public safety; it merely warehouses human potential. It contradicts neuroscience, undermines rehabilitation, and condemns millions to collateral consequences that last longer than any sentence. Courts should presumptively seal these records.
[P1.7] The question is not whether society forgives its children, but whether we allow them to become adults.

But a court seal does not unpublish a data broker. Once an aggregator scrapes a juvenile docket at seventeen, an eighteenth-birthday seal does not scrub the cloud. The order binds the clerk; it does not bind Equifax or a background-check site.
[C1.3] If the harm is commercial republication, the remedy is regulation of background-check use and data-broker resale—not a presumptive seal that mostly hides the government file while the digital footprint marches on. The proposal would therefore fail its own test: the permanent underclass the opening fears would still be screened out by the same algorithms.
[C1.4] Worse, the presumption gets the burden backwards. Under the proposal, [P1.4] records are sealed automatically unless the state demonstrates a compelling interest. So a seventeen-year-old adjudicated for armed robbery, sexual assault, or arson flips to sealed at eighteen by default. [C1.5] The proposal answers that courts can unseal for specific threats, but that is slower than the background check itself and depends on the state noticing in time. Safety becomes an afterthought; erasure is automatic.
[C1.6] Rejecting presumptive sealing does not mean endorsing permanent stigmatization. Keep the record but graduate it: automatic expungement for minor nonviolent offenses after a clean period, retention for serious violent or sexual adjudications, and hard limits on commercial resale. That actually restores the second chance the proposal wants without forcing the public to prove it deserves to remember. Presumptive sealing is not rehabilitation; it is amnesia by paperwork.

[P2.1] The opponent mistakes the mechanism for the disease. Yes, a court seal does not magically scrub the cloud—but it creates the legal predicate that makes scrubbing possible. While a juvenile record remains public, data brokers repudiate it as fact; once sealed, republication becomes tortious and aggregators can be compelled to delete under consumer protection and fair reporting laws. The proposal does not merely hide the government file; it delegitimizes the entire commercial afterlife.
[P2.2] Without presumptive sealing, regulation of data brokers has no foundation—you cannot force removal of information the courts still certify as public. The opponent’s critique actually proves our point: if commercial republication is the harm, we must change the legal status of the source record.
[P2.3] The opponent’s safety argument also conflates sealing with destruction. Sealed records remain accessible to law enforcement, courts, and licensing bodies conducting genuine threat assessments. A school hiring a coach can still verify a sealed record through proper channels; what it cannot do is rely on a stale algorithmic screen that denies the applicant any chance to explain, contextualize, or demonstrate rehabilitation. The proposal shifts the burden to the state to justify continued public exposure—not to prove innocence, but to demonstrate why a childhood adjudication should override an adult’s demonstrated growth.
[P2.4] Most tellingly, the opponent’s “graduated” alternative concedes our premise: juvenile records should not haunt adults forever. They simply draw the line at violent offenses. But developmental neuroscience does not exempt seventeen-year-olds who commit serious crimes from the capacity for change. If we believe in rehabilitation for shoplifters, we must believe it for those who made graver mistakes—because the stigma of a permanent digital record is precisely what prevents the maturation that public safety requires.
[P2.5] The opponent offers amnesia for the minor and permanent branding for the serious. We offer a presumption of adulthood for all.
