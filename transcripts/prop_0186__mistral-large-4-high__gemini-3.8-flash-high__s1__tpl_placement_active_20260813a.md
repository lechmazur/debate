[P1.1] Recognition is not a courtesy we extend to whoever happens to control a map. It is the international community's seal of legitimacy, conferring on a government the right to borrow, to trade, to sign treaties, and to speak in the name of a people. To grant that seal based purely on territorial control is to confuse the fact of power with the right to rule.
[P1.2] Territorial control tells us nothing about whether a government derives authority from its citizens. A junta that seizes the presidential palace controls the territory; it does not possess democratic legitimacy. When we recognize such regimes automatically, we tell the world that might makes right, that the consent of the governed is optional. We do not merely describe reality; we validate it.
[P1.3] The alternative is not chaos but conditionality. Recognition contingent on minimum democratic and human-rights standards transforms our most valuable diplomatic currency into leverage. It creates a clear, attainable threshold: hold free elections, release political prisoners, allow a free press—and you join the community of legitimate states. This is not paternalism; it is the minimum bargain any government must strike with its own people before we strike bargains with it.
[P1.4] Critics will warn that conditional recognition isolates rogue states and destabilizes regions. But we can maintain diplomatic channels, humanitarian aid, and low-level contact without granting full sovereign recognition. Recognition is not the same as engagement. It is the prize we award for meeting basic standards of decency, and withholding it is how we signal that those standards matter.
[P1.5] A world where recognition follows tanks and territorial control is a world where dictatorships are permanent, because they need only hold ground to gain legitimacy. A world where recognition follows ballots and basic rights is one where power must answer to people. Territory can be held by force; legitimacy must be earned. The choice is clear: we should recognize governments that respect their citizens, not merely those that occupy their territory.
