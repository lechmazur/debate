[P1.1] Car insurance is a legal requirement to drive, and driving is an economic necessity for most people. When insurers use continuous in-car surveillance—tracking every location, speed change, and braking event—they are demanding unprecedented access to our private lives. We argue that such surveillance must be restricted to genuinely fair opt-in models to protect consumers from coercive data extraction.
[P1.2] First, without genuinely fair opt-in requirements, "consent" becomes an illusion. If an insurer offers a standard rate to those who accept tracking but charges prohibitive premiums to those who refuse, that is not a choice—it is a privacy tax. It coerces individuals into sacrificing their right to privacy simply to afford their daily commute. Genuinely fair terms dictate that baseline, non-tracked insurance remains accessible and affordable, ensuring that sharing behavioral data is a voluntary exchange, not a financial mandate.
[P1.3] Second, continuous surveillance algorithms are fundamentally flawed because they erase context. Telematics systems record *what* happens, not *why*. A driver might brake hard to avoid a pedestrian or swerve to miss a collision; the algorithm simply registers a "harsh driving event" and penalizes them. Furthermore, these systems frequently penalize late-night driving.
[P1.4] While insurers claim this reflects higher accident risk, in practice, it operates as a backdoor penalty for third-shift workers, nurses, and service industry employees. This effectively launders socioeconomic discrimination through an opaque algorithm, punishing people for their working hours rather than actual recklessness.
[P1.5] Finally, the scope of this surveillance is inherently invasive. Telematics map out exactly where drivers go—health clinics, political protests, places of worship—and when they go there. Allowing insurers unfettered rights to harvest, price against, and potentially monetize this deeply personal data leaves consumers vulnerable.
[P1.6] Insurance should be based on driving history and verifiable risk, not panoptic surveillance. We must require genuinely fair opt-in standards to prevent a system where privacy becomes a luxury only the wealthy can afford, and where working-class drivers are nickel-and-dimed by algorithms that do not understand the real world.
