[P1.1] Patents are a social contract, not an absolute right. Society grants a temporary monopoly to incentivize innovation, but the ultimate goal is public benefit. When life-saving medicines are kept out of reach by exorbitant prices, this contract is broken. Countries must therefore treat access to essential medicines as grounds for compulsory licensing whenever prices exceed a defined affordability threshold.
[P1.2] First, the market for essential medicines cannot self-regulate. Because demand for life-saving drugs is perfectly inelastic, patent holders can dictate prices entirely disconnected from research or production costs. We saw this during the HIV/AIDS epidemic, where millions died because antiretrovirals were priced out of reach until the threat of compulsory licensing forced prices down. Today, the same crisis plays out with modern cancer treatments.
[P1.3] A government’s primary duty is to protect its citizens' lives, not to guarantee infinite profit margins for corporations.
[P1.4] Second, a defined affordability threshold creates vital market predictability. Currently, compulsory licensing is often treated as a politically fraught, arbitrary nuclear option. Establishing a clear, objective threshold removes this ambiguity. It gives pharmaceutical companies a transparent choice: price drugs reasonably below the threshold to maintain your exclusive monopoly, or face generic competition.
[P1.5] This structure actively incentivizes voluntary price reductions, correcting the market failure before a license is even issued.
[P1.6] Finally, this policy preserves research and development incentives. Compulsory licensing is not theft; international frameworks require paying reasonable royalties to the original patent holder. Companies still profit from their inventions; they are simply prevented from price-gouging. Furthermore, the foundational research for many essential drugs is already heavily subsidized by public tax dollars and university grants.
[P1.7] It is economically and morally indefensible to force the public to pay extortionate monopoly prices for medicines they helped fund in the first place.
[P1.8] A defined affordability trigger ensures that intellectual property serves human life, rather than holding it hostage.
