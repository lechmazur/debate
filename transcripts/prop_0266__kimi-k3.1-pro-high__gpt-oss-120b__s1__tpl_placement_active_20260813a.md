This proposition requires a narrow disclosure at the moment that disclosure matters: before payment, show the nearest lower-fee independent pickup or direct-order option. It does not fix prices, cap commissions, forbid delivery, or require apps to recommend a competitor.
[P1.2] The case rests on total-price transparency. Delivery apps increasingly separate the headline food price from service fees, delivery charges, and other checkout costs. A customer comparing a restaurant’s direct pickup price with the app’s final total may be comparing different services, but that is precisely why the option must be labeled clearly, not hidden. The customer can decide whether doorstep delivery is worth the premium; the app should not make that decision by engineering ignorance.
[P1.3] Imagine a meal whose direct pickup total is substantially lower than the app’s checkout total once fees are included. The restaurant is nearby and accepts direct orders. Showing “Direct pickup available; lower fees; delivery not included” costs the consumer nothing and may save a meaningful amount. If the app’s convenience genuinely justifies its premium, many customers will still pay it.
[P1.4] If it survives only because customers never see the alternative, that is not a virtue worth protecting.
[P1.5] The wording also answers the practical objections. “Materially higher” prevents prompts over trivial differences. “Nearest” keeps the result relevant rather than creating an endless directory. “Independent” prevents the platform from satisfying the rule through an affiliated channel it controls. [P1.6] Opponents may call this forced advertising. It is better understood as checkout context from an intermediary that already solicits the order and controls the comparison screen. Platforms remain free to explain their insurance, courier network, refunds, and convenience—and to win by lowering fees.
