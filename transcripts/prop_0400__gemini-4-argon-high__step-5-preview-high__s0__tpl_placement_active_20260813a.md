[P1.1] When the state takes away your liberty, deports you, or cuts off the disability checks that keep you housed, "the computer said no" is never an acceptable answer.
[P1.2] A legal right to a human decision-maker in high-stakes government actions does not ban technology from public administration. Agencies can still use software to sort data or fast-track routine approvals. A right simply ensures that when a state decision significantly alters your life, you can demand that an empowered human being—not an uncomprehending script—weighs your case and makes the call.
[P1.3] First, high-stakes justice requires individualized context that machines cannot comprehend. Algorithms process statistical correlations; they do not understand human reality. Automated state systems have repeatedly stripped innocent families of unemployment and disability benefits because a clerical quirk or atypical work history triggered a fraud flag. Code cannot grasp that a missing document burned in a fire, or that an asylum seeker’s halting testimony reflects trauma rather than deceit.
[P1.4] Due process requires the chance to explain yourself to a mind capable of understanding the explanation. You cannot reason with a formula or cross-examine a black box.
[P1.5] Second, state coercion requires accountability. When the government cages a person or severs their financial lifeline, someone must own that choice. A machine cannot feel the gravity of detention, cannot exercise mercy when rigid rules produce absurd cruelty, and cannot be held liable for negligence. Without a right to a human decision-maker, the state launders its worst errors through code—letting bureaucrats blame the software while vendors hide behind trade secrets.
[P1.6] Efficiency is fine for sorting mail. When the stakes are incarceration, deportation, or destitution, administrative speed must yield to due process. Every person deserves the right to human judgment when their future is on the line.

[C1.1] A right to a “human decision-maker” sounds humane, but it is the wrong rule for the reasons PRO gives. PRO’s real objections are to unaccountable automated systems, rubber-stamping, secrecy, and poor outcomes. Those are not cured by requiring human biology. A low-level clerk who clicks “approve” within ninety seconds is still “the computer said no” with a heartbeat.
[C1.2] The proposition asks for a person, not a meaningful person—no standard of competence, independence, time, information, or authority. That makes it easy for agencies to comply while changing nothing.
[C1.3] Human decision-makers are not reliably more accurate or compassionate. They are biased, corruptible, exhausted, inconsistent, and often dependent on the same algorithmic triage they pretend to override. The abuse PRO describes—mass benefit denials from bad fraud flags—could continue unchanged if a human merely certifies the machine’s output. Worse, a broad right could clog already-delayed immigration, benefits, and criminal processes, creating new harms: families lose housing while waiting for a ceremonial review, and governments reduce services to avoid litigation.
[C1.4] The alternative is better targeted. People should have enforceable rights to reasons, records, discovery of the logic and data used, meaningful review by an independent tribunal, correction of bad data, and relief when automated systems cause unlawful outcomes. These rights address the actual failure: government power without explanation or recourse. They can also require genuine human review where it matters—complex disability determinations, asylum credibility, criminal risk—without pretending that every significant life outcome needs a new biological gatekeeper.
[C1.5] PRO says due process requires someone who can understand an explanation. But understanding is a function of authority, information, and independence, not species. The law should demand accountable decisions with real review, not a symbolic human face on automated governance.
