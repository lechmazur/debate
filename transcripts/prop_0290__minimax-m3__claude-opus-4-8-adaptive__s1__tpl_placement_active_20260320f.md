[P1.1] The motion before us is not a ban on biometric payments. Read it carefully: it merely requires that, whenever a shop, transit gate, or stadium rolls out a face- or fingerprint-based system, a convenient non-biometric path remains open. That is a modest, pro-choice floor for inclusion, and defending it requires nothing exotic — only recognition that some categories of people cannot, or will not, hand over their face to buy a coffee.
[P1.2] Start with exclusion. A meaningful share of the population has fingerprints that simply do not read well: older customers, manual workers, people with eczema, arthritis, or scarring. Women in some religious traditions cover their faces in public. A child's face changes year by year; a template that works at twelve can quietly fail at fifteen.
[P1.3] If a turnstile refuses entry because the scan does not match, the system has not modernised payment — it has built a second-class customer.
[P1.4] Then the nature of the data. A leaked card number can be re-issued in a week. A leaked face cannot. Once a biometric template is compromised, the harm is permanent, and it follows the person into every other system that ever shares the same template.
[P1.5] Yet retail payment infrastructure has a breach record that, to put it gently, is uneven. Tying identity to payment therefore magnifies a routine retail risk into an irreversible one.
[P1.6] Notice the word "convenient" in the motion — this is where the pressure really falls. If the biometric lane takes twelve seconds and the card lane takes two minutes, the alternative is technically present and practically coercive. A grandmother paying by cash is not exercising choice; she is paying a time tax for refusing to be scanned. The proposition forbids that, and rightly so.
[P1.7] CON may argue that friction-free payments trump these concerns. But the choice is not between biometrics and the old cash register. It is between biometric-only and biometric-plus. The cost of keeping an NFC terminal, a card reader, or a staffed till is small.

[C1.1] The motion sounds modest, but it smuggles in a heavy default: biometrics are "restricted unless" a vague, ever-present, *convenient* alternative is guaranteed. That word "restricted" does real work, and "always convenient" is the lever that turns a reasonable preference into a legal straitjacket. Three reasons it fails.
[C1.2] First, it aims at the wrong target. Every harm PRO listed — breaches [P1.4], the permanence of a leaked template [P1.5] — is a data-security problem, not a "do we have a card reader" problem. Mandating a second lane protects nobody who actually uses the biometric one. The grandmother who never scans her face is no safer when the retailer's database leaks.
[C1.3] If you genuinely fear irreversible breaches, regulate storage: on-device templates, encryption, deletion rights. The motion polices the menu while ignoring the kitchen.
[C1.4] Second, "always convenient" [P1.6] is unworkable and freezes good systems alongside bad ones. Who adjudicates "convenient" — two seconds versus twelve? That undefined floor becomes a litigation trap that deters deployment of precisely the secure, on-device biometrics that keep your template on your own phone and never touch a retailer's server. We would punish the safe architecture in order to forestall the unsafe one.
[C1.5] Third — and here is the irony — restriction harms the very people PRO claims to protect [P1.2]. For a blind customer, for someone with a tremor who fumbles a PIN, for a person whose card was just stolen, palm or face payment is the inclusive, convenient option. And the market already preserves alternatives, because turning away paying customers is commercial suicide; no shop survives by serving only flawless faces.
[C1.6] So weigh it honestly. The real choice is not biometric-only versus biometric-plus. It is whether we address breach and exclusion through smart, technology-neutral regulation, or through a clumsy mandate that leaves the actual dangers untouched while chilling the safest technology and the customers it empowers.
[C1.7] Keep the security rules. Drop the rigid leash.
