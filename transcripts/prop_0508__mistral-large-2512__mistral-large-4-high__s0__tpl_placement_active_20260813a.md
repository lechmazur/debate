[C1.1] The proposition mistakes regulatory convenience for economic progress. It asks trade blocs to divert scarce negotiating capital toward mutual recognition for digital services and professional credentials while leaving tariff walls standing that crush developing exporters and working-class consumers.
[C1.2] Consider who actually benefits. When a bloc recognizes a foreign architect's license or a tech firm's cloud certification, it serves a narrow professional class—consultants, engineers, financiers—who already operate across borders. When that same bloc cuts tariffs on textiles, agriculture, or manufactured goods, it lowers prices for the factory worker in Lagos, the farmer in Punjab, the shopkeeper in Mexico City. Tariffs still bind: many developing countries face applied rates well above 10% on agricultural exports, and even modest cuts translate into cheaper medicine, machinery, and food for millions.
[C1.3] Prioritizing credential recognition over tariff cuts is a transfer of diplomatic energy from the many to the few.
[C1.4] Worse, mutual recognition is not a neutral efficiency gain. [C1.5] The political economy is equally clear. Manufacturing still employs hundreds of millions in the Global South, and those workers compete directly against tariff-protected rivals. Meanwhile, "digital nomads" and credentialed elites already navigate borders with relative ease. [C1.6] Tariffs are not yesterday's metric; they are today's lifeline for those left behind by globalization. A bloc that spends its capital on mutual recognition while ignoring tariff cuts is not modernizing—it is abandoning the working class for the professional class.
