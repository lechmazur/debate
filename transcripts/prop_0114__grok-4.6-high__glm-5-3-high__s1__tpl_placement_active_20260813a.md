[P1.1] Family caregiving is work. It feeds people, keeps the sick alive, raises the next generation of taxpayers, and substitutes for nursing homes, hospitals, and childcare that would otherwise be billed to the public. Treating that labor as invisible is not neutrality. It is a decision about whose years count when pensions and social insurance are calculated.
[P1.2] Social-insurance systems already convert work into later-life security. A nurse's shifts generate credits. A daughter's years of dementia care do not, even though they reduce public expenditure and keep a parent out of institutional care. The second job is no less real.
[P1.3] It simply fails a bureaucratic test: it was unpaid, performed at home, and done by someone the system still treats as economically idle.
[P1.4] A state that would pay an agency to bathe an elderly patient, yet records the daughter's identical hours as nothing, is not measuring contribution. It is measuring invoices.
[P1.5] That idle label is expensive. Caregivers interrupt careers, lose promotions, and arrive at old age with thinner contribution records. The penalty is cumulative and gendered, because unpaid care still falls disproportionately on women. The result is not a private family story.
[P1.6] It is a predictable old-age poverty trap created by the rules of the insurance system itself.
[P1.7] Credits do not romanticize sacrifice or turn every household task into a claim. They recognize periods of substantial, necessary care—childrearing, disability, elder care—the way systems already recognize unemployment, sickness, or military service. Those credits stop stripping social rights from people who were working the whole time.
[P1.8] If governments refuse those credits, they are not defending fiscal prudence. They are harvesting unpaid labor and then denying the workers the ordinary insurance status that labor is supposed to confer. Visibility is the minimum of justice: count the years, or admit that the social contract only covers work that comes with a paycheck.

[C1.1] The proposition's emotional appeal is real, and my opponent's best rhetoric is in it. But the policy fails on its own terms. Pension credits are the wrong instrument for a real problem, and choosing the wrong instrument is how you protect the appearance of justice while entrenching the injustice.
[C1.2] Social insurance is not a ledger of moral worth. It is a mechanism that converts contributions into entitlements so that the system remains self-funding and intergenerationally fair. Credits break that conversion. Every credit is a promise of future payout with no corresponding contribution—so the cost lands on the same working-age taxpayers, disproportionately women in paid employment, who are already stretched.
[C1.3] You do not fix the exploitation of unpaid caregivers by quietly taxing other women's paychecks to pay for it.
[C1.4] Worse, credits pay the most to those who need them least and nothing to those who need help most. A woman who leaves a career to care for a parent gets a paper credit decades later. A woman caring today, with no income, no respite, and no retirement savings, gets nothing when she needs it. If the state is going to spend money on caregivers, immediate support—cash allowances, respite care, wage replacement—dominates a distant pension adjustment on every timeline that matters.
[C1.5] And the incentive effects are not hypothetical. Systems already struggle to verify care claims; formalizing pension payouts for household care invites both fraud and, more insidiously, a state logic that says staying home is now "compensated"—which families will hear, especially in cultures where daughters are expected to do it. Governments that pay people to remain unpaid caregivers make it cheaper to keep the current gendered division of labor than to build the professional care infrastructure that would actually free caregivers.
[C1.6] My opponent calls visibility the minimum of justice. But visibility at the wrong register is worse than invisibility: it prices intimate obligation, substitutes a voucher for structural reform, and calls it equity. We should reject that trade.
