[P1.1] The proposition draws a precise, evidence-aligned line between two fundamentally different uses of educational technology. For mastery practice—low-stakes, repeatable skill-building like grammar drills, math problem sets, or thesis statement rehearsal—AI tutoring is a transformative tool. It delivers personalized, instant feedback at scale that overworked secondary teachers cannot provide individually: a student struggling with quadratic equations can get 20 customized practice sets a week with targeted hints addressing their specific gap, instead of waiting for a teacher juggling 30 other students’ needs.
[P1.2] That is exactly the use case the proposition greenlights, and it is an unambiguous win for student learning. But substantive secondary school writing feedback is not mastery practice. It is developmental, context-dependent work that centers helping students cultivate a unique voice, take intellectual risks, and refine ideas rooted in their lived experience. AI does have a narrow role in writing instruction for discrete mastery tasks like grammar rule drills or sentence structure exercises, but it is wholly unsuitable as the main feedback source for full writing assignments.
[P1.3] AI feedback, by design, prioritizes statistical convention, generic correctness, and formulaic structure over the messy, nuanced choices that make writing meaningful. Consider a 9th grader writing a personal narrative about their grandmother’s immigration story: if they rely on AI as their main feedback source, the tool will likely flag culturally specific colloquial phrasing or non-standard narrative structure as “errors,” even when those choices are intentional, central to the story’s emotional weight. Worse, AI normalizes generic, one-size-fits-all writing that strips student work of the critical thinking and self-expression secondary school is designed to foster.
[P1.4] A human teacher, by contrast, can ask “why did you choose to open with that dialogue?” and help the student articulate the purpose of their creative choice—something no AI can replicate as a primary feedback source. This line is not a rejection of technology, but a recognition that secondary writing instruction is as much about human development as it is about skill. The proposition’s split is exactly the right one.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to draw a line that writing itself refuses to hold. Mastery and writing are not two separate domains—the act of writing is itself an act of mastering, and the feedback loop that builds strong writers is structurally identical to the one that builds strong algebra students.
[C1.2] Consider what a secondary writer actually needs to learn: how to frame a defensible thesis, embed evidence without smothering it, vary sentence rhythm, transition between ideas. These are skills, with right answers, near-right answers, and wrong answers—exactly the work AI tutoring excels at delivering. When a tenth grader submits a draft, the most useful response is granular, immediate, repeatable, and targeted to the specific weakness on the page. That is what AI provides, and what a single overworked English teacher with 150 essays cannot.
[C1.3] The proposition paints a romantic image of a teacher asking "why did you open with that dialogue?" That image is lovely. It is also rare. In most secondary classrooms, students receive a grade, a few margin comments, and a request to revise—returned two weeks later.
[C1.4] The aspirational human feedback the proposition defends is the exception, and it is the children of well-resourced schools who receive it. For everyone else, AI as the main feedback source is not a downgrade. It is an upgrade.
[C1.5] The argument that AI strips writing of voice confuses the tool with the outcome. A student writing in a culturally specific register does not lose that voice because a model flagged a comma splice; they gain a reader who can engage with the substance of their choices. AI feedback accelerates revision cycles from one per unit to one per night, and the writers who improve fastest are the ones who revise most. We do not tell piano students to wait three weeks for a masterclass before practicing scales.
[C1.6] We should not tell secondary writers to wait three weeks for a teacher either.
[C1.7] The proposition's split is not principled. It is nostalgia in a teacher's smock.

The equivalent of full writing feedback is a masterclass on musical interpretation—something no algorithm can provide, no matter how many times a student practices.
