[P1.1] A child with an ear infection, a diabetic who needs a blood-pressure check, a man with a suspicious cough. None of these are expensive to treat, and all of them become expensive fast when nobody treats them. That is the whole case in miniature. Primary care is the cheapest medicine we have and the most catastrophic to ration by wallet, because the thing being rationed is not comfort but early detection.
[P1.2] Three reasons a guaranteed floor belongs in public hands.
[P1.3] First, price signals fail worst at the front door. When someone is deciding whether a lump is worth a $150 visit, they are not making an informed consumer choice; they are guessing about information they don't have yet. Cost-sharing at the primary level does not deter frivolous care so much as it deters the visit that would have caught something at stage one instead of stage three. Health systems don't save money when the poor stay home; they pay for it later in emergency departments, amputations, and disability.
[P1.4] Second, primary care is where public goods live. Vaccination, infectious disease surveillance, maternal health, antibiotic stewardship, chronic disease management that keeps hospital beds free — these spill over onto everyone, including the people who could afford private care. A society with untreated tuberculosis is not a society where the wealthy are safe.
[P1.5] Third — and this is why the proposition is stronger than a full nationalization — leaving electives and supplements in private markets is a feature, not a compromise. Cosmetic surgery, private rooms, faster knee replacements, orthodontics: these are genuine consumer preferences where willingness to pay carries real information, and where the private sector absorbs demand, capital, and impatience that would otherwise clog the public queue. The guarantee is a floor, not a ceiling. Money continues to buy convenience; it stops buying survival.
[P1.6] CON must therefore defend something uncomfortable: that a person's access to a first diagnosis should depend on their ability to pay for it, or that no government anywhere can manage a basic-tier guarantee that dozens already do. [clipped]

[C1.1] The proposition treats a guarantee as if it were a service. It is not. A government can promise a first-contact visit, but it cannot guarantee the doctor, the appointment slot, the diagnostic follow-up, or the budget next fiscal year. In practice, universal primary-care guarantees usually become universal queues: the same scarce clinicians, more demand, thinner visits, and hidden rationing by waiting time rather than price.
[C1.2] That is not a neutral floor; it is a ceiling dressed as compassion. PRO says money should stop buying survival, but universal queues let connections, persistence, and the ability to wait decide who is seen.
[C1.3] PRO’s examples are real, but they prove too much. An ear infection, a blood-pressure check, and a suspicious cough need timely access—not necessarily an open-ended, zero-price entitlement to all primary care. Primary care is a mixed bundle: some visits are urgent and high-value, many are routine, and some are low-value reassurance. When the state removes price at the front door for everyone, it expands demand faster than training, clinics, and appointment time can respond.
[C1.4] The predictable result is not better triage but shorter consultations, deferred complex cases, and burnout. The people harmed first are those the proposition claims to protect: patients without connections or the ability to wait.
[C1.5] The private carve-out does not fix this; it worsens it. If elective and supplementary markets remain open, clinicians, nurses, and administrators will move toward better pay and working conditions. Public systems then face staffing shortages while still carrying universal obligations. That is how parallel private sectors siphon capacity from public floors.
[C1.6] Better alternatives exist: targeted coverage for low-income patients, sliding-scale fees with exemptions, public funding for vaccines and infectious-disease control, and scope-of-practice reforms that let nurses, pharmacists, and telehealth handle routine needs. These protect early detection without locking society into a universal guarantee that is fiscally brittle and clinically diluted.
