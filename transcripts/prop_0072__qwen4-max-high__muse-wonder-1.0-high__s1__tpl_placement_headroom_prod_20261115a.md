[P1.1] A public school is not a family photo album. It is the institution a society funds to produce people capable of judging their government — including when that government is the perpetrator. Every other civic lesson depends on that capacity. We teach students that power must be checked, that rights are not self-enforcing, that majorities can tyrannize.
[P1.2] Then we hand them a national story with the crimes edited out, and wonder why "it can't happen here" is such a durable belief. It can happen here. In most countries, it already did. Knowing that is not cynicism; it is the precondition of vigilance.
[P1.3] Silence is not neutrality. A curriculum that lingers on battlefield heroism and reduces Japanese American internment to a wartime footnote has already made a choice about what a citizen deserves to know. Canada went generations without teaching residential schools, and when that history finally entered public consciousness, it reorganized the country's understanding of its own institutions. That upheaval is the measure of what the omission had been doing.
[P1.4] And the fear that this destroys national loyalty gets the mechanism backwards. Germany teaches the Holocaust in relentless detail and has not dissolved into self-hatred; it produced citizens unusually alert to authoritarian language precisely because they were shown where that language ends. Loyalty built on ignorance is brittle — it shatters the first time a student encounters the omitted fact somewhere less careful. Loyalty built on knowledge survives contact with evidence.
[P1.5] Note what the motion actually requires: study, alongside achievements. Not replacement, not mandated guilt, not shame as pedagogy. So the opposition cannot win by describing a bad teacher assigning collective blame. They must defend the omission itself — must explain why a democracy is better served by graduates who cannot name their own state's worst crimes.
[P1.6] A country that can only be loved by people who don't know it isn't being loved. It's being protected.

[C1.1] The question is not whether ugly history exists, or whether capable teachers may teach it. It is whether government should require every public-school student to study their own country's major atrocities and abuses. That compulsion turns inquiry into state-prescribed self-indictment, and it fails on the civic terms its defenders invoke.
[C1.2] First, a mandate needs an author, and that author is the state judging itself. Ministers and curriculum boards must decide which episodes count as major, which victims count, how much time shame deserves, and what conclusion students should draw. When the governing party changes, the list changes. Students do not learn how to weigh evidence; they learn that history is whatever today's coalition needs it to be.
[C1.3] Second, compulsory atrocity study mistakes exposure for understanding. Children acquire civic judgment in sequence: basic chronology, geography, institutions, and the ability to read a source before they can fairly judge a nation across centuries. A required thread of national wrongdoing, repeated before that foundation exists, does not produce vigilance. It produces either numbness or inherited guilt — a diffuse belief that their country is uniquely tainted, without the knowledge needed to reform anything specific.
[C1.4] Time makes the cost concrete. School hours spent satisfying a mandated catalogue of national abuses are hours not spent on literacy, mathematics, scientific reasoning, or the shared achievements and sacrifices that give diverse students a common civic language. A graduate who can recite internment or residential schools but cannot explain a constitution, a budget, or a court has been moralized, not educated.
[C1.5] Teach difficult history honestly when teachers and local communities judge students ready. Do not let government conscript children into a national confession. A curriculum imposed to humble the state will ultimately serve the state that writes it.

[P2.5] And no hours are taken from mathematics. The motion adds a thread inside history already timetabled; the displacement is a few lessons of triumph, not literacy. Weigh the risks. CON's is a redistributed unit and a badly taught class.

[C2.1] The authorship objection does not prove too much; it proves exactly the asymmetry my opponent ignores. A state mandating literacy or chronology builds a common scaffold. A state mandating study of its own atrocities prescribes a moral verdict about itself, then grades children for reproducing it. That invites two failures at once: protective whitewash by governments shielding themselves, and ritual denunciation by governments performing repentance at children's expense.
[C2.2] Both teach deference to the official line, not independent judgment.
[C2.3] Local discretion is then caricatured as a veto by the guilty. But the alternative is worse: a central list that flattens different regions, ages, and communities into one compulsory reckoning. Parents and teachers close to a child can judge readiness, language, and local relevance in ways a ministry cannot. That some places taught residential schools badly is an argument for better materials, professional standards, and public pressure — not for converting every omission into proof that only compulsion is honest.
[C2.4] Internment can indeed teach constitutional failure — when a mature student, well taught, studies it voluntarily within a broader civic course. That conditional is the point. A mandate cannot guarantee maturity or quality; it guarantees exposure, repetition, and assessment. What gets tested gets simplified into slogans: our courts failed, our nation betrayed its values.
[C2.5] The stress test becomes a catechism long before students understand the institutions allegedly failing.
[C2.6] Finally, the nineteen-year-old discovering an omission does not refute us. Discovery is normal intellectual growth; adults constantly learn their schooling was incomplete. Betrayal follows only if school promised a complete national story. It does not follow if school honestly teaches foundational knowledge, admits complexity, and equips students to investigate further.
[C2.7] Require skills and institutions. Permit difficult history. Do not mandate national self-accusation and call the coercion vigilance.

[C5.1] My opponent’s image is a child trapped where every local institution prefers silence. That danger is real, but a government mandate does not free that child. It replaces many silences with one authoritative voice — the voice of the very state whose crimes are being examined.
[C5.2] That voice writes the criterion, appoints the board, approves the textbook, and sets the examination. When it leaves its own abuse outside the word “major,” the student does not meet a contestable claim. He meets an official settlement, taught as civics and rewarded when repeated. Challenging it means challenging the teacher, the marks, and the ministry.
[C5.3] Plural provision is messier, but its failures remain corrigible from outside: another textbook, another teacher, another archive, another community can expose what one omitted. Once omission is ratified by the state as the required reckoning, correction must fight the legitimacy of the state itself.
[C5.4] That is why compulsion defeats its stated purpose. Independent citizenship cannot be manufactured by requiring every child to rehearse the government’s approved account of governmental guilt. The encounter may be permitted, encouraged, and richly supported. It must not be conscripted.
[C5.5] A democracy earns vigilance by limiting state power over conscience, not extending it.
