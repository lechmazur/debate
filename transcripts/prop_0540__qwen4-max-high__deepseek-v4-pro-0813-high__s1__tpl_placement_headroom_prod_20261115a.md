[P1.1] Newborn screening already rests on a presumption: test before symptoms, because symptoms mean damage already done. Genomic sequencing is a sharper instrument for that same settled purpose, not a new moral enterprise. The only live question is whether the door stands open or stays shut until someone signs.
[P1.2] The motion's limit does the heavy lifting. Only conditions with effective early interventions qualify — not adult-onset risks, not carrier status, not untreatable findings. That removes the standard objection about a child's right not to know. What is left is knowledge a child needs, about a disease we can treat, inside a window that closes.
[P1.3] Spinal muscular atrophy makes this concrete. Affected babies look healthy at birth while motor neurons die quietly. Gene therapy works best before symptoms; once weakness appears, some loss is permanent. The difference between walking and never walking can turn on a few weeks, and on a consent form left unsigned on a postnatal ward.
[P1.4] That is where opt-in fails, and fails unequally. Uptake under active consent tracks language, literacy, trust in medicine, and how much staff time a hospital can spare for counselling. Exhausted parents, translation gaps, a rushed discharge — and the child who loses the window is disproportionately the one whose parents were least equipped to navigate paperwork. Opt-out spends public effort on informing everyone rather than extracting signatures from the articulate.
[P1.5] Opt-out is not coercion. Refusal must be real, easy, penalty-free, and clearly explained. Choice survives; it simply stops being a toll gate. Meanwhile the harms are asymmetric: an unwanted result is a manageable informational burden, narrowed by the actionability limit; a missed diagnosis is irreversible.
[P1.6] So the opposition must defend a signature as worth more than a preventable disability, and explain why the children who pay for it are the poorest.

[C1.1] The proposition is not a modest extension of newborn screening; it is a change in the meaning of consent. Current screening tests for a handful of well-understood biochemical markers. Genomic sequencing, even when filtered, produces variants of uncertain significance, incidental findings, and reanalysis obligations that no actionability limit can fully contain. Calling the result “only treatable conditions” assumes a clean line that does not exist in practice.
[C1.2] A variant may be pathogenic in one database and uncertain in another; a condition may have a promising but unproven therapy. Parents are then asked to refuse something they cannot see in advance.
[C1.3] Opt-out does not remove the consent problem; it buries it. A signature is not the only cost of opt-in. The real cost is the conversation that lets a parent understand what sequencing can and cannot tell them. Under opt-out, that conversation becomes a rushed refusal form on a postnatal ward.
[C1.4] The parent who is tired, in pain, or facing a language barrier is more likely to be included by default without ever grasping what was tested or what a positive result means. That is not equity; it is passive enrollment of the least resourced.
[C1.5] The SMA example is real but narrow. It does not justify a default genomic scan. We can screen for SMA with targeted testing, not whole-genome or broad panel sequencing. The motion’s “genomic newborn screening” invites far more data than a single treatable disease requires.
[C1.6] Once the data exist, they persist, are reanalyzed, and can be requested by insurers, researchers, or family members. The child never chose to generate that record.
[C1.7] The right question is not whether early treatment matters. It does. The question is whether the state should create a permanent genomic file on every newborn unless a parent manages to object. That is a much larger step than PRO admits, and the burden should stay on those who want the data, not on the child who will live with it.
