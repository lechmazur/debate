[P1.1] Public pensions were built for shorter lives. Leave the retirement age frozen while longevity rises, and retirement lengthens automatically relative to working life. In a pay-as-you-go system that is not a gift. It is a growing claim on whoever still pays in.
[P1.2] The bill does not vanish. It is shifted.
[P1.3] Raising the retirement age with life expectancy restores the original bargain: a long career, then a comparable span of rest. People keep the extra years of life. They simply take more of those years while still contributing. That is how a public defined-benefit system stays solvent without endlessly rewriting contribution rates.
[P1.4] Two workers with similar careers, born a generation apart, should not receive vastly different retirement durations merely because medicine improved. Indexing the age is the clean way to hold the deal constant.
[P1.5] Relying mainly on higher payroll taxes does the opposite. It taxes work in aging societies that need more workers, not fewer. Higher rates raise labor costs, push activity into informal jobs, and fall hardest on younger cohorts already carrying larger implicit debts. The tax base can shrink even as the rate climbs.
[P1.6] You can keep squeezing wages and still fail to match the extra years of benefits a frozen age creates. That is a worse shock absorber than aligning the start of payments with remaining lifespan.
[P1.7] A transparent rule—when remaining life expectancy at retirement rises, the age rises with it—also beats repeated tax fights. Physically demanding work, disability, and late-career job loss can still be handled with targeted exceptions. Those exceptions do not justify freezing the general age and loading the extra years onto payrolls. Longevity is a gain.
[P1.8] Public pensions should share it as extra capacity to work, not as an expanding unfunded claim on wages.

Raising the general retirement age in line with the average therefore takes the most from those with the least to give.
[C1.2] It is a benefit cut that lands hardest on short-lived, low-income workers—the exact people pensions exist to protect.
[C1.3] Payroll taxes, by contrast, can be made progressive. You can lift or remove contribution ceilings, so high earners pay on their full income. You can supplement with general revenue or consumption taxes. The proposition treats "higher payroll taxes" as one blunt rate hike, but that is a choice, not a necessity—a tax floor can be structured to spare young and low-wage workers, while an age hike cannot be structured to spare the construction worker whose knees are finished at 62.
[C1.4] Then there is the labor market. You cannot legislate jobs for 67-year-olds into existence. Age discrimination is pervasive; employers restructure away from older workers long before any statutory age. Where retirement ages have been pushed up, the practical result is often not more employment but more people parked on disability rolls, unemployment benefits, or early-exit schemes—just with worse targeting and more stigma.
[C1.5] That doesn't save money; it launders the cost through other budgets.
[C1.6] And note what automatic indexing does politically: it removes the decision from voters every year, quietly, forever. Pension reform deserves democratic contestation precisely because it redistributes between generations and classes. A ratchet that only moves one direction—"you work longer, always"—is not a neutral technical rule. It is a permanent transfer from retirees to employers and treasuries, dressed as arithmetic.
[C1.7] Longer lives are a shared gain. Sharing them means asking more from those who can most afford it, not more years from those who can least.
