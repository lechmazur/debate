[P1.1] Mass graves are not neutral ruins. They are unresolved murders. The dead cannot consent to being made into teaching tools, specimens, or tourist stops. The only consent that can stand in for them is the consent of those who carry their blood, name, and trauma.
[P1.2] For that reason alone, descendant communities must hold both the brake and the steering wheel.
[P1.3] The alternative is a pretense of objectivity that has repeatedly failed. Museums and state agencies have turned plantations into wedding venues and called enslaved people "servants." City archaeologists paved over an African burial ground in Manhattan to build offices until Black descendants forced recognition. Governments that built forced-labor camps are the last institution that should independently narrate what happened there.
[P1.4] Expertise without accountability produces sanitized exhibit text, not truth.
[P1.5] Substantial control corrects the record. Descendants remember what archives burned. When the Tulsa community demanded excavation of its massacre victims, they refused a purely forensic show. When Jewish survivor families shaped Holocaust memorials, they insisted bodies not be displayed as curiosity and names replace numbers.
[P1.6] That is not censorship; it is superior historical accuracy. It tells visitors that atrocity was done to people, not populations.
[P1.7] Control means real power: veto over whether remains are exhumed at all, whether bones are photographed, what words go on the wall, whether reburial takes precedence over storage in a box. That power does not exclude scientists; it hires them. But it reverses the hierarchy that has treated descendant pain as input and professional preference as decision.
[P1.8] No public education worthy of the name can begin by re-violating its subjects. Every archaeologist already knows to get permission before touching a recently dead white body. Descendant control simply extends that same basic human respect to those whose death was made political, and whose burial must therefore be made just.

[C1.1] A mass grave is not private property. Grant any single group substantial control—a veto over exhumation, over photography, over "what words go on the wall"—and you have privatized the truth about a public crime.
[C1.2] Start with the problem PRO cannot solve: who is the descendant community? Atrocity sites are almost never owned by one lineage. Auschwitz holds Jews, Roma, Poles, and Soviet prisoners, groups with genuinely conflicting memorial demands. American plantations have descendants of the enslaved and descendants of enslavers, both with blood claims.
[C1.3] Post-genocide societies contain rival factions each insisting they speak for the dead. "Substantial control" doesn't empower the murdered; it empowers whoever wins an ugly contest to be their spokesperson—often self-appointed leaders, political entrepreneurs, or governments claiming to embody the victim group. Communities are not immune to sanitizing history either: many have suppressed inconvenient truths about collaboration, internal violence, or resistance myths.
[C1.4] Second, veto power kills accountability. Exhumations at Srebrenica and in Guatemala produced the forensic evidence that convicted perpetrators and demolished denialism. If a traumatized community's leadership—or one infiltrated by those with something to hide—can block excavation and documentation, war criminals walk and deniers fill the silence. The dead have an interest PRO ignores: that their murder be proven.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's own examples refute the motion. Tulsa, the African Burial Ground, reformed Holocaust memorials—every one was fixed through consultation, legal standards, and professional reform, not community veto. Respect, participation, and ethical codes are the actual remedy; CON defends all of them. What CON rejects is the leap from "consult and honor" to "steer and brake."
[C1.6] The right framework is shared stewardship under public law: descendants heard everywhere, obeyed nowhere absolutely. Grief deserves a voice. It does not deserve a monopoly on truth.
