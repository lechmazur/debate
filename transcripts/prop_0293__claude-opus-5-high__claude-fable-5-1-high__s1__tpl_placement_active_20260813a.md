[P1.1] A statue on a plinth in a public square is not a history lesson. It is a civic endorsement. We put people on pedestals for the same reason we name streets and hang portraits in city halls: to say to every passerby, *this is who we admire, this is what we aspire to be*. That is a claim made in the name of all citizens, funded by all citizens, and unavoidable by all citizens who walk that street to work.
[P1.2] Which means the question is not "should we remember?" but "whom do we honor?" Those are different acts, and the proposition draws exactly that line. Nothing is destroyed. The bronze goes to a museum, an interpretive site, a sculpture park — places where a viewer gets what a pedestal can never give: dates, victims, argument, the full record.
[P1.3] Confederate generals, Leopold II, Stalin in the former Eastern bloc: in each case the object survives, but it stops speaking in the state's voice.
[P1.4] So the "erasing history" objection inverts itself. A statue with no plaque, no context, and a heroic pose is the weakest possible history teacher. It flatters. A museum wing on the Atlantic slave trade, with the same statue inside it, teaches vastly more about slavery than the statue did outdoors.
[P1.5] If our opponents genuinely care about public memory, they should want the object where memory can actually be transmitted.
[P1.6] The stakes are concrete. Imagine a Black schoolchild in a courthouse square, taught that the law treats her as an equal, standing beneath a man cast in bronze who fought for the right to own her great-grandmother. That is not a neutral piece of stone. It is a standing contradiction of the promise the courthouse behind it makes.
[P1.7] Civic honor is a finite, meaningful resource, and spending it on architects of enslavement, colonial plunder, and mass repression tells a portion of the citizenry that their subordination is still something the community celebrates.
[P1.8] Honor is a choice we renew daily. We should choose better, and keep the history.

[C1.2] Start with the criterion. "Primarily associated with slavery, colonial domination, or mass repression" is not a fact about a person; it is a verdict, and verdicts move. Washington and Jefferson owned hundreds of human beings. Churchill presided over an empire and a famine.
[C1.3] Roosevelt interned a hundred thousand citizens. Nearly every founder of every modern state fought or ruled by means we now condemn. Once the rule is that honor must be withdrawn from anyone whose dominant legacy can be argued as repression, there is no principled stopping point, only whoever holds the microphone this decade. A standard that cannot say why Lee goes and Washington stays is not a standard.
[C1.4] Second, the choice offered is false. PRO's best argument is that a statue with no plaque, no context, and a heroic pose teaches nothing. Correct. That is an argument for the plaque, not the crane.
[C1.5] A statue in the square with an honest inscription, a counter-monument beside it, the names of the enslaved carved at its base, stops speaking in the state's old voice and starts speaking in a truer one. [C1.6] Third, "relocated to a museum" is often a euphemism. Museums have finite walls and their own politics. Many removed statues have spent years in storage yards, seen by no one. The plinth itself is evidence of how a community once allocated its honor, and stripping it launders that record.
[C1.7] Honor is a conversation a city has with its own past. Editing the square erases the argument; annotating it wins it.
