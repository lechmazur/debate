[P1.1] Mass deportations treat a structural labor market reality as a temporary policing problem. Across the Gulf and North Africa, irregular migration is sustained by relentless domestic demand—for construction crews and domestic workers in Riyadh and Dubai, or agricultural, construction, and service laborers in Tunis, Cairo, and Rabat. Expelling workers en masse does not erase the jobs that drew them there. It merely inflicts sudden labor shortages on local industries, wastes state resources on a revolving door of raids, and hands a lucrative monopoly right back to human smugglers waiting to refill the vacuum at higher prices.
[P1.2] Crucially, in both regions, irregular status is frequently manufactured by broken legal architecture rather than defiant border-crossing. Under rigid sponsorship systems in the Gulf, a legally admitted worker who flees wage theft or an abusive employer instantly becomes "irregular." In North Africa, Sub-Saharan migrants who could fill vital local labor gaps are pushed into the shadow economy—or toward dangerous sea crossings—simply because no functional work permits exist for them. Prioritizing legal labor corridors—built on bilateral matching agreements, renewable seasonal visas, and the right to change employers—eliminates irregularity at its root.
[P1.3] When workers and employers have a fast, lawful way to connect, smuggling networks lose their customer base overnight.
[P1.4] Ultimately, legal pathways deliver the genuine border control and stability that mass expulsions only mime. High-profile roundups and desert dumpings drain security budgets, spark severe diplomatic crises with African and Asian origin states, and perversely incentivize remaining migrants to hide deeper in the underground economy.
[P1.5] By contrast, legal corridors bring workers into the light. They allow Gulf and North African governments to vet entrants before arrival, track visas, collect revenues, and enforce labor standards. A state does not project sovereignty by repeatedly deporting the very workforce its economy depends on; it achieves control by bringing that workforce inside the law.
