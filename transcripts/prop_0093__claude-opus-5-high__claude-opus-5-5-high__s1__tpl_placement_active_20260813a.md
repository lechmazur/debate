[P1.1] A curb lane is about eight feet of public land, and the only question worth arguing is what we get back for it. Right now, in most cities, the default answer is: one private car, stationary, for hours, usually free or nearly free. That is the least productive thing you can do with valuable land in a place where land is the binding constraint.
[P1.2] Compare the alternatives on the same eight feet. A bus lane moves a bus every few minutes carrying forty or sixty people — a throughput that a row of parked sedans cannot approach on its best day. A loading zone serves dozens of deliveries in a morning, each one a transaction for a local business. A protected bike lane carries a continuous stream of people at low cost and low space per rider.
[P1.3] Parking serves one household's vehicle, once, and then sits there.
[P1.4] The second point is that parking's costs are hidden but real. When there is no loading zone, the delivery van does not vanish; it double-parks, and a whole travel lane seizes up behind it. When there is no bus lane, the bus queues behind that van, and every rider's trip gets slower, which pushes more of them into cars, which makes the queue longer. Curbside parking doesn't just fail to produce much value — it actively manufactures the congestion that everyone then blames on transit and cycling.
[P1.5] Notice what the motion actually asks. Not abolition. "More" curb space for buses, deliveries, and bikes, rather than a policy of protecting *most* on-street parking as an entitlement. Loading zones can be metered; disabled bays, pickup spots, and residential permits can survive.
[P1.6] What cannot survive scrutiny is the rule that the majority of the curb is reserved, by default, for free vehicle storage.
[P1.7] So the test is simple: for each eight feet, which use moves the most people and goods, and who pays when we get it wrong? On that test, parking loses.
