[P1.1] Picture a bus. We don't put a coin box at the entrance of the sidewalk beside it, or charge per use at the fire hydrant on the corner, or meter the streetlight overhead. We treat those as shared infrastructure, funded broadly because everyone benefits from a city where people can move, stay safe, and see at night. Public transit belongs in exactly that category, and the motion simply asks us to fund it the way we already fund the rest of the public realm.
[P1.2] Here is the path I'll defend. First, fares are a strange and self-defeating tax. They fall hardest on the poorest riders, who spend a real share of income just to reach work, clinics, and school. Broad-based taxation spreads that cost across everyone who gains from a functioning city, including businesses whose workers and customers arrive by transit.
[P1.3] That is more progressive and more honest than nickel-and-diming people at the turnstile.
[P1.4] Second, collecting fares is expensive and corrosive. Fare boxes, gates, ticket machines, inspectors, and enforcement consume money and staff, and they generate exactly the kind of policing confrontations over a few dollars that erode trust, especially in poorer neighborhoods. Remove the fare and you remove that whole apparatus, speed up boarding, and let drivers drive.
[P1.5] Third, and this is why the "modest ridership" clause is a feature, not a bug: the case for fare-free transit does not depend on packing the trains. The benefits are dignity, reliability, faster service, lower administrative cost, and guaranteed mobility for those with no alternative. Even flat ridership leaves us with a fairer, cheaper-to-run, more inclusive system.
[P1.6] CON will warn about cost and crowding. But every service worth having costs money; the question is who pays and how fairly. A stable tax base is more dependable than volatile fare revenue that collapses in every recession precisely when people need transit most.
