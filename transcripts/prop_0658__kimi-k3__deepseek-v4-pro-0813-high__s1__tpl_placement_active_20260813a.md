[P1.1] Facial recognition in public does not watch people; it names them. That difference is the heart of this motion. Ordinary CCTV records a crowd; biometric mass surveillance identifies every person in it, continuously, without suspicion or consent, with no opt-out short of staying home. Free societies have always depended on practical anonymity — the ability to attend a protest, a mosque, a clinic, or a union meeting without the state compiling a dossier.
[P1.2] Mass biometric identification abolishes that zone by default, for everyone.
[P1.3] The harms are not speculative. U.S. police have already wrongfully arrested people — disproportionately Black Americans — on the strength of false facial-recognition matches, including a woman arrested while eight months pregnant. These systems err most on minority faces, so the misidentification burden lands on those least positioned to fight it. And the infrastructure never stays narrow: a network sold to catch fugitives gets pointed at protesters, then at everyone.
[P1.4] That is why cities like San Francisco, Boston, and Portland banned government facial recognition outright — lawmakers saw that once the cameras exist, the promises limiting them do not.
[P1.5] Notice what the motion actually requires. Not a ban. Targeted biometric use against a named suspect, with judicial authorization, remains fully available; police can still match a photo to a watchlist when they have grounds. What becomes presumptively unlawful is only the dragnet: scanning everyone to find no one in particular.
[P1.6] And a narrow emergency authorization process covers the rare genuine exigency — an abducted child, an imminent attack — with speed, oversight, and an expiry date.
[P1.7] The real clash is about defaults. CON will say the technology catches criminals; so would searching every house on a street, yet we require warrants because intrusive power must be justified before use, not apologized for after. Presumptive unlawfulness simply restores that burden: the state proves necessity before building the machinery of identification, rather than citizens proving harm after their anonymity is already gone. Liberty surrendered at scale is rarely handed back.

[C1.1] The motion gets the default backwards. A face is not a diary. In public, people are already visible, photographable, and identifiable by witnesses, officers, and private cameras. Biometric systems automate comparison of what a person voluntarily exposes.
[C1.2] That does not abolish private life; it changes recognition speed and scale in settings where anonymity has never been legally guaranteed. Presumptive unlawfulness is therefore not modest; it treats ordinary public identification as a rights violation before any misuse is shown.
[C1.3] PRO’s wrongful-arrest examples prove a different problem: unreliable systems and sloppy procedure. False matches also occur in targeted uses PRO accepts. The answer is accuracy thresholds, audits, human confirmation, disclosure, and liability—not a blanket presumption suppressing both bad and good deployments. A city could meet high standards yet still be blocked under the motion’s default because the system starts illegal.
[C1.4] Worse, emergency-only is too narrow. Finding a missing child or adult with dementia, identifying a suspected trafficker moving through a transit hub, reuniting disaster victims, disrupting a robbery series—these are regular investigative and protective work, not rare exigencies. Requiring a special emergency declaration before scanning starts ensures the systems are least tested and least supervised exactly when the stakes are highest.
[C1.5] The better regime is presumptive lawfulness with hard statutory limits: specified permitted purposes, no discriminatory use, retention caps, audit logs, independent review. That protects civil liberties without making every camera suspect. PRO’s house-search analogy fails because the home is private; the street is not. We should regulate identification in public, not outlaw it by default.
