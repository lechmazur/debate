[P1.1] Patent protection should reward invention, but it should not create a permanent veto over lifesaving treatment. A targeted public-interest licensing pathway would let governments respond when an essential gene-editing therapy is clinically effective yet financially inaccessible to the patients who need it. Rather than abolishing intellectual property, this mechanism would allow authorized use, manufacture, or import at negotiated prices, with appropriate royalties protecting the innovator.
[P1.2] The central issue is monopoly power without adequate counterweight. Gene-editing therapies can require complex research, specialized facilities, and lengthy trials. Those realities justify exclusivity for a limited period. They do not justify years of extreme prices after a therapy has become essential, especially where insurers exclude the treatment and affected families cannot afford it.
[P1.3] A public-interest license would preserve the incentive to invent while preventing the inventor’s reward from becoming an unbounded claim over public health.
[P1.4] Such a pathway need not sacrifice safety or invite arbitrary state intervention. It could apply only when independent experts determine that a therapy is essential, approved for a serious indication, and priced beyond meaningful access. Due process, transparent criteria, quality requirements, and reasonable compensation would protect developers, manufacturers, and patients alike. Governments would also bear the responsibility for using the power narrowly rather than treating compulsory access as a substitute for ordinary price negotiation.
[P1.5] Consider a curative gene-editing treatment for a severe inherited blood disorder. Without a licensing backstop, the patient’s only realistic choices are an unaffordable treatment or no treatment. With one, governments can secure additional supply and negotiate access without granting the developer unlimited control over who is treated. This solves a market failure without rejecting the legitimate purpose of patents.
[P1.6] The question is therefore not whether innovation deserves protection—it does—but whether society should have a lawful, limited means to prevent essential innovation from remaining permanently beyond reach. Public-interest licensing draws that necessary line.

[C1.1] Public-interest licensing treats gene editing like a chemical pill: lift the patent, authorize a generic factory, and affordable supply follows [P1.1]. Gene editing defies that model. Whether modifying a patient’s harvested stem cells for the inherited blood disorder invoked in [P1.5] or delivering in vivo editors through engineered vectors, the biological process is the product. Patents do not transfer the tacit cell-culture know-how, proprietary vector supply chains, release assays, and accredited clinical networks required to edit human DNA safely.
[C1.2] Replicating complex editing protocols without the originator’s cooperation risks dangerous off-target mutations or failed treatment. Conversely, wielding licensing mandates merely as a coercive price hammer backfires: because bespoke therapies require developers to accredit local hospitals and manage patient-specific logistics, punitive regimes simply deter innovators from launching in those markets.
[C1.3] Even worse, requiring this pathway selectively penalizes medicine’s hardest breakthroughs. Unlike chronic drugs earning revenue across decades of refills, curative gene edits are typically one-shot treatments for small genetic cohorts; each cured patient permanently shrinks the market. Research capital absorbs steep failure rates only because a rare success can capture upfront value reflecting years of avoided medical care. Promising "reasonable compensation" [P1.4] alongside compelled affordable prices is a contradiction: royalties low enough to slash prices cannot recoup the failed trials behind each breakthrough.
[C1.4] Capping returns the moment a therapy proves "essential" [P1.2] drives capital out of high-risk cures toward lifelong symptom-management drugs, stranding thousands of hereditary diseases without a cure.
[C1.5] Ultimately, mandatory licensing misdiagnoses a financing mismatch as a patent failure. Curative gene therapies strain budgets because payers compress a lifetime of clinical value—plus intensive hospital conditioning and specialist care that patents never govern—into a single upfront bill. Governments can secure real patient access through outcomes-based installment payments tied to durable efficacy, pooled rare-disease procurement, and negotiated volume guarantees. Compelling licenses trades real cures tomorrow for paper access that cannot safely deliver therapies today.

A compulsory domestic license can grant, without consent, the right to use the patented process, make the therapy, or import it, with necessary technical assistance as a condition. For a foreign holder, the authority acts through the patent it recognizes domestically: to enforce or profit from that patent there, the holder must make the licensed technology available there. [P4.2] For autologous therapies, access means exercising the patented process through accredited domestic clinical networks, not warehousing a dose that does not yet exist. These powers cannot bypass another country’s law, but they are meaningful enforcement mechanisms.
[P4.3] A2: A compulsory royalty and an affordable patient price are not the same thing. The patient or public payer can receive the therapy at an affordable price while the licensee pays royalties on sales; documented successful and failed program costs can shape the royalty, and publicly supported research can be credited against it. If rarity makes full commercial recovery incompatible with access, public procurement or research support can carry that cost rather than deny treatment. CON’s financing alternatives can operate under the license; they do not answer the need for enforcement when bargaining fails.
[P4.4] This resolves the manufacturing objection without pretending every government can coerce every foreign laboratory. The clash is now clear. CON has identified design details—jurisdiction, technical assistance, and royalty calculation—that the pathway must handle, but has not shown why governments should lack a lawful backstop when negotiation fails. Installments and pooled purchasing depend on developer agreement; a public-interest license does not.
[P4.5] With independent essentiality findings, due process, quality controls, fair royalties, and an affordability condition, the proposal addresses the failure CON leaves intact: an essential cure remains legally beyond reach. Because the motion asks for a targeted pathway rather than universal compulsory licensing, it can protect both access and innovation.
