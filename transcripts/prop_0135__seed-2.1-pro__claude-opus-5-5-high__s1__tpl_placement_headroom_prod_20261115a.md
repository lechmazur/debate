[P1.1] This policy is no blanket anti-tourism ban: it is a proportional, evidence-based trigger applied only when verifiable harms to permanent residents outstrip the actual economic value of those arrivals. That guardrail makes it not just fair, but self-evidently sensible. Cruise ship day-trippers and large bus tour groups deliver a fraction of the economic benefit of slower, overnight visitors: they rarely book paid local accommodation, often take meals included in their tour fare, and concentrate spending on low-margin souvenir stalls rather than the neighborhood restaurants, shops, and service providers that sustain resident livelihoods.
[P1.2] Worse, most of the ticket revenue from cruise lines and large tour operators flows to out-of-town corporate headquarters, not local municipal budgets that fund road repair, waste management, and public health services stretched thin by the surge of visitors. The harms they impose when volumes cross that break-even threshold, by contrast, are durable and unfair. Idling bus engines and coastal cruise ship emissions push localized air pollution to levels that worsen childhood asthma and chronic respiratory illness for people who live there year-round.
[P1.3] Crowds clog narrow historic thoroughfares so thoroughly that ordinary, necessary trips—dropping a child at school, picking up medicine, getting to a work shift on time—become hours-long slogs. The constant churn of high-volume day traffic drives up commercial and residential rents, as property owners convert long-term housing and neighborhood-serving storefronts to short-term rentals and tourist-focused vendors, pushing out the working-class communities that give historic cities their living, distinct character, rather than reducing them to hollow, curated open-air museum sets. Capping those high-volume, low-value arrivals at that break-even point does not kill tourism; it protects the irreplaceable living asset that draws tourists in the first place, and centers the rights of the people for whom these cities are home, not just a day-trip photo op.

[C1.1] PRO's motion sounds modest because its trigger does all the work. But "when harms outweigh revenue" is not a gauge any city council can actually read. Who prices a child's asthma attack against a waiter's lost shift? In practice, the threshold becomes whatever the loudest neighborhood lobby says it is, and a cap, once imposed, rarely comes off.
[C1.2] My case is simple: even when harms are real, a cap is the wrong instrument, and choosing it leaves residents worse off than the alternatives.
[C1.3] First, PRO's own argument defeats the housing claim. PRO says these visitors "rarely book paid local accommodation" [P1.1]. Exactly. People who sleep on a ship or in a hotel forty kilometers away do not occupy city apartments.
[C1.4] Housing pressure in historic centers comes from short-term rentals and overnight demand. Capping day arrivals leaves those conversions untouched, and may push operators toward overnight packages that consume even more housing.
[C1.5] Second, caps target the wrong variable. Pollution comes from idling engines and diesel generators, not from headcounts. Require shore power at berths, ban idling, mandate low-emission coaches, and emissions fall while visitors keep coming. Crowding comes from timing: several ships unloading at ten in the morning.
[C1.6] Stagger berthing slots and coach arrival windows, and the same visitors spread across the day.
[C1.7] Third, PRO complains that revenue leaks to corporate headquarters [P1.2]. A cap doesn't fix that; it forfeits the revenue entirely. A per-passenger levy or coach-entry charge does fix it, funding waste collection, road repair, and affordable housing while automatically pricing out the lowest-value trips. Caps instead create scarce permits that the biggest operators hoard, and they shut out the cheapest visitors: students, pensioners, families who can't afford a hotel night.
[C1.8] The real question is whether a blunt ceiling beats targeted tools. It doesn't, so the motion fails.
