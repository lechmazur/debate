[P1.1] Of course individuals choose. The policy question is why identical choices keep producing predictable population outcomes—and which lever can change them at scale. Food environments and marketing are that lever. A parent can decide to feed a child well, then meet candy at the checkout, soda branding in the school corridor, and a meal deal where the cheapest calories are the least nourishing.
[P1.2] A behavior campaign asks that parent to resist all of it, every day. Environment policy changes what has to be resisted.
[P1.3] Marketing matters because it shapes the baseline before deliberation starts. Advertising to children does not merely inform; it trains preference and pester power. Restricting it changes the demand side at its source. By contrast, consumer campaigns depend on attention, literacy, time, and motivation—resources unequally distributed.
[P1.4] Telling busy, low-income households to read labels and cook from scratch while unhealthy defaults surround them is not empowerment; it is a transfer of responsibility without a transfer of power. Structural changes—product reformulation, default portions, checkout rules, school food standards—reach people who never enrolled in a campaign.
[P1.5] Individual campaigns have a place, but their effects are typically modest and fragile; they must be repeated, funded, and remembered. The environment is always teaching. A single ad restriction or checkout rule keeps working after the poster comes down. And the proposition says focus more, not exclusively: campaigns can support structural change, but they cannot substitute for it.
[P1.6] The decisive comparison is scale and permanence. A campaign persuades the already-motivated one at a time; a marketing environment shapes the default for everyone. If we want population-level obesity policy, we must fix the current, not just shout at the swimmer.

[C1.1] Choices are shaped, but they are still made—and calories are consumed by persons, not by checkout aisles. The proposition asks where policy should place its greater weight. It should not place it on food environment and marketing restrictions, for four reasons.
[C1.2] First, environmental fixes are elastic, not permanent. PRO says the environment “keeps working after the poster comes down” [P1.5]. But profit follows demand. Ban candy at the register and it moves to the shelf; restrict child TV ads and marketing shifts to influencers, games, and packaging; mandate smaller defaults and people compensate with second helpings or cheaper calories elsewhere.
[C1.3] The current does not disappear; it reroutes.
[C1.4] Second, individual consumer behavior campaigns target the actual mechanism: the person who chooses, cooks, and eats. A campaign is not merely to “shout at the swimmer” [P1.6]; it is swimming lessons and navigation. Teaching label literacy, portion control, meal planning, and impulse management builds capacity that travels with a shopper into any store. It also shifts demand.
[C1.5] If enough consumers want smaller portions or fewer sugary drinks, firms reformulate without a regulator’s threat. Campaigns change the market from the inside out.
[C1.6] Third, focus is a budget, not a metaphor. Structural rules are blunt, slow, and legally fragile; their average effects are diffuse and easily eroded by substitution. Behavior programs can be targeted to high-risk groups, tested, and improved. With finite political capital, the higher marginal return is often on the consumer, not the checkout.
[C1.7] Finally, there is a democratic cost. A policy that leans more on restricting what others may market or sell treats adults as objects to be managed. Campaigns treat them as agents to be equipped.
[C1.8] PRO’s “fix the current” sounds decisive, but the current includes appetite, habit, and inertia. Equip the swimmer, and any current can be navigated; reroute only the water, and people will find the next one.

[P4.1] A1: The mechanism is not “other than choice”; it is choice under a different default. Adults can still buy candy, but many purchases are not deliberated from first principles—checkout placement exploits impulse, habit, and tiredness. Move the candy and you reduce how often the choice is presented at the moment of least resistance. That is a population mechanism: many small friction changes produce fewer unplanned purchases across millions of shoppers without persuading anyone.
[P4.2] CON sets a false test—unless a rule makes candy impossible, it is “merely” individual choice. But campaigns also work only through individual choices. The difference is whether policy must recruit each chooser or adjust the common environment they all share. Friction at scale does not erase agency; it gives agency a fairer default.
[P4.3] A2: The treadmill is real for both sides, but it bites campaigns harder. A campaign skill does not “travel automatically” to every package or app; it requires the person to notice, interpret, remember, and resist while industry counters with new cues. The parent who never enrolls gets nothing. Restrictions may need updating, but between updates they apply to every transaction and every child in the regulated channel; a campaign applies only to those reached and retained.
[P4.4] That is the scalability comparison: campaigns must re-earn attention; rules keep shaping the baseline until changed.
[P4.5] So the decisive clash is reach without opt-in. CON’s best case is that structural rules are imperfect and must be maintained. Our case is that campaigns are structurally limited by voluntary uptake and decay. A checkout rule changes thousands of impulse moments; a poster teaches only those who read it.
[P4.6] Even a rerouted restriction changes the next baseline, while a faded campaign leaves the old baseline untouched. “Focus more” does not mean abandon campaigns; it means stop asking them to carry a load they cannot bear.
