[P1.1] When a university sells a degree it knows the curriculum, the faculty, the job prospects of its graduates. Yet today the financial fallout of a failed program lands on the student and, through federal guarantees, on every taxpayer. That is a misallocation of risk that props up low‑quality offerings and penalizes the public.
[P1.2] Consider the pattern: for‑profit schools that pushed high‑tuition health‑technology certificates saw default rates climb above thirty percent while graduates earned less than half the national median wage. Community colleges that expanded cosmetology programs without updating licensing requirements produced similar debt‑to‑earnings gaps. In both cases the institution reaped tuition revenue while the borrower shouldered the loss. Sharing part of that loss with the university forces the very actor who designs the program to internalize the consequences of its design.
[P1.3] The proposition is narrow: it applies only when a program repeatedly yields poor earnings relative to debt. That protects innovation—experimental fields may initially under‑perform—but it draws a line at chronic under‑performance. Universities can meet the standard by improving career services, revising curricula, or cutting programs that cannot demonstrate viable outcomes. The cost they bear is a fraction of the total default, enough to shift incentives without bankrupting institutions.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim this discourages access or chills academic freedom. But the status quo already chills access: students avoid fields they cannot afford, and taxpayers fund bailouts that could be prevented. Other industries already bear product‑failure costs: automobile makers recall unsafe cars, pharmaceutical firms compensate for harmful drugs. Universities are no different; they sell educational products and should share the liability when those products systematically under‑deliver.
[P1.5] A modest risk‑sharing clause aligns the university’s profit motive with student success, reduces the fiscal burden on the public, and forces a market‑driven correction of programs that consistently fail their graduates.
[P1.6] That is the core of the case: the party that knows the product and profits from it should bear part of the product’s failure.

Employers set wages, graduates choose jobs, locations, and how much to borrow for living costs, and recessions intervene. Making the university pay for defaults therefore assigns the bill to the one actor that controls neither the loan nor the labor market, while giving it no way to price that risk per student.
[C1.2] An earnings-to-debt test sounds objective, but it measures who a program serves and what society pays for the work. Teacher preparation, social work, early-childhood education, ministry, and the arts will look worse than finance or engineering, not because the teaching was defective but because public and nonprofit wages are low. Open-access colleges, HBCUs, tribal colleges, and regional publics enroll more low-income, first-generation, part-time, and working students — people who face higher default risk before they enter a classroom because of prior wealth gaps and hiring discrimination.
[C1.3] Penalizing their schools confuses disadvantage with educational failure.
[C1.4] The predictable response is not better teaching. It is defensive exclusion. A rational administrator will raise admissions thresholds, shrink programs in low-paid public-service fields, cut evening and remedial pathways that serve riskier students, and favor applicants who need to borrow least. Remaining students will pay through higher tuition, larger classes, or fewer advisors as schools build reserves against loan losses.
[C1.5] Thinly financed colleges anchoring rural communities may close, leaving students with the same debts and fewer options.
[C1.6] Education is not a car with a defective brake that a manufacturer can recall. A degree's value unfolds through a graduate's effort, choices, and circumstances over decades, and universities never promised a particular salary. Fraud and deceptive recruiting should be punished as fraud, and programs should publish honest outcomes.
[C1.7] But converting every chronically low-earning program into a financial liability for its school will narrow access, punish public-service missions, and still leave the lending decisions untouched.

Penalties do not make institutions braver; they make risk easier to avoid.

[P4.3] A2: A teacher-preparation program is not helpless before district salary schedules. The university can cap recommended borrowing, restructure the program toward specializations with better pay, or—if district wages are genuinely too low—reduce cohort size.
[P4.4] That is exactly the market correction the proposition demands, not exclusion of poor students but honest assessment of what the credential can deliver.
[P4.5] This is why PRO still wins. CON's entire case collapses into a single bet: that schools will always choose exclusion over improvement. But the status quo gives schools zero financial stake in outcomes; they collect tuition regardless of default. A modest cost share flips that calculus.
[P4.6] Even if some defensive trimming occurs, the alternative—public bailouts of chronically failing programs while taxpayers and borrowers absorb the loss—is far worse.
