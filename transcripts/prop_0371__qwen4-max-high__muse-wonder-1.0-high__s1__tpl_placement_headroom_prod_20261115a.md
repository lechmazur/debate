[P1.1] A ticket buys carriage for a person and the things a person cannot travel without: medication, a change of clothes, a laptop, a phone charger. Those are not luxuries the airline generously lends space to; they are the ordinary contents of a human being moving from one city to another. A carry-on baseline belongs in the fare for the same reason a seat does — it is the product, not an upgrade to it.
[P1.2] Notice where the fee actually lands. Almost nobody buys a basic economy ticket planning to check a bag; they discover at the gate that the roller they have carried onto planes for twenty years now costs more than the fare difference they thought they were saving. There is no comparison shopping at a boarding door, no refund, no alternative airline. That is not a market transaction; it is a toll collected at the one moment the customer cannot walk away.
[P1.3] This also breaks price competition upstream. Search engines rank by base fare, so the airline that strips the most out of the headline price wins the click, then rebuilds the revenue in fragments. Every competitor is pushed to fragment further. The consumer who diligently reads three baggage policies pays for the confusion of everyone else.
[P1.4] A reasonable baseline is a floor, not a price control. Checked bags, second bags, seat selection, priority boarding — all still sellable. Airlines can raise fares to cover the bin space they already own; the difference is that the number on the screen would finally be the number you pay.
[P1.5] The opposing case must show that this fragmentation produces genuine savings, not just a transfer from the uninformed traveler to the airline's ancillary revenue line. Ask which traveler actually gets the discount.

[C1.1] A ticket buys carriage for a person, not a guaranteed slice of the scarcest real estate on the plane. A seat scales one-to-one; overhead bin space does not. On a full flight there is not room for every passenger to bring a full-size roller, which is exactly why that space should be priced and allocated rather than promised to everyone in the base fare and then rationed by boarding order, gate-check chaos, and arguments in the aisle.
[C1.2] Unbundling is what lets a traveler who needs none of that space stop paying for it. The student flying home for a weekend with a backpack, the commuter making a same-day return, the family pooling clothes into one checked bag — under a mandated baseline they all subsidize heavier packers whether they use a bin or not. Remove the cheapest, lightest option and you do not make travel fairer; you raise the entry price for the people most sensitive to it, while calling the increase consumer protection.
[C1.3] That choice already exists without a mandate. Airlines sell bundled fares that include carry-ons, seat selection, and flexibility alongside stripped-down basic fares, and passengers can filter for them. If some shoppers choose the headline price anyway, the answer is honest total-price disclosure at search and checkout, not outlawing the cheaper product. Fixing a misleading display does not require forcing every buyer to purchase the same bundle.
[C1.4] A legal right to a 'reasonable' carry-on also freezes a vague standard into fleets that differ wildly — small regional jets, dense short-haul planes, and long-haul aircraft cannot sensibly promise the same bag. Regulators would end up litigating inches while airlines design cabins around the rule instead of around passengers.
[C1.5] Fragmentation can be annoying, but a compulsory bundle is still a bundle someone else chose for you.
