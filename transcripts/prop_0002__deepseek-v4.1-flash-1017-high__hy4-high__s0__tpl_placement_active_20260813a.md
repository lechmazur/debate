[P1.1] Affordability in American cities is a scarcity price, not just a construction-cost problem. In productive metros, demand rose for decades while law made most residential land illegal to use intensively. When a city permits only one home on most lots, and jobs and population grow, the price of the legal right to live near those jobs gets bid up. Legalizing denser housing two decades ago would have let more duplexes, fourplexes, midrises, and small apartments exist on the same land.
[P1.2] More supply near opportunity would have meant lower rents and prices today than the restricted alternative.
[P1.3] This is not a promise of instant affordability. Housing is durable, construction is incremental, and legalization is not a subsidy. But two decades is exactly the horizon on which incremental supply compounds. A few thousand additional units a year in a tight market is not a rounding error; it is the difference between bidding wars and vacancies.
[P1.4] Market-rate homes relieve pressure across the stock: when higher-income renters take new units, older units filter down. Legal density also lowers the cost of land per home, making modest and family-sized housing easier to produce.
[P1.5] Construction costs, demand, and local politics make legalization insufficient, not irrelevant. The motion is comparative: denser rules versus the restrictive rules cities actually had. Even with rising costs, places that allowed more homes would have built more than places that banned them. Even with strong demand, supply responds over years and decades.
[P1.6] Even with neighborhood resistance, citywide legalization changes the default.
[P1.7] Picture two similar cities: one allows fourplexes and apartments on many more blocks; the other freezes most land for detached houses. After twenty years, the first has more homes, more vacancies, lower land bids, and less displacement pressure. Zoning need not explain everything to explain a large avoidable gap. The legal chokehold on density made American cities less affordable than they would otherwise be—and that cost is still on the rent roll.

[C1.1] A permission to build is not an affordable home. The proposition skips the forces that decide whether extra units lower urban rents: demand for access to high-wage jobs, incomes, credit, construction costs, and the rent a developer must charge after paying for land. PRO treats legalization as if it creates cheap units; in prized markets it first creates a more expensive right to build.
[C1.2] When a one-house lot becomes a legal fourplex site, the owner does not donate that value. The parcel sells for the capitalized value of future apartments; builders who pay it need high rents to pencil. So [P1.7]'s "more homes, more vacancies, lower land bids" omits the first bid: upzoning can raise the land-price floor before concrete is poured.
[C1.3] Legalization also does not repeal cost. Midrise housing needs more structure, engineering, fire systems, and longer financing than a small house. Allowed fourplexes can sit unbuilt if expected rents do not cover costs; when built, they often enter at the high end. A city can legalize density and still get luxury units, demolition of cheaper stock, investor purchases, and fees passed into rents.
[C1.4] The "incremental supply compounds" line in [P1.3] forgets that demand and land values compound too. Job clusters, cheap credit, household formation, and capital inflows kept bidding for the same locations. Permissive or sprawling places still saw prices climb with jobs and incomes; low-demand restrictive places did not become boomtowns. That damages [P1.1]'s claim that affordability is mainly a "scarcity price" produced by law.
[C1.5] Filtering cannot carry twenty years. Older units become cheaper only if they are not demolished, renovated, converted, bought by investors, or absorbed by new households. Those are normal outcomes, not exceptions. Density can add units, but it also adds redevelopment pressure and land capitalization.
[C1.6] The question is net effect, not whether rules matter: earlier density legalization alone would not reliably have produced lower rents and prices across American cities today.
