[P1.1] Long-term care is a certainty for society, but a financial lottery for the individual. While some retirees will require no assistance, others will face years of severe cognitive or physical decline, racking up catastrophic costs that easily exceed hundreds of thousands of dollars. Expecting individuals to self-insure against this risk through private savings or private insurance is an economic fantasy. Private long-term care insurance markets have largely collapsed globally due to adverse selection and skyrocketing premiums, leaving families stranded.
[P1.2] When private savings fail, the default alternative is means-testing and unpaid family labor. Means-tested systems do not prevent poverty; they mandate it. They force elderly citizens to liquidate their modest life savings and sell their homes—destroying generational wealth mobility—just to qualify for basic, dignified care.
[P1.3] For those who do not qualify or cannot afford private care, the burden falls on family members. This is not a free solution; it is a massive, hidden tax on the economy. Millions of working-age adults, predominantly women, are forced to reduce their hours or exit the workforce entirely to provide unpaid care. This reduces national productivity, shrinks the tax base, and decimates the caregivers’ own retirement security, perpetuating a multi-generational cycle of financial vulnerability.
[P1.4] A universal or near-universal long-term care system resolves these crises by treating elder care as a shared social risk, much like healthcare or basic education. By pooling the risk across the entire population, a universal system stabilizes financing, establishes consistent standards of care, and liberates families from ruinous financial decisions. It ensures that growing old does not require entering poverty, and that caring for a parent does not require sacrificing one’s own economic future. We must build a system that guarantees dignity by design, not by desperation.
