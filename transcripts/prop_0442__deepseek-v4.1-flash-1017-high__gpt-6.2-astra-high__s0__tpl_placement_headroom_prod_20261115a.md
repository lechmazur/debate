[P1.1] International shipping is the textbook case for a binding fuel mandate. It moves most of world trade, burns some of the dirtiest fuel, and operates beyond any single government's reach. If every state or port sets its own rules, owners will simply bunker where standards are weakest. The result is a race to the bottom, exactly what we have seen with voluntary efficiency measures.
[P1.2] A timetable set by the IMO changes the investment math: shipowners know when they must order dual-fuel vessels, fuel producers know when to scale ammonia, methanol, and hydrogen, and ports know when to build bunkering infrastructure. Without a date, everyone waits for everyone else. With one, capital moves. That is why the absence of a binding schedule has produced pilot projects, not a fleet.
[P1.3] The freight-cost objection is real but not decisive. Shipping is cheap relative to the value of what it carries, so a carbon price or fuel standard translates into a small increase in most retail prices, not an economic shock. For bulk commodities the percentage is larger, but still far below the cost of repeated disruption from uncoordinated rules.
[P1.4] More importantly, the alternative is not stable cheap freight. It is a disorderly transition: port-by-port levies, litigation, stranded assets, and eventual emergency rules. Binding rules make the cost predictable and spread it across the industry instead of punishing early movers.
[P1.5] And this is a global commons problem. Low-carbon fuels will stay boutique and expensive until demand is guaranteed. A binding IMO timetable creates that guaranteed market, drives scale, and lowers costs over time. The question is not whether shipping decarbonizes; physics and climate policy make that inevitable.
[P1.6] The question is whether it happens by design or by crisis. Design is the responsible choice.
