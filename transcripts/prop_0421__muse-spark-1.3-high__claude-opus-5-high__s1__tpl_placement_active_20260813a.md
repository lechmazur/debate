[P1.1] A mandatory fee is the price. When a delivery app lists a burger at $12, a ticket site lists a seat at $49, or a menu lists an entree at $20, but the customer cannot leave without paying service, platform, or large-party charges added only at payment, the first number was false. Requiring disclosure before the final checkout screen simply demands truth while choice is still possible.
[P1.2] The damage is not hurt feelings, it is rigged comparison. Shoppers cannot choose the cheaper option if the real prices are hidden. The $12 meal beats the $15 meal until small-basket fees, service fees, and delivery fees invert the ranking at the last click. The $49 ticket beats the $58 ticket until per-order and facility charges flip the winner.
[P1.3] By the final screen, time is sunk, details are entered, hunger or excitement is high, and most people surrender and pay. That sequence is not accident; it profits from exhaustion and time pressure to collect money that would be refused if stated upfront.
[P1.4] Honest sellers lose too. A restaurant with a slightly higher menu price but no hidden surcharge looks expensive next to a rival that hides 18% until the bill. Early disclosure rewards the genuinely cheaper option and punishes bait pricing, pushing competition toward real value instead of deception skill.
[P1.5] The cost defense fails because firms already compute these charges. They need only compute them earlier. Where a fee truly varies, a clear early estimate or formula — $3.99 plus a stated percentage, a per-mile delivery range — fully satisfies the principle. No one is banning fees, only ambushes.
[P1.6] Markets run on comparable prices. Mandatory means unavoidable, and unavoidable means it belongs in the decision, not in the trap.

[C1.3] PRO's own strongest passage — that by late in the flow "time is sunk, details are entered" and people surrender — indicts this motion, because the motion permits exactly that moment. [C1.4] That is the danger. A timing rule creates a safe harbor. Once a firm can say "we disclosed before final checkout, as the law requires," the ambush is laundered into compliance, and the harder reform — the headline number must be the number you pay — gets crowded out for a decade. Half a fix on a salient problem is how bad pricing gets permanently licensed.
[C1.5] Then there is the word "all." Many mandatory charges genuinely resolve late: large-party surcharges depend on how many people sit down, delivery fees on the address, and local levies on jurisdiction. Demanding all of them upfront pushes sellers toward defensive worst-case quoting — the honest seller now advertises the maximum, and looks *more* expensive than the rival who quotes tight and is occasionally wrong. That inverts PRO's fairness claim at [P1.4].
[C1.6] And the compliance burden lands unevenly. A national platform re-codes a template; the neighborhood restaurant reprints menus and takes on legal exposure for a 4% kitchen-benefits line.
[C1.7] Restaurants and platforms should include mandatory fees in the advertised price. This motion asks them to move the reveal a click. That is not truth; it is choreography.
