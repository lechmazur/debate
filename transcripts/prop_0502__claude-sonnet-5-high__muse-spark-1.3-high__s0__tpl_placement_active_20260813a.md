[P1.1] The Arctic is warming roughly three to four times faster than the global average, opening shipping lanes, fisheries, and seabed resources that eight nations and their militaries are already maneuvering to claim. The current framework—overlapping UNCLOS continental shelf claims, unresolved boundary disputes between Canada, Russia, the US, Denmark, and Norway, and no binding rulebook on militarization—is not a stable status quo. It is a slow-motion race that binding multilateral governance would defuse and that competing national claims cannot.
[P1.2] Three reasons decide this debate.
[P1.3] First, mechanism: the Antarctic Treaty System proves binding multilateral governance actually works where competing claims otherwise invite conflict. Seven states had overlapping, sometimes literally overlapping, territorial claims to Antarctica before 1959. Rather than let that resolve through power politics, the treaty froze claims, banned military activity, and created a durable science-and-conservation regime that has held for over six decades without a single interstate war on the continent. That is a real, verifiable template for converting zero-sum territorial competition into stable joint management.
[P1.4] Second, the Arctic's current framework is failing on its own terms. UNCLOS lets Russia, Canada, and Denmark all submit competing extended continental shelf claims over the same North Pole seabed, arbitrated by a slow, non-binding commission with no enforcement teeth. Meanwhile Russia has reopened and expanded Soviet-era military bases along its Arctic coast, and NATO members have responded in kind. Sole reliance on flag-planting and bilateral diplomacy management the way this played out — think Russia physically planting a titanium flag on the seabed under the North Pole in 2007 — signals exactly the symbolic, prestige-driven competition a binding treaty is meant to prevent.
[P1.5] Third, the stakes are compounding, not stable. Melting ice is not a fixed pie to divide by legal formula; it is a race for newly accessible resources and shipping routes where speed and military presence, not law, currently decide outcomes. Only a binding treaty framework—with agreed dispute resolution, demilitarization norms, and joint environmental stewardship—can convert that race into cooperation before an incident at sea becomes a crisis between nuclear powers.
