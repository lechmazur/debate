[P1.1] The central question is not whether grief tech should exist. It is who gets to decide whether a dead person's intimate traits — their voice, their humor, their private way of speaking — get packaged into software that anyone can talk to.
[P1.2] Voice and intimate personality traits are not generic content. They are constitutive of identity. A person's voice is how children and spouses recognize them. Their inside jokes, their cadence, the phrases they used with you alone — these are not public domain.
[P1.3] They are the residue of a specific human relationship. Treating them as raw material for anyone's chatbot is a categorical mistake. It treats a person as a dataset.
[P1.4] Without prior consent, that decision falls to whoever has the data and the engineers. That is the worst possible allocation. The deceased cannot object, the family may be split, and a company with a recorded interview or a stack of voicemails gets the de facto authority to resurrect someone in their own image. Consent flips the default.
[P1.5] It puts the choice where it belongs: with the person while living, or with the estate they trusted afterward.
[P1.6] Consider the asymmetry of harm. A wrongfully replicated parent, sibling, or child can keep a bereaved family trapped in a synthetic loop, mistaking a model for the real person. Grief counselors already warn about ambiguous loss — the grief that has no clear resolution. A memorial chatbot that none of the family approved deepens that pathology exactly when the vulnerable are most exposed.
[P1.7] Consent mechanisms, whether opt-in records, estate permissions, or licensed release forms, are the minimal safeguard, not a luxury.
[P1.8] The objection that consent is impractical is overstated. Existing law already requires release forms for voice likeness in advertising, for narrative rights in biography, for the commercial use of a person's image after death. We do not need new doctrine; we need to apply existing dignity-of-the-person rules to a new medium.
[P1.9] The principle is simple: a person's voice and intimate traits should not become a product without their permission.

The estate fallback creates a veto market: one estranged sibling, an executor who barely knew the person, or a company that acquired rights can block a spouse of forty years from hearing a familiar voice built from her own saved voicemails.
[C1.2] A rule meant to respect the dead empowers the least intimate claimant.
[C1.3] That veto rests on a false picture of ownership. A voice in a voicemail left for you, a nickname used only with you, the cadence of bedtime stories heard nightly — these are not solo assets carried away in death. They are co-created memories, and the living have always been free to retell, imitate, write, and record them. A daughter can publish a memoir quoting her mother verbatim; a widower can read old letters aloud and mimic her laugh.
[C1.4] Using software on messages lawfully in your possession changes the medium, not the morality.
[C1.5] Real risks — deception, dependency, commercial exploitation — can be punished without a consent gate. Require disclosure that it is a simulation, forbid marketing it as the real person, ban selling a likeness to strangers, allow removal for harassment or fraud. Those target misuse. Prior consent punishes use itself, including private, transparent, noncommercial use by the people closest to the loss.
[C1.6] It also treats silence as refusal. Many people would want to comfort those they leave behind but never signed a tech-specific form. Their presumed generosity is overridden by missing paperwork, while autonomy for the living — to preserve, interact, and let go on their own timeline — is sacrificed to autonomy the deceased can no longer exercise and we can only guess at. Memory belongs first to those who must live with it.

[C2.6] Regulate those.
[C2.7] Finally, silence-as-refusal fits bodies and organs because they are singular, invasive, and gone once taken. A voice modeled from words already shared is not taken from anyone; it can exist in the widow's memory, imitation, memoir, and recordings without depleting the deceased. We have never required estate leave for those retellings, and interactivity does not turn remembrance into theft.
