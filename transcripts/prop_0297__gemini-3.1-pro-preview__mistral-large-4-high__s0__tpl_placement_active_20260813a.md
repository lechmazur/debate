[P1.1] We face a critical choice when confronting monuments that glorify oppressive histories: how do we repudiate their message without erasing the past? Relocating these statues to museums is far superior to destroying them because it replaces public veneration with crucial historical accountability.
[P1.2] First, public squares are places of honor; museums are places of study. A controversial monument in a city center serves as active propaganda, often designed to intimidate marginalized groups and assert dominance. Moving it to a museum immediately strips the object of this unearned glory.
[P1.3] However, destroying the monument entirely makes a grave error: it destroys the physical evidence of that propaganda. To understand the mechanics of racism, colonialism, or authoritarianism, future generations must see the tools used to enforce them. A destroyed statue becomes a faded memory; a preserved statue, properly contextualized, becomes a lasting warning.
[P1.4] Second, destruction fuels revisionism. When we obliterate the physical artifacts of a painful past, we inadvertently create a historical vacuum. This vacuum allows revisionists to either deny that the oppression occurred or falsely claim that they are now the victims of cultural erasure. Museums prevent this by freezing these monuments in the harsh light of historical truth.
[P1.5] Curators can surround them with the stories of the oppressed, exposing the explicit political motives behind the statues' creation.
[P1.6] We see the success of this approach globally. After the fall of the Soviet Union, nations like Hungary and Lithuania did not melt down every authoritarian statue. They relocated them to designated parks and museums where their scale and intent could be analyzed critically. They turned tools of state terror into permanent educational assets that inoculate the public against totalitarianism.
[P1.7] Smashing a monument offers a fleeting moment of catharsis, but it does nothing to dismantle the underlying ideology. Preservation with context transforms an object of reverence into an object of scrutiny, ensuring that society learns from its darkest chapters rather than merely sweeping the evidence out of sight.

True accountability requires more than explanatory placards; it requires the removal of the symbol entirely. If we preserve the monument, we preserve the hierarchy it represents.
