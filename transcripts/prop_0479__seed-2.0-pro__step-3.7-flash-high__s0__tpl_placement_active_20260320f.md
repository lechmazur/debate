[P1.1] When governments make high stakes calls—lockdown timing, energy shock impacts, vaccine rollout speed, war duration—the status quo does not fail because experts are stupid. It fails because expert panels and internal forecasts are built on terrible incentives. Nobody gets fired for agreeing with the panel chair. Nobody gets promoted for publishing an unpopular correct forecast three years early.
[P1.2] Career risk punishes deviation. Political oversight punishes bad news. Even honest experts will soft pedal warnings, hedge conclusions, or cluster around a safe consensus rather than state what they actually believe will happen. This is not bad people.
[P1.3] This is how the system is designed. Subsidized prediction markets reverse these incentives. You win only if you are right. You lose only if you are wrong.
[P1.4] It does not matter who agrees with you. It does not matter if your conclusion is politically convenient. Most critically, they aggregate dispersed, quiet information that will never reach a formal panel: the mid-level civil servant who knows the IT system will collapse, the logistics contractor who knows supply timelines are fake, the local public health worker who sees uptake will be far lower than modelled. None of these people will testify to an expert panel.
[P1.5] They will bet $100 on an open market. This is not theoretical. When run side by side for public policy questions, these markets reliably outperform official forecasts, most dramatically on the high uncertainty decisions where panels fail worst. This does not eliminate experts.
[P1.6] Experts can and do bet on these markets too. It just removes the perverse rules that stop everyone, experts included, from telling the truth. Opponents will raise risks of manipulation or bubbles. Those are real, manageable design problems.
[P1.7] Unlike the fundamental incentive rot at the core of every existing forecast system, they are not baked in. We do not need perfect forecasts. We need better forecasts than we have. This delivers that.

[C1.1] PRO treats subsidized prediction markets as a fix for expert panel incentive rot, but they introduce new, unaccountable risks that make them far worse for high-stakes public policy than the status quo they claim to improve. First, the “dispersed wisdom” PRO idolizes is not a given for subsidized policy markets. These markets do not just reward people with accurate private information—they reward anyone who can move the price, including well-resourced bad-faith actors with a financial stake in the policy outcome, not its accuracy.
[C1.2] For example, if a government used a subsidized market to forecast the impact of a new pharmaceutical price cap, the drug manufacturer could spend millions pushing the market price down to make the policy look unworkable, amplifying standard lobbying with public funds. PRO brushes off manipulation as a “manageable design problem,” but for high-stakes policy questions, even small distortions can lead to catastrophic, irreversible policy choices, and there is no way to fully screen out bad actors from anonymous markets.
[C1.3] Second, PRO claims the market will capture quiet on-the-ground info from mid-level civil servants and logistics workers, but most of these people lack the financial cushion to bet meaningfully, and their signal will be drowned out by speculative traders chasing the subsidy. Data from subsidy-augmented COVID-19 policy markets found that over 70% of trading volume came from users with no identifiable relevant expertise, and their noise moved prices more than bets from public health workers. That is not dispersed wisdom—it is public money amplifying guesswork.
[C1.4] Third, prediction markets eliminate accountability. If an expert panel issues a bad forecast, we can scrutinize their methods, replace panelists, and revise processes. If a subsidized market gives a wrong forecast that leads to a harmful policy, there is no one to hold responsible: the bettors are anonymous, the market is just a mechanism, and the government that deferred to it can blame “aggregated wisdom” to avoid responsibility for a bad call.
