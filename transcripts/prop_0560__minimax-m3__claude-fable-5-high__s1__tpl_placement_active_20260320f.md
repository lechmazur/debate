[P1.1] The choice before us is not whether to abandon the dying. It is where the next marginal dollar of public health funding should go: into prevention that averts decades of illness, or into small expansions of end-of-life treatment that buy weeks at extraordinary cost.
[P1.2] Consider what prevention delivers. Childhood immunization, hypertension control, tobacco taxes, clean water, maternal nutrition — these are not boutique interventions. They compress morbidity and extend life expectancy across entire cohorts. A dollar spent on screening for high blood pressure or helping smokers quit returns many times its value in strokes, heart attacks, and cancers that never occur.
[P1.3] Prevention compounds: a smoker who quits at fifty avoids not one disease but a trajectory of them, for decades.
[P1.4] Now compare that to a "marginal expansion of high-cost end-of-life care." We are not talking about palliative comfort, which has its own moral claim. We are talking about the next round of expensive late-line therapy — a second or third targeted drug with marginal survival benefit, an ICU extension that gains a few weeks of consciousness, a procedure whose survival curve flattens within months. These interventions can cost six figures per year of life gained, and the gain itself is small and often burdened by toxicity.
[P1.5] The opportunity cost is not abstract. A single six-figure course of late-line therapy in one patient could fund hundreds of vaccinations, a community health worker for a year, or screening for thousands. The math is not subtle.
[P1.6] I anticipate the objection that this is cold calculus. It is not. We are asking how to honor the equal moral worth of patients we have not yet met — the future stroke patient, the future infant, the future smoker — against a small, named benefit for a few. A system that consistently chooses the second is not more compassionate.
[P1.7] It is systematically trading many lives for a few weeks, and calling that ethics.
[P1.8] That is why the priority must flip: prevention first, marginal end-of-life expansion last.

[C1.7] Fourth, innovation.
