[P1.1] The 21st century great power competition with China and Russia demands a fundamental shift in US defense procurement, away from a small number of ultra-expensive, high-value exquisite platforms and toward massed, attritable systems that can survive contested anti-access/area-denial environments. Current procurement prioritizes assets like the $80 million F-35, $13 billion Ford-class carrier, and $2 billion B-2 bomber, each representing an irreplaceable share of US combat power if lost. Adversaries have built A2/AD strategies explicitly around this vulnerability, fielding thousands of $1 million to $10 million long-range missiles, anti-ship ballistic missiles, and drone swarms designed to sink or disable these high-value targets.
[P1.2] Even a 10:1 exchange rate favoring US losses is a catastrophic strategic defeat, and no defense budget can afford to replace a single carrier or stealth bomber lost to a cheap adversary countermeasure. Shifting significant procurement share to attritable systems solves this core flaw. Cheaper systems ranging from $100,000 counter-drone munitions to $5 million collaborative combat aircraft can be mass-produced on commercial industrial base lines, scaled to replace losses, and used to attrit enemy defenses ahead of more capable assets.
[P1.3] This rebalancing does not require abandoning all exquisite platforms, but ensures the bulk of US inventory can absorb casualties, overwhelm adversary targeting systems, and project power in contested regions. The Pentagon’s Replicator initiative, which aims to field thousands of attritable autonomous systems within two years to counter Chinese massed drone and missile forces in the Indo-Pacific, validates this logic: attritable mass is the only sustainable counter to adversary mass per dollar spent.

[C1.2] Exquisite platforms concentrate capabilities that attritable systems physically cannot replicate. A $13 billion carrier provides sustained sorties, command-and-control, logistics, and deterrence that thousands of $5 million drones cannot replace. The F-35's sensor fusion gives pilots a battlespace picture that no swarm of cheap sensors can match, because individual platforms lack the processing power, power generation, and aperture size to replicate that capability. When PRO says "this rebalancing does not require abandoning all exquisite platforms," the motion's exact words mandate reducing investment in them.
[C1.3] That reduction directly degrades capabilities the military depends on for missions where quality is not optional, including penetrating defended airspace, sustaining operations over weeks, and integrating allied forces.
[C1.4] The exchange-rate argument collapses on inspection. PRO assumes exquisite platforms are easily killed, but stealth, electronic warfare, and layered defense make carriers and fifth-generation aircraft extraordinarily difficult targets. China's DF-21D missile cannot hit what it cannot find or track reliably, and the US Navy does not steam carriers within range without escorts and countermeasures specifically designed to defeat such weapons.
[C1.5] Meanwhile, attritable systems face their own asymmetric vulnerabilities: electronic warfare can neutralize swarms; cheap platforms lack redundancy and degrade rapidly in contested electromagnetic environments.
[C1.6] Quantity has a quality of its own, but only when the quantity in question can accomplish the mission. Flooding a battlespace with attritable systems that cannot survive jamming, cannot persist past the first engagement, and cannot replace the command-and-control functions of exquisite platforms is not a strategy, it is a mathematical exercise in losing differently. The United States wins wars through decisive capability, not disposable volume.
