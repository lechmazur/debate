[C1.1] The motion asks us to move from regulating treatment to conferring a new legal status: non-human persons with basic rights against captivity and exploitation. That shift is not a small upgrade to animal welfare; it is a constitutional change in who can hold rights, and it fails on its own terms.
[C1.2] The strongest move for personhood is to say it is functional, not biological. As [P1.2] argues, 'We grant it to corporations, ships, and rivers—entities with no minds at all.' But that comparison destroys the case. Those are legal fictions created to serve human purposes.
[C1.3] They have human agents, human beneficiaries, and human courts to interpret their interests. A river does not sue; a lawyer does. An orca cannot be a rights-holder in that sense. It cannot assert, waive, or bear duties, and any guardian appointed to speak for it would simply be a human official imposing contested human judgments about 'interests'—exactly what welfare law already does, but now dressed as constitutional right.
[C1.4] Personhood is not a trophy for intelligence; it is a role in a system of answerable conduct.
[C1.5] Worse, basic rights against captivity and exploitation are absolute-sounding but radically unclear. Does a rescued, unreleasable dolphin in a sanctuary have a right to be released when release would kill it? Does a zoo breeding program that prevents extinction count as exploitation? Does indigenous fishing that accidentally stresses a whale violate its rights?
[C1.6] 'Against captivity' sounds tidy; applied to rehabilitation, quarantine, conservation, and even veterinary care, it becomes impossible to administer.
[C1.7] This argument treats welfare protections as incoherent because an elephant can grieve. But grief shows vulnerability, not the capacity to occupy a legal office of rights. The coherent answer is stringent anti-cruelty, habitat preservation, and tight limits on commercial use—not inventing a status that cannot be enforced without unlimited guardian discretion and that would make ordinary animal care legally incoherent.
