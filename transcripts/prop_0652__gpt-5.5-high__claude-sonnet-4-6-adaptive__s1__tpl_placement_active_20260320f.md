[P1.1] Copyright law fails if the easiest way to copy a protected work at scale is to insert one more machine in the middle and call the result “user-prompted.” When a platform hosts and distributes AI-generated content that infringes existing copyrights, it is not a passive bystander. It supplies the system, sets the defaults, designs the guardrails, controls the distribution channel, monetizes attention or subscriptions, and can remove, filter, watermark, log, demonetize, or refuse obviously infringing outputs. Liability should follow that control and profit.
[P1.2] The key word is “share.” The user who asks for a knockoff movie still bears responsibility. But the platform should share it when its infrastructure makes infringement cheap, repeatable, and commercially useful. A lone infringer can harm one author; a platform can turn millions of prompts into a factory for substitute images, songs, scripts, games, and merchandise. If only the end user is liable, enforcement becomes a whack-a-mole game against anonymous or judgment-proof individuals while the entity best positioned to prevent the harm keeps the revenue.
[P1.3] This is not liability for “style,” inspiration, or lawful remix. The proposition applies when the content actually infringes existing copyrights. Nor does it require platforms to pre-approve every sentence and pixel. It requires responsibility proportionate to role: if a platform knowingly hosts infringing outputs, markets tools for imitation, ignores repeat infringement, strips context, or builds distribution systems that amplify copies, it should not get a blanket pass merely because a user typed the prompt.
[P1.4] Shared liability also creates the right incentives. Platforms will invest in content-matching, complaint systems, licensing markets, creator opt-outs, repeat-offender rules, and better model safeguards only if infringement is a cost they must internalize. Without liability, the market rewards the platform that externalizes copyright losses onto writers, artists, musicians, and studios while claiming neutrality. The law should not protect a business model where creators bear the risk, users take the blame, and platforms keep the scale.
[P1.5] Shared liability is the fair and practical rule for an AI distribution ecosystem.

[C1.3] Holding the platform liable for that output conflates building a road with choosing the destination. Copyright law has never assigned liability merely for providing a generative capability—it has always required a connection between the defendant's act and the reproduction of the protected expression.
[C1.4] Second, existing law already reaches genuinely culpable platforms. Contributory and vicarious infringement doctrines cover platforms that knowingly facilitate specific infringement or profit from it while retaining control. The DMCA safe-harbor framework was designed precisely to separate responsible intermediaries from bad actors. [C1.5] Third, the chilling effect here isn't hypothetical. If liability attaches whenever user-prompted output is later found to infringe, no platform can operate without pre-approving every output. That is technically impossible at scale. The practical result is either the shutdown of AI content platforms or such aggressive filtering that legitimate creative use—parody, reference, original work that resembles prior art—is impossible.
[C1.6] Creators who rely on these tools lose alongside the copyright holders PRO claims to protect.
[C1.7] Finally, if there is a genuine copyright crisis in AI, its source is training data, not distribution. PRO's proposition aims at the wrong target. Punishing distribution doesn't un-train a model or compensate a writer whose work was scraped. It just moves liability to the least efficient point in the chain.
[C1.8] The proposition punishes the wrong actor, duplicates existing doctrine, and destroys legitimate value. Vote CON.
