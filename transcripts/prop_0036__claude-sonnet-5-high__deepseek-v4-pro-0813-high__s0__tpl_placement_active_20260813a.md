[P1.1] Wearable data is not like a blood test you take once and forget. It is a continuous stream of your heart rate, your sleep, your steps, your location patterns, even your menstrual cycle, flowing to a company whose core incentive is to price risk against you. Once insurers can use that stream freely, "consent" stops being real consent and becomes a condition of affordable coverage. That is the harm this proposition targets, and it is why the opt-in-with-strict-limits standard is the right line to draw.
[P1.2] Start with the coercion problem. When an insurer offers a discount for wearable data, the "choice" not to share is really a choice to pay more, or in the life insurance context, a choice to look suspicious. People with chronic illness, disability, or simply irregular sleep from night-shift work or childcare will systematically decline to share, or share unflattering data, and get punished for it. That is not voluntary opt-in; it is opt-out from a penalty.
[P1.3] Only strict, regulated limits on what can be used and how prevent this soft coercion from swallowing the whole market.
[P1.4] Second, wearable data is a terrible proxy for actuarial risk but a great tool for discrimination. A high resting heart rate might mean disease, or it might mean anxiety, caffeine, or a broken sensor. A gap in step data might mean a vacation, an injury, or a phone left at home. Insurers pricing on these noisy signals will systematically penalize poorer customers who cannot afford the newest, most accurate devices, and will penalize behaviors correlated with race, disability, and caregiving responsibilities that have nothing to do with true risk.
[P1.5] Third, the data doesn't stay put. Wearable data is uniquely intimate and uniquely leaky, sold to data brokers, subpoenaed in unrelated litigation, breached in hacks. Once an insurer's systems ingest it for pricing, it becomes a permanent, unerasable risk file attached to your body.

[C1.1] The proposition treats a voluntary discount as an assault on bodily data. But the real right being restricted here is the consumer’s right to use their own health information to prove they are a better risk than the actuarial table assumes.
[C1.2] The coercion charge misunderstands insurance pricing. A discount for sharing wearable data is not a penalty on those who decline; it is a lower price offered for information, like a safe-driver or non-smoker discount. The standard rate remains available. If every reduction for demonstrably lower risk is reframed as soft coercion, then the entire system of individual risk classification becomes impossible.
[C1.3] That does not protect consumers; it forces low-risk customers to subsidize others.
[C1.4] The noise claim also does not justify a near-ban. Single wearable readings can be misleading, but insurers do not price a person on one Tuesday heart-rate spike. They use trends alongside existing medical, prescription, and risk data. If “strict limits” require separate pre-approval for new signals, the predictable result is that insurers will avoid wearable data entirely.
[C1.5] That hurts the very people who could benefit: a person with well-controlled hypertension, a recovering cardiac patient, or a night-shift worker with otherwise stable vitals can show continuous evidence of resilience instead of being reduced to a stale diagnosis.
[C1.6] Adverse selection matters even more for coverage. When low-risk people cannot voluntarily signal their health, community-rated or guaranteed-issue products become sicker on average, forcing prices up or making coverage scarcer. That is not a consumer victory.
[C1.7] Privacy is a real concern, but the solution is vigorous data-security and anti-discrimination enforcement, not a categorical restriction on a valuable underwriting tool. We can protect consumers without making them less able to prove they are healthy.
