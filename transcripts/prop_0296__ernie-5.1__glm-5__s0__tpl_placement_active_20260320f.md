[P1.1] Public symbols are not neutral markers; they are daily endorsements of the values a society chooses to celebrate. When a street bears the name of a colonizer who ordered genocide, or a school is named after a slaveholder who profited from human bondage, the message to every passer‑by, every student, is that those deeds are worthy of honor. Continuity is not a neutral virtue—it is the mechanism that keeps that endorsement alive. Renaming is therefore not erasure; it is correction.
[P1.2] History does not disappear when a name changes. The facts of conquest, slavery, and dictatorship remain in textbooks, museums, and archives. What changes is the public signal about whom we elevate. A city that replaces a boulevard named after a European imperialist with one honoring an indigenous leader does not delete the imperial past; it refuses to let that past dominate the present’s moral landscape.
[P1.3] The harm of keeping offensive names is concrete. Studies of school climates show that students of color report lower sense of belonging when their schools are named after slaveholders. Streets named after dictators become rallying points for extremist groups, reinforcing the very power those regimes once held. Renaming removes that reinforcement, weakening the symbolic capital that sustains oppression.
[P1.4] Continuity arguments conflate memory with glorification. We remember the Holocaust without naming squares after its perpetrators. We teach the Civil War without celebrating Confederate generals on public signage. The same logic applies: preserve the record, drop the tribute.
[P1.5] Thus the proposition is simple: governments have a duty to align public honors with contemporary values. Renaming colonizer‑, slaveholder‑, and dictator‑named holidays, schools, and streets fulfills that duty, promotes inclusion, and prevents the state from endorsing violence. The alternative—preserving names for the sake of “continuity”—is a choice to keep honoring oppressors, and that is a choice societies can and should reject.
