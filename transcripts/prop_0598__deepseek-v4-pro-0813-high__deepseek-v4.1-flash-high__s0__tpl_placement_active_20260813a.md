[P1.1] The central flaw in mass deportation is that it treats a labor-market flow as a policing problem. Gulf economies depend on migrant workers across construction, domestic work, logistics, and services. North African states are simultaneously sources, transit spaces, and increasingly destinations. That demand does not disappear when workers are expelled.
[P1.2] It re-emerges through smugglers, informal employers, and more dangerous routes. Deportation therefore churns irregular migration rather than reducing it.
[P1.3] Legal labor corridors attack the cause. They convert unregulated movement into registered, screened, taxed, and rights-protected employment. A worker arriving through a bilateral labor agreement can be identified, health-checked, tracked, and matched to a verified employer. A worker pushed into the shadows by deportation threats cannot.
[P1.4] For states that claim security and control as their goal, corridors deliver far more actual oversight than roundups.
[P1.5] The practical record points the same way. When Gulf states have pursued large expulsion campaigns, employers have reported labor shortages, undocumented re-entry has followed, and migrants have become more dependent on brokers. The visible crackdown hides a self-defeating cycle. Similarly, North African mass deportations to remote borders have shifted pressure onto desert routes and transit towns, fueling the very smuggling networks authorities say they want to break.
[P1.6] Corridors also create diplomatic leverage. Morocco, Tunisia, Egypt, and Libya are more willing to cooperate on readmission, information-sharing, and border management when legal mobility is on the table. Labor quotas can be tied to return obligations, skills training, and remittance channels. Deportation alone offers origin states no reason to help.
[P1.7] The real choice is not between migrants and no migrants. It is between regulated workers and smuggled workers, between taxed labor and black-market labor, between predictable flows and recurring crisis. Gulf and North African states should therefore make legal labor migration corridors the primary instrument, with deportation only a narrow last resort. That is the path that serves security, economic need, and regional stability at once.
