[P1.1] A person’s voice is part of their social identity: it carries reputation, relationships, and earning power. AI now lets a company copy that identity without hiring the person, compensating them, or even telling them. When the imitation is identifiable and the distribution is commercial, the correct default is simple: ask first.
[P1.2] The first reason is autonomy against appropriation. A media company should not be able to make an actor appear to endorse a product, make a singer perform material they rejected, or replace a voice worker with a synthetic replica trained on their performances. The company is not merely discussing that person; it is using the recognizable product of their identity as a commercial input. Consent preserves the individual’s power to set boundaries and negotiate payment rather than forcing them to chase profits already made from their likeness.
[P1.3] Second, voice clones impose unusually severe reputational risks. Hearing is instinctively persuasive. A clone can place obscene, political, defamatory, or fraudulent words in someone’s mouth with emotional realism that plain text lacks. Disclaimers are inadequate: clips are detached from labels, reposted, edited, and remembered without context.
[P1.4] Once distributed at scale, correction cannot fully restore trust. Requiring consent prevents the harm upstream, where platforms and media companies can actually control it.
[P1.5] Third, affirmative consent assigns responsibility to the cheapest risk avoider. Commercial distributors know what model they are deploying, whose voice it resembles, and how they plan to monetize it. The imitated individual cannot monitor every platform continuously or prove harm after countless copies spread. An opt-out regime therefore rewards speed and imposes enforcement costs on the victim.
[P1.6] An opt-in rule makes businesses internalize the cost of permission before exploiting identity.
[P1.7] This does not ban AI voices, criticism, reporting, or creativity. Companies remain free to use non-identifiable synthetic voices, willing performers, or licensed clones. What they lose is only the claimed entitlement to monetize a specific human identity without permission. Innovation does not require that entitlement; exploitation does.
[P1.8] When profit depends on sounding recognizably like someone, that someone must get the first say.
