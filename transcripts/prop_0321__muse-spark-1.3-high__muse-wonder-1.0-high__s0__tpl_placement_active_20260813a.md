[P1.1] A musician posting tracks to find listeners has not agreed to supply free training data for a company selling music on demand. A photographer building a portfolio has not agreed to teach a model to clone her style. Public means visible, not ownerless.
[P1.2] Platforms profit enormously while creators absorb the harm. Commercial image and text generators directly substitute for the freelancers, illustrators, writers, and coders whose work trained them. That is not inspiration; it is replacement at scale. Without a default right to refuse, creators are forced to subsidize their own competitors.
[P1.3] The burden question decides this. Opponents will say creators can opt out or set permissions. That reverses fairness. The party taking value for a new commercial purpose should get permission, not impose a scavenger hunt of toggles, forms, and legal fine print on millions of individuals.
[P1.4] Strong defaults protect those with least leverage: small creators without lawyers or bargaining power.
[P1.5] The old excuse that public work is fair game confuses viewing with manufacturing. Search indexing points people toward creators. Model training turns creators into interchangeable inputs, then competes against them with cheaper copies. Different use, different harm, different consent required.
[P1.6] This still allows AI progress, just on legitimate terms. Platforms can ask, license, pay, or train on public-domain, licensed, and synthetic data. Markets for stock photos, music libraries, and freelance writing already show licensing works. Innovation does not require a right to take without asking.
[P1.7] Default refusal is efficient: one clear rule instead of millions of futile takedowns.
[P1.8] If we want a living internet rather than a strip-mined one, creators must control commercial training use by default. Otherwise sharing becomes self-harm, and rational creators will lock work away, watermark it, or quit. A stronger right to say no is what keeps creation sustainable.

[C1.1] Publishing publicly online is a bargain, not an ambush. Creators upload for free hosting, global distribution, search discovery, and an audience they could never build alone. In return, platforms have long indexed, recommended, and analyzed that work to make the service function. Training is another analysis: a model learns statistical patterns — what chords resolve, what a caption describes, how code solves a problem — rather than republishing the song, photo, or article itself.
[C1.2] A stronger default veto would give creators control not just over their expression, which copyright already protects, but over ideas, facts, and styles others learn from it. No human illustrator needs permission to study a public portfolio, absorb a style, and compete for commissions. No student needs a license to learn from public code before writing similar code. An opt-in right against machine learning therefore creates a monopoly broader than copyright ever granted, chilling the derivative creativity creators themselves depend on.
[C1.3] That veto will not mainly help small creators. It will help whoever owns a large, negotiable catalogue — major labels, stock libraries, media conglomerates — because only they can strike lucrative licensing deals. A startup, university lab, or open-source project cannot negotiate with millions of posters, reconcile conflicting permissions, or prove the provenance of every item in a web-scale dataset. Giants like Google and Microsoft can pay or retreat behind walled gardens.
[C1.4] The result is consolidation, with fewer competing tools for independent creators to use.
[C1.5] Narrower tools already address real harms: copyright enforcement when an output actually copies protected expression, opt-out signals for creators who object, and rules against impersonation or verbatim regurgitation. A default ban on training swaps those targeted remedies for legal uncertainty over almost all public culture. It makes the open web less learnable and raises the price of AI for translation, accessibility, editing, and discovery — leaving creators a right to refuse inside an ecosystem with less reach and fewer tools to be found.

If no one owns a chord resolution or a style, a veto triggered at training—before any output copies anything—is ownership of exactly those things. “Designed to replace me” also describes a cheaper illustrator who studied the same portfolios, or software letting non-artists skip hiring one. Copyright permits that painful competition; a training veto forbids it because the learner is a machine and too effective.
[C2.5] Licensing pools will not empower the small. A buyable pool must be cleared, warranted, and aggregated, so labels, stock libraries, and collecting societies become gatekeepers and capture most fees. Training then survives only on corporate catalogues and data giants can self-license from their own platforms—precisely today’s concentration, formalized.
[C2.6] Imperfect opt-outs argue for one enforceable, machine-readable standard, not affirmative permission that is impossible at web scale and therefore functions as a ban. Aim remedies at memorized copying, impersonation, and deceptive substitution. A default veto protects catalogue incumbents, starves open tools, and gives creators formal control over an internet that can no longer learn from what they chose to share.
