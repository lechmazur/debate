[C1.1] The proposition asks universities to become insurers of labor-market outcomes they do not control. A school can design curriculum, support completion, and disclose results honestly, but it cannot control a regional recession, employer demand, family obligations, student effort, or a graduate’s decision to take lower-paying public-service work. A repeated earnings-to-debt pattern is not automatically proof that a program “does not deliver.” It may show that a program enrolls first-generation students, rural applicants, or graduates entering teaching and social work. Liability would therefore punish institutions precisely for serving students most exposed to labor-market risk.
[C1.2] The warranty analogy fails. A contractor controls the materials and workmanship of the building; a university does not control the price a local labor market later pays for its graduates. The relevant actors are different, and so is the causal chain.
[C1.3] The incentives also cut against the intended goal. If an institution bears default costs, the cheapest response is not always improvement. It may be to raise admission standards, shrink seats in risky-but-valuable fields, or stop enrolling students with weaker preparation. The students most likely to default then lose access to the credential intended to improve their position.
[C1.4] Skin in the game can become incentive to cream-skim, especially when liability attaches to programs rather than to deceptive conduct.
[C1.5] There is a real problem worth addressing: inflated promises, weak completion support, and outright fraud. But those are failures of disclosure, academic quality, or honesty. They should be penalized directly. Using student defaults as a proxy for institutional fault converts a complex outcome into a fine that schools will pay by closing opportunity.
[C1.6] The state should police misrepresentation and require transparent outcome reporting; it should not make universities bear losses caused by markets, borrowers, and macroeconomics beyond their control.

[P2.1] The defense rests on a false binary: either universities control the labor market entirely, or they bear no responsibility for outcomes at all. The proposition occupies the sensible middle—"bear *part* of the cost" when poor results are *repeated*. Partial causation justifies partial liability. That is how risk-sharing works everywhere else.
[P2.2] Take the warranty point seriously and it collapses. Contractors don't control the weather, the soil under a foundation, or how a homeowner treats the building—yet warranty liability persists, because they control design, materials, and workmanship. Universities control tuition, program design, completion support, and career placement. Those are precisely the levers that shape debt-to-earnings outcomes.
[P2.3] The causal chain is imperfect in both cases; the incentive logic survives.
[P2.4] Now the cream-skimming objection, which is the strongest card CON holds. It proves too much. If liability attaches to a *program's repeated track record*, not to individual high-risk admits, then admitting one struggling student changes nothing. More importantly, the cheapest institutional response is not cutting seats—it is cutting *price*.
[P2.5] Debt-to-earnings ratios improve instantly when tuition falls. A school facing exposure can lower its sticker price, tighten time-to-degree, or invest in placement. CON assumes the only adjustable margin is which students get in. That is simply false.
[P2.6] The "just police fraud" alternative is weaker still. Misrepresentation cases are slow, litigated, and require proving intent to deceive. Meanwhile the common case is not fraud—it is an honestly marketed program that costs far more than it returns. Disclosure rules don't fix that; students cannot arbitrage their own futures against a data table.
[P2.7] Ongoing liability creates a standing incentive to price and design responsibly, without waiting for prosecutors.
[P2.8] Finally, the equity worry is inverted. Who is harmed when an overpriced program keeps enrolling first-generation students into unpayable debt? The students CON says he is protecting. Real equity means the institution shares the cost of a broken promise—not that access to a bad bargain counts as opportunity.
