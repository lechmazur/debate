A fake account on a music app is annoying; a fake account on a dating app can show up at your apartment, drug your drink, or spend months grooming you for money. Intimacy requires vulnerability, and vulnerability requires knowing the other person is who they claim to be.
[P1.2] Right now that basic assurance is missing. With a throwaway email, a VPN, and photos scraped from social media, anyone can spin up dozens of personas in an afternoon: a deployed soldier asking for gift cards, a charming divorcé who is actually married, a “22-year-old woman” who steers matches to a robbery. Platforms profit from user counts while real users pay the price in scams, stalking, sexual assault, and shattered trust. Telling people to “just video-chat first” outsources safety work to the most vulnerable person in the interaction.
[P1.3] Stronger verification flips that burden. Requiring an ID matched to a live selfie, a verified phone number, and ongoing fraud signals like duplicate faces, mass messaging, and device switching does not guarantee sainthood, but it destroys scale and deniability. A scammer can burn ten fake emails; burning ten real identities is far harder, riskier, and more traceable for police.
[P1.4] And the supposed cost is wildly overstated. Verification is to the company, not exposure to every match. You can still go by “Alex, 29” publicly while the platform quietly knows you are real. A few extra minutes at sign-up is trivial next to the hours users already spend swiping, chatting, and preparing for dates.
[P1.5] In fact friction helps: it repels bots, tourists, and abusers while reassuring honest people — especially women and younger users — that the pool is real.
[P1.6] When the product is access to real human bodies, hearts, and bank accounts, fast and anonymous onboarding is negligence. Safety must come first.
