[P1.1] Online dating asks people to do something genuinely risky: meet an intimate stranger alone on the strength of a profile that stranger controls. The current system puts the burden of proof on the person most exposed to harm. Stronger identity verification and fraud checks reverse that: before a profile can reach others, the platform has to establish that there is a real, unique person behind it and that the account does not match known fraud patterns. That is the case to decide.
[P1.2] Consider what unverified anonymity buys the bad actor. Stolen photos support a convincing catfish. A romance scammer can run many profiles, move conversations off-platform, and vanish. A banned harasser can return with a new email.
[P1.3] An underage user can claim to be older. Verification by ID plus liveness checks, duplicate-account detection, and payment or device fraud signals does not eliminate these harms, but it raises the cost and creates a trail. Fraud checks also catch behavior that a single profile review misses: repeated accounts, reused payment instruments, mass messaging, inconsistent identity data.
[P1.4] The tradeoff is real but badly framed. Less anonymous does not mean publicly identified. A platform can verify privately, retain minimal data, and display only a badge. What the user loses is the ability to be an unaccountable stranger—which is exactly what victims need.
[P1.5] And slower sign-up? Minutes of friction versus months of a scam, or a dangerous first date, is not a close comparison. Friction is a feature when the product is access to other people.
[P1.6] Voluntary, ease-maximizing competition will undersupply this. A requirement levels the field. Perfect verification is impossible; that is not the test. The test is whether unverified strangers plus reactive reporting is acceptable.
[P1.7] It is not. Require stronger checks.
