[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a real platform-design failure for a shortage of parental authority. The self-harm feed described in [P1.1] demands safer ranking, effective moderation, age-appropriate defaults, and enforceable duties on companies. It does not establish that parents need still more power over children’s devices. A veto can feel decisive while leaving the underlying danger untouched.
[C1.2] First, blocking “recommendations” is a poor safety boundary. Harmful material can still arrive through search, subscriptions, messages, external links, or a web browser. Meanwhile, recommendation systems can also filter age-inappropriate material and help a child find accessible lessons, disability communities, or constructive interests. The relevant distinction is not algorithmic versus non-algorithmic; it is safe versus unsafe design.
[C1.3] A new switch risks substituting parental reassurance for platform responsibility.
[C1.4] Second, expanded parental power is not automatically expanded child welfare. Parents are usually protective, but they can also be controlling, uninformed, or abusive. An older teenager exploring mental-health support, sexual identity, or a different religion may urgently need information a parent would suppress. “Optional” controls do not answer this objection: the child, not the parent, bears the restriction and often has no practical way to contest it. The proposition contains no age threshold, duration limit, or escalating autonomy.
[C1.5] It therefore treats a six-year-old and a seventeen-year-old as equally subject to unilateral control.
[C1.6] Third, making such power effective across devices invites intrusive account linkage, age verification, monitoring, and persistent parent-child credentials. Those systems create privacy and security risks, especially where family relationships are hostile. Children can also migrate to less supervised services, leaving parents with a comforting dashboard and less actual visibility.
[C1.7] Parents can already use powerful blunt tools—removing apps, limiting access, and restricting devices. The hard problem is making children’s digital environments safer without giving either engagement-seeking companies or invariably trusted adults unaccountable control. The proposition chooses the latter. Child welfare is better served by binding safety obligations and progressively greater youth agency, not by mistaking another parental override for protection.
