[P1.1] Try to apply for a job, enroll a child in school, file taxes, book a medical appointment, or claim government benefits today without the internet. In most countries you cannot — or you can only do so slowly, expensively, and at the mercy of shrinking offline alternatives. When a resource becomes the mandatory gateway to the economy, education, healthcare, and citizenship itself, it has crossed the line from convenience to utility. Our case is simple: the law should catch up with that reality.
[P1.2] Three pillars.
[P1.3] First, the essential-gateway argument. Water and electricity are legally guaranteed not because they are pleasant but because modern life is impossible without them. Internet access now sits in that category. During the pandemic, children without broadband simply vanished from school; telehealth, remote work, and e-government have made connectivity the precondition for participating in society.
[P1.4] A right that gates all other rights deserves legal protection.
[P1.5] Second, markets alone predictably fail here. Rural areas, low-income neighborhoods, and remote regions are unprofitable to serve, so providers skip them — exactly the pattern we saw with electrification a century ago, which was only solved by universal service mandates. Legal enforceability is the crucial ingredient: voluntary targets and subsidies without obligations produce press releases, not connections. Countries like Finland, which recognized broadband as a legal entitlement over a decade ago, show this is administrable, not utopian.
[P1.6] Third, the economics favor us. Connectivity is infrastructure with massive positive spillovers — small-business growth, cheaper service delivery, rural revitalization. Governments already spend heavily maintaining parallel paper-based systems for the disconnected; universal access lets them retire that duplication. The cost of extending networks is real but one-time and modest next to decades of compounding returns.
[P1.7] Note what we need not defend: free internet, luxury speeds, or government-run ISPs. The motion asks whether access to high-quality service should be a legally enforceable guarantee — like water, delivered by various providers, but with no one lawfully left behind. It should.

[C1.1] The gateway argument proves too much. Food, shelter, and transport are also mandatory for modern participation, but we do not turn every essential need into a legally enforceable network utility. Water and electricity are stable, uniform inputs with clear delivery and safety standards; courts can know when service is absent. High-quality internet is layered and fast-moving: it depends on devices, latency, software, content, and user needs.
[C1.2] A justiciable right requires a stable definition. Without one, it becomes either an arbitrary minimum speed that legislatures must keep revising or an open-ended entitlement inviting endless litigation over network quality that no court is equipped to manage.
[C1.3] Legal enforceability is not a harmless add-on to subsidies. It creates a right to sue and shifts investment risk onto providers. In rural and low-income areas, liability-threatened universal service obligations can make expansion slower and more expensive, because firms build to the legal minimum rather than real demand, and smaller entrants avoid compliance exposure altogether. Unlike electrification, the cost is not a one-time grid extension; networks require continuous spectrum, backhaul, security, and capacity upgrades.
[C1.4] A rigid legal guarantee locks in a particular technology and price structure while satellite and mobile alternatives can reach remote users more cheaply without a water-style mandate.
[C1.5] The equity case is also upside down. The main obstacles to meaningful use are usually affordability, devices, electricity, and digital literacy, not the absence of a legal cause of action. A universal-service right can be captured by households that are already connected, while scarce public funds are spent on litigation-proof build-out instead of clean water, sanitation, or schools. If the goal is inclusion, subsidies, vouchers, public Wi-Fi, and infrastructure auctions are better targeted and more adaptable.
[C1.6] Treating high-quality internet as a legally enforceable water-like service is the wrong legal technology for a real but dynamic problem.
