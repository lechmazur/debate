[P1.1] Torture does not require physical blows; it requires the state to intentionally subject a person to conditions known to inflict severe, lasting mental agony. Solitary confinement beyond fifteen consecutive days meets that definition in full, and it must be abolished.
[P1.2] Human brains depend on social interaction and sensory variation to maintain a stable grip on reality. When a person is locked in a tiny cell for nearly twenty-four hours a day without meaningful human contact, the mind rapidly deteriorates. Within days, acute stress triggers insomnia, panic, and disorientation. Past fifteen consecutive days, that distress predictably transitions into chronic, often irreversible psychiatric damage: hallucinations, paranoia, severe self-harm, and the collapse of impulse control.
[P1.3] Because authorities know this neurological destruction is the foreseeable result of prolonged isolation, caging someone alone past fifteen days is not mere custody—it is psychological torture.
[P1.4] Abolishing solitary beyond fifteen days does not strip prisons of the ability to stop violence; it strips them of the habit of confusing physical separation with social deprivation. When an inmate assaults a guard or directs gang activity, the state must physically separate that person from the general population. For an immediate crisis, up to fifteen days is ample time to halt an active emergency, investigate, and secure the facility.
[P1.5] Beyond day fifteen, however, long-term safety requires secure separation, not sensory starvation. Prisons can house the most dangerous individuals in secure, high-supervision units that prevent physical attacks through reinforced barriers and small, tightly managed cohorts, while still providing daily out-of-cell time, rehabilitation, and meaningful human contact.
[P1.6] Indeed, prolonged solitary sabotages the very security it claims to serve. Taking volatile prisoners, destroying their emotional regulation and grip on reality, and eventually releasing them back into prison corridors or public streets does not contain violence; it incubates it. The state has every right to restrain dangerous bodies, but no right to systematically break human minds.
