[P1.1] A "deal" that expires in 09:47, then quietly resets; "only 2 rooms left" for a hotel with floors of empty rooms; "17 people are looking at this flight" while you are trying to check whether the dates work for childcare. None of that helps you choose better. It helps you choose faster, before doubt, comparison, or a second tab can intervene.
[P1.2] That is the practice that should not be the default in travel and delivery apps: countdown timers and scarcity prompts whose main function is to pressure impulsive spending.
[P1.3] First, this is manipulation, not information. Honest information answers a question: when does the kitchen actually close, when does check-in actually end, is this fare actually refundable? A pressure cue does something else. It exploits loss aversion — the fear of missing out feels larger than the benefit of thinking — under manufactured urgency.
[P1.4] On a phone screen, hungry, tired, or rushing through an airport, you are least able to verify the claim and most likely to pay for add-ons, insurance, priority fees, or a nonrefundable room you would not have taken five calm minutes later.
[P1.5] Second, the asymmetry is structural. The app knows whether scarcity is real, whether the timer will restart, and exactly which wording was tested to lift conversion. You know none of that. Travel purchases magnify the harm because they are expensive, time-locked, and often hard to reverse; delivery purchases magnify it through repetition, small rushed fees, tips, and minimums accumulating night after night.
[P1.6] The cost is not just money. It trains people to distrust every claim, including true ones.
[P1.7] A default matters because defaults stick. Saying users can ignore the banner or dig through settings is no answer when the entire interface is built to be acted on in seconds. Removing pressure as the baseline does not ban truthful deadlines stated plainly. It restores competition on price, quality, and fit instead of who can panic a user most effectively.

[C1.3] Faster is not dumber here; faster is often cheaper and more successful.
