[P1.1] The airline already knows everything a claim form asks. It knows which flight was late, why it was late, whether the delay was crew scheduling or maintenance rather than weather, and which passengers on that flight scanned into a connection they then missed. Automatic compensation isn't a new investigation; it's the airline acting on data it generated. The complaint process asks the person with the least information to reconstruct the records of the party with all of it.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the whole case. A missed connection means a night in an unfamiliar airport city, a rebooking queue, a lost workday. Then comes a form, a receipts requirement, and a queue measured in weeks. Friction isn't neutral.
[P1.3] Every extra step suppresses claims, and unclaimed entitlements quietly become revenue. A refund system that only pays the persistent is not a fairness system; it's a rationing mechanism with a customer-service logo.
[P1.4] Notice what CON must defend. Not that compensation is unowed — in much of the world it's already a legal duty. They must defend making people fight for money the airline concedes it owes. Their likely answer is cost and fares.

[P1.6] Automatic payment also fixes incentives. When a thin crew buffer or an over-tight connection bank generates predictable payouts instead of paperwork attrition, reliability becomes an operating cost rather than a free option. Default rules should sit with whoever controls the failure and holds the records. Set the default to pay, let the airline contest the rare error, and stop making passengers prove what the airline already knows.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on one sentence: "Controllable is a category they maintain themselves." That is exactly the problem. Automatic compensation does not remove the dispute. It moves the dispute into the airline's internal cause codes, where passengers cannot see it, contest it, or even know it happened.
[C1.2] Today, an operational code is a planning tool with no payout attached. Make it the trigger for mandatory payment and every code becomes a liability decision. Real delays are rarely clean. A morning thunderstorm slows an inbound aircraft, the crew then times out, and a spare crew is unavailable because staffing was thin.
[C1.3] Weather or crew scheduling? Under a claim process, a passenger can challenge that label and a regulator can review it. Under automation, the airline labels it once, silently, and the passenger never learns a claim existed. PRO promises to "let the airline contest the rare error," but the error that matters is the airline deciding there was nothing to contest.
[C1.4] Second, automation means standardized payouts, which fit nobody. The business traveler who lost a contract and the tourist who gained a hotel night get the same figure. The person with serious losses still has to file a claim, now while the airline argues the automatic payment settled the matter.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's incentive story runs backward. Make delays and missed connections automatically expensive, and airlines respond rationally: pad schedules so "on time" means slower, stop holding departures for connecting passengers, avoid tight connections on thin regional routes, and push disruptions into categories that don't trigger payment. Travelers get longer itineraries and fewer options, and pay for them through fares.
[C1.6] The real fix is narrower: enforce existing entitlements, impose deadlines on claims handling, require simple one-page digital filing, and penalize airlines that stonewall. That cuts friction while keeping a reviewable claim, without handing the airline the power to judge its own case.
