[P1.1] We already have an institution built for exactly this problem: what to do when we experiment on living neural tissue that might have a stake in what happens to it. It is called animal ethics review. The proposition is that when an organoid demonstrates learning, it enters that system.
[P1.2] Notice what the trigger is. It is not any clump of neurons in a dish. It is learning: tissue that changes its behavior in response to feedback. Researchers have already reported cultured human neurons adapting their activity to play a simple paddle game when given structured feedback.
[P1.3] Learning is exactly the kind of functional evidence that led us to extend protection to animals we cannot interview. Nobody proved an octopus or a zebrafish suffers. We saw capacities that made it a live possibility, and decided that a live possibility deserves a committee's attention.
[P1.4] Notice also what is triggered. It is not a ban but a review. Animal frameworks ask whether the experiment is necessary, whether something less capable could do the job, whether potential harm is minimized, and whether an independent body agrees. Those frameworks are already proportionate: a mouse protocol is not a primate protocol.
[P1.5] An organoid protocol would get scrutiny scaled to its evidence.
[P1.6] Without this, a learning organoid falls into a gap. Human-subjects review protects the cell donor, not the tissue. Animal review does not apply, because an organoid is not an animal. So the only entity in the lab built from human neurons, and trained with reward and punishment signals, is the one nobody is tasked with thinking about.
[P1.7] The stakes are asymmetric. If we review and the organoids turn out to be inert, we have spent paperwork. If we do not review and they are not inert, we have industrialized something we cannot take back, on human neural tissue, at scale.
[P1.8] The opposition must defend a strange position: that demonstrated learning in human brain tissue warrants less oversight than a fish.
