[P1.1] Consent is a permission, not a moral laundry service. It can turn trespass into borrowing and theft into a gift, but it cannot turn every bargain into a fair or decent one. Ethics asks more than whether someone said yes; it asks what was traded, on what terms, under what leverage, and with what consequences.
[P1.2] Consider voluntary slavery. Imagine the contract is explained slowly, signed without threats, and paid for handsomely. On a consent-only view, the buyer thereafter acts ethically in owning another person. That conclusion refutes the view.
[P1.3] Some arrangements are wrong because they surrender the very status that makes consent meaningful — equal personhood and future autonomy. A yes that permanently deletes the ability to say no cannot, by itself, sanctify what follows.
[P1.4] Or take exploitation without coercion. A factory owner offers informed workers dangerous jobs for wages far below what the business can easily afford, knowing they will accept because the alternative is hunger. The workers may genuinely prefer the job to nothing and understand the risks. The deal can still be unethical because one side uses another’s desperate baseline to extract lopsided terms.
[P1.5] Mutual benefit is not fairness; a drowning person may consent to pay everything for a rope, but charging it is profiting from vulnerability, not respecting agency.
[P1.6] The same gap appears elsewhere. Paid duels, selling a kidney to a broker, selling a vote, or paying someone to take a criminal penalty can all involve competent, informed agreement. Yet they can create markets that pressure the poor to supply the risk, corrupt shared institutions, or impose costs beyond the pair who shook hands.
[P1.7] Consent therefore matters, often decisively, but it is one condition among others: minimally fair terms, no abuse of desperation, no surrender of basic rights, and no grave harm that agreement magically erases. A yes opens ethical evaluation; it does not end it.
