[P1.1] Sugar-sweetened beverages are not ordinary consumer goods. They deliver concentrated fructose without triggering satiety, driving obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease at epidemic rates. When governments tax these drinks at rates sufficient to shift behavior—roughly twenty percent or higher, as implemented in Mexico, the United Kingdom, and cities like Berkeley and Philadelphia—consumption drops measurably, particularly among heavy users and price-sensitive populations. This is not paternalistic meddling; it is correcting a market failure where the true costs of diabetes care, amputations, and lost productivity are externalized onto public health systems while beverage corporations pocket the profits.
[P1.2] The proposition explicitly accepts that the tax burden may fall disproportionately on lower-income households. That concession is honest, but it inverts the equity analysis. Lower-income communities suffer the highest rates of obesity-related disease and the least access to treatment. When a soda tax reduces consumption among the poor, it reduces their diabetes risk more than it reduces that of the wealthy.
[P1.3] The financial cost of the tax is immediate and visible; the health benefit is delayed but permanent and life-saving. To reject the tax because it costs poor families a few dollars a week is to condemn them to years of dialysis, blindness, and premature death.
[P1.4] Moreover, revenue from these taxes can be—and in successful implementations, is—recycled into progressive programs: subsidizing fresh produce in food deserts, funding school nutrition, or expanding community health coverage. This transforms a regressive instrument into a net transfer from wealthy beverage corporations to poor communities. The alternative is not a world without the tax; it is a world where the poor pay for their sugar addiction with their kidneys and their lives. Governments should tax sugar-sweetened beverages at rates high enough to meaningfully shift consumption, even if the burden falls on those who can least afford it—because they are precisely the people who stand to gain the most from escaping that addiction.

[C1.1] The proposition asks governments to impose a certain, immediate cost on the poorest households in exchange for a health benefit that is speculative, small, and unevenly delivered. That trade is the heart of this debate, and it fails.
[C1.2] First, look at who actually pays. A tax "high enough to meaningfully shift consumption" still leaves most drinkers drinking. Even the celebrated results PRO cites involve purchase declines of roughly a tenth to a quarter. Everyone else keeps buying and keeps paying.
[C1.3] And who keeps buying? The heaviest, most habitual consumers, the very people PRO calls addicted in [P1.4]. Addiction means inelastic demand. So the tax extracts the most money from precisely the people it fails to change.
[C1.4] A single mother who buys soda because it's cheap, shelf-stable, and safer-tasting than her tap water doesn't get a diabetes cure. She gets a smaller grocery budget.
[C1.5] Second, the health payoff PRO treats as settled in [P1.3] is not. Reduced soda purchases do not reliably become reduced weight or diabetes. People substitute: juice, sweets, untaxed sugary products, or cheaper soda from the next town over. Philadelphia's sales drop was partly people driving across city lines, which shifts the purchase but not the sugar.
[C1.6] A sales decline is not a health outcome, and PRO's case quietly treats them as identical.
[C1.7] Third, PRO's strongest example undercuts the motion. Britain's levy worked largely by pushing manufacturers to cut sugar from their recipes before the tax even bit. That is reformulation, aimed at corporations, not a high consumer price aimed at the poor. If the goal is less sugar in bodies, target the product, not the shopper's wallet.
[C1.8] Fourth, revenue recycling in [P1.4] is a promise, not part of the motion. Revenue often flows to general budgets or unrelated priorities, and even good programs don't return the money to the households who paid.
[C1.9] The motion demands we accept a guaranteed burden on the poor for a contested benefit, when less regressive tools exist. That should be rejected.

A sales decline is not automatically a health outcome, but when the displaced calories are liquid sugar, the metabolic difference is substantial.
[P2.3] The UK example does not undercut the motion; it confirms it. Manufacturers reformulated precisely because the tax made sugar content expensive. That is the tax working through the supply side, not a reason to abandon the demand-side tool. Both channels reduce sugar in bodies.
