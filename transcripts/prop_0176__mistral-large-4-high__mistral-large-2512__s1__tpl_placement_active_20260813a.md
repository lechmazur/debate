[P1.1] Resource windfalls are finite; a nation's needs are not. When a developing country discovers oil or minerals, the choice is between consuming the wealth immediately or securing prosperity for generations after the wells run dry. This is fundamentally a question of intergenerational justice: do we treat the resource as ours alone, or as a trust held for children not yet born? Sovereign wealth funds answer correctly by design, converting ephemeral geology into enduring capital.
[P1.2] Direct cash distributions treat temporary bonanzas as permanent income. They flood limited-capacity economies with liquidity, triggering inflation and currency appreciation that crushes domestic manufacturing—the textbook Dutch disease. Citizens spend transfers on imports, stimulating foreign economies rather than building local industry. The windfall evaporates within months, leaving behind higher prices, eroded purchasing power, and zero productive assets for the next downturn.
[P1.3] Future generations inherit a depleted treasury and a hollowed-out economy.
[P1.4] Sovereign wealth funds invert this logic. They transform depleting natural capital into diversified financial capital generating perpetual returns. Norway's Government Pension Fund, seeded by petroleum revenues, now exceeds $1.5 trillion—wealth that will outlast the oil beneath the North Sea. When extraction ends, the fund continues paying dividends while preserving principal for grandchildren who never saw a drilling rig.
[P1.5] During recessions, the fund stabilizes budgets without austerity or emergency borrowing.
[P1.6] Institutionally, SWFs impose discipline that cash cannot. Direct distributions invite political capture—leaders buying votes with resource rents, fueling corruption and patronage networks that entrench poverty. Professionally managed funds, governed by transparent rules and global diversification, insulate wealth from electoral cycles and elite capture. Botswana's Pula Fund illustrates this: decades of diamond revenues saved and invested rather than squandered, funding schools and clinics while cushioning downturns that devastated neighbors reliant on spending sprees.
[P1.7] Cash offers a sugar rush; sovereign wealth offers lifetime security. For resource-rich developing countries, stewardship beats splurging every time.
