[P1.1] High-frequency trading has turned equity markets into a digital nervous system with no cortex: reflexive, ultra-fast, and prone to seizures. The 2010 Flash Crash erased a trillion dollars in market value in minutes—not because any real company had failed, but because algorithms fed on one another in a feedback loop no human could interrupt. That was not a one-off glitch; it was the logical outcome of a system that rewards speed above all else. When velocity becomes the only competitive advantage, stability becomes collateral damage.
[P1.2] A financial transaction tax targets this pathology with surgical precision. It imposes a microscopic cost on each trade, trivial for investors holding positions for months or even days, but lethal for strategies that depend on executing thousands of meaningless orders per second. The revenue is welcome but secondary; the real mechanism is structural friction. By forcing every transaction to carry a tiny economic weight, the tax filters out algorithmic noise—quote stuffing, momentum ignition, and spoofing—while leaving long-term capital formation and genuine market-making virtually untouched.
[P1.3] The activity that disappears is precisely the activity that adds no information to prices and no resilient depth to markets.
[P1.4] The proposition explicitly accepts that liquidity may fall slightly, and that concession is strategically astute, because the liquidity HFT provides is phantom liquidity. It floods in during calm periods and evaporates during stress, exactly when buffers matter most. A market with fewer trades but steadier prices serves ordinary borrowers, pensioners, and businesses far better than a hyperactive order book that detonates without warning. What replaces the churn is a quieter market where prices reflect economic fundamentals rather than microsecond races between server farms.
[P1.5] Ultimately, capital markets exist to allocate resources to productive enterprise, not to serve as an arena for technological rent-seeking. Slightly thinner markets that do not melt down are preferable to hyper-liquid casinos that require circuit breakers to prevent daily collapse. A modest transaction tax purchases systemic resilience at the cost of marginal volume. That is a bargain any prudent country should seize.

[C1.1] The proposition sells a fantasy: that a transaction tax is a scalpel when it is in fact a sledgehammer wrapped in a bandage. Three points establish why this motion should be rejected.
[C1.2] First, the tax is not surgical. A levy on every trade, no matter how small, falls on every participant in the market: pension funds rebalancing quarterly, insurance companies hedging long-dated liabilities, corporate buyback programs, and ordinary retail investors placing limit orders. The proposition calls the cost "microscopic," but the only participants for whom any cost is truly microscopic are precisely the deep-pocketed firms who can absorb it. The burden of compliance and the frictional drag on legitimate activity are real, while the supposed benefit—driving out HFT—is hypothetical and contestable.
[C1.3] Second, the word "slightly" in the proposition is doing extraordinary work. Sweden introduced a transaction tax in 1984 and abolished it in 1991 because trading volumes migrated abroad and domestic liquidity collapsed. When governments have tried this experiment, the result has not been slightly thinner markets but substantially crippled ones. The proposition asks us to accept harm on faith while promising benefit on theory.
[C1.4] Third, HFT is not purely destabilizing. High-frequency market makers narrow bid-ask spreads to fractions of a cent, reducing the cost of every trade for every investor, retail and institutional alike. They absorb inventory risk that traditional market makers will not, and they accelerate price correction when new information arrives. The 2010 Flash Crash was a serious event, but post-mortems identified specific abusive strategies that were already addressed through improved circuit breakers and limit-up/limit-down rules, not through taxation.
[C1.5] The proposition treats correlation between HFT and volatility as causation, when the more honest reading is that HFT reflects and amplifies shocks but is not their origin.
[C1.6] The motion asks countries to tax productive financial infrastructure to prevent problems that smarter regulation already addresses. That is a poor bargain.

[P2.2] Equating the two is like warning against surgical lasers by citing the trauma of amputation. A modern tax calibrated to hit millisecond churn—quote stuffing, layered spoofing, momentum ignition—does not send long-term capital abroad, because the asymmetry is the whole design.
[P2.3] Nor is the liquidity HFT provides the sturdy depth the opponent imagines. It is phantom liquidity: millimetres wide during calm, vanished under stress. Bid-ask spreads narrow on the surface, but the inventory risk is not absorbed; it is rotated away the instant volatility spikes. When algorithms retreat in unison, the market faces a liquidity mirage.
[P2.4] Traditional market makers stayed through storms because their economics required it; HFT firms have no such obligation. The Flash Crash was not solved by circuit breakers; they merely yanked the power cord after the seizure began. [P2.5] Finally, the opponent’s faith in “smarter regulation” ignores the structural problem. Rules against spoofing are perpetual whack-a-mole against firms whose entire competitive advantage is regulatory evasion. The proposition explicitly accepts slightly thinner volume because the activity displaced adds no resilient depth.
[P2.6] We are choosing between markets that serve productive enterprise and markets that serve technological rent-seeking. The modest friction proposed purchases precisely the systemic resilience that hyper-liquidity promises but fails to deliver.
