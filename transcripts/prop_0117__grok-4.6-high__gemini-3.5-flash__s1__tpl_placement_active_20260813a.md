[P1.1] The worst crimes do not require the worst tool. Life without parole already takes the offender off the street forever. Execution adds nothing the public actually needs and several things it cannot undo.
[P1.2] It adds error. After a bombing or a massacre, pressure to convict is enormous. Witnesses are terrified, intelligence is politicized, and confessions come cheap. We later learn that some "certain" defendants were the wrong people.
[P1.3] A living prisoner can be released. A corpse cannot. That risk is not a footnote for ordinary cases. It is highest when the crime is most hideous and the demand for finality is loudest.

[P1.5] For terrorism especially, the noose is a recruitment poster. It hands the movement a martyr, freezes a contested narrative, and lets the next cell say the state is just another killer with better paperwork. That is not strength. It is strategic self-harm.
[P1.6] Deservedness is not a policy. Many victims' families want death; many do not. The law cannot track grief. What it can do is refuse to make killing a public rite.
[P1.7] Once the state claims the right to execute the worst, it will define "worst" under panic, wartime statutes, and political need. Terrorism and mass murder are exactly the categories where that expansion happens.
[P1.8] Abolition is the harder discipline: punish without copying the crime. The state exists to interrupt private revenge, not to perfect it. If a country can hold a mass murderer until natural death, it has already demonstrated supremacy. It does not become more just by becoming lethal.
