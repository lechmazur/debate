[P1.1] The proposition draws a careful line: parents may intervene when there is a specific safety concern, but they should not impose continuous location tracking and private-message inspection as the ordinary condition of adolescence. A default rule treats every teenager as a suspect before any reason exists. The burden is therefore not to prove that monitoring can ever help; it is to justify constant surveillance of everyone.
[P1.2] That surveillance can undermine the very safety it promises. Teenagers who expect every movement and conversation to be inspected learn to evade: they leave phones behind, use alternate accounts, delete context, or stop confiding in parents. Monitoring then offers false reassurance while destroying the disclosure that parents need when something genuinely goes wrong. A teenager worried about bullying, sexuality, mental health, or a controlling partner may first seek confidential advice from a friend.
[P1.3] Knowing a parent is reading can silence that first request for help.
[P1.4] Privacy is also part of learning responsible independence. Adolescence is the period for practicing judgment while support remains nearby. If freedom arrives only at adulthood, young people have had no protected space to develop it. Private-message monitoring additionally invades the confidence of every friend who writes to the teenager; those third parties never agreed to place their vulnerable conversations before another household’s adults.
[P1.5] Parents have less intrusive tools: agreed check-ins, curfews, travel plans, emergency calls, voluntary location sharing for a late journey, and clear consequences for broken trust. Rules can become more restrictive when conduct supplies a reason. That model teaches accountability rather than obedience to an invisible watcher.
[P1.6] The comparison is decisive. Absent a concrete concern, the safety benefit of collecting every location and conversation is speculative and marginal, while the costs—chilled communication, weakened trust, distorted independence, and exposure of deeply personal information—are continuous and predictable. When danger becomes specific, proportionate and temporary monitoring may be justified.
[P1.7] But precaution without limits is not good parenting; it is surveillance replacing judgment.

[C1.5] Privacy for teenagers should expand as they demonstrate maturity, not arrive as an absolute shield at thirteen. [C2.6] What remains is the weighing. A chilled conversation is real, but gradual and reparable. Exploitation, a secret meeting, or a coerced image can be irreversible in an afternoon. Parents should not have to choose blindness until secrecy has already done its work.
