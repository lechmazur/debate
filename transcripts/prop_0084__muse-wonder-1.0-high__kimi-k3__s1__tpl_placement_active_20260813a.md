[P1.1] Dating apps ask strangers to trust each other with the most vulnerable parts of life: their bodies, homes, hearts, and bank accounts. Yet most let anyone create that trust with a stolen photo, a fake name, and a disposable email. That gap is not freedom. It is an invitation to fraud.
[P1.2] Stronger verification — a government ID or equivalent check matched to a live selfie, verified phone, age confirmation, and repeat screening against ban and fraud lists — changes the economics of abuse. A scammer can spin up a hundred profiles in an afternoon when each one is free and untraceable. Make each account tied to a real, hard-to-replace identity, and mass deception becomes expensive, detectable, and punishable. Bans finally stick because the person, not just the username, is excluded.
[P1.3] The harms at stake are not a bad date. Romance scams routinely groom victims for months before draining savings for a fake emergency. Catfish lure people to isolated meetings under false pretenses. Predators banned for assault or coercion re-register and find the next victim.
[P1.4] Teenagers lie about age and are matched with adults. In each case, anonymity does not protect ordinary intimacy; it shields the actor who plans to lie from the start.
[P1.5] Yes, sign-up would be slower and less anonymous. That is a worthwhile trade, and a modest one. Spending a few extra minutes proving you are real is trivial beside meeting a stranger in person. Privacy can be protected without preserving fake identities: verify privately, display only a first name or chosen name, store documents securely, and never show ID details to other users.
[P1.6] Users need to know not your legal name, but that the platform checked it.
[P1.7] If verification stays optional, bad actors choose the lax app, and responsible platforms are punished for adding friction. Require it for everyone, and dating apps meet the standard expected anywhere else strangers are matched for private, high-risk encounters.

[C1.2] First, verification checks identity, not intent. The worst harms described — coercive predators, catfish who groom victims for months — are mostly committed by people acting as themselves: repeat offenders never yet reported, first-time offenders with clean records, scammers abroad under their real names, beyond any jurisdiction a ban reaches. A real name does not stop a fake emergency. [P1.2] promises bans will "stick," but bans stick only to people already caught; verification waves the uncaught through with a badge.
[C1.3] Industrial scammers already buy stolen IDs and pre-verified accounts — friction rises slightly for them, completely for everyone else. Worse, the badge itself is the weapon: fraud depends on manufacturing trust, and verification manufactures it for free. Victims will wire money to a real name instead of a fake one.
[C1.4] Second, [P1.5]'s privacy promise — verify privately, store securely, show nothing — assumes every app, including the small ones, defends a database linking IDs to sexuality, photos, and messages forever: against breaches, subpoenas, rogue employees, and whoever buys the company. Dating data is uniquely blackmailable; past breaches of intimate platforms have already ended in extortion. This does not reduce risk; it concentrates it.
[C1.5] Third, mandatory ID excludes exactly those whom anonymity protects: closeted users, trans users whose documents out them, abuse survivors whose abuser controls the paperwork or is hunting for them, the undocumented. Anonymity is not the shield of liars — for many it is the only safe way to date at all.
[C1.6] Target fraud instead: behavioral detection, rapid bans, payment warnings, reverse image checks. Aim at scammers, not at everyone.

[P5.1] The question left is not whether verification reads intent. It is what happens the second time.
[P5.2] Under anonymity, every consequence resets. A report deletes a username, not a person. A ban costs four minutes. A catfish who burns twenty targets starts fresh on the twenty-first, and the platform cannot know they were the same man.
[P5.3] CON's tools do not escape this: devices are replaced, SIMs swapped, payment methods borrowed — each failure returns us to that reset.
[P5.4] Verified identity breaks the reset. One credible report can now link complaints, close the aliases, and make return require a new scarce credential, not a new email. That does not need the badge to lull anyone, and it does not need to catch a first offence to matter. It stops harm from being infinitely repeatable at zero cost.
[P5.5] Against that, CON offers a slower sign-up, less anonymity toward the platform — not toward matches, who still see only the name you choose — and hypothetical worst-case retention the requirement does not compel.
[P5.6] A few minutes and a private check are a finite price. A system where exclusion never excludes is paid for by every next victim. Choose the system where consequences last.
