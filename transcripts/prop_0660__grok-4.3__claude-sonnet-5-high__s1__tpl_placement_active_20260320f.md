[P1.1] Right-to-disconnect laws improve worker welfare by restoring reliable recovery time that knowledge work steadily removes. When employees cannot be reached after hours, chronic low-level stress drops because they no longer monitor inboxes during family time or sleep. Reduced stress preserves attention and decision quality over successive weeks instead of letting both degrade into burnout.
[P1.2] The same boundary raises net productivity rather than lowering it. Knowledge tasks depend on focused cognition that requires downtime to consolidate learning and generate insights. Without enforced disconnection, teams default to fragmented attention and deferred decisions, which lengthen project cycles and increase revision work. The law forces resolution inside working hours, which rewards precise scoping and reduces the hidden cost of after-hours patches that rarely survive review the next day.
[P1.3] A concrete case is the engineering team that stops pushing changes after 6 p.m. Code review quality rises because reviewers arrive rested, and production incidents fall because no one is merging fixes while tired. Turnover costs also decline once skilled staff stop leaving for roles that respect their evenings, cutting the repeated onboarding drag that subtracts from output in every replacement cycle.
[P1.4] Claims that unrestricted availability is essential therefore rest on a false trade-off. The measurable output lost to exhaustion and churn exceeds any short-term responsiveness gained by keeping workers tethered.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to weigh worker welfare gains against productivity losses, but it hides a deeper problem: right-to-disconnect laws often fail to improve welfare at all, while reliably degrading the flexibility that knowledge work depends on. That asymmetry is where this debate should center.
[C1.2] First, these laws attack the wrong variable. The source of burnout in knowledge work is workload, not the mere existence of a Slack notification at 8 p.m. A mandated blackout window does not remove a single deliverable from anyone's plate; it just compresses the same volume of tasks into a narrower window. Firms respond by tightening deadlines during business hours, increasing meeting density, or adding monitoring to ensure compliance.
[C1.3] Workers report the same stress, now with less control over when they experience it.
[C1.4] Second, disconnection laws destroy the autonomy that many knowledge workers value most. A parent who wants to log off at 3 p.m. for a school pickup and finish two emails after the kids are asleep is not a victim of exploitation; that flexibility is often the actual welfare gain of knowledge work. A blanket after-hours ban removes exactly this kind of self-directed scheduling and replaces it with a rigid, employer-enforced clock that suits nobody's actual life.
[C1.5] Third, knowledge industries are globally networked. A software team with engineers in Berlin, Bangalore, and Austin cannot simply switch off at 6 p.m. local time without breaking coordination across every other node. Firms facing rigid disconnect mandates in one jurisdiction respond rationally: shift headcount to jurisdictions without the mandate, or automate roles that required cross-timezone responsiveness. That is a direct loss of jobs and income, not a productivity footnote — and lost income is a welfare loss too, often a larger one than reduced evening emails.
[C1.6] So the real comparison is not "some stress relief versus some productivity friction." It is uncertain, possibly illusory welfare gains against concrete costs to autonomy, coordination, and employment. That comparison favors CON.

[P2.3] Global coordination claims also fail on their own terms. [P2.4] The decisive comparison is therefore between measurable recovery gains that sustain long-term contribution and the narrower coordination frictions that better internal processes can absorb.
