[P1.1] A supermarket promises next-day delivery. The picker who filled the order is not its employee; a platform calls the worker a partner, a temp agency calls them self-employed, and the multinational that sets the algorithms and productivity targets pays no employer pension or insurance. When that worker is injured or idle, the cost lands on the public. The EU should end that free ride.
[P1.2] Large companies use labour platforms and subcontracting chains because flexibility is valuable. But flexibility is not free. Its hidden price is borne by workers with fragmented entitlements and by stretched unemployment, health, and pension systems. Social contributions should follow control and benefit: the firm that captures the upside of flexible labour must help fund the security that makes it sustainable.
[P1.3] National rules cannot fix this. Supply chains cross borders: a lead firm in one member state sources workers through a platform in another and a payroll agent in a third. If only some countries act, contracts are re-routed. An EU-wide requirement closes the arbitrage and defends the social market economy inside the single market.
[P1.4] This is not a ban on outsourcing. Specialised subcontracting stays efficient and legal. The reform targets a distortion: outsourced labour looks cheap because the social bill is shifted onto the worker or the state. A supplementary contribution on large users of contingent labour reveals the true cost.
[P1.5] Firms will outsource for real efficiency, not to shed obligations; responsible employers stop being undercut by rivals using disposable labour.
[P1.6] Opponents will warn about cost and competitiveness. The deeper threat is work that no longer supports a life while welfare states hollow out. Administration is manageable: large firms already map supply chains for tax and due diligence, and a de minimis rule protects small platforms and genuinely independent professionals.
[P1.7] When flexible work becomes insecure work, who pays—the worker, the taxpayer, or the company that profits from it? The company.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes an indiscriminate surcharge for social justice. By forcing large companies to pay a higher share of social contributions whenever they use platforms or subcontracting chains, the European Union would not protect vulnerable workers. It would penalize legitimate economic specialization, disrupt national welfare systems, and ultimately inflict its costs on the very workers it seeks to shield.
[C1.2] First, consider economic incidence. Pro assumes large firms will simply absorb this levy. In reality, labor demand is elastic. Imposing a punitive surcharge on contracted labor incentivizes lead firms to cut procurement budgets, automate entry-level roles, or demand steep price discounts from suppliers.
[C1.3] That cost inevitably cascades downward to the end of the chain through lower piece rates, reduced hours, and cancelled contracts. Marginal workers who rely on flexible platform shifts will find fewer opportunities, pushing them into outright unemployment or the untaxed informal economy.
[C1.4] Second, the proposal wrongly treats all subcontracting as tax evasion. Subcontracting is not inherently an avoidance loophole; it is the engine of specialized productivity. An aerospace manufacturer contracting precision engineering, a clean-energy firm hiring specialized turbine installers, or an enterprise relying on logistics networks are dividing labor efficiently, not dodging duties. Penalizing this structure handcuffs European industry against global competitors who operate without artificial frictions, chilling investment and competitiveness across the single market.
[C1.5] Finally, the EU is the wrong vehicle for this blunt instrument. Under EU treaties, social security financing belongs strictly to Member State competence. An EU-wide surcharge overrides carefully balanced, distinct national systems—from Nordic collective bargaining frameworks to continental Bismarckian social funds. The EU has already acted directly against bogus self-employment through the Platform Work Directive, ensuring legal reclassification where actual control exists.
[C1.6] Replacing targeted legal classification with an arbitrary supply-chain tax creates administrative chaos while guaranteeing zero new social security for workers.
