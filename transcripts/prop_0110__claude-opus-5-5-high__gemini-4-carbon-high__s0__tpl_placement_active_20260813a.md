[P1.1] Start with the cleanest natural experiment in health policy: the United States. It runs the world's most prominent predominantly private insurance system, and it spends roughly twice per person what comparable wealthy democracies spend. If private insurance delivered better health per dollar, America should be lapping the field.
[P1.2] Instead, Americans live several years shorter than people in Canada, France, Japan, or the UK. They have higher infant mortality, higher maternal mortality, and more deaths from conditions that timely medical care should prevent. That last measure matters most: it isolates what the health system itself controls. The US doesn't just lose on value.
[P1.3] It loses on outcomes while paying the highest bill on earth.
[P1.4] This isn't a fluke. Three mechanisms explain it, and each favors public systems.
[P1.5] First, buying power. A single public payer negotiates for an entire nation. That is why the same insulin vial or MRI costs a fraction abroad of what it costs in America. Fragmented private insurers each negotiate alone, and providers and drug makers exploit that weakness.
[P1.6] Second, administrative waste. Private systems require armies of billing staff, prior-authorization fights, claim denials, and marketing, all multiplied across hundreds of plans. Even within America, public Medicare runs with far lower overhead than private insurers. Every dollar spent arguing over a claim is a dollar not spent treating a patient.
[P1.7] Third, coverage drives prevention. When everyone is covered, the diabetic gets her blood sugar managed in a clinic rather than her foot amputated in an emergency room. Private systems leave gaps: the uninsured, the underinsured, the person who skips the doctor because of a deductible. Untreated illness is both deadlier and more expensive.
[P1.8] CON will likely point to Switzerland or the Netherlands. But those systems are heavily regulated, universally mandated, and publicly subsidized, and they still spend more than most public systems for comparable results. CON may blame American diet or guns. But preventable deaths from treatable conditions don't come from guns.
[P1.9] The question is outcomes per dollar. On that question, the evidence is lopsided.
