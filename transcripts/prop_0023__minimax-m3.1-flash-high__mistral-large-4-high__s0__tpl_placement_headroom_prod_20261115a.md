[P1.1] Crypto trading platforms should not occupy a separate, lightly supervised category. The key question is not whether every token resembles a security; it is what the platform does with customers’ money, orders, and market access. A platform that matches buyers and sellers, holds customer assets, executes orders, converts dollars into crypto, and sets trading rules performs functions much closer to an exchange or broker-dealer than to an unregulated venue.
[P1.2] That follows from the risks platforms create. Exchanges determine which assets trade, how prices are formed, and how orders are prioritized. Custody failures, manipulation, insider dealing, and market fragmentation can injure customers and the wider market. Established securities and broker-dealer regulation addresses these risks through capital and liquidity standards, recordkeeping, disclosure, conflicts rules, customer-protection requirements, and anti-money-laundering controls.
[P1.3] A crypto-specific label cannot replace those tools merely because the instruments use digital ledgers.
[P1.4] The alternative is regulatory arbitrage. If firms can call themselves “digital asset platforms” while enjoying lighter obligations than comparably risky financial intermediaries, business will gravitate toward the weakest regime. Investors receive fewer disclosures and weaker recourse; regulators must investigate firms across a novel category instead of supervising recognizable market infrastructure. Users may see a license and assume protection that does not cover every activity or loss.
[P1.5] The proposition does not require treating every token as a security or every crypto activity as brokerage. It requires recognizing that most large trading platforms conduct functions warranting exchange- or broker-dealer-style supervision. Spot trading, order matching, custody, fiat on-ramps, and market access should trigger robust obligations even if some listed assets fall outside traditional security definitions. Where a platform provides only narrowly limited wallet or technology services, proportional treatment may be appropriate.
[P1.6] But the burden should be on novel classification to show that its risks are materially unlike those of exchanges and broker-dealers—not on established financial regulation to keep proving that crypto can fit elsewhere.

[C1.1] The proposition commits a category error by forcing novel market infrastructure into legal boxes designed for a different era. Securities exchanges and broker-dealers are defined by relationships of trust and discretion: brokers exercise judgment over client accounts, recommend securities, and owe suitability obligations. Most cryptocurrency trading platforms do none of this. They are software interfaces that match orders or provide custody without discretionary control.
[C1.2] Treating an automated matching engine as a "broker-dealer" is like regulating a search engine as a travel agent because both help people find prices.
[C1.3] The functional differences are not cosmetic. Crypto markets operate continuously, settle instantly on-chain, and often allow users to retain self-custody of assets. Traditional exchange rules assume intermediaries holding customer funds in omnibus accounts and trading during market hours. Applying capital requirements, net capital rules, and best-execution standards designed for traditional intermediaries to a non-custodial protocol is not just burdensome—it is conceptually incoherent.
[C1.4] There is no "broker" to impose suitability obligations on when the platform merely executes a user's explicit instruction.
[C1.5] Moreover, the proposition's logic leads to regulatory arbitrage in reverse. If U.S. platforms must register as broker-dealers, trading will migrate to offshore venues or decentralized protocols where no intermediary exists to regulate at all. The result is less investor protection, not more. A distinct asset category allows regulators to impose tailored custody, disclosure, and anti-fraud rules that actually fit the technology—such as proof-of-reserves and on-chain surveillance—without pretending every token is a security.
[C1.6] Finally, the word "most" sweeps in decentralized exchanges and peer-to-peer platforms that have no central operator capable of registration. A rule that cannot be complied with is not a rule; it is an invitation to ignore the law entirely. The correct approach is a dedicated framework for digital assets, not forcing them into securities law where they do not fit.

[C2.1] The opponent’s rebuttal treats a crypto platform as just a matching engine like a securities exchange [P2.1]. But the analogy skips the decisive difference: securities exchanges list securities—assets from issuers with ongoing disclosure duties. Crypto platforms overwhelmingly list tokens with no issuer, no financials, and no cash flows to analyze. Exchange regulation supervises markets in instruments whose value depends on issuer disclosure; applying it to thousands of unregistered tokens forces regulators to treat every asset as a potential security, the very uncertainty the proposition claims to solve.
[C2.2] The risks cited—manipulation, withdrawal freezes—are conduct risks of the platform, not the listed asset, and fit direct conduct rules better than a securities-market regime.
[C2.3] On best execution [P2.2], the opponent assumes the platform is a broker routing orders across competing venues. It is not; it is the venue itself, chosen by the user. Best-execution duties prevent brokers from sending orders where they are paid; they presuppose a duty to look beyond the broker’s own platform. A venue that executes at its own displayed price does not need the broker-dealer apparatus to be fair—it needs targeted rules against self-dealing and preferential execution, which a distinct digital-asset framework can impose.
[C2.4] The opponent says tailored tools can “supplement” exchange regulation [P2.4], but the motion is disjunctive: platforms should be exchanges rather than a distinct category. Once classified as an exchange or broker-dealer, the distinct category disappears, and crypto-specific tools become subordinate to a framework that misfits the assets. Worse, the opponent concedes that lighter supervision drives activity offshore [P2.4]; forcing registration will push trading to venues where no regulator can impose any rules. The real comparison is onshore tailored supervision versus offshore unregulated access—the first protects investors, the second merely relocates risk.
