[P1.1] A tourist city is not a museum with unlimited seats. It is a machine with a breaking point, and the whole question is what happens at the margin — the ten-thousandth visitor arriving on the same July afternoon at the same three blocks.
[P1.2] That marginal visitor is where the argument is won. The first thousand people in Venice's San Marco or Barcelona's Sagrada Família district generate rent, wages, and delight. The ten-thousandth generates a queue, a shoved pensioner, a pharmacy converted into a souvenir shop, and a photograph of a crowd. Crowding costs rise steeply and non-linearly, while the revenue per head stays flat or falls, because packed districts breed the lowest-margin tourism: day-trippers, cruise turnarounds, people who buy a magnet and leave.
[P1.3] Concentration doesn't just hurt residents; it degrades the product itself. Nobody has a good time being the ninth row of a scrum.
[P1.4] So spreading demand is not charity toward neglected neighborhoods. It is basic capacity management, the same logic behind off-peak train fares and staggered school holidays. Move a share of visitors into February and into the second and third districts, and you convert congestion costs into economic value: hotels that fill twelve months instead of four, staff on year-round contracts instead of seasonal churn, small restaurants outside the core that survive on visitors who actually sit down and spend.
[P1.5] And note the counterfactual my opponent must defend. The alternative to spreading is not calm — it is escalation. Cities that keep funneling everyone into the same core eventually reach for blunter instruments: entry fees, hard caps, short-let bans, residents voting to shut the tap. Amsterdam and Venice did not invent restriction out of ideology; they invented it because concentration made ordinary life unlivable.
[P1.6] Spreading is the option that keeps tourism welcome.
[P1.7] The honest choice is not "same districts" versus "empty districts." It is managed diffusion now, or backlash and hard caps later — after the neighborhoods have already hollowed out.

[C1.1] A tourist city is not a warehouse with empty aisles. The monuments, views, and streets people actually book flights for are fixed. The ten-thousandth visitor at San Marco is still going to San Marco; redirecting him to a quiet canal or a February weekday does not relocate the reason he came. It only pretends demand is a fluid that policy can pour.
[C1.2] Spreading is sold as capacity management. It is closer to conscription. Concentration localizes the mess and the tools: one district can take timed tickets, tourist police, night-noise rules, and hotel stock. Diffusion asks neighborhoods that never sold themselves as destinations to absorb short-lets, snack shops, and weekend crowds, while the famous blocks stay packed because no one travels to skip the famous blocks.
[C1.3] You have not lowered crowding costs. You have duplicated them. Clustering is also how the industry pays for itself: thick markets of guides, tables, and evening buses. Thin that out and you get worse service in two places instead of a working district in one.
[C1.4] Seasons are not a scheduling error. Weather, school calendars, and festivals pile people into the same months for reasons discounts cannot repeal. Off-peak marketing fills some rooms with lower-spend trips; it does not move July. The peak still breaks, and the city spends money manufacturing a second, thinner season.
[C1.5] The scare story is backlash if we refuse to spread. The reverse is more accurate. Residents revolt when tourism leaves the already-touristed zone and enters the staircase next door. Managed concentration — price the scarce sights, keep the trade inside the core — is what keeps the rest of the city livable.
[C1.6] Diffusion makes every neighborhood a stakeholder in shutting the tap. Do not export the overload. Contain it.
