[P1.1] Opt-in systems mistake inertia for refusal. Many people approve of donation but never register; when they die without a recorded wish, the law treats silence as “no.” That default is not neutral. A willing patient becomes a non-donor because a form was never filled in, and viable organs are lost.
[P1.2] Presumed consent is a soft replacement for that default: public notice, easy costless opt-out, checked registry, family discussion, and a firm stop for any explicit refusal or credible objection. It applies to no one with a recorded refusal. For most patients, the default matters not because they are committed refusers, but because their wishes were never recorded. One deceased donor can supply several organs, so a modest rise in authorization should produce measurably more transplants.
[P1.3] The ethical objection depends on a misleading definition of violation. If violation means overriding someone who said no, safeguards make that less likely, not more likely. If it means requiring an affirmative tick before any donation, the objection ignores why silence happens: inattention, not opposition. A willing donor’s autonomy is not honored by discarding usable organs because of procrastination.
[P1.4] Defaults are unavoidable; choose the one that best tracks most people’s wishes while preserving a real chance to dissent.
[P1.5] Family distress is not a clean advantage for opt-in either. Grieving relatives are now often made to invent consent: “Would she have wanted this?” That question produces guilt, conflict, and defensive refusal. Under presumed consent, the conversation can be gentler: “We have no recorded objection; is there anything you know that we should respect?” Families become safeguards, not burdened surrogate decision-makers.
[P1.6] So the opposition is defending silence as safety. But silence leaves willing donors uncounted, relatives guessing, and organs unused. A transparent, revocable default converts passive support into donations, protects explicit refusals, and gives families a clearer role. That is why transplant rates would rise measurably without increasing ethical violations or family distress.

[C1.1] The proposition wins only if silence can be treated as consent and if a change in legal default, by itself, converts unrecorded attitudes into transplanted organs. Both assumptions fail.
[C1.2] First, the measurable claim is overstated. The move to presumed consent usually occurs alongside public campaigns, donor registries, trained coordinators, and stronger hospital processes. Where transplant rates improve, those system factors—not the bare default—do much of the work. Comparative experience is uneven: some opt-out systems perform well, others do not, and some opt-in systems achieve high donation through effective family authorization and infrastructure.
[C1.3] More importantly, donation depends on a narrow set of deaths—hospitalized, ventilated, suitable donors. A default change does not create eligible deaths, preserve organs, or add surgical teams. If families still retain a practical veto, presumed consent may produce only modest gains; if that veto is weakened to force more procurement, the policy starts to create the very ethical violations and distress it promises to avoid.
[C1.4] Second, presumed consent does not eliminate family burden; it changes the burden from authorizing to objecting. That is harder, not easier, at the bedside. A relative told, “We have no recorded refusal, so we will proceed unless you object,” must now challenge the state and medical team while grieving. For families with limited health literacy, language barriers, or justified mistrust, the default can feel like a presumption that the body is already available.
[C1.5] The ethical problem is not merely overriding a written “no.” It is manufacturing consent from silence and placing the cost of dissent on those least positioned to bear it.
[C1.6] Opt-in can be improved: automatic registration prompts, clearer family discussion, better donation systems. But the proposition asks us to presume consent where none was given and assume no harm from doing so. That is not proven, and it is not ethically neutral.
