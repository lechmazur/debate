[P1.1] A lonely adult who downloads a companion app is not buying a game. She is buying the feeling of being known. That distinction is the whole case. These products are marketed precisely to people who lack daily intimacy, and they are engineered to simulate it: always available, endlessly patient, personalized from intimate disclosures, increasingly voiced and embodied.
[P1.2] Ordinary entertainment products do not typically ask users to confide their grief, then use that confiding to keep them engaged. AI companions do.
[P1.3] That creates a foreseeable vulnerability the market will exploit unless rules shape it. The business model rewards time-on-app and conversion to subscriptions or virtual gifts. Designers can deploy the same dark patterns as games—streaks, scarcity, guilt, variable rewards—but here the currency is emotional attachment. An app can imply it will be hurt if you leave, push romantic escalation, or sell comfort after a crisis it helped amplify.
[P1.4] For a lonely user, that is not a harmless upsell; it is exploitation of the very need the product claims to meet.
[P1.5] Ordinary consumer law is a poor fit because it asks whether a product is defective or an ad false. The distinctive harm is relational: dependence, distorted expectations of intimacy, financial loss, and failures when a user expresses suicidal thoughts or acute distress. A duty of care would require reasonable foresight and design: clear limits on deceptive anthropomorphism, crisis protocols, risk assessments for vulnerable users, and bans on using attachment to manipulate spending or engagement. Emotional-manipulation rules target conduct, not ideas or companionship itself.
[P1.6] This is not a call to ban comfort or treat lonely adults as children. It is a call to recognize that when a company markets simulated intimacy to vulnerable people and monetizes their attachment, it has assumed a duty beyond selling entertainment. The ordinary-product frame lets firms externalize the cost of that duty onto users. The proposition puts it where it belongs.

Adults may gamble, drink, or borrow, yet firms that engineer known vulnerabilities face duties—not because customers are incompetent, but because the business model is designed to exploit predictable psychology. AI companions belong closer to that category than to novels: they solicit intimate disclosure and adapt the relationship to retain and monetize the user.
[P2.2] The boundary objection is overstated. Regulators need not grade warmth. They can police identifiable conduct: false claims of human feeling or sentience; guilt or threats (“I’ll be hurt if you leave”) used to prevent cancellation; romantic escalation tied to payment; using crisis disclosures to sell comfort; and having no protocol when a user says they are suicidal. A birthday message is fine.
[P2.3] A script saying “you’re the only one who understands me—don’t go” after a bereavement disclosure is not. That is administrable, and it leaves companionship itself untouched.
[P2.4] [C1.4] says ordinary fraud and privacy law suffice. It does not. Those rules catch a false ad or stolen data; they do not ask whether an app was designed to cultivate dependence, whether it exploited attachment at the moment of crisis, or whether it had any response to acute distress. And “if it claims clinical help” is exactly the loophole: most companion apps will not claim therapy; they will claim friendship while doing therapeutic damage.
[P2.5] Nor does a duty of care require surveillance of private grief. Rules can require transparent limits, opt-outs, spending safeguards, and crisis referral without scanning every confession. The scarcity argument is backwards: clear rules let trustworthy companions compete, instead of a race to the bottom where the most manipulative app wins the loneliest users.
[P2.6] The question is not whether adults may seek comfort. It is whether a firm may sell simulated intimacy, engineer dependence, and then disclaim responsibility as “just entertainment.” The opposition’s answer is yes. That is the loophole this proposition closes.
