[P1.1] This cap is not a ban on investment, it is a guardrail against a known, observed failure mode. Across every region that has allowed unconstrained institutional buying of farmland, three things happen reliably. First, land prices decouple entirely from what working farmers can pay. A pension fund does not need this land to turn a profit growing food; they buy it as an inflation hedge, collateral, or a pure land appreciation play.
[P1.2] They will outbid every family farmer every single time, because they are bidding for an entirely different product. Second, absent an operating farmer with a long term stake, stewardship collapses. Institutional owners run maximum extraction: minimum tillage, no soil restoration, the single highest-yield cash crop planted year after year with no rotation, and short 12 month leases that give tenants zero reason to invest in the land’s long term health. This is not ideology.
[P1.3] This is the default incentive for an owner who will never set foot on the field. Third, when every farm around a small town is owned by a fund 300 miles away, local tax revenue leaks out, machinery dealers close, school enrolment drops, and the rural community ceases to function as a living place. This rule does not lock out institutional capital. It merely sets an upper limit on how much of the critical, finite supply of agricultural land any single class of non-operating owner can control.
[P1.4] That is the minimum guardrail required to ensure farmland first serves the purpose of feeding people, not just balancing distant investment portfolios.

[C1.1] The cap targets the wrong variable. Food-system resilience is not determined by the deed holder’s occupation; it is determined by water access, soil practices, lease duration, storage, and access to capital. If a government wants to stop short leases and soil mining, it can require or subsidize long leases, conservation rotations, cover cropping, and enforceable soil-quality standards. Those rules bind every owner equally.
[C1.2] A cap on institutional ownership does none of that; it simply removes one source of capital from the sector.
[C1.3] In fact, that capital is often what keeps farms viable through shocks. A family operator may be one bad drought or interest-rate spike from selling out. A diversified institutional owner can wait out a bad year and fund climate adaptation—drainage, irrigation, storage, fencing, or transition to higher-value crops—that a hand-to-mouth balance sheet cannot. A long-horizon investor also has reason to keep the asset productive: collapsing soil and water destroys the investment.
[C1.4] The claim that non-operating owners inevitably extract maximum yield and abandon stewardship assumes the only possible lease is a bad one. That is a regulatory failure, not an ownership fact.
[C1.5] The cap would also weaken rural communities by shrinking the market for farmland. Most farm families hold their wealth in land. When no child takes over, institutional buyers are often the only patient buyers left. Capping them depresses sale prices, strands retiring farmers, and pushes land toward developers or large industrial operators.
[C1.6] That does not protect the small town; it accelerates its decline.
[C1.7] The real guardrails are stewardship rules, lease standards, and rural infrastructure. The cap is a costly way to avoid regulating actual conduct.

[C2.5] PRO’s incentive story is also backwards. A long-horizon fund holding land as collateral has reason to preserve the asset; soil loss destroys the very value it bought as an inflation hedge. [C2.6] Finally, the young-farmer point fails. A cap does not provide down payments, operating credit, or leases.
