[P1.1] The subscription economy has turned inertia into a business model. Sign-up is frictionless: autofill card, one click, free trial with a countdown. Cancellation is a gauntlet: links hidden in account menus, mandatory chat with a bot, phone-only cancellation during business hours, and a final sequence of "are you sure?" offers. That asymmetry is not an accident.
[P1.2] It is how firms convert consumer forgetfulness and frustration into recurring revenue.
[P1.3] That is a serious welfare problem. A few dollars a month feels trivial individually, but it compounds across streaming, apps, news, fitness, cloud storage, and delivery. For families on tight budgets, zombie subscriptions can mean overdrafts, skipped bills, or less food. The harm falls hardest on people with less time, less energy, or less digital confidence: caregivers, disabled consumers, older adults, anyone dealing with illness or grief.
[P1.4] They are not failing to shop carefully; they are being outmatched by design.
[P1.5] The market cannot fix this on its own. Firms compete to acquire customers, but once a customer is enrolled, exit friction is pure profit. Shoppers cannot easily compare cancellation processes before they subscribe; the bad experience is discovered only after the money is gone. That is a classic consumer-protection failure, and it justifies a simple rule: cancellation must be as easy as sign-up.
[P1.6] Same channel, comparable steps, no new information, no forced retention maze. If you can subscribe in an app, you can cancel in the app. If sign-up took one confirmation, cancellation should not take five.
[P1.7] This does not ban retention offers, fraud checks, or reasonable verification. It only forbids making exit harder than entry. If firms claim friction protects impulsive consumers, the answer is obvious: do not let companies profit from the trap. Make sign-up as deliberate as cancellation, or make cancellation as easy as sign-up.
[P1.8] Regulators should choose the latter.

[C1.3] Upgrading ordinary hassle into a welfare emergency distracts regulators from fraud and price-fixing that actually destroy consumer surplus.
[C1.4] The deeper flaw is the remedy. "As easy as sign-up" mistakes equal clicks for fairness. [C1.5] Worse, firms facing a hard symmetry mandate have a predictable reply: make sign-up worse. [C1.6] The market already disciplines this. Awful cancellation processes generate reviews and reputational damage; app stores centralize subscription cancellation; card networks enable chargebacks. A rigid legal mandate also imposes compliance costs that hit small developers hardest, reducing exactly the competition that benefits consumers. The proportionate response is to ban genuine deception and require upfront disclosure of cancellation method—not to fetishize equal clicks.
[C1.7] If regulators confuse frustration with injury, they will adopt a rule that feels satisfying and quietly lowers welfare.
