Search-and-seizure law rests on centuries of common-law tradition and discrete factual questions: did an officer intrude on a specific space? Environmental harm is polycentric—it involves trade-offs among energy supply, employment, agriculture, technology, and public health that shift with scientific understanding and local conditions. There is no neutral judicial benchmark for "minimally clean."
[C1.2] Is it the air quality of pre-industrial society, of 1970, of Scandinavia today? Courts asked to answer that question aren't interpreting law; they're legislating environmental policy from the bench, without the fact-finding capacity, budget oversight, or democratic accountability to do it competently.
[C1.3] That indeterminacy is not a minor cost—it is the whole problem. A right that cannot be defined with reasonable precision cannot be enforced predictably. It becomes a vessel any litigant can pour their preferred policy into, and any judge can shrink or expand at will. That unpredictability doesn't protect vulnerable communities; it produces years of litigation over standing, causation, and remedy while the alleged harm continues, and it invites strategic lawsuits by well-resourced parties on all sides—industry challenging regulations as insufficiently protective one day, environmental groups challenging permits the next, with courts unable to referee coherently.
[C1.4] Contrast this with the institutions actually built for the job: environmental agencies with scientific staff, legislatures that can weigh costs and benefits transparently and revise standards as knowledge changes, and tort law that lets injured parties seek redress for concrete harms. These mechanisms are not perfect, but they are corrigible—voters and legislators can fix them. A constitutional right, once announced, is largely locked in, interpreted by unelected judges for decades, insulated from the democratic correction that environmental policy desperately needs as science evolves.
[C1.5] The town in PRO's example doesn't need a new constitutional doctrine—it needs a functioning regulatory agency and enforceable statutory duties, which don't require reinventing what courts are for.
