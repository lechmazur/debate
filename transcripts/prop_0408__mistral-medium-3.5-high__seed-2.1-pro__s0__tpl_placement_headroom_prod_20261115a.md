[P1.1] Every year, millions of young adults face financial decisions about loans, credit, and rent without the basic tools to navigate them. The result is a society where financial insecurity is common: two-thirds of Americans cannot cover a $500 emergency, credit card debt exceeds a trillion dollars, and predatory lending targets the least informed. Schools currently treat financial literacy as optional, leaving it to chance whether students learn to budget, understand compound interest, or file taxes. This is not merely an oversight but a systemic failure that deepens inequality, as those who most need these skills are least likely to receive them at home.
[P1.2] Requiring a dedicated financial literacy course corrects this by ensuring every graduate can read a loan agreement, compare insurance policies, and plan for retirement. These are not niche electives but foundational life skills that every citizen needs. The alternative guarantees that many will enter adulthood vulnerable to debt cycles and financial exploitation. The average college graduate carries over $30,000 in student loans, yet many do not understand the terms of repayment or how interest compounds over time.
[P1.3] A single misstep with credit can haunt someone for years, affecting their ability to buy a home or start a family. Schools already mandate math and history because they are essential; financial literacy is no less critical for modern life. States that have implemented mandatory financial education show measurable improvements in savings rates and credit scores among young adults. This is not about adding unnecessary curriculum but about equipping students with the knowledge to avoid preventable financial disasters and build stable, independent lives.
[P1.4] Without this requirement, we perpetuate a system where financial competence is a privilege of upbringing rather than a guaranteed outcome of education.

[C1.1] A mandatory graduation requirement for a standalone financial-literacy course sounds like an easy fix, but it is a box-checking exercise that trades real, tangible student learning for the illusion of solving financial precarity, while even worsening the very inequality pro claims to address. School curricula are zero-sum. There is no pool of unused class time waiting to be filled: a required semester-long course will mean cutting existing offerings, from extra writing and math support for struggling students, to career and technical education that teaches context-specific financial skills tied to actual jobs students will hold, to art and music programs that keep vulnerable kids engaged enough to graduate at all.
[C1.2] The vast majority of these mandated courses are delivered by teachers with no specialized finance training, using generic, out-of-touch curricula built for middle-class, stable households rather than the low-income students most at risk of predation. A rote, canned unit on retirement accounts and mortgage amortization is useless to a student navigating irregular gig work, public benefit cliffs, and local payday lenders, but that is exactly the material most off-the-shelf courses prioritize. Worse, these one-off classes often breed overconfidence: students leave believing they are financially savvy, making them more susceptible to polished scams and risky credit offers they think they understand.
[C1.3] Wealthier districts will hire qualified instructors and tailor material to their students, while underfunded schools will assign a study hall monitor to walk students through a decade-old online module, check the graduation box, and call it equity. The end result is not universal financial competence, but another shallow, hollow mandate that widens gaps between rich and poor districts while doing nothing to address the structural drivers of student debt, stagnant wages, and predatory lending that actually cause widespread financial ruin.
