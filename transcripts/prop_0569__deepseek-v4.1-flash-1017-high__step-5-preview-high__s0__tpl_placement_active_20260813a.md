[P1.1] AI chatbots are cheap, tireless, and fluent enough to feel like care. That is precisely why they cannot be the only clinician indefinitely. The proposition does not ban AI therapy; it imposes a legal tripwire: after a fixed number of sessions, the chatbot must hand the patient to a human professional. That single rule changes the default from “automated until complaint” to “human review before harm compounds.”
[P1.2] Mental-health intervention is not just sympathetic text. It requires risk assessment, differential attention, and accountability. A chatbot can miss escalating suicidality, reinforce disordered eating, or mistake trauma for treatment resistance—not because it is malicious, but because it is optimizing conversation, not safeguarding a person. One flawed model can do that at scale.
[P1.3] A fixed session cap guarantees that a human sees the trajectory while there is still time to intervene. A patient who is getting worse often cannot self-advocate; the system must trigger review.
[P1.4] The alternative—vague “clinically appropriate” oversight—is unenforceable. Vendors control the transcript, the metrics, and the definition of progress. A fixed number is auditable: either handoff happened or it did not. It is not a claim that every patient needs the same number of sessions; it is a safety checkpoint, like a mandatory inspection.
[P1.5] Regulators can set the number by risk, age, or condition. The point is that no chatbot gets to be the sole provider forever.
[P1.6] Opponents will say humans are scarce and handoff burdens access. But scarcity is an argument for triage, not for leaving vulnerable people alone with an unaccountable system. Handoff need not mean abandonment: a human can continue care, adjust the plan, or discharge back with oversight. The motion preserves AI’s reach while adding the one thing AI cannot supply on its own: a responsible person who can be wrong, corrected, and trusted to escalate.
[P1.7] Mental-health care should not have an infinite unsupervised trial period.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that the number of sessions is a meaningful clinical boundary. It is not. Mental-health care does not work like a vehicle inspection, where a fixed mileage triggers a mandatory check. A patient receiving structured cognitive-behavioral exercises through a chatbot may be finished after four sessions; another with complex trauma may need twenty, or none at all because the intervention is a self-help adjunct, not standalone therapy.
[C1.2] A fixed cap substitutes bureaucratic convenience for clinical judgment, forcing either premature termination or sham handoffs that satisfy the letter of the law while wasting scarce clinician time.
[C1.3] Worse, the rule creates a legal cliff. Once a vendor knows that session eight triggers mandatory human referral, the rational commercial choice is to avoid the cost and liability: either cut patients off before the threshold or degrade the service so it never qualifies as “mental-health intervention” in the first place. Patients who benefit from continuous, low-intensity support—people managing anxiety between therapy appointments, those in rural areas with no local clinician, or individuals avoiding stigma—lose access. The handoff mandate does not conjure a clinician into existence; it builds a bottleneck where help used to be.
[C1.4] PRO’s alternative of “clinically appropriate” oversight is dismissed as vague, but vagueness is not the enemy; misaligned incentives are. A fixed session cap is auditable, yes, but it audits the wrong variable. It checks whether a handoff occurred, not whether a patient deteriorated. Meanwhile, it floods human professionals with mandated referrals that are clinically unnecessary, diverting attention from patients in genuine crisis.
[C1.5] If the problem is that chatbots miss escalating suicidality, the solution is mandatory risk-triggered escalation and clear liability for missed warning signs—not a blanket session quota that treats the stable same as the unstable.
[C1.6] Mental-health intervention should be governed by patient need and clinical risk, not by a session counter.
