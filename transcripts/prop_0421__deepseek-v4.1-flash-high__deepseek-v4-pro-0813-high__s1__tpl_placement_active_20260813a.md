[P1.1] If a charge is mandatory, it is part of the price. The real question is whether a business may advertise one price and collect another. The proposition draws a simple line: mandatory service charges must appear before the final checkout screen, while the customer is still choosing and comparing.
[P1.2] Late disclosure works by exploiting commitment. A diner sees a $30 entree, mentally commits, chooses sides, then learns a 20% service charge makes it $36. The plate is not sent back; the fee is accepted. A fan sees a $75 ticket, enters a queue, selects seats, then a $22 service fee appears.
[P1.3] Abandoning now means losing the seats and starting over. A delivery customer sees $15 of food, then at payment finds a delivery fee, service fee, and small-order fee that push the total toward $28. These are mandatory charges. They are knowable earlier.
[P1.4] Hiding them is not service; it is leverage.
[P1.5] Transparency also makes competition work. Suppose one restaurant charges $18 plus a $3 mandatory service charge; another charges $21 with none. If the first hides the charge until payment, it falsely looks cheaper while customers are choosing. The same is true for ticketing and delivery: an all-in price before checkout lets people compare what they will actually pay.
[P1.6] Honest providers with lower total charges stop losing to providers with the loudest low subtotal and the quietest add-ons.
[P1.7] The objection is that customers can still cancel at payment. But a formal exit after selecting seats, entering an address, and investing time is not informed consent. The motion does not ban fees or demand billboard advertising. It sets a minimal timing rule: before final checkout.
[P1.8] Businesses already calculate these mandatory charges at payment; moving that disclosure earlier is clerical, not costly. Optional add-ons can wait. Mandatory charges are price, and price disclosed only after commitment is a trap. Vote PRO.

[C1.1] The checkout screen is not an afterthought; it is the point at which the total becomes binding. That matters because many mandatory charges cannot be responsibly disclosed earlier without misleading the customer. Delivery fees may depend on the delivery address, distance, order size, demand, and courier availability, none of which are fixed while someone is merely browsing. A restaurant's mandatory service charge may depend on party size or dine-in versus takeout.
[C1.2] Putting speculative fee figures on earlier screens would either be wrong or generic, and wrong earlier disclosure is worse than accurate disclosure at checkout.
[C1.3] The claim that pre-checkout clicks create commitment is overstated. A diner who sees a $30 entree and later sees $36 before paying has not been forced to accept anything; payment is the moment of acceptance. The same is true online: cart abandonment is normal. A ticket buyer can abandon the cart and search again; a delivery customer can delete items or close the app.
[C1.4] Time spent is inconvenience, not consent. The system already lets customers review and refuse the actual total before money changes hands.
[C1.5] There is also a cost to the proposed timing rule. Small restaurants and independent venues often use standard payment platforms. Requiring a new pre-checkout fee disclosure means redesigning purchase flows, making variable estimates, and risking compliance disputes over what counts as sufficiently early. That burden falls hardest on the least resourced sellers, while the consumer harm is modest and often self-correcting through reviews and repeat purchasing.
[C1.6] The real trade-off is between an accurate, itemized total at final checkout and premature fee clutter that may not reflect the final price. Accurate checkout disclosure preserves choice without imposing a rigid design mandate. The final checkout screen is not a trap; it is the last clean exit before payment. That is enough.
