[P1.1] Ecosystems are not museums of plants; they are systems with predators built in. Remove wolves, lynx, and other apex predators, and the system does not become peaceful—it becomes unbalanced. Deer and wild boar browse unchecked, young trees never reach the canopy, riverbanks lose shade and root structure, scavengers lose carrion, and mesopredators such as foxes and jackals expand. The classic Yellowstone example is debated in its details, but the broad mechanism is sound: predators change where and how long herbivores feed, and that can let willow, aspen, and other vegetation recover.
[P1.2] Lynx do similar work on roe deer across European forests. Restoring predators is not sentimental rewilding; it is repairing a missing regulatory layer.
[P1.3] The objection is real. Livestock losses hurt, and fear is not irrational. But the motion does not say ignore rural communities. It says their objection cannot be an absolute veto.
[P1.4] That distinction matters, because a veto means reintroduction never happens: some people will always object to wolves, just as some objected to national parks, clean-air rules, and flood defences. The answer is to make coexistence work—compensation for verified losses, livestock guardian dogs, electric fencing, night enclosures, herders, insurance pools, and targeted removal of repeat problem animals. These tools are imperfect, but they are real, and they are cheaper than the slow ecological bill of erosion, lost woodland regeneration, and declining biodiversity.
[P1.5] The counterfactual is not zero cost. It is cost shifted onto everyone else: degraded watersheds, poorer wildlife, fewer tourism and restoration jobs, and a countryside with less resilience to climate stress. Democratic countries routinely make decisions that override local objection when a broader public good is at stake. Apex predator reintroduction is exactly that kind of decision.
[P1.6] Consult, compensate, adapt—but do not let a local veto sentence ecosystems to permanent impoverishment.
