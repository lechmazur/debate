[P1.1] A daily cap is clean to count and bad to think with. Children are not mainly shaped by minutes of glow; they are shaped by what the media demands, what it displaces, who is present, and what happens after the screen is off. A rule built around “one hour” lumps together a video call with a grandparent, homework research, a shared game, and an isolated autoplay feed at bedtime. That is false precision: the number looks objective while ignoring the causes parents actually need to manage.
[P1.2] Quality changes the mechanism. Creation, learning, humor, and family contact can build competence or connection; compulsive scrolling, social comparison, age-inappropriate material, advertising pressure, harassment, and use that steals sleep can harm even inside a modest cap. Social context changes it again. The same chat can be ordinary friendship with a parent nearby, or a pile-on at midnight that a child has no help processing.
[P1.3] The device is the same; the developmental situation is not.
[P1.4] Cap-first advice therefore fails in both directions. It penalizes the child who spends longer making music, reading accessible text, or collaborating on homework, yet absolves the child who stays under the limit for hostile messages, disturbing videos, or secretive nighttime use. It also produces the wrong household ritual: bargaining over minutes, watching clocks, hiding use, and mistaking compliance for self-regulation. A timer can end a session; it cannot teach why the session mattered.
[P1.5] A cap belongs downstream of better questions: What is the child doing, why, with whom, when, under what design pressures, and with what adult conversation afterward? Those questions generate practical guidance on co-use, sleep, privacy, age fit, emotional state, alternatives, and repair after mistakes. They are harder than a number, which is why parenting advice should dwell on them. Make media time worth something, and make its social world safe; then let the cap follow.
