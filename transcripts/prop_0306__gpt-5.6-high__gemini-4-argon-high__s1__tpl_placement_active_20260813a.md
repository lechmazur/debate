[P1.1] Ethical justification requires necessity and proportionality. Zoos deliberately confine sentient animals, often restricting roaming, hunting, migration, social choice, and escape from public exposure. Better enclosures may reduce these harms, but cannot remove the defining deprivation: animals exist inside human-designed boundaries for human purposes. Once conservation and education can be achieved effectively without that captivity, the traditional zoo loses its moral license.
[P1.2] For conservation, the decisive question is not whether zoos sometimes help, but whether maintaining broad public collections is the best use of limited money, expertise, and political attention. Species decline is driven chiefly by habitat destruction, climate disruption, pollution, invasive species, and hunting. Protecting habitat addresses those causes while preserving entire ecological communities, including unglamorous species no zoo exhibits.
[P1.3] By contrast, many zoo animals are not realistic candidates for release: they may lack survival skills, genetic suitability, secure habitat, or freedom from the original threat. Display collections therefore too often preserve specimens while the ecosystems that make wild populations possible continue disappearing.
[P1.4] None of this rules out genuine sanctuaries, veterinary rescue, or narrowly targeted captive-breeding programs when extinction is otherwise imminent. But those are exceptional interventions governed by animal and species needs—not a justification for permanently exhibiting elephants, big cats, primates, and other crowd-drawing animals. A rescue center need not become an entertainment venue, and an emergency ark does not justify the whole fleet.
[P1.5] Education likewise no longer requires cages. Immersive video, virtual reality, live remote cameras, interactive simulations, and field-based learning can show animals hunting, migrating, raising young, and shaping ecosystems—behaviors that enclosures often distort or suppress. These tools are repeatable, globally accessible, and can connect wonder directly to threats and solutions. Seeing a tiger nearby may create excitement, but proximity is not understanding; it can instead teach that wildlife is available for our consumption.
[P1.6] The ethical comparison is therefore clear: habitat protection conserves more directly, digital tools teach with richer context, and both avoid routine captivity. When less harmful means can perform the claimed functions, continuing the harmful institution is no longer justified.
