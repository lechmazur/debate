[P1.1] The motion concedes mixed academic evidence, so let's be clear about what that means: it is not a reason to freeze a policy that serves families on dimensions test scores never capture.
[P1.2] A child whose local school is unsafe, bullying-ridden, or simply the wrong fit is not comforted by a regression showing marginal reading gains across a population. Vouchers give families exit options that affluent families already exercise through moving or paying tuition. Denying that exit to lower-income families—while their wealthier peers shop freely—is an equity problem, not just an efficiency one. The status quo is already a voucher system; it just routes the vouchers through mortgages and ZIP codes.
[P1.3] The "public schools lose resources" objection inverts the proper relationship between schools and students. Per-pupil funding follows the child. When a student leaves, the school loses revenue but also loses the obligation to educate that student. The question is not whether the institution is harmed but whether the child is better served.
[P1.4] We do not trap children in mismatched schools to keep buildings fully staffed.
[P1.5] Mixed evidence is also not no evidence. Voucher programs vary enormously—in eligibility, funding levels, and regulatory burden. Variation in outcomes is itself information, not a verdict. Halting expansion because some programs show null effects while others show gains is like abandoning medical research because one trial failed.
[P1.6] The rational response to mixed evidence is iteration, not paralysis.
[P1.7] Educational pluralism has value independent of test scores. A religious family seeking faith-based formation, a parent of a neurodivergent child needing a specialized environment, a student thriving in a smaller setting—these are real goods that standard metrics do not measure but families immediately recognize.
[P1.8] States should expand vouchers because the decisive question is not whether average scores move by a fraction of a standard deviation. It is whether families gain real power to choose, and whether children trapped in schools that fail them gain a way out. On both counts, expansion wins.
