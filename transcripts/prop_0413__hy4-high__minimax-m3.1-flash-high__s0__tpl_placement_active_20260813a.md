[P1.1] An adoption record is not a stranger’s diary; it is the state’s file about one person’s birth, parentage, and legal origin. Once that person is an adult, the state should not withhold from the person most affected what officials and biological parents already know. Sealed records grew out of an era that treated illegitimacy, infertility, and surrender as shameful secrets. That shame belongs to history, not to the adopted adult’s future.
[P1.2] The right must include identifying information, because a name is often what makes the rest usable. A non-identifying medical summary can leave a thirty-year-old guessing about heart symptoms, pregnancy risks, cancer patterns, or inherited conditions. A name opens the way to updated family history, identification of genetic relatives, and the answers a doctor’s intake form routinely asks for. Making this a legal right rather than an agency favor matters: adults should not have to prove a good reason to know their own origins.
[P1.3] The privacy objection confuses knowledge with contact. A birth parent may have an interest in avoiding intrusion, but not in controlling another adult’s identity or medical reasoning. Access to a name and record does not impose a duty to call, visit, forgive, support, or form a relationship. Harassment rules, no-contact notices, counseling, and careful release protect peace after disclosure; a permanent gag order is broader and less defensible.
[P1.4] It makes ignorance the price of someone else’s comfort.
[P1.5] Old promises of anonymity do not cure that defect. Officials promised secrecy about a person who never consented and was not an adult party to the bargain. Voluntary registries and mutual-consent schemes repeat the injustice by leaving self-knowledge dependent on another person’s permission. Adulthood is the fair line: childhood stability is protected, lifelong wardship is not.
[P1.6] The law should give adopted adults the equal status other adults take for granted: to know who they are, then decide for themselves what to do with that knowledge.

[C1.1] The right to access adoption records and the right to identify biological parents are not the same. Adults should be able to obtain information about their own origins, but that does not make another person’s identity the adopted adult’s property. A state file may be about the adoption, while a birth parent’s name remains personal information belonging partly to that parent and to relatives who never consented to disclosure.
[C1.2] The absolute right proposed here would override confidentiality on the basis of the adoptee’s desire alone. That is especially troubling where disclosure could expose a vulnerable birth parent, intended parents, or a donor to unwanted contact, harassment, or publicity. “They can simply refuse contact” does not answer those harms. The people exposed by disclosure may never have chosen adoption, may have surrendered a child under pressure, or may have no obligation to become a stranger’s family. Their silence should not automatically count as consent to identification.
[C1.3] The medical argument proves less than the opponent claims. A medical summary, family-history information, genetic testing, and access to relatives’ voluntarily shared histories can provide substantial benefits without requiring a name. The state can also use a review process to order identifying information disclosed when there is a compelling need, such as a serious genetic emergency, while allowing an interested biological parent to object in appropriate cases.
[C1.4] Anonymity and consent are not simply relics of shame. In some adoptions, they are what made the original arrangement safe and lawful. The proposition is also too broad: it requires no inquiry into circumstances, consent, or consequences. A legal right should not mean that every adult adoptee, regardless of motive, is entitled to the identifying information of every biological parent.
[C1.5] The defensible principle is meaningful adult access to one’s origins, with privacy protections and individualized exceptions—not an unconditional entitlement to identify third parties. The motion should not make biological parents’ safety and consent merely collateral considerations in the adopted adult’s quest for information.
