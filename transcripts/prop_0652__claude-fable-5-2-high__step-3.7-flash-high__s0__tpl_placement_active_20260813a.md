[C1.1] This motion misplaces liability on the party whose compliance would censor far more legitimate expression than it stops actual infringement, while letting the intentional wrongdoers escape accountability. First, PRO’s opening example of a “Mickey Mouse fighting Batman” prompt is not infringement at all—it is transformative parody, the exact kind of expression copyright law is designed to protect. Infringement only occurs when a user intentionally prompts for protected material: a request to output the full text of a copyrighted novel, a direct copy of a living artist’s signature style for commercial use, or a verbatim replication of a copyrighted song.
[C1.2] The platform’s generative model is a neutral tool, no different from a word processor: if I use Microsoft Word to type out a pirated book, we do not sue Microsoft. The user is the sole party who knows their request is infringing, and the sole party who chose to break the law.
[C1.3] Second, shared liability would force platforms to deploy pre-publication copyright filters that are systematically overbroad, censoring vast swathes of legitimate expression to avoid lawsuits. We already see this with content classifiers that block prompts for “nude Renaissance sculpture” or “historical war photography” because they cannot distinguish art from explicit content; copyright filters would be far worse, blocking transformative fanfiction, fair use commentary, parody, and original work that accidentally shares surface similarities with protected material. The cost of this censorship would fall almost entirely on marginalized creators, educators, and small users who cannot afford to fight false takedowns, while actual pirates would simply move to unmoderated platforms.
[C1.4] Third, PRO’s claim that platforms can prevent harm “most cheaply” is backwards. Catching intentional infringement requires parsing the intent behind millions of prompts a day, a far heavier lift than holding the deliberate wrongdoer accountable. The existing DMCA takedown system already lets rightsholders remove infringing content quickly without preemptive censorship, and there is no evidence that adding platform liability would improve outcomes for creators—only that it would strangle access to generative AI for lawful users.
