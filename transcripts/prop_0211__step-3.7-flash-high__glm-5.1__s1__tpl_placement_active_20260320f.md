[P1.1] Broad reimbursement of preventive obesity drugs without accountability mechanisms would waste public funds and fail to deliver on the core promise of reducing long-term obesity-related disease burden. Tying coverage to outcome-based pricing or spending caps fixes this misalignment without restricting access for patients who benefit. GLP-1 receptor agonists, the leading class of preventive obesity drugs, cost upwards of $1,000 per month, with annual per-patient costs exceeding $13,000. Unrestricted broad reimbursement would impose unsustainable burdens on public health budgets: covering all eligible adults with obesity in the US alone could cost over $300 billion annually, diverting funds from essential services like pediatric care and cancer screening.
[P1.2] Clinical data exposes a critical value gap: only 60-70% of patients taking these drugs achieve clinically meaningful weight loss (5% or more of body weight), and long-term adherence drops sharply after 12 months. Without accountability measures, health systems pay full price for drugs that deliver no benefit to nearly a third of patients. Outcome-based pricing and spending caps solve both problems. Outcome-based deals, like the recent agreement between Novo Nordisk and the UK’s NHS for Wegovy, tie reimbursement to pre-specified weight loss or cardiometabolic targets, ensuring public funds only go to patients who benefit.
[P1.3] These deals also incentivize manufacturers to invest in patient support programs to improve real-world adherence and effectiveness. Spending caps add an extra layer of budget protection, capping total per-patient or system-wide costs to avoid unexpected overruns from high uptake. Opponents will claim these guardrails limit access, but the opposite is true: outcome-based pricing expands coverage by giving health systems confidence to approve drugs they would otherwise reject due to cost uncertainty. This model ensures broad, sustainable reimbursement for obesity drugs that deliver real clinical value, without burdening taxpayers or siphoning funds from other critical health priorities.

[C1.2] Consider the practical sequence. A health system says: "We will cover this broadly, but only if the manufacturer agrees to our terms." If the manufacturer refuses—perhaps because tracking patient-level outcomes costs more than the rebate, or because they believe in their drug's value and won't accept caps—then broad reimbursement simply doesn't happen. Patients who need preventive treatment wait indefinitely while negotiations drag.
[C1.3] The manufacturer holds veto power over access, and the health system has no fallback mechanism.
[C1.4] Outcome-based pricing also introduces a clinical distortion PRO ignores. When reimbursement hinges on demonstrable outcomes, prescribers face pressure to select only "likely responders"—patients who are younger, more compliant, with fewer comorbidities. This systematically excludes the patients most burdened by obesity, who often have complex social and medical barriers to the "ideal" response. Preventive care that only reaches easy cases isn't prevention at all.
[C1.5] Spending caps carry their own cruelty. Once a cap is reached—whether per-patient or system-wide—what happens to the next patient who walks in? Rationing by queue position is not a health system principle anyone should endorse.
[C1.6] The alternative isn't unconditional blank-check reimbursement. Health systems already have tools: volume-based discounts, tiered formularies, reference pricing, and negotiations that don't hold patients hostage to manufacturer deal acceptance. The proposition demands one specific mechanism as a gatekeeper, and that conditionality is its weakness. When OBP negotiations stall—as they routinely do, with years-long delays in several European systems—patients pay the price.
[C1.7] Health systems should reimburse preventive obesity drugs broadly because they prevent disease. Cost mechanisms should follow, not gate, access.
