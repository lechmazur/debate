[P1.1] Governments should treat permanent metropolitan concentration as a policy failure, not a natural equilibrium. They already shape the economic map through transport spending, housing finance, public employment, university funding and infrastructure. When those choices repeatedly favour a handful of large cities, decline elsewhere looks inevitable only because it was built into the system.
[P1.2] Accepting that decline is expensive. The largest metros now ration opportunity by price: housing costs push key workers out, congestion wastes productive time, and stretched services impose a daily tax on families. At the same time, a hollowed-out small town is not a harmless loss. Food processing, energy maintenance, water systems, freight, logistics and emergency response all require distributed workers and skills.
[P1.3] A country cannot warehouse every essential activity in a few unaffordable corridors without becoming more fragile.
[P1.4] Doing more does not mean propping up dying industries or freezing towns in amber. It means building the modern platform for regional viability: reliable broadband, regional transport, community and technical colleges, primary care, child care, and serviced land. These are public goods. A town with fast internet, a working clinic, safe child care and a local college can turn lower land costs, shorter supply chains and renewable-energy sites into jobs.
[P1.5] Without those basics, even a strong local firm cannot recruit one new family.
[P1.6] The market will not supply this platform. The same market logic consolidates warehouses, clinics, bank branches and flights into hubs; it does not price the national cost of regional collapse. Remote and hybrid work has widened the possible map, but only where public services and connectivity are adequate.
[P1.7] The aim is not equal population across every village. The aim is to reject managed decline as the default. Governments should do more because the costs of collapse are national, the needed investments are public goods, and viable smaller regions are not nostalgia—they are infrastructure for a more resilient economy.

[C1.1] Prosperity concentrates for a reason, and punishing that concentration to subsidize dispersion makes whole countries poorer. Big metros generate disproportionate productivity, wages, tax revenue and innovation because dense labor markets match workers to jobs, suppliers to firms, and ideas to capital. That surplus is what pays for pensions, health systems and transfers to poorer areas. Deliberately diverting scarce funds away from where they earn the highest return shrinks the pie for everyone, including small towns.
[C1.2] The promise of just broadband, clinics and colleges sounds modest, but per-person costs explode where populations are thin and shrinking. A rail spur, hospital wing or technical program serving a few thousand costs multiples of the same service for hundreds of thousands, while struggling to recruit staff and students. The result is familiar: empty business parks, under-enrolled campuses, and firms that arrive for the grant and leave when it ends. Markets consolidate warehouses and flight networks into hubs not from malice but because duplication is wasteful.
[C1.3] Keeping places on life support also traps people. A young worker in a town with one major employer, few alternative jobs and a thin marriage and friendship market faces lower lifetime earnings and higher risk if that plant closes. Real fairness is people-based, not place-based: portable education, job search assistance, relocation support and income security that let residents move toward opportunity rather than wait for opportunity to be airlifted to them.
[C1.4] Metros are expensive not because they are too successful but because governments choke housing supply and transport capacity inside them. The fix is to build up and out around jobs, not to bribe families to endure isolation while leaving the housing blockade intact. Essential rural industries will still command the wages needed to attract rotational or local crews without preserving every settlement as if population maps were museum pieces.

[C2.1] Concentration is not a policy conspiracy; it is how modern productivity works. Firms cluster to find specialized workers, suppliers and customers quickly, workers cluster to find better matches and higher wages. Taxing that engine to duplicate its infrastructure across thinly settled places does not spread prosperity, it shrinks it. The surplus generated in productive metros is precisely what funds schools, hospitals and pensions everywhere else.
[C2.2] The promise of just broadband, transport, colleges and clinics [P1.4] sounds restrained until you price it per person. A bus route, maternity unit or technical course serving 3,000 people costs multiples per user of the same service serving 300,000, and then cannot recruit drivers, doctors or instructors to stay. That is why the landscape is littered with subsidized industrial parks without tenants and training programs without employers. Firms that only come for the grant leave when it expires.
[C2.3] High metro housing prices do not prove metros have failed. They prove governments have blocked building where demand is strongest through zoning, height limits and veto-driven planning. The humane answer is to allow far more homes and transit near jobs, letting millions share in high wages, rather than urging families to accept long drives, thin job ladders and single-employer risk for a cheaper house.
[C2.4] Consolidating warehouses, clinics and flights into hubs [P1.6] is not market cruelty; it is avoiding triplicate overhead so prices stay low for rural consumers too. Nor does resilience require preserving every settlement. Farming, energy maintenance, freight and emergency cover need mobile, well-paid crews and regional hubs, not a fully staffed town every twenty miles. Pouring money into places with shrinking workforces traps young people where earnings, partners and second chances are scarcest.
[C2.5] Help people move, train and insure, not places pretend.

That is not efficiency; it is deferred cost shifting. If policy made the map lopsided, refusing to invest in functional smaller regions is not market neutrality—it is choosing to pay the hidden bill later.

Give the small town the infrastructure; if it still cannot hold, consolidate. But do not pretend abandonment saves money.
