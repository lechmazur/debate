[P1.1] Cable news narrowed the lens. Social media shattered it into millions of personalized mirrors — and that is why it polarized democratic politics more.
[P1.2] Cable was still one-to-many. Fox, MSNBC, and CNN competed for large, identifiable audiences, with anchors, producers, and advertisers who needed a program millions could watch at the same hour. Viewers could choose a slant, but they still encountered a shared rundown of events, corrections at least sometimes, and opponents presented in a form the other side would recognize. It sorted Americans into a few big camps.
[P1.3] Social media sorted them into endless micro-camps built around identity, grievance, and taste, each with its own facts, villains, and vocabulary.
[P1.4] The engine is different. Cable chased ratings; platforms chase engagement minute by minute, and moral outrage, humiliation, and threat are reliably engaging. Algorithms therefore learn to surface the most enraging version of the other side, not the most representative one. A cable segment ends.

[P1.6] Most importantly, social media made polarization participatory. Cable viewers shouted at the television; users now perform politics for peers, employers, and strangers, where compromise looks like betrayal and nuance loses status. It also lets fringe claims skip gatekeepers entirely, recruit in private groups, and arrive as social proof from friends rather than as a claim from a distant network.
[P1.7] Cable polarized the audience. Social media polarized the incentives, the identities, and the information supply itself — for voters, activists, journalists, and officeholders alike. That is a deeper and wider transformation.

[P2.2] That transformation is social. Cable gave viewers two or three rival storylines. Platforms gave each user a continuously tested, personalized enemy — and then made politicians, journalists, and activists perform for that reaction. Even people who rarely post are governed by people who do.
[P2.3] A representative does not fear a cable segment next week; they fear a primary challenger, a quote-post pile-on, and a fundraising email built from a fifteen-second clip today.
[P2.4] The audience argument mistakes Twitter for social media. Algorithms let that minority set the tone everyone else scrolls past, donates against, and votes inside.
[P2.5] Nor is cable still the factory and social merely the pipe. A cable clip becomes politically potent on social when it is stripped of context, captioned as proof of evil, and sorted to the people most likely to rage-share it. [P2.6] A synchronized audience can be answered by a rival channel. A million personalized feeds cannot even agree on what happened. That is more polarization, not less.
