[P1.1] Every major policy rests on a forecast. This rail line will cost so much, this tax cut will pay for itself, this program will cut waiting lists. The real question is who makes that forecast and what happens to them when they're wrong.
[P1.2] Under the current system, the answer is usually nothing. Megaproject budgets overrun so routinely that planners have a name for it: optimism bias. Internal forecasters work for the ministers whose pet projects they are evaluating. Expert panels are chosen by the people they advise, speak in unscoreable phrases like "significant risk," and face no penalty for confident error.
[P1.3] The failure is one of structure, not of intelligence. The people closest to the decision have the strongest incentive to tell it what it wants to hear.
[P1.4] A subsidized prediction market changes that structure in three ways.
[P1.5] First, it pays for accuracy and charges for error. The junior engineer who knows the tunnel estimate is fantasy cannot overrule her department, but she can trade. Dissent that hierarchy suppresses becomes profitable.
[P1.6] Second, it produces a number. "Sixty percent chance of completion by 2030 under Plan A, thirty-five under Plan B" is public, continuous, and can be checked against reality. A panel's prose can never be scored. A market builds a track record that governments, and voters, can audit.
[P1.7] Third, the subsidy answers the standard objection. Policy questions attract thin trading on their own, so the state funds liquidity, which is in effect a bounty for information. [C1.1] The proposition sounds like accountability. In practice, it would substitute one bad forecasting method for a worse one, and then hide the substitution behind a number that looks rigorous.
[C1.2] The core flaw is simple: most major policy decisions are not prediction problems. They are choices about values under deep uncertainty. Should we tax carbon or subsidize green R&D? Should a central bank prioritize inflation or employment?
[C1.3] These questions have no single correct outcome to bet on. A prediction market can tell you what traders think will happen under a given policy. It cannot tell you whether that outcome is worth the trade-offs the policy demands. Expert panels, for all their faults, can at least deliberate about distributional consequences, equity, and second-order effects that no contract can capture.
[C1.4] Even where prediction is relevant, subsidized markets introduce new distortions. The subsidy pays speculators to participate, not to be right. You are essentially bribing people to trade on questions they may know nothing about, inflating liquidity with noise rather than signal. And well-resourced actors—lobbyists, corporations, foreign governments—can afford to move prices deliberately.
[C1.5] The motion claims distortion is self-correcting because better-informed traders will arbitrage. But that assumes someone knows the right answer, which is precisely what we lack when we are deciding.
[C1.6] Worse, formalizing market prices as a policy input gives politicians a dangerous alibi. A minister can now point to a market number and say "the market says this works," deflecting responsibility for decisions that should be made on democratic and ethical grounds, not on what a subsidized pool of traders guessed. It launders political choices through a veneer of quantitative objectivity.
[C1.7] The real failure the PRO identifies—optimism bias, captured forecasts—is genuine. But the cure they propose trades accountability for apparent precision, and that trade is not worth making.
