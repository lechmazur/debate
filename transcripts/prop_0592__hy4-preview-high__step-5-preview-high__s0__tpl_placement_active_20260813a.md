[P1.1] The case for QMV is not procedural tidiness; it is the difference between a sanction that bites and one that arrives after the money has moved. Unanimity turns every sanctions package into a hostage negotiation. One capital, citing energy dependence or domestic politics, can delay, dilute, or block action while the aggressor adapts. While governments haggle over exemptions, the target reroutes finance, builds a shadow fleet, and locks in military gains.
[P1.2] Speed is not a luxury; it is the core of sanctions. Asset freezes and export controls must precede the next offensive, not follow it.
[P1.3] QMV changes the incentives. Under majority rule, a reluctant government must build a blocking minority instead of issuing a veto; the default becomes action, and the Union moves at the pace of the threat rather than the pace of its slowest member. A dissenting state still has a voice, but it must persuade partners rather than hold them ransom. That also improves quality, because measures no longer have to be drafted for the most exposed or most sympathetic government.
[P1.4] Stronger, coherent packages are more than symbolic: they signal resolve, deny resources, and raise the expected cost of aggression.
[P1.5] Defenders of unanimity will dress it as protection for small states. That is backwards. A veto does not empower small states; it exposes them. It invites the aggressor to identify the most vulnerable capital and apply bilateral pressure, aid, or energy leverage until it caves.
[P1.6] Collective majority voting protects every member by making the Union the decision-maker, not a single exposed government. QMV already governs trade; foreign policy should not let one veto outrank collective security. Sovereignty is not the ability to paralyse your allies while a war economy adapts; it is the ability to act together before the next attack. The EU should replace unanimity with QMV so that aggressor states face a union that can decide, and decide in time.

[C1.1] The proposal mistakes a faster vote for faster impact. Sanctions do not bite in Brussels; they bite when every finance ministry, customs service, port authority and court treats them as binding and unavoidable. Unanimity is slow because it forces the Union to design a package the most exposed member can actually implement. That friction is not a defect; it is part of the credibility of the measure.
[C1.2] Under QMV, an exposed member can be outvoted and still control the levers that matter—transit, licensing, re-export controls, enforcement priorities and domestic litigation. The likely outcome is not immediate denial but formal adoption followed by quiet dilution: exemptions, delayed transposition, conflicting national rules and selective seizures. A shadow fleet can exploit one reluctant administration more easily than it can exploit a package every government has signed and defended at home.
[C1.3] PRO's comparison to trade misses this. EU trade policy has centralized enforcement through the customs union; foreign-policy sanctions remain national in execution. Speed of adoption is not speed of effect. A package adopted in days but unevenly enforced after months gives the aggressor time to reroute finance, insure cargo and rebuild supply chains—the very adaptation PRO says must be prevented.
[C1.4] QMV also relocates leverage rather than abolishing it. Instead of pressuring one government to maintain unanimity, an aggressor can lobby for a blocking minority, offering carve-outs to exposed capitals until delay becomes rational. The veto may be visible; the resulting breakdown is more dangerous because it is hidden inside implementation.
[C1.5] The decisive test is not how quickly ministers can vote. It is how quickly money, technology and energy revenue stop reaching the aggressor. Unanimity buys comprehensive, enforceable ownership at the cost of some speed; QMV buys votes and risks a slower, weaker reality. Keep the rule that forces Europe to move together, not merely together enough.

[P2.6] QMV raises the price of obstruction and lowers its payoff.
[P2.7] The choice is not speed of voting versus speed of effect [C1.3]. It is early, broad, binding measures with enforcement follow-up, versus late, pre-diluted measures that look consensual because the objecting government got its exemption.
