[P1.1] Picture an 82-year-old woman who raised three children, worked her whole life, and now cannot feed or bathe herself. Her savings are gone. Her children are comfortable—homes, holidays, retirement accounts. The question before us is simple: when she cannot support herself, should her grown children be legally obligated to help pay, or should that burden fall entirely on strangers and taxpayers while her own family looks away?
[P1.2] I say the law should require them to contribute.
[P1.3] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, reciprocity. Parents who raised a dependent child created an enormous, non-voluntary investment of money, time, and sacrifice. A duty to care flowing back when the parent becomes dependent is not charity—it is the repayment of a debt the child did not choose but unquestionably received. The law already enforces obligations we did not contract into: we tax citizens for roads they never use and require parents to support children they may not have planned.
[P1.5] Intergenerational duty is the natural mirror of the parental duty we already enforce.
[P1.6] Second, fairness to everyone else. When families refuse to contribute, the cost does not vanish—it lands on public budgets funded by other people's taxes. Why should a stranger's wages subsidize the care of a parent whose own affluent children pay nothing? A contribution duty targets those most connected to and most benefited by that person before socializing the cost across society.
[P1.7] Third, this duty can be designed humanely. "Contribute" does not mean ruin. A well-built law scales to ability to pay, exempts the genuinely poor, and accounts for cases of abuse or abandonment. Several countries and some U.S. states already maintain filial responsibility provisions, showing this is administrable, not utopian.
[P1.8] The motion does not demand that children bankrupt themselves. It asks that those who can help, and who owe the most, not be legally permitted to abandon the very people who once kept them alive.

[C1.2] The reciprocity argument fails at its foundation. Parents chose to have children. [C1.3] The law already reflects this asymmetry: we impose support duties on parents precisely because they made the choice; we do not mirror that duty downward as a matter of debt repayment. A child raised well owes gratitude, perhaps moral obligation, but moral obligation and legal compulsion are entirely different instruments.
[C1.4] The administration of such a law would be invasive, divisive, and arbitrary. Investigators must probe adult children's finances, assess their capacity, adjudicate disputes between siblings, and decide which family histories count as mitigating. What about the child raised in neglect who now earns modestly? The sibling who provided years of unpaid physical care while her brother paid nothing?
[C1.5] The estranged family where the parent was abusive? Every variation demands judicial intervention into private relationships the state cannot actually see clearly.
[C1.6] The PRO side claims a humane design can handle these exceptions—but that optimism papers over reality. Existing filial responsibility statutes have been largely dormant or inconsistently enforced precisely because the problems are intractable, not administrative. The mess is the policy.
[C1.7] The real alternative is not "affluent children pay nothing while strangers shoulder everything." It is robust public care funded through progressive taxation and social insurance—systems where everyone contributes according to means, no one is singled out by birth order, and care doesn't depend on whether your children happen to be solvent and cooperative.
[C1.8] The proposition would solve a real problem with the wrong tool.
