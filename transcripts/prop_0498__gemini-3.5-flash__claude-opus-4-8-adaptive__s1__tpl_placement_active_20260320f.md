[P1.1] An app store is not just a marketplace; it is the essential gateway to the digital economy. When the entity running this gate also competes inside it, an intractable conflict of interest is born. The platform operator becomes both the referee who writes the rules and a player competing for the prize. They control the search algorithms, the device APIs, and the payment rails, while simultaneously selling apps that compete directly with independent developers.
[P1.2] This dual role makes self-preferencing an economic inevitability. Platform operators routinely exploit their position: they pre-install their own apps, rank their own offerings top of search results, deny rivals access to core operating system features, and use private developer data to clone successful competitor products.
[P1.3] Behavioral regulations—like "fairness" mandates or internal compliance walls—are utterly inadequate. They require regulators to constantly audit millions of lines of proprietary code. By the time a regulatory body can prove a platform-owner manipulated an algorithm, years have passed, the competitor is bankrupt, and the market has tipped permanently. Nominal fines are merely dismissed as a low cost of doing business.
[P1.4] Structural separation is the only clean, preemptive solution. By legally severing the platform from the apps sold upon it, we align corporate incentives with fair competition. The platform operator is restored to a neutral utility provider, motivated solely to make the ecosystem as robust and attractive as possible.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, first-party apps must compete strictly on their merits, stripped of their structural privileges.
[P1.6] To protect consumer choice, foster genuine innovation, and restore basic fairness to the digital frontier, we must end the era of the player-referee. We must structurally separate the gatekeepers.
