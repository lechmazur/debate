[P1.1] Public subsidies for professional sports teams are not charitable handouts to wealthy owners—they are binding, two-sided exchanges where communities trade scarce public dollars, prime public land, targeted tax breaks, and custom-built public infrastructure in exchange for the tangible, long-term local benefits of having a permanent home team. That trade comes with decades of taxpayer liability for stadium bonds, deferred investment in schools, transit, and core public safety services, zoning changes that displace existing residents and small businesses, and years of small business investment around the reliable draw of steady game-day traffic.
[P1.2] There is no defensible moral or practical reason to let team owners unilaterally void that deal the moment a sweeter public subsidy offer from another city lands on their desk. Cities do not get to walk away from their debt obligations when a team leaves; bond payments still come due for 20 or 30 years, purpose-built stadiums sit half-empty or abandoned, and surrounding neighborhood businesses collapse without the fan traffic they planned for, leaving working residents holding the bill for a broken promise.
[P1.3] A binding community approval process does not ban relocation outright. It simply gives the public that paid the freight a formal, enforceable vote before owners can relocate, requiring teams to either make the case that relocation is justified, negotiate fair exit terms that fully repay unrecouped public investment, or honor the deal taxpayers already paid for. Owners already hold massive negotiating leverage over cities desperate to keep or attract teams; removing this basic democratic check turns public subsidy into a one-way racket, where the public takes all the upfront risk, absorbs years of construction disruption and displacement, forgoes other critical public priorities, and owners hold all the power to walk away whenever it pads their bottom line.

[C1.1] PRO calls subsidies "binding, two-sided exchanges." Good. Then treat them like exchanges. Contracts already solve this problem, and a binding community vote makes it worse.
[C1.2] First, the contract remedy is better. A city that spends hundreds of millions can demand a non-relocation clause, a lease running the life of the bonds, and a clawback that repays unrecouped investment if the team leaves. Those terms are negotiated upfront, priced in, and enforceable in court. When Cleveland fought the Browns' move to Baltimore, legal pressure produced a settlement that kept the name, colors, and history in Cleveland and brought a new team back within a few years.
[C1.3] That is accountability through enforceable terms. PRO's vote adds nothing where good contracts exist and rewrites the deal after the fact where they don't.
[C1.4] Second, this "approval process" is a ban in disguise. PRO says it "does not ban relocation outright." But which community votes to lose its team? Fans, local businesses, and politicians facing reelection will vote no essentially every time.
[C1.5] The predictable result is a permanent veto held by the party with the strongest incentive to use it. And it raises an unanswerable question: who is the community? The city, the county, the state that co-financed the arena? The new host city's voters approved their own deal.
[C1.6] Why does one electorate get to override another?
[C1.7] Third, trapping teams hurts the very taxpayers PRO claims to protect. An owner who cannot leave can still slash payroll, let the stadium decay, and demand renovations. A failing franchise locked in a shrinking market is a worse asset than a clean exit with repayment. Worse still, promising cities permanent control through a vote makes subsidies look safer, encouraging more of the public giveaways that rarely deliver the promised returns.
[C1.8] Taxpayers need better deals, not a veto that freezes bad ones.

[P2.1] The argument that contracts already solve this problem collapses under the real-world leverage imbalance CON refuses to acknowledge. When teams negotiate initial subsidy deals, they come to the table waving explicit relocation threats to more generous cities, which is precisely why most existing agreements lack the ironclad non-relocation clauses and full clawback provisions CON imagines. The Cleveland Browns case CON cites proves this, not refutes it: the Browns left anyway, the legal fight took years, taxpayers still footed the bill for a replacement stadium, and the city never recovered the economic value of the lost decade without a team.
[P2.2] Those after-the-fact legal scraps are no match for a clear, upfront democratic check. A binding approval process is not a permanent veto. It simply requires that any relocation either wins community consent or repays all unrecouped public costs, covers outstanding bond debt, and compensates adjacent small businesses that planned around the team’s presence. Voters have every incentive to approve a move if exit terms are fair, just as they regularly approve reasonable public tradeoffs when costs and benefits align.
[P2.3] The claim that locking teams in lets owners extort more renovations gets the leverage dynamic backwards. Right now, owners routinely demand new publicly funded upgrades mid-lease by threatening to leave; this process removes that extortion tool, rather than amplifying it. The “who is the community?” question CON calls unanswerable is trivial: the public entity on the hook for stadium debt, the same jurisdiction that approved the subsidy, gets the vote. A new host city remains free to offer incentives, but it has no claim to void the original community’s investment.
[P2.4] Far from encouraging reckless subsidies, this check makes cities less likely to throw unprotected cash at owners, because they know they will have a say before the deal can be broken.
