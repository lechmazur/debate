[C1.1] Accessibility is a real duty, but this motion confuses a duty to accommodate with a duty to record, caption, and reformat everything by default. Captions and lecture recordings are not the same thing as access. Automatic captions reliably garble technical vocabulary; a recorded biology lecture can turn "apoptosis" into nonsense, and a mathematics lecture can render "eigenvalue" unrecognizable. If schools may satisfy the presumption with such tools, they can check an accessibility box while the supports that actually include the hardest cases—real-time captioning, interpreting, human-reviewed documents, tactile materials—go underfunded.
[C1.2] Scarce accessibility budgets would chase bulk recording systems and slide conversions, not the individualized help that disabled students need most.
[C1.3] The presumption is also not a neutral default. Because everything is provided unless a "strong pedagogical reason" is shown, instructors and students are forced into exception-hunting. [P1.5] treats a confidential clinical discussion or an unrehearsed exercise as a carve-out, but if the baseline is recording and captioning, the sensitive seminar becomes the rare thing a teacher must justify protecting. The harm happens at capture: when every confused question, aside, or personal disclosure is recorded, candid discussion shrinks.
[C1.4] That is not speculative; it is the predictable effect of making review tools the baseline. Live attendance and spontaneous exchange are not luxuries—they are how misconceptions surface and get corrected, especially for students who need interactive clarification.
[C1.5] PRO's asymmetry is false. PRO calls the teacher's cost "planning" [P1.6], but planning is the substance of teaching. Hours spent managing recordings and tagging files are hours not spent on feedback. And the alternative to a broken accommodation process is not a universal dragnet.
[C1.6] A well-resourced, needs-based system can deliver captions, recordings, and accessible materials quickly and confidentially, without making every class a surveillance artifact or every instructor a media producer.
