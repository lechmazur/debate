[C1.3] Indoor venues generate the payroll, the liquor revenue, and the repeat visits that actually define a working night economy.
[C1.4] Second, trucks are mobile precisely because they don't anchor anything. A hospitality zone is a fixed promise: the lights are on Tuesday in February when it's raining. Food trucks chase good weather and good weekends and vanish otherwise. You cannot revive a district with vendors who disappear the moment conditions turn.
[C1.5] Cities in cold or wet climates would be revitalizing sidewalks that are empty half the year.
[C1.6] Third, my opponent claims trucks make streets feel safe. A cluster of trucks with no restrooms and no seating creates crowding, litter, and noise complaints that turn residents against nightlife entirely.
[C1.7] Fourth, note the hidden harm. Trucks with almost no rent or overhead directly undercut the brick-and-mortar restaurants that do pay rent and property taxes. Flood the streets with them and you don't add to the economy—you cannibalize the fixed businesses that fund city services and employ locals.
[C1.8] The honest comparison is this: indoor zones are the durable backbone; street food is a garnish. You don't revive a nightlife economy by making its infrastructure temporary, weather-dependent, and low-margin. You revive it by anchoring it.
