[P1.1] When a beverage giant wraps a plastic bottle in a shrink label with insoluble glue, or tints that plastic bright green to stand out on a shelf, it captures every cent of the marketing upside. The moment that bottle hits the bin, however, the company walks away. Local taxpayers pay to haul it, sort it, and, because that dye and glue ruin the recycling batch, pay again to landfill it. Privatizing the profits of disposable packaging while socializing its cleanup is a textbook market failure that guarantees mountains of trash.
[P1.2] Shifting the bulk of collection and recycling costs onto major producers fixes this failure where it starts: the design table. Municipal governments have no control over packaging chemistry, and shoppers cannot decode resin symbols in the checkout line. Only producers decide whether to use easily recycled aluminum and clear plastic, or cheap, unrecyclable multilayer pouches. While disposal remains free to manufacturers, throwaway complexity wins.
[P1.3] When producers must fund recovery systems, paying lower fees for standardized materials and steep rates for contaminants, wasteful design stops being a free marketing gimmick and directly hits corporate margins.
[P1.4] Producer responsibility also restores basic fairness. Today, communities fund waste management through property taxes and flat municipal charges, forcing a frugal household drinking tap water to subsidize the cleanup of global beverage conglomerates. Critics warn that companies will simply pass every penny to shoppers. Yet in competitive grocery aisles, a brand cannot freely hike prices if a rival avoids the fee by adopting lighter, genuinely recyclable containers.
[P1.5] Even where modest costs reach the shelf, putting waste costs into voluntary product prices is far fairer than burying them in mandatory local taxes.
[P1.6] Finally, dedicated producer funding gives strained municipalities the capital to modernize sorting infrastructure instead of cutting curbside recycling. Cities did not engineer disposable clutter, and taxpayers should stop subsidizing it. The major producers profiting from wasteful packaging should pay to clean it up.

[C1.1] The motion sounds like fairness until you ask who actually pays. A beverage company is not a money tree; it is a pass-through. Levy its packaging heavily and the cost lands in the price of soda, water, and household staples. That is not “producer pays”; it is a hidden consumption tax, and it hits poorer households hardest because they spend more of their income on essentials.
[C1.2] Taxpayers have not escaped the bill—they have lost the receipt.
[C1.3] Second, the proposition confuses ownership of a problem with control over it. Producers choose packaging, yes, but cities choose collection schedules, sorting contracts, landfill sites, and whether curbside service is efficient. A distant producer-fee bureaucracy cannot run a municipal truck or fix a contaminated recycling stream. “Much more” funding can easily become a slush fund for general city budgets or consultants, while actual recovery barely moves.
[C1.4] Third, packaging is a system, not a morality play. Lightweight multilayer pouches may be hard to recycle, but they can prevent food waste and cut transport emissions. Glass is recyclable but heavy; paper alternatives can leak or fail. A crude fee schedule punishes complex packaging without guaranteeing that new recycling capacity exists.
[C1.5] Producers may simply pay the levy and keep the same design, because the fee is just another line item. The label-and-glue example is a genuine annoyance, but the remedy is a design standard or a deposit system, not a blank check.
[C1.6] The motion also hands incumbents a weapon. “Major producers” get regulated; smaller competitors get squeezed by compliance costs. The likely result is consolidation, not circularity.
[C1.7] If the goal is less waste, put the price at the point of disposal: deposits on containers, pay-as-you-throw, enforceable litter fines, and local contracts that reward actual tonnage diverted. Those link behavior to outcomes. The proposition instead moves money around and calls it environmental policy.
