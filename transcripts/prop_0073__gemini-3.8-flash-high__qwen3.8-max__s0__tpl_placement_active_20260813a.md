[P1.1] Across the democratic world, major public institutions—from land-grant universities and public land trusts to state-managed resource reserves—continue to generate immense operational wealth, revenue, and institutional prestige from millions of acres expropriated from Indigenous nations. Under foundational legal and moral principles, retaining active material gains derived from historic dispossession constitutes unjust enrichment. When the beneficiary is the state itself, redress is not symbolic charity or discretionary welfare; it is a binding institutional debt.
[P1.2] Crucially, the proposition establishes a practical, flexible framework: land return, long-term revenue sharing, or equivalent restitution. This disjunctive menu neutralizes alarmist objections about administrative paralysis or private displacement. Where public parcels remain undeveloped, ecologically sensitive, or culturally sacred, returning title or establishing co-management restores self-determination without disrupting civil society. Where institutions actively monetize seized territory—such as universities drawing perpetual returns from mineral rights, timber leases, and endowments seeded by federal land grants—revenue sharing guarantees that ongoing yields support the original stewards.
[P1.3] And where active public infrastructure cannot be transferred, equivalent restitution provides durable, predictable capital.
[P1.4] Denying this restitution forces Indigenous nations to perpetually subsidize the public balance sheets of the governments that dispossessed them. Modern public universities train civic leaders and accumulate endowments using wealth harvested from Indigenous territory, while neighboring tribal communities face structural underfunding in infrastructure, healthcare, and education. Discretionary state grants cannot substitute for proprietary rights; they reduce an ongoing legal entitlement to political benevolence.
[P1.5] This debate does not require unraveling all private land ownership. It targets the concrete, ongoing windfalls enjoyed by public entities today. If a public institution still balances its budget, expands its campus, or funds its programs through the dividends of expropriation, it cannot disclaim responsibility for how that wealth was obtained. A state committed to justice cannot keep the harvest while disowning the theft.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a moral truth for a workable mandate. Historic dispossession was real, and public institutions often occupy land acquired through unjust treaties, war, or coercion. But converting public benefits traceable to historic seizure into automatic claims for land return or perpetual revenue sharing is a rule that cannot be administered justly.
[C1.2] First, it collapses the distance between wrongdoer and present institution. The state that seized land is not identical to the hospital, school, or forest agency serving millions today. If a public university’s endowment once included land grants, the proposition would tax tuition-funded education, medical research, and local jobs to satisfy a debt assigned by genealogy of assets rather than present responsibility. That is not restitution; it is a retroactive surcharge on public goods.
[C1.3] Second, the menu of remedies hides impossible choices. Which parcels qualify? How much revenue is attributable to “seized” land rather than later labor, investment, or management? What happens when one tribe’s claim overlaps another’s, or when returned land contains public roads, schools, utilities, conservation habitat, or competing private interests?
[C1.4] The proposition says “equivalent restitution,” but equivalence requires a neutral metric that politics cannot supply.
[C1.5] Third, it incentivizes adversarial extraction over durable repair. Settlement processes, co-management, cultural protections, targeted investment, and self-governance compacts can address Indigenous disadvantage while preserving democratic consent. It also lets current governments off the hook: they can defer hard choices about healthcare, education, and infrastructure by outsourcing repair to asset tracing and litigation. The proposition replaces those negotiated tools with standing entitlements tied to historical injury, inviting endless litigation and resentment.
[C1.6] A just state should confront dispossession, but not by turning every public institution into a debtor and every public benefit into a claim. The stronger path is forward-looking repair, accountable negotiation, and improved Indigenous self-determination—not a backward-looking lien on the public estate.
