[P1.1] When a university expands its campus, a state capitol stands on ceremonial ground, or a national park generates tourism revenue from land that was never lawfully ceded, the injustice is not historical. It is present-tense. The land is still occupied. The institution still profits.
[P1.2] The dispossessed community still bears the loss. That is the core of the proposition: where public institutions continue to benefit from land taken through coercion, broken treaties, or outright seizure, states have an obligation to make restitution—through land return where feasible, long-term revenue sharing where it is not, or equivalent structural compensation.
[P1.3] This is not a claim about guilt across centuries. It is a claim about ongoing advantage. A public university that charges tuition, builds endowments, and controls real estate on land it never legitimately acquired is not an innocent bystander inheriting a distant wrong. It is the direct, current beneficiary of dispossession.
[P1.4] The same applies to state parks, government complexes, and resource-extraction sites on public land. The wealth flows today. The moral debt compounds today.
[P1.5] The proposition is deliberately practical. It does not demand the impossible—dismantling cities, uprooting infrastructure, or reversing populations. It offers a spectrum: return land where return is workable; share revenue where the land cannot be returned; design equivalent restitution where neither fits. What it refuses is the third option states have always chosen: doing nothing while pretending time has dissolved the obligation.
[P1.6] Consider a state park generating millions in visitor revenue on land guaranteed by treaty to a tribal nation. The treaty was broken. The land was seized. The park still collects fees, sells concessions, and builds facilities.
[P1.7] Under any coherent theory of justice, the benefiting institution owes something more than an interpretive plaque. Revenue sharing, co-management, or land trust transfer are not radical gestures. They are the minimum consistent with the principle that no institution should be allowed to keep what it acquired through theft simply because the theft happened long enough ago to become convenient.

[C1.1] The proposition offers a clean principle but produces a permanent title war. Almost every public acre has some coercive, contested, or broken-promise moment in its past. Once “historically seized” is enough, there is no limiting principle: a single campus, park, or trust parcel could face overlapping claims from different displaced groups, and every settlement invites older ones. The state would no longer manage public land for current citizens; it would spend decades negotiating genealogies of conquest.
[C1.2] The costs fall on the wrong people. Many western states hold public trust lands to fund K-12 schools. If those lands must be returned or their revenue shared because the original acquisition was coercive, the immediate loss hits schoolchildren, rural districts, and public universities, not the officials who committed old wrongs. A public university or hospital on former treaty land is not actively profiting from theft; it is delivering education and care, including to Indigenous students and patients.
[C1.3] Diverting its revenue penalizes those users as much as anyone.
[C1.4] The proposed “return where feasible, share revenue when not” test does not solve the problem; it moves the question into politics. States under pressure will pay to quiet the loudest claim, not the strongest one, and “equivalent restitution” becomes an open-ended subsidy with no endpoint. A rule without clear eligibility, limit, or finality is not justice; it is leverage.
[C1.5] The deeper issue is time. Ordered property systems require finality. If long-settled public boundaries and titles remain permanently conditional, the state cannot confidently build schools, roads, parks, or water systems. The better standard is present disadvantage and present legal rights, addressed through direct funding, self-governance, and negotiated agreements.
[C1.6] That helps living communities. The motion instead chases the land, and in doing so makes every public institution a permanent defendant for regimes none of us chose.

[P4.1] A1: The burden falls on the claiming community, as in any property or tort claim. The standard is preponderance of historical evidence—treaty text, legislative records, court findings, documented seizure. Mixed histories—some purchase, some coercion—do not defeat the claim; they are what evidentiary standards exist to sort. Where records are absent, that absence is often itself a product of state conduct: governments that seized land also controlled the archives.
[P4.2] When evidence is genuinely thin, the claim is weaker—but that is a problem of proof, not a reason to default to the institution still holding the benefit.
[P4.3] A2: Equivalent restitution is not a permanent lien. It is satisfied by a concrete, negotiated settlement: a land trust transfer, a time-limited revenue-sharing agreement, co-management authority, or a compensation fund. The endpoint is the settlement itself—exactly as a civil settlement resolves a claim. Revenue sharing can be capped, renegotiated, or converted to a lump sum.
[P4.4] What makes it restitution rather than an open-ended subsidy is that it tracks a specific identified harm to a specific community. CON demands a cap written into the motion, but the motion sets a principle; legislatures and tribunals set terms, as with any remedial framework.
[P4.5] CON's pattern is revealing. Every objection—burden of proof, finality, cost—defaults to the institution holding seized land. CON never denies the benefit is real and ongoing. Its own alternative faces the same proof problem: "recognized legal rights" requires the same historical chain; "present disadvantage" requires the same documentation.
[P4.6] CON doesn't escape the evidentiary burden; it relocates it. Its alternative of "direct funding and self-governance" either is restitution under another name—conceding the obligation—or is disconnected from the specific harm. It cannot be both. Once you accept that an institution benefiting from dispossession owes something, the only question is form and terms—and the proposition provides the flexibility CON says is missing.
