[P1.1] Employer‑funded training is a direct investment in a worker’s productive capacity. When that investment is locked to a single firm, it creates artificial friction: skilled employees stay put not because the job fits, but because they would lose the training they paid for with their labor. Allowing portability within the same sector removes that friction while preserving the employer’s legitimate interest in recouping costs across a competitive labor market.
[P1.2] Consider a registered nurse who completes a hospital‑sponsored critical‑care certification. If she moves to another hospital in the same health‑care sector, the certification does not become obsolete; the skill is identical, the patient need is identical. Forcing her to forfeit the credential or start over wastes the hours she and the first hospital invested, and forces the second hospital to duplicate training that already exists. The same logic applies to software engineers who earn cloud‑platform credentials on one firm’s dime and later join a rival tech company.
[P1.3] Portability also incentivises employers to train more aggressively. When firms know that trained workers can carry credentials to competitors, they are less likely to hoard training as a retention tool and more likely to view it as a sector‑wide upgrade that raises the overall talent pool. This dynamic spurs competition on wages and working conditions, because firms must attract talent with more than just a captive training account.
[P1.4] The “same‑sector” limiter is crucial: it prevents free‑riding across unrelated industries where the skill may not transfer, while still recognising that within a sector the marginal cost of an additional trained worker is near zero. The result is a more fluid, efficient labor market where human‑capital investments flow to where they are most needed, rather than being stranded in a single employer’s balance sheet.

[C1.2] Making those follow the worker means Hospital A is compelled to write cheques for Hospital B's staff.
[C1.3] Think about what that does to incentives. The reason firms fund training at all is that they expect to capture some of the return. Within the same sector is precisely where that return is easiest for a rival to steal, because the skills transfer perfectly and competitors know exactly whom to poach. PRO's "same-sector limiter" in [P1.4] is therefore not a safeguard; it is a targeting system.
[C1.4] It tells every firm that the most valuable training it could provide is the training most likely to walk out the door with a funded account attached.
[C1.5] The predictable response is not more training, as [P1.3] hopes, but less. Firms shrink discretionary training budgets, convert them into wages, or route investment only to firm-specific skills no competitor wants. The losers are small employers, who lack the scale to absorb repeated poaching, and workers at the bottom, who lose the one benefit that converted their time into marketable skill.
[C1.6] There is also a fairness problem PRO ignores. A training account is not deferred pay the worker has already earned; it is a conditional offer from an employer who bears the cost. Transferring it by mandate is expropriation dressed as mobility.
[C1.7] If the goal is sector-wide skill, the answer is sector-wide funding through pooled levies and apprenticeship schemes, not forcing the one firm that trained to subsidise every firm that didn't.
