[P1.1] Stronger labor protections are necessary because esports concentrates risk on the people least equipped to refuse it: young players with short careers, scarce alternatives, and organizations that control their pay, playing time, housing, travel, and public reputation. Calling that relationship “voluntary” does not make its terms fair. A signature is not meaningful consent when refusing means losing a rare professional opportunity before adulthood.
[P1.2] Start with contracts. Players can be bound by broad restrictions while teams retain wide discretion to bench them, terminate them, or control their earnings and future transfers. Standard labor safeguards—clear written terms, prompt payment, fair termination rules, transparent revenue obligations, and accessible dispute resolution—prevent organizations from converting bargaining power into private immunity. They do not abolish negotiated contracts; they stop contracts from legalizing abuse.
[P1.3] Underage exploitation makes the case stronger, not merely more emotional. A minor cannot reliably assess a long-term career restriction, and a parent excited by a professional offer is not always an independent negotiator. Stronger protections should therefore include independent contract review, limits on exploitative duration and penalties, protection of education, and safeguards over minors’ earnings. Otherwise, the industry can treat a child’s ambition as a discount on their rights.
[P1.4] Extreme schedules reveal the same power imbalance. Practice, competition, travel, content obligations, and constant public performance can merge into one endless workweek. Because players fear replacement, “train harder or lose your spot” becomes coercion disguised as dedication. Enforceable rest periods, recovery time, reasonable scheduling duties, and access to health support protect careers rather than weaken competition.
[P1.5] A player driven into injury, exhaustion, or early burnout is not a model of sporting excellence.
[P1.6] CON may invoke flexibility and rapid growth, but flexibility for organizations cannot mean unlimited insecurity for labor. Every developing industry benefits when someone else absorbs its hidden costs. Here, those costs fall on teenagers’ health, education, and futures. Basic, enforceable protections are not a ceiling on esports’ success; they are the minimum condition for calling that success legitimate.
