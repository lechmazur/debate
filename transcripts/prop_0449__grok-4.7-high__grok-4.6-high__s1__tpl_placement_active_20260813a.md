[P1.1] Unabated coal is not a sovereign energy preference. It is the highest-carbon power source still running at scale, and every tonne it emits is dumped into an atmosphere every other country has to live with. A global phaseout by 2040, backed by trade or finance penalties, is the least demanding rule that still matches the physics and the politics.
[P1.2] Climate pathways that keep warming from becoming unmanageable require coal’s rapid exit: sooner in rich economies, and by about 2040 everywhere else. Plants built or refurbished now will still be emitting when today’s children are raising their own. Waiting for a later, cleaner bargain does not buy development. It buys stranded assets, a messier shutdown, and damages—heat, floods, failed harvests—that already outweigh the short-run savings of dirty power.
[P1.3] Voluntary pledges have not closed that gap. Coal survives where it is politically protected, where pollution is treated as free, and where any government that moves first fears losing industry to rivals who do not. Trade and finance penalties fix the collective-action failure. They raise the cost of continued unabated coal relative to wind, solar, storage, hydro, nuclear, or coal that is genuinely captured and verified.
[P1.4] Capital then flows toward replacements instead of another forty-year plant. Early movers stop being punished for doing the thing everyone claims to want.
[P1.5] This is not a ban on electricity or on growth. Abatement that actually works remains compatible with the deadline. Poorer countries are not told to go dark; they are told they cannot keep exporting climate risk while asking others to restrain themselves. Penalties can be paired with transition finance.
[P1.6] What they cannot be is optional. Without a cost for noncompliance, the phaseout is a press release, and the atmosphere does not read press releases.
[P1.7] The choice is predictable rules now, or a later scramble under worse climate and worse politics. Countries should commit, and they should make the commitment expensive to break.

[C1.1] A global 2040 cutoff for unabated coal, backed by trade or finance penalties, is not the minimum rule physics requires. It is a rigid industrial diktat that would hit late developers hardest, invite retaliation, and still leave the atmosphere dependent on countries that will not submit.
[C1.2] Coal is dirty. It is also, in large parts of Asia and Africa, the remaining path to 24-hour power, steel, cement, and jobs that do not vanish when the sun sets. Rich states burned it for a century, then moved the smokestacks offshore. A single date for everyone, with tariffs or withheld finance for missers, turns that history into a development tax.
[C1.3] Transition money is always announced and chronically late. Penalties are not.
[C1.4] “Unabated” is sold as a loophole. Capture on power plants is still costly, parasitic on output, and scarce at real scale. For most operators the deadline is a coal ban. Replacing dispatchable capacity everywhere by 2040 assumes mines, grids, permits, storage, and nuclear can be built on the same clock in weak institutions and monsoon climates.
[C1.5] Where that bet fails, the result is not cleaner air. It is load-shedding.
[C1.6] Penalties do not solve free-riding. They create a two-track system: states that can absorb the hit, and exporters that cannot. China can wait out pressure. A poorer manufacturer cannot.
[C1.7] Leakage follows, along with food and mineral retaliation and a trading system recast as climate police. Early movers are not protected; households pay higher prices and climate politics turns toxic.
[C1.8] Voluntary pledges are incomplete. Coercion on this timetable is worse: it pretends a shared atmosphere erases differentiated capacity, energy poverty, and grid physics. Countries should retire coal as cheaper, reliable substitutes actually arrive—on different clocks, without making noncompliance a trade crime. The alternative in this motion is not “press releases.” It is a later, messier shutdown plus a poorer, angrier world that burns what it can get.
