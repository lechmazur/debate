[P1.1] Nobody serious denies that IQ-style tests measure something real. The question is whether that something is broad enough to justify the gatekeeping power we hand it. It isn't, and the gap between what the test captures and what we let it decide is the whole debate.
[P1.2] Start with what these tests actually measure: abstract pattern-matching, verbal and numerical reasoning, working memory, and speed under a clock. Valuable, yes. But look at what's missing: persistence, practical judgment, creativity, the ability to read a room, to learn from failure, to build expertise over years. Ask any manager what separates a great nurse, teacher, or engineer from a mediocre one, and "solves matrix puzzles quickly" won't top the list.
[P1.3] Even defenders of these tests typically report correlations with job and academic performance in the moderate range. That is meaningful, but it means most of the difference between people's real-world outcomes comes from things the test never sees.
[P1.4] Now look at how the score is used. It is rarely treated as one modest signal among many. It becomes a cutoff. A child scores just below the line for a gifted program and gets a different track, different peers, different expectations, often for years.
[P1.5] An applicant fails an online aptitude screen and no human ever reads their record. A cutoff takes a moderate correlation and turns it into a hard exclusion. The test's narrowness then decides who gets the chance to show everything else.
[P1.6] Worse, the errors aren't random. Scores respond to coaching, test familiarity, language background, and schooling quality. The long rise in average scores across generations shows how much environment moves them. So the narrow band doesn't just miss talent.
[P1.7] It misses talent systematically, among those with the least access to preparation.
[P1.8] My burden is clear: the tool is narrow, and its role is outsized. CON's burden is harder than showing that IQ predicts something. They must show that a measure explaining a minority of performance deserves to be the first, and often final, gate. Prediction is not justification.

[C1.2] The "outsized role" charge misses why these tests are used: they are objective, scalable, and resistant to the subjective biases that plague essays, interviews, or recommendation letters. PRO’s cutoff critique applies to any selection tool, but IQ screens at least rest on a metric with demonstrated predictive power, not intuition. And the environmental sensitivities PRO cites—coaching, schooling—are features, not bugs: they reflect that cognitive ability itself develops in response to stimulation.
[C1.3] Most importantly, removing or diminishing IQ testing doesn’t eliminate sorting; it just shifts it to less transparent, often less fair criteria. The debate isn’t whether IQ tests are perfect, but whether we’re better off with their disciplined, evidence-backed signal or with the alternatives. The answer is clear: no other single tool offers comparable predictive validity at scale.
