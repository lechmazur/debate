[P1.1] Major platforms should compete to serve teenagers, not to make leaving harder. For users under eighteen, the default feed should prioritize relevance and deliberate choice over compulsive continuation, even when that means fewer minutes watched. The question is not whether teenagers may enjoy social media. It is whether platforms should automatically deploy their strongest attention-capturing systems against them.
[P1.2] An engagement-driven feed learns what keeps someone watching, not necessarily what they value afterward. Repeatedly escalating emotionally provocative material or recycling an irresistible interest can prolong a session without improving it. Teenagers are still developing habits and self-regulation; putting that development against a system constantly adapting to their reactions creates an unequal contest. The predictable risk is time displaced from sleep, schoolwork, and relationships they themselves care about.
[P1.3] A less addictive feed need not be dull, chronological, or censored. It can preserve personalized discovery while reducing repetitive amplification and the weight placed on uninterrupted viewing. Evaluate it by whether teenagers can end sessions when intended and report fewer unwanted returns, not merely whether total usage drops. A student should still find useful chemistry videos without the ranking system treating another hour online as an automatic success.
[P1.4] Defaults matter because protection buried in settings often misses families without the time and knowledge to find it. Parents cannot inspect and counteract every personalized ranking decision; platforms control those decisions and can change them at scale. This is a safer starting point, not a ban on connection or entertainment. And lost engagement is not automatically lost welfare: a teenager who gets the same value in less time is better served, even if fewer advertisements are sold.
[P1.5] Commercial success should follow useful service, not depend on making minors struggle to stop.
