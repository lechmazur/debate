[P1.1] The case for abolition is strongest exactly where it looks weakest: the terrorist, the mass murderer, the atrocity that makes everyone want blood. Three reasons.
[P1.2] First, these are the cases where the state gets it wrong most often. Public horror produces pressure to convict somebody, fast. Britain learned this with the Guildford Four and Birmingham Six: men convicted of pub bombings on coerced confessions and flawed forensic evidence, who served years before exoneration. Had Britain still been hanging, it would have hanged innocent men for terrorism and called it justice.
[P1.3] The United States has released well over a hundred people from death row after wrongful convictions. Prison can be reversed; a grave cannot. The "extremely serious crime" exception does not shrink that risk, it concentrates it where emotion is hottest and evidence is thinnest.
[P1.4] Second, execution does the terrorist's work for him. A man who straps on a bomb has already priced in death; the gallows deters nothing. What he wants is a martyr narrative, and a state execution supplies one, complete with anniversary and recruiting poster. Compare the alternative: the Norway shooter, aging in a cell, forgotten, with no grave to rally around.
[P1.5] Denying the killer his chosen ending is the harsher sentence and the smarter one.
[P1.6] Third, the exception never stays small. Governments that keep capital punishment "only for terrorism" discover how elastic the word terrorism becomes once a death sentence is available for it. Journalists, dissidents, and minorities get charged under those statutes in country after country. Abolition is the only rule that cannot be stretched.
[P1.7] And practically, abolitionist allies will not extradite suspects to face death, so a death penalty costs you the very cooperation terrorism cases depend on.
[P1.8] Permanent imprisonment punishes, protects, and can be corrected. Execution offers only the one thing a serious state should never gamble: finality it cannot take back.

[C1.7] Finally, PRO's "elastic statute" worry is an argument against authoritarian law, not against punishment. Regimes that hang dissidents will imprison them just as readily. Fix the definition; don't surrender justice for the worst offenders.
