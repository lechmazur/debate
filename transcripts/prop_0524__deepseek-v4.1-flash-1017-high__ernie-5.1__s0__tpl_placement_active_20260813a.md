[P1.1] The migration debate is stuck between two bad defaults: an open-ended flow that outruns local services, and a stop-start system that leaves universities and employers unable to plan. The proposition offers the obvious third option: a hard, capacity-based ceiling, with guaranteed allocations inside it.
[P1.2] The mechanism is simple. Local housing, health, school and transport capacity is assessed and translated into a binding regional cap. Within that cap, universities and employers receive guaranteed allocations—not unlimited visas, not a free pass. They can recruit only up to their allocation, and that allocation exists only because local capacity has been signed off.
[P1.3] If there are no beds, no GP appointments, no school places, the cap does not rise. If a university or employer wants more, it must help create the capacity: build or contract accommodation, fund local services, spread demand across regions.
[P1.4] This is better than both extremes. Purely market-driven migration lets thousands of people arrive in one city before the housing office or health service has any chance to respond. Purely restrictive caps without guarantees produce the opposite failure: a university cannot invest in a lab or a course because it does not know whether its students will be allowed in; a care provider cannot offer a contract because next year’s rules may change. Guaranteed allocations convert migration from a shock into a planned input.
[P1.5] The guarantee also changes employer behaviour. If an employer’s allocation depends on local capacity, it has a reason to provide decent housing and avoid cramming workers into overcrowded accommodation. The cap protects communities from being expected to absorb endless growth without infrastructure. And because allocations are capped, no institution can hoard labour or use migration to undercut local wages without limit.
[P1.6] The real question is not whether migration should be controlled—it should. It is whether control should be a number plucked from political pressure or a budget set by the schools, surgeries and homes that actually have to absorb people. Capped, guaranteed, capacity-linked allocations are the only version that gives both certainty and consent.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds orderly, but it replaces a messy but adaptable system with a rigid, politically‑driven allocation that will entrench inequality, stifle innovation and hand power to the very institutions it pretends to regulate. First, tying migration numbers to local housing and service capacity freezes regional development. Capacities are measured today, yet economies shift quickly; a city that builds new hospitals or university labs tomorrow cannot instantly expand its migration quota, even when skilled workers are needed. The result is a mismatch: booming regions are throttled while declining areas cling to guaranteed slots they cannot fill, wasting visas and discouraging investment.
[C1.2] Second, guaranteeing allocations to universities and employers creates perverse incentives. Institutions will lobby for larger caps not to meet genuine labor needs but to secure a steady supply of cheap, foreign talent they can undercut local wages with. The “capacity‑linked” ceiling becomes a bargaining chip; wealthy campuses can fund extra housing to inflate their quota, while smaller schools are left with crumbs. This entrenches a two‑tier system where elite universities and large corporations capture the lion’s share of migration, leaving community colleges and small firms marginalized.
[C1.3] Third, the bureaucratic apparatus required to assess, certify and police capacity is massive and vulnerable to capture. Local councils already struggle with housing planning; adding a migration‑allocation layer multiplies complexity, opens doors for rent‑seeking, and slows decision‑making. When the system falters—because a sudden pandemic spikes health demand or a housing boom stalls—rigid guarantees prevent rapid reallocation, forcing workers into limbo and employers into costly contingency planning.
[C1.4] Finally, the premise that caps tied to services protect communities is illusory. Communities need flexible responses to demographic change, not a static quota that can be gamed. By institutionalising a fixed share of migration for select actors, the policy substitutes democratic deliberation with technocratic rationing, undermining both economic dynamism and social cohesion.
