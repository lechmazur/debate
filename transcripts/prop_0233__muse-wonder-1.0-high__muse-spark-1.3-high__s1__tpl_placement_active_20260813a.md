[P1.1] A teenager can hide a knife in a waistband, board a bus across town, and use it in seconds. That is why knife and gang violence defeats ordinary policing: the weapon is concealable, the attack is fast, and retaliation is planned in private messages and repaid the same night. If officers must already have strong, individualised suspicion before they can search, the law effectively tells them to intervene after the stabbing, not before it.
[P1.2] Expanding preventive stop-and-search changes that calculation in two ways. First, it gets weapons off the street during the narrow window when they are being carried to a confrontation, a drug deal, or a revenge attack. Second, and more importantly, it deters carrying in the first place. Carrying becomes a risk, not an insurance policy, when police can search in a designated hotspot without first watching a crime unfold.
[P1.3] Gangs rely on the confidence that their youngest members can move blades undetected; remove that confidence and you disrupt the whole logistics of violence.
[P1.4] This is a proportionate trade. A brief, recorded stop on a public street is an inconvenience. A knife wound is irreversible, and its costs fall overwhelmingly on the same poor neighbourhoods that suffer gang intimidation, closed shops, and children afraid to take certain routes home. Protecting a suspect's freedom from being searched cannot outweigh residents' freedom to live without fear, or a victim's right to life.
[P1.5] Expansion does not mean arbitrary power. Powers can be time-limited, geographically targeted to proven hotspots, authorised by senior officers, filmed on body cameras, and audited with published data. Europe already accepts preventive searching at airports, stadiums, and major public events because concentrated risk justifies earlier intervention. A street corner with repeated stabbings deserves at least that logic.
[P1.6] Youth services, mentoring, and jobs matter, but they do not disarm someone tonight. Preventive policing does, and without it every long-term strategy arrives too late for the next victim.

[C1.1] Expanded suspicionless searching promises to intercept knives before stabbings, but the maths defeats it. Knives are small, widely owned, and carried by a tiny fraction of people at any moment. Sweeping hundreds to find one blade means thousands of innocent stops, almost all fruitless, while determined carriers adapt routes, times, stash weapons, or use younger children police hesitate to search. Activity rises; availability of knives does not fall.
[C1.2] Nor does fear of being searched deter those most at risk. A teenager ordered to carry by older gang members, or terrified of a rival estate, does not weigh police procedure like a lawyer. The immediate threat of humiliation or death outweighs the distant possibility of a stop. If anything, hostile, unpredictable searches confirm that the street is dangerous and that adults offer no protection, reinforcing the logic of arming oneself.
[C1.3] The burden is not shared inconvenience. In practice wider powers concentrate on young, poor, and minority boys in already over-policed neighbourhoods — the same communities PRO claims to protect. Being repeatedly spread against a wall on the way home from school is not minor; it teaches contempt for law, chokes cooperation, and dries up the witness testimony and family tips that actually solve gang murders. You cannot inform on a gang to officers you fear.
[C1.4] Europe already knows better tools: intelligence-led stops based on genuine suspicion, focused work on known carriers, mediation of feuds, amnesties, schools and jobs that pull recruiters’ audience away. Airports and stadiums prove nothing; everyone is searched equally to enter voluntarily. Street powers search selectively under compulsion. Expanding them trades the trust that prevents violence for the illusion of control, leaving the knives and deepening the gangs’ hold.

[P2.1] Their maths assumes police will wander Europe stopping everyone at random. That is not this expansion: time-limited, senior-authorised powers in micro-hotspots — the estate stairwell, bus route, or school gate where threats are circulating. In that narrow place and window, the denominator is not the general public, it is people in a known flashpoint when retaliation is expected.
[P2.2] Even then, counting only seized blades misses the mechanism. If carriers change routes, stash knives, delay a revenge ride, or send someone else because a search zone is live, CON calls that adaptation. It is disruption. A stashed blade is not in a waistband during an argument.
[P2.3] A delayed retaliation gives mediation, families, and intelligence time to work. Friction is prevention when attacks depend on speed and confidence.
[P2.4] Their deterrence claim proves too much. A frightened teenager may not calculate perfectly, but gang organisers do calculate logistics — who carries, when, and along which route. Raise the risk of routine carrying and you need not deter every desperate act; you deter the normalisation of carrying as daily insurance. And the answer to ‘he fears rivals more than police’ is not to leave the street to rivals, but to make police visibly present before the rival encounter.
[P2.5] The trust argument also ignores who pays for police absence. Repeated searches done badly can alienate, which is why recording, senior sign-off, published audits, and strict place-time limits matter. But repeated unsolved stabbings alienate faster. Residents who will not let children play out do not experience inaction as neutrality; they experience abandonment, and they stop cooperating because cooperation does not keep them safe.
[P2.6] Finally, their alternatives depend on the very information gap they create. Intelligence-led stops work for known carriers, not for the younger cousin recruited this week or the first retaliation after an online threat. Mediation and youth work are essential, but they cannot search the boy heading to a pre-arranged fight. Preventive powers fill that tonight-sized gap; the alternatives do not replace them.
