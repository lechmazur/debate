[P1.1] Domestic subsidies and regulations can clean production inside the United States, but they cannot clean the emissions America imports — and over the next five years that blind spot decides whether global emissions fall.
[P1.2] First, a border adjustment stops leakage. When U.S. rules raise the cost of making steel, aluminum, cement, or chemicals at home without a comparable charge on imports, production does not disappear; it moves. A ton of steel shifted from a relatively efficient U.S. mill to a coal-heavy mill abroad can leave global emissions higher even as the domestic ledger improves. Subsidies soften that pressure for a few favored projects, but they do not change the price signal facing every import.
[P1.3] A carbon border adjustment does: foreign goods face a cost tied to their embedded emissions, so dirty production loses its advantage and cleaner U.S. production is not punished for cleaning up.
[P1.4] Second, a border adjustment exports the incentive to cut carbon. The United States is too large a consumer market for exporters to simply abandon. To preserve access and lower the fee, foreign producers have a reason to measure their emissions, switch fuels, improve efficiency, and invest in lower-carbon processes — reductions domestic subsidies cannot buy outside U.S. borders. Trading partners gain a reason to price carbon themselves, because a domestic price can reduce what their exporters pay at the border while keeping revenue at home.
[P1.5] That creates diffusion, not displacement.
[P1.6] Subsidies and regulations alone work on only one side of the trade equation, take years to permit and litigate, and leave import-intensive heavy industry exposed. A border adjustment reaches emissions that domestic policy misses, prevents backsliding through leakage, and turns U.S. market access into leverage for cuts abroad. That broader reach is why it reduces global emissions faster over the next five years.

[C1.3] These actions can begin reducing emissions quickly, especially when standards create certainty and subsidies cover capital costs.

[C1.5] Retaliation could further weaken trade and investment channels.

A border adjustment may complement such policies, but it has no demonstrated advantage on the five-year test merely by changing border prices. PRO bears the burden of showing that trade diversion is outweighed by additional global cuts.
