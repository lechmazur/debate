[P1.1] Widening urban freeways is a proven losing bet for cities. Induced demand means every added lane draws more drivers, erasing congestion gains within 18 to 24 months while locking in decades of higher emissions, noise pollution, and sprawl. Doubling down on this failed model wastes billions in public funds that could be spent on durable, equitable fixes, while committing cities to long-term maintenance costs that crowd out funding for schools and social services.
[P1.2] Removing freeways instead breaks this cycle. Cities that have torn down elevated urban freeways report faster, more stable congestion relief than those that chose widening, while reclaiming massive swaths of prime central land. That land can be repurposed for affordable housing, public transit corridors, or green space, delivering far more value per dollar than a wider road that will clog again in two years. Removal also cuts maintenance costs drastically: surface boulevards require a fraction of the upkeep of elevated multi-lane freeways.
[P1.3] Widening also entrenches historic inequities. Urban freeways were disproportionately routed through marginalized neighborhoods, displacing residents, suppressing local property values, and concentrating toxic tailpipe emissions in low-income areas. Expanding these roadways only deepens that harm, while removal lets cities repair divided communities, restore local business districts, and cut pollution burden for residents who have borne the brunt of freeway harm for generations. Freeway-adjacent homes also sell for far less than comparable non-adjacent properties, stripping wealth from already disadvantaged households.
[P1.4] The only viable metric for city planning is long-term livability, not short-term driver convenience. CON will frame widening as a necessary fix for gridlock, but the pattern is clear: widening solves nothing, while removal delivers lasting gains for transit, housing, public health, and community cohesion. Even by CON’s narrow peak-hour speed metric, widening fails: average commute times in widening-focused cities have risen faster than in those that removed freeways. Cities should default to removal first, widening only as a last, rarely used resort.
