[P1.1] Every year, thousands of people die waiting for a kidney that never comes. The shortage is not a mystery. We ask strangers to undergo major surgery and forbid anyone from compensating them for it.
[P1.2] Then we act surprised that altruism alone falls short.
[P1.3] The real choice is between two kinds of market, not between a market and no market. Prohibition has not abolished organ selling; it has driven it underground. Transplant tourism and trafficking networks thrive because desperate patients will pay and desperate sellers will sell. In that shadow market, brokers take most of the money, donors get no screening and no follow-up care, and recipients risk infection and rejection.
[P1.4] Prohibition leaves the poor with the worst version of the transaction.
[P1.5] Strict regulation replaces that with something humane. Picture it concretely: the government or a single public agency is the only purchaser. Compensation is fixed, so there is no bidding and no auction for the rich. Organs are allocated by medical need through the existing waitlist, never by the donor's choice of buyer.
[P1.6] Donors receive rigorous medical and psychological screening, a mandatory waiting period, and guaranteed lifelong health coverage. Brokers are banned outright. This is not a bazaar. It is a public health program that finally stops treating donors as the only unpaid participants in a system where surgeons, hospitals, and coordinators are all paid.
[P1.7] We already accept this logic elsewhere. Plasma donors, egg donors, surrogates, and workers in dangerous jobs receive payment for bearing risk to their bodies. Living kidney donation carries real but modest long-term risk. Nobody would call it dignified to let someone take that risk for free but criminal to let them receive fair compensation.
[P1.8] Iran has long operated a regulated paid kidney system and has reportedly reduced its kidney waitlist far below what most prohibition countries achieve. Regulation saves lives that prohibition lets die.
