[P1.1] Even the word "anonymized" does heavy lifting in this motion, and that is precisely where the problem begins. In a workplace setting, population-level genetic data is drawn from a small, identifiable community of employees. Aggregation provides a thin veil: when an employer knows that, say, 15% of its workforce carries a BRCA variant, that information is never truly detached from the people who carry it. The smaller the population, the thinner the anonymization.
[P1.2] A workplace is not a country.
[P1.3] Once employers can use aggregate genetic health data to shape wellness programs or insurance design, the incentive structure is clear. Wellness programs reframed around genetic risk profiles shift the burden of health management onto individuals whose "risk" was assigned at conception. Insurance design informed by genetic prevalence invites subtle underwriting: higher copays for conditions concentrated in the workforce, narrow networks for the "wrong" specialties, or plan structures that quietly discourage employees with costly genetic profiles from staying. None of this requires malice.
[P1.4] It requires only the ordinary tools of cost management aimed at data the employer was never supposed to hold.
[P1.5] The prohibition matters because it blocks the pipeline before it opens. If employers can collect and act on aggregated genetic data for benevolent purposes, they will also use it when purposes turn managerial. The line between "wellness" and "surveillance" is not drawn by intent; it is drawn by power. Employees cannot opt out of their genome.
[P1.6] They cannot renegotiate the terms under which their collective genetic profile informs the benefits they receive. Strict prohibition is the only standard that matches the asymmetry.
[P1.7] This is not about whether genetic data could improve health outcomes in the abstract. It is about whether employers, given the data and the motive, can be trusted to use it without distorting the employment relationship. The answer is no, and the proposition stands as the necessary guardrail.

[C1.5] Under "strictly prohibited," that is forbidden — and the workforces that lose most are the ones whose conditions the default plan already ignores.
