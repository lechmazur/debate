[P1.1] Fundamental science is a long game. The discoveries that reshape civilization—CRISPR gene editing, mRNA vaccine platforms, semiconductor physics—rarely emerge from three-year project cycles. They emerge from decades of sustained inquiry where researchers can follow unexpected leads without watching the funding clock expire. Short-cycle competitive grants systematically punish exactly this kind of work.
[P1.2] They force scientists to propose narrow, safe projects with predictable outcomes, because a failed hypothesis in year two means no renewal in year three. The result is a research ecosystem optimized for publication counts and grant-writing prowess, not for the high-risk, high-reward exploration that produces transformational knowledge.
[P1.3] Block grants invert this logic. By providing stable, multi-year funding to labs or institutions, governments buy the freedom to pursue genuinely difficult questions. A researcher studying protein folding, quantum materials, or climate feedback loops needs a decade, not a biennium. Block funding eliminates the perverse incentive to fragment work into grant-sized chunks and removes the administrative burden that consumes a substantial share of researcher time—time that belongs at the bench.
[P1.4] When funding is secure, scientists take intellectual risks. They chase anomalies. They build the deep expertise that makes serendipity possible.
[P1.5] The proposition accepts reduced accountability per dollar, and that trade-off is not merely acceptable—it is necessary. The accountability demanded by short-cycle grants is largely performative: it measures compliance with proposal timelines and publication quotas, not whether the science actually advanced human understanding. True accountability for fundamental research cannot be measured in quarterly deliverables. It is measured in whether, twenty years later, the funded work enabled a cure, a technology, or a new field.
[P1.6] Block grants shift accountability from bureaucratic process to institutional stewardship—trusting proven research teams to deliver transformative science rather than policing their monthly progress reports. The cost of that trust is worth paying when the alternative is a system that reliably funds mediocrity and starves genius.

[C1.1] The case for block grants rests on a seductive story: give brilliant people money and leave them alone, and transformation follows. The story is false, and the proposition's own framing admits it—"even at the cost of less accountability per dollar" is a confession that we'd be trading away the only mechanism that distinguishes productive science from expensive drift.
[C1.2] Consider who actually loses under block grants. Money flows to institutions and labs that already have it. A twenty-eight-year-old postdoc with a heterodox idea about gene editing competes against a department chair with forty steady hands and a building. Competitive grants, whatever their friction, are one of the few levers that let new entrants break in.
[C1.3] Block grants calcify the hierarchy: the incumbents get guaranteed income, and the next generation gets whatever's left. That is not how fields get transformed—it's how they get stale.
[C1.4] The serendipity argument is also overdrawn. CRISPR, mRNA platforms, and semiconductor physics did not emerge from unconstrained blank checks; they emerged from scientists who had to keep convincing peers their direction was worth pursuing. Even the counterexamples PRO will reach for—the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, for instance—are not block grants. They are intensely competitive long-term awards, renewed through rigorous review.
[C1.5] That model proves the opposite of this proposition: the solution to short-termism is better competition, longer competitive horizons, not the abolition of accountability.
[C1.6] And notice what "performative accountability" misses. Yes, progress reports are imperfect. But review forces scientists to ask whether the work is going anywhere—and to abandon lines that aren't. Without it, we get labs that drift for a decade on questions nobody stopped to interrogate.
[C1.7] The taxpayer funds that drift invisibly, because by definition no report flags it.
[C1.8] Fundamental science is a public investment measured in billions. A system that says "trust us, we'll show results in twenty years" isn't tolerating risk—it's abandoning feedback. Competitive grants, lengthened and improved, give us both freedom and correction. The motion asks us to give up the second.
[C1.9] We shouldn't.

[P2.1] CON mistakes the mechanism for the mission. Howard Hughes investigators are competitively selected, yes—but once chosen, they receive roughly seven-year terms with minimal reporting burdens. That is a block grant to an individual, wrapped in a competitive entry ticket. The proposition does not oppose merit-based selection; it opposes the tyranny of the three-year project cycle.
[P2.2] We can use rigorous peer review to choose recipients, then trust them for a decade. CON conflates entry competition with ongoing surveillance, treating the selection process as if it must be replicated every twenty-four months.
[P2.3] The hierarchy argument inverts reality. Competitive project grants structurally favor incumbents who possess preliminary data, established labs, and grant-writing staff. A twenty-eight-year-old postdoc with a heterodox idea cannot win a project grant against a department chair because reviewers demand preliminary results the postdoc cannot yet generate. Block grants, when distributed with early-career carve-outs or across a broader institutional pool, remove the grant-writing tax that favors the well-resourced and allow risky ideas to find a home without requiring venture capital.
[P2.4] CON fears invisible drift, but short-cycle grants produce their own pathology: researchers chasing the next funding cycle rather than the truth. Block grants replace milestone compliance with scientific accountability—peer judgment of the cumulative body of work, hiring decisions, and reputational standing. A lab that produces nothing for ten years loses credibility, staff, and future funding. That is accountability measured in intellectual capital, not quarterly deliverables.
[P2.5] The choice is not between accountability and drift. It is between performative compliance and substantive stewardship. CON defends a system that funds safe mediocrity to avoid the risk of funding a genius. We should fund the genius.

[P4.1] A1: The distinction is structural, not semantic. A block grant is defined by what happens during the funding period: stable, unrestricted support without quarterly milestone policing or the threat of mid-course termination. Entry competition selects recipients; renewal review judges the cumulative result at the end. That is precisely the model that produced CRISPR and mRNA platforms—long-term trust anchored by rigorous selection, not the twenty-four-month project cycle CON defends.
[P4.2] What remains of the block grant is the freedom to pursue multi-year questions without fragmenting them into grant-sized deliverables or abandoning lines that don't fit the next reporting period.
[P4.3] A2: Block grants need not flow through department chairs. They can be awarded directly to individual investigators—early-career researchers included—or institutions can be required to run transparent internal competitions for allocation. The twenty-eight-year-old postdoc applies for a five-year individual block grant or an early-career set-aside, judged on the promise of the idea rather than a preliminary data portfolio she cannot yet possess. The alternative is worse: project grants demand exactly the track record that entrenches incumbents and excludes the heterodox.
[P4.4] This is the decisive point. CON treats accountability as a dial to be turned up continuously; fundamental science requires it to be turned down during the work and up at the end. The motion accepts less accountability per dollar because the accountability lost—quarterly compliance—buys only safe mediocrity, while the accountability retained—entry merit and final peer review—buys the freedom to solve genuinely hard problems. When a researcher has a decade of stable funding, she can chase the anomaly that leads to the next field.
[P4.5] When she has three years, she chases the renewal. We should choose the system that funds the decade, not the one that funds the proposal.
