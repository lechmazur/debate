[P1.1] Waiting for a full global treaty before recognizing lunar resource rights is a recipe for either permanent paralysis or an unregulated scramble. History proves this: the 1979 Moon Agreement attempted to defer resource rules to a future global regime, and every major spacefaring nation refused to ratify it. In today’s fractured geopolitical climate, demanding universal consensus before a single ton of lunar ice is sold does not produce harmony; it holds technological progress hostage to the slowest, most obstructionist states.
[P1.2] Worse, it leaves actual lunar operations in a legal vacuum where state-backed actors operate in secret while law-abiding commercial firms are starved of investment.
[P1.3] Recognizing commercial extraction and sale is entirely consistent with existing international law. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibits national appropriation of the Moon’s territory, not the use and sale of extracted materials. The logic is identical to the high seas: no nation or company may claim sovereignty over international waters, yet fishermen legally own and sell the fish they catch. Extending that principle to lunar regolith and water ice unlocks the private capital necessary to turn lunar ice into rocket propellant and regolith into radiation shielding—transforming the Moon from a sterile museum into a stepping stone for deep-space science.
[P1.4] Most importantly, conditional recognition gives us immediate regulatory leverage where it matters most. The urgent threat on the Moon is not a company selling water; it is physical interference and contamination. High-velocity dust plumes from uncoordinated landings can sandblast scientific instruments miles away, and careless operations can clutter cis-lunar orbits with debris. By tying legal title over extracted resources directly to strict transparency and debris-control obligations, we turn property rights into an enforcement mechanism.
[P1.5] Operators desperately want legal certainty to secure insurance, financing, and buyers. Offering that certainty in exchange for open operational disclosure and strict debris mitigation gives the world safe, transparent lunar activity today—rather than empty diplomatic debates while the Moon is degraded in the dark.
