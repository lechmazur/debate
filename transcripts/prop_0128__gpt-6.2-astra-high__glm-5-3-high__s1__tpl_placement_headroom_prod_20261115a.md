That phrase does all the work, and it cannot do it. Job requirements are rarely necessary for performance in the strict sense. No single credential, interview, or reference is *necessary* — plenty of great hires lack any given one.
[C1.2] If the standard is strict necessity, the rule proves too much: it would forbid considering references, portfolios, or years of experience too. If the standard is loose, then the employer's own judgment about what counts as "genuinely necessary" is exactly what a degree requirement is, and the proposition becomes an empty command to do what it says employers may already do.
[C1.3] The payroll example is telling, but not in my opponent's favor. Nobody claims a degree *guarantees* a good payroll coordinator. Requirements are floors, not verdicts. Employers screen thousands of applicants with limited time and real legal exposure; a degree is a cheap, uniform, defensible signal of persistence, baseline literacy, and trainability — traits that predict performance across many roles even when no specific course is used on the job.
[C1.4] My opponent's alternative — sample tasks, structured scoring, open-ended questions — is exactly the kind of costly, litigation-prone assessment machinery that small and mid-sized employers cannot build for every position. [C1.5] And there is a systemic stake: when employers individually abandon degree floors, the private incentive to finish education weakens for exactly the students on the margin.
