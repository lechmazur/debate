[P1.1] Parents already hold legal responsibility for nearly every domain of a teenager's life — school enrollment, medical care, curfews, what enters the home. The proposition simply extends that settled authority to the one environment where it currently evaporates: the device in the teenager's pocket. Nothing about parenting ethics changes because the babysitter is made of silicon.
[P1.2] The key word in this motion is "operating-system-level," and it's doing real work. App-level controls have failed for a decade, and the failure is structural. Any single app can be deleted, reinstalled under a fresh account with a fake birthdate, or replaced by a clone.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, recommendation algorithms are engineered by teams whose entire business depends on maximizing watch time; a fourteen-year-old's prefrontal cortex is not a fair opponent. OS-level limits flip this: the rules live where the platform controls the levers — app installs by age rating, screen time windows, recommendation categories — so enforcement is uniform rather than a hopeless game of whack-a-mole across forty apps. "Cannot easily bypass" doesn't mean unhackable; it means parents aren't forced into an arms race against YouTube tutorials.
[P1.4] Consider the concrete stakes. Sleep deprivation in adolescents is driven substantially by late-night device use. Predatory contact, gambling-adjacent loot mechanics, and extreme content reach teens precisely through recommendation engines optimized for engagement, not wellbeing. A parent who installs age and recommendation limits isn't censoring ideas; they're restoring the friction that every playground, movie theater, and school library already applies to children.
[P1.5] And notice the narrowness of what's defended: age ratings and recommendation curation — not message monitoring, not location tracking, not reading a diary. This is the digital equivalent of keeping R-rated films and slot machines out of reach, not surveillance.
[P1.6] Teenagers deserve increasing autonomy, and good parents calibrate. But calibration is only possible when the tool actually works. The proposition gives parents a functional, proportionate instrument. The status quo gives them settings screens their kids route around in an afternoon.

[C1.2] The instrument is far blunter than “age and recommendation limits” suggests. Age ratings vary by store, culture, and moral panic, and recommendation curation is not neutral filtering. An OS-level override means the platform decides before context: a 16-year-old researching sexual health, eating disorders, or LGBTQ identity can be steered away from highly recommended material without the parent ever choosing that specific result. A rule that cannot be bypassed lightly by apps becomes a rule the teenager cannot contest when it misfires.
[C1.3] “Not easily bypassed” is also a liability. It does not teach self-regulation; it teaches that autonomy is forbidden. The harder the lock, the more it punishes the kid who obeys it and rewards the one who finds the riskiest detour.
[C1.4] Finally, the motion imagines well-calibrated parents. But we do not build escape-proof domestic infrastructure on that assumption. OS-level controls are broad, quiet, and hard for an adolescent to challenge—exactly the tools that coercive, negligent, or catastrophically anxious parents can misuse. Legal responsibility for a child is not a blank check to automate every boundary.
[C1.5] If parents need help, app-level rules, shared agreements, and graduated privileges allow supervision without turning a personal device into a remote-control architecture. The proposition fails because it sacrifices the very competence and trust that adolescence is supposed to build.
