[P1.1] The question isn't whether a sugar-sweetened beverage tax is regressive in isolation—it's whether lower-income households end up better off with the tax than without it. That is the real test, and the answer is yes.
[P1.2] Sugar-sweetened beverages are not a neutral good. They are the single largest source of added sugar in many diets, and their consumption tracks closely with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease—conditions that already devastate lower-income communities at disproportionate rates. A tax high enough to meaningfully shift consumption changes that trajectory.
[P1.3] Yes, lower-income households spend a larger share of income on these beverages. But they also suffer most from the diseases these beverages cause. A tax that reduces consumption delivers a health benefit that is sharply progressive: the households paying most into the tax are the same ones reaping the most health benefit from changed behavior. Calling the tax "regressive" without counting the health side of the ledger is accounting fraud disguised as compassion.
[P1.4] Consider tobacco. Cigarette taxes were called regressive too. They still saved millions of lives, disproportionately among lower-income smokers who were more price-sensitive and more likely to quit. Nobody serious argues today that we should repeal cigarette taxes because poor smokers paid them.
[P1.5] The same logic applies here.
[P1.6] The alternative—doing nothing, or taxing too lightly to matter—preserves a status quo where lower-income families face cheap, ubiquitous, disease-promoting products and bear the consequences in lost years, medical debt, and diminished quality of life. A meaningful SSB tax disrupts that status quo. It makes the healthier choice the easier choice, and it does so most forcefully exactly where the harm is greatest.
[P1.7] That is not a burden. It is a rescue with a price tag.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept a known, immediate burden on lower-income households in exchange for a speculative, delayed, and uneven health benefit. That is not a rescue; it is a regressive gamble with other people's budgets.
[C1.2] A tax “high enough to meaningfully shift consumption” must be large enough to sting. For low-income families, a small increase in beverage prices is not a nudge; it is a claim on scarce income. If they keep buying the taxed drinks, they pay more while receiving no direct health gain. If they cut back, the burden still falls hardest on those least able to absorb it.
[C1.3] And if they substitute, there is no guarantee they move to healthier options. A household facing tight food budgets may switch to cheaper, calorie-dense alternatives, or to whatever is available, affordable, and shelf-stable. The tax does not ensure clean water, affordable milk, or better diets; it only makes one category more expensive.
[C1.4] PRO’s central move is to count health benefits while ignoring that the policy’s incidence remains regressive. The motion does not require the revenue to be returned. Health benefits are conditional and uncertain. Sugar-sweetened beverages contribute to excess calories and diabetes risk, but they are not like cigarettes.
[C1.5] Tobacco is harmful in any amount, and smokers often face a clear cessation goal. Beverages are consumed in moderation, and their harms depend on total diet, activity, and access to alternatives. A blunt price penalty aimed at one product does little about food deserts, marketing, stress, or underfunded clinics—factors that drive poor health in low-income communities.
[C1.6] The better path is to improve health without taxing poverty: subsidize water and healthy drinks, require reformulation, restrict misleading marketing, and invest in primary prevention. Those tools can be progressive, targeted, and durable. Because the motion accepts disproportionate burden on lower-income households as tolerable, and because the promised benefits are far less certain than that burden, the proposition fails.
