[P1.1] Children under fourteen lack the cognitive and moral development to understand criminal responsibility as adults do. Neuroscience confirms the prefrontal cortex—critical for impulse control and consequence assessment—is not fully mature until the mid-20s, and even at fourteen, capacity is still limited. Holding younger children fully punishable ignores this biological reality and risks entrenching antisocial behavior through premature exposure to the criminal justice system, which can lead to a lifetime of stigma, reduced opportunities, and increased likelihood of future offending, further harming their development.
[P1.2] Punitive approaches consistently fail this age group. Jurisdictions with low ages of responsibility, like England and Wales at ten, see higher recidivism because punishment does not address root causes such as trauma or poverty. Rehabilitation-focused systems achieve better outcomes, and raising the age to fourteen would redirect resources toward intervention programs that actually reduce reoffending. Moreover, incarcerating young children is costly and ineffective compared to community-based interventions, which have proven more effective in reducing youth crime.
[P1.3] The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child has urged states to raise the minimum age, noting that ages below twelve are not internationally acceptable, reinforcing the global consensus that younger ages are inappropriate.
[P1.4] Morally, it is unjust to equate a child’s actions with adult culpability. Children cannot reasonably foresee consequences like adults, and they lack the autonomy to resist coercion or adverse environments. Treating them as fully punishable offenders undermines proportional justice and the principle that punishment should fit the criminal’s capacity, not to mention the long-term social costs of damaged young lives. Fourteen is a reasonable floor that reflects developmental science, aligns with international norms in most of Europe, and avoids the absurdity of prosecuting seven-year-olds for minor offenses.
[P1.5] The alternative perpetuates a system that harms children and fails to make society safer.

[C1.1] The motion rests on a sleight of hand: that a low age of criminal responsibility means treating children as "fully punishable offenders." It does not. In England, Canada, or Australia, criminal responsibility for a ten- or twelve-year-old triggers a distinct youth justice track—welfare assessments, supervision orders, mandated treatment—not adult courts and adult prisons. PRO is attacking a system that barely exists.
[C1.2] The real choice is whether the law may respond to serious wrongdoing before age fourteen at all.
[C1.3] That response matters most in the hardest cases. When two ten-year-olds murdered James Bulger, society needed a formal mechanism: fact-finding, public acknowledgment for the victim's family, and secure intervention for two dangerous children. Under PRO's blanket rule, a thirteen-year-old who commits rape or homicide is untouchable by the criminal process no matter how clearly they understood what they did. The victim's family gets nothing; the child gets, at best, voluntary services they are free to refuse.
[C1.4] Their own neuroscience cuts against them. Young brains are plastic; intervention works best early. A hard floor of fourteen tells the state to wait—through years of escalating behavior—until patterns are entrenched and the window for change is closing. Criminal responsibility is often the legal key that unlocks compulsory supervision and treatment.
[C1.5] Raising the age does not redirect children toward help; it removes the only lever that can compel it.
[C1.6] A single chronological line is also developmentally dishonest. Capacity varies. Several jurisdictions already require proof that a young child grasped the wrongness of the act. An individualized capacity test protects the genuinely uncomprehending child far better than an arbitrary birthday that declares every thirteen-year-old identical.
[C1.7] Finally, there is no global consensus. Canada, England, Scotland, Switzerland—functioning democracies with strong child protections—set ages below fourteen and pair them with rehabilitative systems. One UN committee's recommendation is not settled science.
[C1.8] The question is not punishment versus protection. It is whether the law keeps a calibrated tool or accepts a blind spot.

[P2.2] Modern legal systems already possess robust alternative mechanisms—civil commitment orders, child protection proceedings, and secure welfare placements—that can address even extreme youth violence without branding children as criminals for life.
[P2.3] CON’s attempt to co-opt neuroscience fails spectacularly. [P2.4] Their claim that criminal responsibility provides the only lever for compulsory treatment is demonstrably false. Individualized capacity tests are a false precision, forcing vulnerable children into adversarial courtrooms, imposing impossible burdens, and yielding arbitrary outcomes. A clear line at fourteen reflects the scientific consensus that most under-14s lack mature judgment, while preserving non-criminal options for genuine exceptions.
[P2.5] The UN’s recommendation and Europe’s prevailing practice demonstrate a growing consensus: childhood itself should not be criminalized.
