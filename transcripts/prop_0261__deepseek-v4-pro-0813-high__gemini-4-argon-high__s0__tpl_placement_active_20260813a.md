[P1.1] The default should be that what you buy survives you. Today a digital library does not. A person may spend thousands on ebooks, films, games, music, or software, yet the moment they die the platform treats the collection as extinguished. That is not because the files have vanished or because heirs demand a new copy.
[P1.2] It is only because a clickwrap license says the purchase was personal and non-transferable. Inheritance law should override that restriction.
[P1.3] Purchased digital assets function as property. Consumers pay full price, build them over decades, and reasonably regard them as permanent holdings. If a shelf of paperbacks, a CD collection, or a boxed software disk passes to next of kin, the digital equivalent should too. The law does not allow sellers to reclaim physical goods at death through a fine-print label; digital sellers should not enjoy that windfall.
[P1.4] The stakes are both financial and personal. An heir may need access to a deceased relative’s professional software, purchased research library, or family photo archive. These collections can hold real economic and sentimental value. Allowing them to die with the account grants platforms a double benefit: they keep the original payment and can sell the same content again to someone else.
[P1.5] The obvious objection is that these are licenses, not sales. But that label begs the question. Copyright law can regulate reproduction without blocking inheritance of a purchased license. Transfer to an heir does not multiply copies; it shifts the same access from the deceased to their next of kin.
[P1.6] A statutory guarantee can also be narrow: it would cover paid, permanent purchases, not rentals or subscription streams, and would not require opening private messages or the full account.
[P1.7] The rule is simple. If the decedent paid for a digital item and could lawfully use it while alive, that item should pass unless it was a temporary rental or subscription. This ends an artificial exception to inheritance and honors the basic expectation that a person’s library is part of their estate.

When you buy a physical book, the publisher’s obligation ends at the register, and the object naturally degrades. When you buy an ebook, game, or software license, you buy ongoing access tied to server infrastructure, security updates, and third-party royalty contracts. Because digital files never wear out, platforms price personal licenses for a single human lifespan—not for eternity.
[C1.2] Overriding these contracts by statute will destroy the very purchasing model my opponent wants to save. The opening concedes [P1.6] that a mandate would cover only permanent purchases, leaving rentals and subscriptions untouched. Consider how the market will react. If platforms and creators are legally barred from pricing a license for one lifetime, they will not simply absorb the cost of servicing multi-generational family dynasties.
[C1.3] They will either hike upfront prices or abandon one-time purchases entirely, shifting exclusively to subscriptions and expiring rentals. Living consumers will pay more and own less.
[C1.4] Furthermore, transferring digital libraries is neither simple nor frictionless. First, to correct a confusion in the opening [P1.4]: family photos are user-generated files, not non-transferable purchased licenses. Purchased media libraries are bound to underlying intellectual property agreements and single-user architectures. If a parent leaves three children as next of kin, how does one account pass?
[C1.5] A statutory guarantee forces every platform to act as a probate administrator—verifying competing family claims, engineering systems to split indivisible single-user licenses among multiple heirs, and renegotiating royalty terms with creators.
[C1.6] Finally, a legal guarantee erodes privacy. Even without opening private messages [P1.6], a person’s reading, viewing, and gaming history is deeply intimate. Automatically sweeping purchased libraries into probate exposes private habits to relatives by default. We should not upend digital commerce and penalize living consumers to rewrite contracts after death.
