[P1.1] Criminal conviction must rest on evidence whose meaning can be tested. AI-generated summaries, translations, and reconstructions do not merely display a source; they transform it. In that transformation, a system may omit context, resolve ambiguity, or invent detail while sounding completely confident. A mistranslation can turn uncertainty into intent; a summary can erase an exculpatory qualifier; a reconstruction can present guessed pixels as observed reality.
[P1.2] Polished output makes those errors especially dangerous because jurors may mistake fluency for accuracy.
[P1.3] Traditional safeguards depend on accountability. A witness can be cross-examined. An expert must explain methods, assumptions, and limitations. But an AI output has no accountable speaker.
[P1.4] Counsel cannot ask it why one translation was chosen, what context it ignored, or which details were inferred. Model updates, prompt choices, and opaque internal processes can also make reproduction difficult. Proving that a file came from a particular system establishes provenance—not truth.
[P1.5] The proposed rule is a presumption, not a ban. Independent human verification supplies the missing foundation: a qualified person checks the underlying material, evaluates the transformation, and accepts professional responsibility for its accuracy. The original recording, document, or image remains available under ordinary evidentiary rules, and parties may still use AI internally to locate or organize material. What cannot become proof without verification is the machine’s transformed version.
[P1.6] That is a narrow and practical safeguard.
[P1.7] Convenience cannot reverse the burden. The party offering a synthetic or interpretive exhibit is best positioned to validate it; a defendant should not have to discover a hidden model error after the jury has absorbed it. Criminal trials also involve asymmetric risk: delay or expense can be remedied, but a wrongful conviction may take years to undo. Requiring verification encourages preserved prompts, audit trails, and careful review.
[P1.8] Without that default, uncertainty is shifted from the proponent onto the accused. Where liberty is at stake, unverifiable machine interpretation should not enter first and be questioned later.

[C1.1] The question is not whether AI can make mistakes. Everything offered in a courtroom can: eyewitnesses, interpreters, fingerprint examiners, breathalyzers. The question is whether one category of tool should be presumed inadmissible because of what it is, rather than tested on what it does. Criminal evidence law already rejects that shortcut, and it should here.
[C1.2] First, the gatekeeping PRO wants already exists. A proponent must authenticate an exhibit and show it is reliable enough for the jury. The opponent can demand a hearing, challenge the method, examine whoever ran the tool, and put the original beside the output. PRO concedes the original recording or document "remains available" [P1.5].
[C1.3] That concession matters: when the source is in the room, a flawed summary or translation can be exposed directly. Courts already admit machine outputs, from breath-testing devices to DNA-mixture software, after validating the method rather than having a human redo every result. A blanket presumption replaces that scrutiny with a label.
[C1.4] Second, this rule hurts the accused most. The motion covers all AI-generated evidence, whoever offers it. Picture a public defender facing thousands of hours of wiretaps in a language she doesn't speak, or grainy footage that, once enhanced, suggests the shooter wore a different jacket. The prosecution can hire experts to verify its exhibits.
[C1.5] An underfunded defender often cannot. PRO warns about "asymmetric risk" [P1.7], but a verification requirement is a cost barrier, and cost barriers fall hardest on poor defendants. That makes exculpatory evidence harder to admit, not wrongful convictions rarer.
[C1.6] Third, "human verification" is weaker than it sounds. For some regional and indigenous languages, qualified interpreters are scarce or unavailable. For a summary of massive records, verifying means either redoing the whole job, which defeats the tool, or rubber-stamping it, which adds a signature without adding accuracy. Forensic history shows that confident human experts can be badly wrong.
[C1.7] A credential does not guarantee truth.
[C1.8] The better rule is to judge each exhibit on its reliability, with disclosure and adversarial testing. That catches bad AI without excluding good evidence.

A jury cannot meaningfully compare an untranslated wiretap with an English transcript, or distinguish observed from synthesized details in an enhanced image. By the time an adversary exposes an error, the polished reconstruction may already have framed what jurors think they saw. The breathalyzer analogy also fails: the motion concerns outputs that interpret or reconstruct meaning.
[P2.3] Where ordinary instruments are admitted, someone must still establish validated operation and explain the result. That principle supports verification rather than admission on trust.
[P2.4] The defense-cost argument identifies a funding problem, not a reason to lower evidentiary reliability. An indigent defendant deserves access to necessary expertise; jurors should not be asked to compensate for underfunding by treating an unchecked machine inference as proof. The unequal system CON invokes makes a clear verification burden more necessary, not less.
[P2.5] Finally, human experts need not be infallible for verification to matter. They can inspect the source, identify assumptions, defend choices under cross-examination, and bear professional responsibility. If a language is so obscure or a dataset so vast that no qualified person can determine whether the transformation is accurate, that is not evidence of reliability; it is precisely why the jury should not receive it as evidence. CON’s rule effectively says uncertainty may be admitted first and litigated afterward.
[P2.6] In criminal trials, the proponent must establish reliability before a machine-generated interpretation shapes the verdict.
