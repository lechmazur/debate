[P1.1] Public broadcasters remain relevant only if they are encountered by the publics they are meant to serve. For younger citizens, that encounter no longer happens primarily by turning on a television and browsing channels; it happens in vertical feeds, short clips, and creator communities. So the decisive question is not whether public media can keep launching channels, but whether it can earn attention in the environments where the next generation forms habits, tastes, and expectations of media.
[P1.2] Prioritizing youth-oriented short-form video and creator partnerships does that. Short-form is not merely a gimmick; it is a grammar. It rewards immediacy, clarity, personality, and repeated contact. A ninety-second explainer on a constitutional issue, a behind-the-scenes documentary clip, or a local-language comedy sketch can travel further and be shared more easily than an hour of scheduled programming.
[P1.3] Creator partnerships extend that reach because trusted creators already know the conventions and communities that broadcasters struggle to enter. When a public broadcaster funds or collaborates with a creator, it can combine public-service standards with authentic voice.
[P1.4] The alternative—traditional channel expansion—spends scarce money on distribution that fewer young people use. Adding another linear channel may please legacy stakeholders, but it does not solve the core problem: discoverability. A channel requires audiences to come to it. A short-form strategy goes to audiences.
[P1.5] It also gives public broadcasters measurable feedback, faster iteration, and the ability to test formats without the sunk costs of channel infrastructure.
[P1.6] This is not a demand to abandon long-form journalism or children’s television. It is a claim about priority: marginal resources should go where the relevance gap is widest. If public broadcasters fade from young people’s daily media diets, their educational and democratic mission becomes a monologue to an aging audience. To stay public in any meaningful sense, they must meet young citizens where they already are.

[C1.1] A public broadcaster does not become relevant by appearing in a stranger's feed for eleven seconds. It becomes relevant by being the place people go when something matters: an election night, a flood warning, a national final, a documentary that changes a conversation. That is a destination, and destinations are built through channels you own, not clips you rent.
[C1.2] Start with the word "prioritize." The motion asks broadcasters to steer scarce marginal money away from owned distribution and toward platforms they do not control. Every view on a vertical feed is subject to an algorithm that can be retuned overnight, a recommendation system that rewards outrage over accuracy, and a company that treats public media as free inventory. When a platform changes its rules, the broadcaster's "audience" evaporates, because it was never the broadcaster's audience.
[C1.3] It was borrowed. Channel expansion, whether a new linear service, a stronger streaming app, regional or minority-language outlets, or audio networks, builds something the institution keeps.
[C1.4] Second, creator partnerships trade the one asset public media cannot buy back: trust. The creator brings the following; the broadcaster brings the credibility and the cheque. When the creator later posts something reckless, the public broadcaster owns that scandal. Editorial standards cannot be franchised out to personalities whose incentives run toward engagement.
[C1.5] Third, the youth premise itself is shaky. Young people are the demographic most likely to leave a format the moment it feels institutional, and the least likely to convert a clip into sustained use. Chasing them with mimicry produces content that looks like everyone else's, and once a public broadcaster looks like everyone else, the case for public funding collapses.
[C1.6] Meanwhile the people who actually depend on public media, older, rural, lower-income, non-English-speaking, are told their service is the "legacy stakeholder" problem.
[C1.7] Relevance for a public institution means being indispensable to the whole public and durable across platform cycles. Short-form is a marketing tactic. Channels are the mission.
