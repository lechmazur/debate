[P1.1] The proposition before us rests on a straightforward empirical and moral claim: work requirements, on net, do more good than harm. I'll argue they do, for three reasons—mechanism, evidence, and proper design.
[P1.2] First, the mechanism. Dependency isn't a neutral state. Long-term reliance on safety-net programs correlates with deteriorating labor force attachment, weaker social networks, and worse self-reported well-being. Work requirements interrupt that trajectory by creating a structured expectation that pushes recipients toward employment.
[P1.3] Employment brings not just income but employer-sponsored benefits, workplace connections, and the psychological rewards of self-sufficiency. The alternative—open-ended benefits with no expectation of movement—doesn't just fail to help; it actively risks trapping people in a cycle where the rational short-term choice is to stay enrolled rather than seek work that might temporarily disqualify them. Requirements realign those incentives.
[P1.4] Second, the evidence from welfare reform. When the federal government replaced Aid to Families with Children with TANF in 1996, attaching work requirements and time limits, the results were striking. Welfare caseloads dropped by roughly half over the following years. Employment among single mothers—previously the core TANF population—rose substantially.
[P1.5] Child poverty fell. Critics predicted catastrophe; it didn't materialize at scale. This isn't proof that every work-requirement policy is perfectly designed, but it demonstrates the core claim: conditioning benefits on work engagement moves people toward independence without a comparable surge in deprivation.
[P1.6] Third, proper design protects those who genuinely cannot comply. Most work-requirement proposals include exemptions for people with documented disabilities, caregivers of young children or disabled family members, those in addiction treatment, and the elderly. States can build transition periods, good-cause exceptions, and streamlined appeal processes. The question isn't whether a badly designed system could cause harm—any policy can be implemented poorly.
[P1.7] The question is whether the principle of work requirements, competently applied, reduces dependency more than it harms the genuinely unable-to-comply population. The answer, given appropriate exemptions and support services, is yes.
[P1.8] The residual harm to edge cases is real but manageable. The harm of persistent dependency is widespread and compounding. Work requirements favor the larger good.
