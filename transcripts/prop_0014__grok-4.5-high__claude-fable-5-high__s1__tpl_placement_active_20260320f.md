[P1.1] The U.S. national debt has reached a level where serious deficit reduction must take priority over most new spending commitments. At more than $34 trillion, with debt-to-GDP near peacetime peaks, the burden is no longer theoretical. It already dictates the budget: interest alone runs hundreds of billions annually and is set to rival or exceed major discretionary categories as principal grows and rates normalize.
[P1.2] That interest buys nothing of value. It merely services yesterday’s borrowing. Every new deficit-financed commitment accelerates the clock. Compounding debt raises future service costs, crowds out private investment by competing for capital, and narrows the space for genuine emergencies—wars, pandemics, deep recessions—when borrowing becomes unavoidable.
[P1.3] Growth alone cannot be counted on to erase the gap; slower private capital formation and higher rates both work against the arithmetic that optimists invoke.
[P1.4] Markets still accept Treasuries at modest yields, but that is a privilege, not a law of nature. Sudden risk premia, forced austerity, or inflationary episodes have appeared before when confidence frays. Maintaining fiscal room requires deliberately reducing the primary deficit rather than treating new permanent outlays as the default. “Most” new commitments means precisely that: high-return investments can still compete, but only after they survive real trade-offs instead of automatic financing by further borrowing.
[P1.5] Serious reduction—restraining the trajectory of outlays, pairing any expansions with offsets, and addressing structural drivers—does not equate to blanket austerity. It is the precondition for lasting capacity. Without it, interest will steadily devour the rest of the budget, leaving later Congresses with only worse choices. The threshold has been crossed; new spending must now queue behind the harder work of restoring sustainability.

Deficit-first governance shrinks the denominator: cancel infrastructure repair, R&D, workforce programs, and childhood investments, and you get a smaller future economy servicing the same debt.
[C1.3] Britain's post-2010 austerity and U.S. sequestration delivered exactly that: weaker recoveries, lost output, and debt ratios that barely budged. Deferred maintenance is not savings—a bridge you don't fix at ten million costs a hundred million later.

[C1.5] Third, the correct decision rule is comparison, not priority. When a program's return exceeds the government's borrowing cost, funding it improves the long-run balance sheet even if financed by debt. A blanket presumption against new spending replaces arithmetic with anxiety.
[C1.6] Finally, the crisis framing overreaches. The U.S. borrows in its own currency, issues the world's reserve asset, and faces no market signal of insolvency. Prudence is warranted; panic-driven prioritization is not.
[C1.7] The path to sustainability runs through a bigger, more productive economy and honest reform of actual cost drivers—not through starving the future to flatter this year's deficit line.
