[C1.1] This motion takes a real privacy concern and turns it into an indiscriminate ban on user choice. It prohibits not only manipulative price discrimination, but every advertising or personalization use, regardless of how limited, revocable, or beneficial the processing is.
[C1.2] Start with the overbreadth. “Personalization” is not merely an advertising trick; it can be the service itself. A consumer focus device might adjust exercises when attention wanders, or a sleep wearable might tailor feedback to measured patterns. A user may deliberately buy the device for exactly that function. The motion nevertheless prohibits the use because the data came from brain signals.
[C1.3] That does not protect people from manipulation; it prevents them from obtaining features they knowingly want.
[C1.4] PRO’s consent argument also proves too much. Dense clickthrough terms can be inadequate, but the remedy is to require better consent and stronger substantive safeguards, not to declare every possible agreement worthless. Separate opt-ins, plain-language explanations, purpose limitation, withdrawal, deletion, and restrictions on onward sharing can all distinguish an informed choice from a buried checkbox. The motion’s words—“even if” users consent—mean the prohibition remains even when those defects are removed.
[C1.5] At that point, the state is not repairing consent; it is overriding competent adults because it dislikes their decision.
[C1.6] The bodily-trespass analogy fails as well. Processing a revocable data stream for a defined service is not permanent enslavement or an irreversible invasion of the body. Nor should speculative rhetoric substitute for precision: consumer signals and their inferences may be noisy and probabilistic, not a literal window into thoughts unavailable to the user.
[C1.7] Some uses deserve outright prohibition. A hotel exploiting a stress inference to raise a crisis-room price is a strong candidate. But that supports banning exploitative inference-based pricing and manipulative advertising, alongside enforceable privacy protections. It does not justify abolishing every personalized feature and every consent-based advertising model.
[C1.8] The decisive question is whether regulation can target the abuse while preserving legitimate use. It plainly can. A blanket prohibition therefore sacrifices autonomy and beneficial services without being necessary for protection.

[C2.2] Their reassurance is an amendment, not an interpretation.

A user can understand “use attention estimates to recommend exercises,” agree to that purpose, and later withdraw.
[C2.4] Raw-signal opacity may demand stronger disclosure and security; it does not make every agreement inherently invalid. [C2.6] Dynamic pricing includes transparent discounts, not only crisis-room surcharges. These uses may require strict safeguards, but influence is not automatically manipulation, and a commercial benefit to the company does not erase a benefit to the user.
[C2.7] The hotel example remains PRO’s strongest ground—and it condemns exploitation, not personalization or advertising as such. PRO has not shown why targeted rules cannot separate a stress-based price hike from a user-requested adaptive feature.
