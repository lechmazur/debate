[P1.1] A device that reads your steps can be taken off. A device that writes to your brain can change the person deciding whether to take it off. That asymmetry is why stimulation-capable brain-computer interfaces belong in tightly regulated medicine, not on store shelves.
[P1.2] First, the harm is direct, dose-sensitive, and invisible to the user. Electrical or magnetic stimulation that is too strong, misplaced, or frequent — or used with epilepsy, a metal implant, a psychiatric condition, or interacting medication — can provoke seizures, burns, mania, sleep disruption, or changes in mood and impulse control. Safety varies by person. A consumer headset cannot reliably screen for that, calibrate to it, or follow up when anxiety is blamed on stress rather than last night's 'focus mode.'
[P1.3] Second, consumer markets optimize for the opposite of clinical caution. Medicine titrates slowly, documents adverse events, excludes risky patients, and stops when benefit fails. Consumer tech ships fast, gamifies intensity, pushes subscriptions and firmware updates, and treats stronger sensation as proof it works. Wellness and do-it-yourself stimulation devices show the pattern: users chase stronger currents, share electrode placements online, and normalize use by teenagers whose brains are developing.
[P1.4] Third, write-access creates an autonomy and security threat. A hacked phone steals data; a compromised stimulation device can nudge affect, craving, attention, or compliance while the user experiences those shifts as their own thoughts. Employers, schools, or platforms need not force electrodes on anyone — pressure to perform, sleep less, or stay calm is coercive enough once the product is normalized as ordinary.
[P1.5] None of this bans the technology. Under medical regulation it can still treat depression, epilepsy, Parkinson's symptoms, or chronic pain, where need is serious, supervision is real, and consent is protected by a clinician. What it prevents is the weaker consumer bargain: unproven boosts to gaming, productivity, or mood in exchange for unsupervised access to the organ that makes choice possible.

We don't put coffee, liquor, contact sports or sleep apps behind a prescription because they act on the brain; we cap dose, demand labeling, and punish fraud. Electrodes deserve that same proportionate treatment, not reclassification as surgery.
[C1.2] The proposition erases the gap between opening the skull and wearing a headset. Implanted stimulators that can drive seizures or mania are already medical and no one proposes selling them beside headphones. Consumer write devices are noninvasive, low-energy and diffuse — closer to over-the-counter TENS units than neurosurgery. Headache, skin irritation, broken sleep, passing mood shift: we already trust adults to weigh such risks with painkillers, energy drinks and loud music.
[C1.3] That calls for power ceilings and age limits, not medical gatekeeping.
[C1.4] Medical-only would make brains less safe and less equal. A neurologist bottleneck for every gamer, meditator, student or older adult seeking focus means concierge clinics sell enhancement to the rich while everyone else solders do-it-yourself rigs from online parts and shared electrode maps. An open consumer market with locked output limits, tamper-resistant design, adverse-event reporting and reputational competition keeps ordinary use visible and improvable instead of driving it underground.
[C1.5] A prescription does not protect autonomy; ownership often does. A worker enrolled in an employer-sponsored brain protocol has less freedom to refuse than someone who owns her device, reads its settings, and leaves it in a drawer. Compromise is an argument for encryption, update standards and liability, just as with routers and cars, not prohibition. If this power matters, spread literacy and access through consumer law rather than reserving self-modification for the diagnosed who can pay.
[C1.6] That is freedom and safety working together.

[P4.1] A1: The line is mechanism plus intended target, not a mystical power number: a device designed or marketed to deliver electrical or magnetic stimulation through the skull to alter brain activity is medical. Regulators can then set power, waveform, placement, and duration rules inside that category. TENS stays consumer because it targets peripheral nerves and muscle, not cortex. Alcohol and contact sports, whatever their other risks, are not software-controlled devices delivering current to the brain.
[P4.2] Different mechanisms justify different regimes without inconsistency.
[P4.3] A2: If stimulation treats a demonstrated condition, it is paid for and scheduled like other medical care, according to need and proven benefit. If a healthy student or shift worker merely wants an edge, there is no entitlement a clinician must supply — and the restriction binds the wealthy buyer at the electronics store just as it binds everyone else. Off-label concierge abuse can be policed in a narrow, licensed channel; a mass consumer market makes that abuse the business model.
[P4.4] It also manufactures the desire and frustration that send users hunting for unlocked DIY rigs, rather than shrinking demand by keeping stimulation clinical and unglamorous.
[P4.5] That dilemma is why their main defenses fail. Either transcranial stimulation is diffuse and weak, in which case consumers are paying for placebo-grade focus claims that sellers will keep intensifying; or it meaningfully shifts mood, sleep, and attention, in which case broad, imprecise current is not reassurance — it is unpredictable exposure varying with skull, placement, medication, and repetition. A questionnaire cannot watch for mania emerging weeks later, and a seller rewarded for engagement cannot be the follow-up system. Signed updates solve forged code, not a manufacturer legally pushing stronger 'focus' protocols to the organ judging whether they feel right.
[P4.6] Medical restriction does not promise perfect access; it keeps a brain-writing technology inside the one setting built to screen, titrate, stop, and refuse.

[P5.5] Ordinary products can be left to ordinary judgment. A product designed to alter judgment itself cannot. Keep it where consent is protected by someone outside the effect and outside the sale.
