[P1.1] When you buy a digital ticket, you own a license to attend an event. Yet modern platforms increasingly lock that license, making it non-transferable except through the organizer's own resale portal—often with inflated fees and restrictive rules. The proposition simply restores the natural property right that existed with physical tickets: the right to sell or give your ticket to someone else. This right should be the default, with organizers permitted to impose only narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits if they can justify them.
[P1.2] This isn't about defending professional scalpers. It's about protecting ordinary fans. A parent who can no longer attend a game should be able to sell their ticket at face value to a neighbor, not be forced to use a platform that charges a 20% service fee or forfeits the ticket entirely. The current system traps fans in a one-sided contract.
[P1.3] Organizers control both the primary and secondary markets, allowing them to engage in dynamic pricing, levy excessive non-refundable "service fees," and harvest consumer data, all while claiming they are merely combating scalping. The proposed legal right creates a necessary check on that control.
[P1.4] The proposition’s key safeguard is the "unless" clause. Organizers are not left powerless. If they have a genuine, demonstrable problem with scalping, they can still implement limits—but those limits must be narrowly tailored. This forces proportionality.
[P1.5] An organizer could, for instance, require tickets to be transferred through their own secure, fee-free portal to verify identity, preventing industrial-scale bots. They cannot simply use anti-scalping rhetoric to blanket-ban all transfers and monopolize the resale market for profit.
[P1.6] This framework prioritizes fan autonomy and market fairness. It treats a digital ticket as the personal property it functionally is, while still providing a legal pathway for organizers to address specific, proven scalping harms. Without it, we consign consumers to a digital marketplace where their "purchase" is merely a revocable, inflexible permission slip. The burden of justification should rest on those restricting our rights, not on fans seeking to use the tickets they paid for.
