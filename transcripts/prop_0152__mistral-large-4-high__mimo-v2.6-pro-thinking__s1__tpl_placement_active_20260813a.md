[P1.1] Religious communities are not merely private preferences; they are forms of collective life with their own logic. When a family chooses a Catholic school or a person seeks work at a faith-based charity, they enter a community whose dress norms are part of its identity, not arbitrary rules imposed from outside. The proposition recognizes this while drawing the essential line: such expectations are legitimate only when participation is genuinely voluntary and alternatives remain realistic.
[P1.2] Consider what the opposition implicitly prefers: a uniform secular dress code enforced everywhere, including in religious institutions. That would flatten the pluralism liberal societies value. A Yeshiva, a Quaker school, or an Islamic academy exists to transmit a particular way of life. Dress is integral to that transmission: modesty standards, head coverings, or symbols of commitment are markers of the community's covenant, not costumes.
[P1.3] Parents enrolling children exercise educational choice; employees accepting positions exercise occupational choice. The state should not secularize these voluntary associations.
[P1.4] The conditions in the proposition are not window dressing; they separate legitimate community standards from coercion. If a religious employer were a monopoly, or if a religious school were the only local option, voluntariness would fail and the expectation would be suspect. But where alternatives exist—other schools, other employers, secular options—these dress expectations reflect diversity, not domination. A teacher objecting to head coverings can teach at a public school; a parent rejecting modest dress can choose a secular academy.
[P1.5] These exits transform imposition into consent.
[P1.6] The opposition will warn of discrimination, but they confuse uniformity with equality. True equality does not require every institution to look the same; it requires that no one be trapped. The proposition protects exactly that balance: religious employers and schools retain room for faith-based dress expectations, while doors remain open for those who do not share the faith. That is not exemption from the common good; it is the recognition that the common good includes freedom to live differently, voluntarily, in community.

But the proposition says faith-based dress expectations are legitimate “only when” participation is voluntary and alternatives remain realistic. [P2.3] They are not exposing a loophole; they are confirming the exception’s boundary. If the local Catholic school is the only option within reach, the condition fails, and the expectation is illegitimate under our own terms.
[P2.4] Their claim that these expectations are “overwhelmingly gendered” discrimination confuses uniform community standards with arbitrary targeting.
