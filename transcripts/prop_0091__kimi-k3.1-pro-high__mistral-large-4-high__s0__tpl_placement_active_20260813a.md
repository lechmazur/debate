[P1.1] A car is durable property, not a terminal through which its manufacturer renegotiates ownership every month. When hardware is installed and its functionality is supplied with the vehicle, the owner should retain that functionality for the life of the hardware. Heated seats should not become cold because a payment lapses; a purchased driver display should not lose functions because the manufacturer changes its business model.
[P1.2] The crucial distinction is between using what is already in the car and receiving an ongoing service. Streaming media, live traffic data, cloud connectivity, and future software improvements can impose continuing costs, so subscriptions may be legitimate there. But activating a heater element, using installed sensors, or operating equipment already built into the vehicle does not become a continuing service merely because software stands between the switch and the hardware. Permanent access means no recurring ransom for existing capability—not free updates forever, free repairs, or immunity from safety recalls.
[P1.3] This matters first because subscriptions weaken the basic bargain of ownership. A buyer cannot meaningfully compare prices when the advertised vehicle may later be stripped down unless additional payments continue. The cost also follows the household rather than any actual consumption: an owner who drives little pays indefinitely for equipment that has already been manufactured and installed. Resale becomes less predictable too, since the next owner inherits a car whose apparent capabilities depend on another contract.
[P1.4] It also distorts competition. Instead of winning buyers with a better vehicle and a clear price, manufacturers can retain control after the sale, making departure expensive and features revocable. A one-time purchase or clearly differentiated trim preserves consumer choice; a recurring charge for dormant hardware primarily monetizes lock-in.
[P1.5] Manufacturers need not give away innovation. They can charge for new services, sell upgrades outright, and set the original price accordingly. What they should not do is sell the physical car while reserving an unlimited right to switch its installed capabilities off. Ownership means the transaction eventually ends; subscription dependence means it never does.

[C1.1] The proposition treats software like a coat of paint—applied once at the factory, owned forever thereafter. But modern vehicle features are not static hardware; they are living systems requiring continuous investment. When a manufacturer installs a heated seat element or an advanced sensor array, the physical component is merely the substrate. The real value lies in the control algorithms, the cybersecurity hardening, the thermal management code, and the compatibility updates that keep the feature safe and functional across a decade of road use.
[C1.2] These development and maintenance costs do not stop at the point of sale, and neither should the revenue stream that supports them.
[C1.3] Standardizing hardware across trim levels while unlocking features via software is not a predatory ransom; it is manufacturing efficiency. By installing the same computing module or sensor in every vehicle and gating access through software, manufacturers reduce SKU complexity, lower production costs, and allow buyers to pay only for capabilities they actually use. A driver who never activates adaptive cruise control should not be forced to subsidize its development through a higher upfront sticker price. This flexibility lowers the barrier to entry for budget-conscious buyers who can add features later as their needs change.
[C1.4] Moreover, permanent access creates a liability trap. If a feature is unlocked forever, the manufacturer remains responsible for patching vulnerabilities, ensuring interoperability with evolving communication standards, and maintaining safety compliance indefinitely—without compensation. That structure disincentivizes the very innovation that improves vehicle safety and efficiency.
[C1.5] Finally, the proposition conflates ownership of physical property with ownership of intellectual property. Consumers own the metal and silicon; they do not automatically own the continuously developed code that makes those components useful. A subscription model aligns payment with ongoing value creation, rather than forcing all lifetime costs into a single transaction that prices budget-conscious buyers out of the market entirely. The alternative is a one-size-fits-all pricing model that forces every buyer to pay for capabilities they will never touch.

[P2.7] So the clash is not flexibility versus a one-size-fits-all car. It is flexible pricing versus revocable ownership. CON preserves the manufacturer’s leverage after sale; PRO preserves choice at sale and optional paid services afterward, while ensuring that installed capabilities remain usable.
