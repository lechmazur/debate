[C1.2] First, "solely reputational" is a fiction. Reputation is not a vibe; it is the market's running tally of risks a processor cannot verify in real time. A card network handling millions of creators cannot confirm that every performer consented, every face is of age, every clip was not uploaded by an ex. When a major tube site was credibly accused of hosting nonconsensual material, the card networks that cut it off were later named in litigation themselves.
[C1.3] What looks from the outside like image management is, from the inside, exposure management. Lawfulness that cannot be verified at scale is not something a processor can bank on.
[C1.4] Second, these are not utilities, and the electricity analogy breaks on contact. Nobody is in the dark. The adult industry has been served for decades by specialist high-risk processors that price that risk honestly. Android permits sideloading; the open web exists.
[C1.5] The real demand is not access to the economy but access to the cheapest, most respectable rails, with mainstream shareholders and cardholders absorbing the risk premium. Compelling that is a subsidy, not a right.
[C1.6] Third, brand trust is the product. A parent who hands a child an iPhone is buying the curated store. Forcing explicit apps onto that shelf does not merely embarrass Apple; it breaks a promise made to hundreds of millions of customers who chose it for exactly that reason.
[C1.7] Fourth, there is no stopping point. If reputational concern can never justify refusal for lawful adult content, it cannot justify refusal for lawful gambling, firearms marketplaces, or shock sites either. Every merchant will claim the same protection.
[C1.8] So the question is not whether creators deserve dignity. It is whether private firms may weigh risks they cannot audit and protect the trust that makes their networks worth joining. They may. Vote CON.
