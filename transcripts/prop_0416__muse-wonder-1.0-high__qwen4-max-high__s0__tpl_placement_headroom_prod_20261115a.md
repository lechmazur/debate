[P1.1] Egg freezing is sold as empowerment, but too often it is marketed as insurance. A woman scrolling past “freeze your future” cannot tell from that ad that freezing is not storing a baby, that most frozen eggs will never become a live birth, and that chances change dramatically with age at freezing and number of eggs stored.
[P1.2] That gap is not a minor omission; it is the decision itself. Egg freezing is expensive, physically demanding, and time-sensitive, and women make hard-to-reverse trade-offs around it: delaying attempts to conceive, relaxing about timing, or spending money they cannot afford twice on another cycle because the first yielded too few eggs. If the central fact — age-based chance per cycle for a realistic number of eggs — is buried in a later consultation or fine print after emotional commitment, advertising has already steered the choice.
[P1.3] The requirement is modest and targeted: in advertising itself, clinics must present realistic age-based success rates and uncertainty more prominently than today. Not a lecture, not a ban, not an individual prediction. A clear, standardized range — the chance of at least one live birth from a typical number of eggs frozen under 35, at 35 to 37, at 38 to 40, and over 40 — alongside the plain truth that many patients will not achieve that outcome and estimates vary.
[P1.4] Clinics already advertise benefits with precision and flair: “pause your biological clock,” “take control.” If they can sell freedom that confidently, they can state probability that clearly. Prominence matters because a vague “success varies” cannot compete with imagery implying a guaranteed future baby.
[P1.5] This is not paternalism; it is the minimum condition for autonomy. No one chooses freely on hope manufactured by selective marketing. Require the odds, prominently, where the hope is sold.

Imperfect recall is no defense for an implied guarantee. A prominent range is not a caveat competing with a number; the range itself is the uncertainty, wide enough to show that 38 is not 32 and that success is possible, not expected.
[P2.2] Nor does variation make age-based disclosure dishonest. Age at freezing is not a minor variable clinics can hide behind ovarian reserve to escape; it is the dominant fact shaping egg quality, and women deciding whether to freeze now or wait need it before individualized tests exist. Of course labs and denominators differ — which is why the standard must fix the denominator, for example chance of at least one live birth for a woman thawing a stated, typical number of eggs, rather than letting each clinic choose per-egg or per-transfer figures that flatter it.
[P2.3] Labeled population ranges are not false precision; imagery suggesting control without probability is.
[P2.4] Existing deception law does not solve this because the harm is omission and implication, not a literal lie. “Take control” is puffery regulators rarely punish, and by the time lawyers parse it, the anchoring has worked. A defined prominence rule — same placement as the main claim, before the booking link, in comparable size — is more enforceable, not less, than case-by-case mind-reading about whether an ad misled.
[P2.5] Finally, consultation cannot rescue a decision already framed by advertising. Women arrive having delayed, budgeted, and emotionally committed; the seller then supplies the personalized numbers. Disclosure at the point of persuasion does not replace consultation. It makes consultation a check on reality instead of a correction after hope has been sold.
