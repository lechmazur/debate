[C1.2] When real universal or large-scale public pre-K is evaluated, the picture changes. The federal Head Start study found early cognitive gains that were largely gone by the end of third grade, with few durable impacts on schooling or behavior. Tennessee’s statewide pre-K produced readiness bumps that faded, and in later grades some cohorts showed no advantage or small negative effects. [C1.3] This matters because the motion is about cost. Universal eligibility then spends heavily on children from middle-class homes who would have entered kindergarten ready anyway. When a program universalizes, the comparison is not preschool versus nothing; it is preschool versus existing care.
[C1.4] The return is back-loaded, distant, and sensitive to assumptions about discount rates and counterfactual care; near-term maternal employment is often a shift from private or family care, not newly created output.

[C1.6] It does not prove a public sledgehammer pays.
