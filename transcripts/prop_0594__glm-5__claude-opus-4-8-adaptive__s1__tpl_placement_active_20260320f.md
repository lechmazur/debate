[P1.1] Export bans on staple foods during crises are a continent-wide collective action problem. The African Continental Free Trade Area needs enforceable rules because voluntary coordination has failed repeatedly.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanics. A drought hits one country. Fearing shortages, officials ban staple exports. Neighboring countries that depend on those imports—often landlocked states with limited alternatives—face sudden supply shocks.
[P1.3] Prices spike. Panic buying follows. More countries impose their own bans in response. The crisis deepens for everyone.
[P1.4] The 2008 food price crisis demonstrated this pattern across Africa and beyond. Export restrictions in major producing countries pushed global grain prices higher, punishing net food importers. African nations learned that unilateral protectionism fragments regional markets precisely when integration matters most.
[P1.5] Soft commitments cannot solve this. The political logic during emergencies is overwhelming: leaders face immediate pressure to "protect our own." Without enforceable constraints, this logic prevails every time. The prisoner's dilemma plays out exactly as predicted—mutual defection, mutual harm.
[P1.6] Enforceable AfCFTA rules would change this calculus. Nations would commit in advance to notify, justify, and limit export restrictions, with dispute mechanisms and potential penalties for violations. This creates both transparency and accountability. Countries could still address genuine domestic emergencies, but within a framework preventing cascading bans.
[P1.7] The AfCFTA's core mission is continental integration. Food trade is foundational. If the agreement cannot govern staple flows during the moments that matter most—crises—then its promise remains hollow. Regional food security requires regional rules.
[P1.8] The question is not whether countries face pressure to restrict exports during crises. They always will. The question is whether Africa builds institutions strong enough to channel that pressure toward coordinated response rather than competitive deprivation. Enforceable rules do exactly that.
