[P1.1] The current gig economy runs on a fiction: that a worker who spends forty hours a week driving for a platform is an independent business with no need for health insurance or paid sick days. When that driver gets injured or falls ill, they do not stop being a cost—society absorbs it through emergency rooms and public assistance, while the platform that profited from their labor pays nothing. This is not flexibility; it is a subsidy to shareholders funded by worker precarity.
[P1.2] The proposition fixes this by replacing the broken binary of employee versus contractor with a simple gradient: benefits prorated to hours worked. A courier logging twenty hours a week receives half the health subsidy; a driver at thirty hours receives three-quarters. The platform already tracks every minute of labor to calculate pay, so extending that same data to benefits is administratively trivial, not the bureaucratic nightmare opponents predict.
[P1.3] Critics claim this destroys the flexibility that makes gig work attractive, but flexibility and security are not opposites. Portable, prorated benefits actually enhance flexibility by letting workers move between platforms without losing coverage or retirement progress. What destroys flexibility is a labor market where people must choose between a desk job with benefits and gig work with none. A government mandate prevents the race to the bottom where no single platform offers benefits for fear of undercutting competitors.
[P1.4] Economically, this is cost internalization. Platforms currently externalize the risks of labor—injury, illness, old age—onto workers and taxpayers. Requiring prorated contributions forces companies to price the true cost of their workforce into their business model. The result is a market where gig work remains available but no longer depends on desperation.
[P1.5] That is the minimum standard a society that values both work and workers should demand.

[C1.1] A government mandate that ties benefits to hours worked sounds like a safety net. In practice, it is a tax on marginal labor that will shrink the exact opportunities low-income and part-time workers depend on.
[C1.2] Consider the student who drives ten hours a week to cover tuition. Today, that work exists because platforms can schedule it cheaply and flexibly. Add prorated health coverage, paid leave, and retirement contributions to every one of those hours, and the platform faces a per-hour cost that exceeds the thin margin on short shifts. The rational response is not generosity—it is rationing: capping the hours of existing drivers, tightening algorithmic acceptance thresholds, or converting work into longer, less flexible blocks that exclude students, parents, and second-job holders.
[C1.3] The mandate does not gift benefits to the gig worker; it prices the gig worker out of the market.
[C1.4] The administrative claim is equally naive. Tracking driving minutes is not the same as administering benefits. Health eligibility rules vary by state and carrier; retirement contributions trigger tax and fiduciary obligations; paid leave requires verifying qualifying events. A worker who delivers food in three jurisdictions over the course of a month creates a compliance spiral that small platforms cannot absorb, driving consolidation toward the largest incumbents and reducing competition for workers and consumers alike.
[C1.5] There is a better path. Decouple security from employment entirely. Make coverage portable and individual—funded through refundable tax credits or public catastrophic plans—so a courier gains insurance whether they work ten hours or thirty, on one app or four. That protects workers without taxing the marginal hour that makes gig work accessible, and it respects the reality that many gig workers already hold full-time jobs with benefits elsewhere.
[C1.6] The proposition promises proration. It will deliver exclusion, not security.

[P2.1] The claim that proration "prices the gig worker out of the market" [C1.3] assumes benefits are a flat cost per hour that erases thin margins. But proration is precisely the mechanism that protects marginal work: a student driving ten hours earns a ten-hour share of coverage, not a full-time load of premiums. If a platform cannot afford a prorated health contribution on a short shift, it was already relying on taxpayers to fund that worker's emergency care [P1.1]. The choice is not between "hours with benefits" and "hours without"; it is between platforms internalizing labor costs and society socializing them through the ER.
[P2.2] On administration, the mandate need not force platforms to become insurers. Governments can require contributions to portable, multi-platform benefit funds—the same architecture as payroll taxes funding Social Security—drawing on the hour-tracking data platforms already maintain [P1.2]. A driver working across three apps contributes proportionally, and the benefit follows the worker, not the employer. This sidesteps the "compliance spiral" [C1.4] while preserving proration.
[P2.3] The proposed alternative of tax credits and public catastrophic plans [C1.5] shifts the entire cost to general revenue while platforms profit from labor without paying in. A worker who needs medication in March cannot wait for a refundable credit in April. And the point that many gig workers hold other jobs misunderstands proration: it guarantees that platform hours accrue proportional security rather than leaving gaps between employments.
[P2.4] Finally, the fear that platforms will ration hours [C1.2] applies to every labor cost—minimum wage, safety rules, payroll taxes—yet part-time work persists in regulated economies because the work itself has value. What the mandate eliminates is not the marginal hour, but the race to the bottom where platforms compete by offloading risk onto workers and public assistance [P1.4]. That is the exclusion worth ending, not the flexibility worth keeping.

[C2.1] PRO's case rests on a false arithmetic: that a ten-hour worker can receive a ten-hour share of coverage at a ten-hour cost. Benefits are not divisible like wages. Risk pools, plan minimums, enrollment windows, carrier contracts, and multi-platform aggregation impose fixed overhead; spreading those costs across scattered hours raises the effective cost per hour far above the nominal percentage. That is why the marginal student shift gets cut first.
[C2.2] PRO calls this a taxpayer subsidy, but if a platform cannot fund even a prorated contribution on a short shift, the mandate does not expose a hidden social cost—it finances benefits by eliminating the hour for someone else.
[C2.3] The portable fund answer concedes the point. Once government creates a multi-platform fund, the mandate is simply a payroll tax on gig hours. Social Security does not make a driver insurable in March, and payroll taxes are a known drag on low-wage hiring. If the fund is public and portable, making platforms collection agents adds bureaucracy without adding security.
[C2.4] The data platforms already track is pay data, not eligibility data; leave, retirement, and health rules still vary by carrier, jurisdiction, and qualifying event.
[C2.5] PRO's "many have other jobs" reply is the strongest case against the mandate. A courier with a full-time job already has coverage and retirement; prorated platform benefits duplicate them, create coordination problems, and may be unusable. Tax-credit timing can be solved with advance payment and a public catastrophic plan; a platform mandate cannot solve duplication or rationing.
[C2.6] Finally, PRO's minimum-wage analogy fails. Wages are cash per hour; benefits carry fixed administrative costs and threshold effects. Employers already schedule around benefit thresholds in ordinary part-time markets; a mandate on platforms will do the same, capping hours or blocking the shortest shifts. The choice is not security versus flexibility.
[C2.7] It is access versus symbolism: decoupled individual security protects the worker, while an hourly mandate protects the policy by taxing the marginal hour.

[P4.1] A1: The Social Security analogy does not require a uniform defined benefit. A public multi-platform pool can aggregate variable hours into contribution credits and actuarially pooled risk: a driver with ten hours across three apps holds ten hours of portable coverage credits, administered by the pool, not by each platform. Carrier contracts, enrollment, and qualifying events are handled centrally, which is exactly why the fixed overhead [C2.1] is absorbed once, not recreated per shift. Leave and retirement become individual accounts; health becomes a portable credit or pooled plan.
[P4.2] Proration lives in the accrual, not in a promise that each hour buys a standalone policy.
[P4.3] A2: Nothing in the remittance itself requires cutting shifts, and the mandate can make that enforceable: remit for every hour worked, prohibit using benefit cost to deny or cap shifts, and audit scheduling records. Economically, the platform still earns revenue from the marginal trip; a proportional contribution leaves the hour's net value intact, while cutting the shift sacrifices that revenue. If a platform eliminates short shifts anyway, that is a choice to shrink its own service, not arithmetic—and it is the race-to-the-bottom behavior the mandate exists to stop.
[P4.4] The debate now turns on CON's access-versus-security claim [C2.7]. It is false. The status quo already taxes the marginal hour through emergency care and public assistance [P1.1], but collects from society rather than the platform. Prorated contributions make that cost visible, portable, and proportionate.
[P4.5] CON's tax-credit alternative [C1.5] may supplement, but if it alone funds security, taxpayers carry the risk while platforms profit. A public pool answers the administration objection [C3.1], and enforceable anti-rationing rules answer the shift-cutting objection [C3.2]. The real choice is whether labor costs are internalized by the platform or socialized after injury. The proposition internalizes them while benefits follow the worker across apps and scale with hours.
[P4.6] That is the decisive ground.
