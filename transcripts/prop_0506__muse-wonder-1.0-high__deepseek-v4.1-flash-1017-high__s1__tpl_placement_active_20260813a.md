[P1.1] A deepfake of an investment adviser promises guaranteed returns. A cloned chief executive endorses a crypto giveaway. The real people verify their identity, report the fraud, victims report it again — and the ads and videos stay up, recommended and monetized, while people lose savings. That is not a platform fooled once.
[P1.2] It is a platform told, repeatedly, and doing nothing.
[P1.3] Civil liability should follow that choice, for three reasons.
[P1.4] First, platforms are not passive bulletin boards in these cases. They sell the ad space, target the audience, attach verification marks and algorithmic boosts, and take a cut of the reach. When they keep distributing a known synthetic impersonation after verified notice, they are materially contributing to the fraud, not merely storing someone else’s speech. Liability limited to post-notice inaction punishes that contribution, not the existence of AI, not user creativity, and not a good-faith moderation error.
[P1.5] Second, the platform is the only actor who can stop the harm and the cheapest actor who can prevent the next victim. The impersonator is often anonymous, overseas, and paid in crypto. Telling defrauded retirees to sue that ghost is no remedy at all. The platform has the report, the identity check, the hash of the video, and the switch to take it down and block re-uploads.
[P1.6] A duty of reasonable care after repeated verified notice simply asks it to use tools it already possesses for the scam it knows about.
[P1.7] Third, without liability, the incentives point the wrong way. Removal takes staff, review, and lost engagement; leaving a viral fake up costs the platform nothing while the losses are outsourced to victims and the impersonated person. Civil damages flip that calculation toward prompt takedowns, re-upload filters, and faster verification desks.
[P1.8] This is a scalpel, not a ban: act reasonably after clear notice and you are safe; ignore it repeatedly and you pay for the foreseeable fraud you helped keep alive.

[C1.1] The motion sounds surgical, but its trigger is a mirage. Who “verifies” that a video is a synthetic scam? A report is not proof, and repeated reports are not adjudication. A platform sees a clip that could be satire, a licensed likeness, a political parody, a dramatized reenactment, or a real recording falsely labeled fake.
[C1.2] The motion supplies no verifier, no evidentiary standard, no notice period, and no cure. It says only: after enough “verified” reports, ignore them and pay. That is not a safe harbor; it is a litigation trap built on the central fact platforms cannot reliably decide.
[C1.3] Civil liability would make platforms insurers of every AI impersonation fraud. Liability would attach not because the platform created the fake or took the victim’s money, but because it failed to act on a report. The causal chain runs through an anonymous criminal and a victim’s decision to send money. The platform cannot arrest the scammer, recover the funds, or know whether the “scam” is authorized marketing, satire, or a competitor’s smear.
[C1.4] It can only guess—and when guessing wrong means damages, the rational response is over-removal. The cheapest way to avoid liability is to take down anything that generates repeated complaints, including lawful parody and public-interest speech.
[C1.5] And the reports themselves are a weapon. Competitors can mass-report a rival’s ads; activists can flag satire; scammers can flood a platform with claims about legitimate content. Under this rule, the platform’s safest path is deletion, not investigation. Add class actions and settlement pressure, and “reasonable care” becomes “remove first, ask never.” The proposition’s “scalpel” is a bludgeon aimed at the wrong defendant.
[C1.6] Fraud is already illegal. The answer is to pursue fraudsters, require transparent notice-and-takedown with due process, and give platforms clear safe harbors for good-faith moderation—not to make them civil insurers for synthetic speech they did not create and cannot adjudicate.

[P2.3] That distinction answers the over-removal story. Liability does not attach for hosting a controversial deepfake or making one close call. It attaches when a platform is told specifically and credibly that a synthetic impersonation is being used to steal, and still keeps selling, recommending, and monetizing that exact content. [P2.4] What it cannot claim is helplessness after its own identity check has identified the lie.
[P2.5] Nor does this make platforms insurers for a criminal. Civil liability routinely reaches defendants who did not commit the original wrong but, with notice, foreseeably let it continue on property they control and profit from. The scammer creates the fake, but after notice the platform keeps supplying the distribution: targeting, trust badges, and re-upload reach. That continuing contribution, not mere hosting, is the causal act.
[P2.6] Weaponization collapses on the same qualifier. A competitor cannot verify as the rival chief executive. Activists cannot verify as the adviser. Only the impersonated person can supply that proof, and a false attestation can be checked.
[P2.7] Flooding would require the victim's own identity working against himself.
[P2.8] Finally, "pursue the fraudster" is not an alternative, it is an abdication. The fraudster is designed to be unreachable; the platform is reachable, informed, and uniquely able to block the hash and stop the next payment. [P4.1] A1: No separate neutral tribunal must rule before a duty arises; the neutral decision is the civil court's afterward, judging reasonable care. A verified denial triggers investigation, not automatic liability. The platform asks the uploader for authorization, checks any license or agency claim, and weighs the fraud markers already in the report: synthetic impersonation, money solicitation, and corroborating victim losses. If the uploader produces a plausible license, or the dispute is genuinely contestable, reasonable reliance on that proof is a defense.
[P4.2] Liability follows only when no authorization emerges and the platform still does nothing. A politician merely denying an embarrassing real clip fails the test because there is no impersonation-for-money scheme corroborated by victims.
[P4.3] A2: No. Preserving evidence, suspending monetization, and promptly seeking authorization is not ignoring a report; it is the beginning of reasonable care. But those steps are not a permanent shield. If the platform leaves the content recommended while an open-ended inquiry drags on and more victims pay, a court can fairly call that ignoring in substance. Interim action must actually reduce exposure while verification proceeds, not merely create a paper trail.
[P4.4] That distinction defeats the over-removal prediction. A platform fearing damages has a middle path cheaper than blind deletion and safer than inaction: pause monetization and amplification, demand authorization, remove or block re-uploads only when the uploader cannot justify impersonating someone to solicit money. Lawful parody and licensed ads survive because their creators can explain them; scams cannot because fraud is their business model.
[P4.5] CON has already conceded feasibility by endorsing preservation and suspension [C2.7]. The remaining question is whether a platform refusing even those steps should externalize the losses. Their court-order route gives victims an adjudicator too late, against a scammer too unreachable to pay. Post-notice liability supplies the same court, but creates an incentive to act before the next transfer—and only for the narrow fraud the platform was credibly told about, could check, and kept distributing.
