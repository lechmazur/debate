[P1.1] High-quality internet is now as essential to full participation in 21st century civic, economic, and social life as water or electricity, and it warrants the same legally enforceable universal service status. The case is rooted in basic equity. During the COVID-19 pandemic, school closures, telehealth appointments, unemployment benefit applications, and even grocery delivery all relied on reliable internet. Millions of low-income, rural, and disabled households were locked out of these basic services, not by choice, but because telecoms had no obligation to serve unprofitable areas or offer affordable plans.
[P1.2] For these communities, lack of internet is not an inconvenience—it is a permanent barrier to education, healthcare, and economic mobility. This is a clear market failure, the exact same gap that justified universal service rules for water, electricity, and telephony in the 20th century. Telecoms prioritize dense, high-income urban areas where profits are guaranteed, leaving rural and low-income neighborhoods chronically underserved. A legally enforceable universal service obligation fixes this by requiring providers to serve all geographic areas and cap prices for low-income users, with noncompliance penalties matching those for water or electricity providers that cut off service without due process.
[P1.3] This builds on the proven framework that delivered essential utilities to nearly every household over the past century. Beyond individual wellbeing, universal internet access is a prerequisite for equal civic and economic participation. Citizens without reliable internet cannot easily access government services, register to vote, or participate in public comment processes. Small businesses, farmers using precision agriculture tools, and remote workers in underserved areas are locked out of economic opportunity, dragging down overall national productivity.
[P1.4] The alternative is a two-tiered society where access to essential services depends on your zip code or income. Treating internet as a universal service is a simple, necessary fix to that structural injustice.

[C1.2] Water and electricity fit universal service because they are stable, uniform, survival-level goods delivered by local natural monopolies. Is the legal floor 25 megabits, 100, or a gigabit?
[C1.3] Fiber to every farmhouse, or is fast satellite enough? Speed, latency, data caps and reliability keep shifting with technology. Write that moving target into enforceable law and you invite endless litigation, regulators locking in yesterday's technology, and providers building to satisfy a court rather than to serve users.
[C1.4] That rigidity carries a brutal price. Laying fiber to the most remote few percent of homes can cost many times more per household than serving a town, yet a universal entitlement forbids asking whether mobile broadband, satellite, community hubs, schools and libraries would connect those people sooner and cheaper. The bill does not disappear: it is paid through higher prices for everyone else, taxes, or deferred upgrades elsewhere. In poorer countries, it can mean diverting scarce public money from water, electricity, clinics and roads that are not yet universal themselves.
[C1.5] A right that forces the most expensive solution first harms the very low-income users it claims to protect.
[C1.6] Nor does importance justify this legal form. Food, housing and transport are essential, but countries generally help through targeted subsidies, vouchers, public provision and competition policy — tools that can be adjusted as need and technology change. Means-tested broadband support, rural build-out funds awarded competitively, and rules against discrimination can close gaps without giving every household a justiciable claim to an undefined premium service and penalizing providers as if they had shut off drinking water.
[C1.7] Equity needs flexibility and prioritization. A universal legal mandate offers rigidity, misallocation, and litigation instead.

It merely shifts the bill into cross-subsidies, higher prices, or unfunded duties contested in court.
[C2.5] The proportionate fix is a durable, means-tested subsidy plus competitive rural grants, not a justiciable right for every address.
[C2.6] Finally, the retreat to “tailored solutions like low-cost mobile broadband” [P2.3] creates a dilemma. If that counts as high-quality universal service, much of the problem is already solved and the right is mostly symbolic.
