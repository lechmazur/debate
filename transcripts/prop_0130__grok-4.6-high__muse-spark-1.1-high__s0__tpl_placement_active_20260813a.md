[P1.1] A stolen object does not become the thief’s because he built a better vault. Colonial-era loot left its homes through conquest, punitive raids, coerced “gifts,” and markets that existed only because imperial force made refusal dangerous. Consent was never given, and title never passed. So the live question is not whether a museum in London can hold humidity more steadily than one in Lagos.
[P1.2] It is whether a later claim of superior care can launder a taking that was never lawful.
[P1.3] It cannot. Preservation is a duty of the rightful owner, not a prize that converts possession into entitlement. If better climate control could perfect a theft, any wealthy collector could keep what he seized by promising a stronger lock. We would reject that rule for a painting taken from a private house.
[P1.4] We should reject it for an altar, bronze, or ancestral figure merely because the taking wore the uniform of empire.
[P1.5] The paternalist argument fails on its own terms. It treats countries of origin as permanently unfit, as if conservation were an inherited gift rather than a funded skill. Those states now run national museums, train conservators, and build facilities because they want these objects back. Where capacity is still uneven, return can be sequenced with partnerships and shared conservation.
[P1.6] What it cannot be is postponed forever by the very institutions that created the gap and now cite it as a reason to keep the spoils.
[P1.7] Keeping the objects also severs them from the living uses that give them meaning: ritual calendars, lineage memory, civic identity. A caption in a vitrine is not a substitute. The claim of “universal heritage” too often means that the former colonizer remains the interpreter of someone else’s past.
[P1.8] Return is not charity. It is the end of an unfinished theft. Better glass cases do not cancel that.

[C1.1] An artifact has no voice except its survival. When a museum knows it can keep a 600-year-old bronze alive for another 600 years, and knows the alternative is humidity, underfunding, or political upheaval, the moral choice is not to hand it over to prove a point about 1897. To return it anyway is to sacrifice the object for the symbol of the object.
[C1.2] The property analogy in [P1.3] collapses because title for private theft never changes, but sovereignty does. Empires fell, borders were redrawn, legal orders replaced each other. International conventions on illicit transfer are explicitly non-retroactive for this reason. The 'country of origin' often did not exist when the object left - Nigeria did not exist when Benin bronzes were taken from the Kingdom of Benin.
[C1.3] Who is the rightful heir then - the federal state, a royal family, a local community? Returning to a modern flag is not correcting theft, it is inventing an heir.
[C1.4] Museums in London, Paris or New York are not better vaults, they are different instruments: encyclopedic, comparative, visited by tens of millions who will never fly to Lagos or Phnom Penh. A child seeing Egyptian, Greek and Yoruba work in one room learns that human achievement is connected. Re-tribalizing heritage into national silos, where foreign curators cannot interpret another's past per [P1.7], destroys that universal education.
[C1.5] Finally the preservation argument is not paternalism, it is risk management. Even recently returned or domestically held collections have suffered fire, looting and decay when states faced wars or budget crises. Dispersal is conservation. If capacity building is the goal, it happens better through loans, training and labs kept in place, not by surrendering the insurance policy.
[C1.6] Unfinished theft ends when we stop treating people alive today as thieves for crimes of an empire they never served, and start treating irreplaceable artifacts as wards we must not gamble with.

[P2.6] Capacity can travel with the object. Title cannot travel with the better lock.

You cannot loan that comparative encounter into existence if owners withdraw permission or lack storage to lend safely.
[C2.7] And risk is not symmetrical [P2.5]. Yes, London can burn. But the probability of loss from underfunded storage, power cuts, or conflict is not equal. An irreplaceable bronze cannot exercise ritual, identity, or scholarship if it is dust.
