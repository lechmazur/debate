[P1.1] The premise that de-extinction and protecting current endangered species are competing priorities is a false choice. Even when de-extinction projects require short-term reallocation of funds and attention, they deliver outsized, long-term conservation benefits that no alternative use of those resources can match, making them a worthy public investment.
[P1.2] First, revived keystone species repair broken ecosystems in ways no current conservation intervention can replicate, reducing the long-term cost of protecting dozens of existing endangered species. Woolly mammoths were ecosystem engineers of the Arctic tundra: their foraging trampled insulating moss layers, slowed permafrost thaw, and maintained open grassland habitat that supports threatened caribou, Arctic foxes, and migratory birds. No amount of targeted spending on protecting these individual species can fix the collapsed tundra ecosystem itself, which continues to degrade without the mammoth’s ecological function.
[P1.3] Similarly, reviving the thylacine, Tasmania’s extinct top predator, would control overpopulated herbivores that overgraze habitat for the endangered Tasmanian devil and ground-nesting birds, cutting the multi-million dollar annual cost of manual culling and habitat restoration for those current species.
[P1.4] Second, de-extinction drives breakthrough conservation technology that benefits all endangered species, more than compensating for any diverted funds. The genome editing, assisted reproductive, and rewilding techniques developed for mammoth and thylacine revival are already being adapted to rescue the functionally extinct northern white rhino and the critically endangered black-footed ferret—species that would have been lost without these tools. The short-term diversion of attention and funds is a trivial cost compared to the permanent, self-sustaining ecological and technological returns that de-extinction delivers.
