[P1.1] Homelessness presents cities with shattered lives and disrupted streets, but treating it mainly as a policing problem mistakes a symptom for the disease. The primary drivers remain a shortage of affordable housing and barriers to mental health, addiction treatment, and basic services. Cities resolve more of the crisis when they prioritize placing people into housing and connecting them to care, not when they cycle people through arrests and sweeps.
[P1.2] A person surviving outdoors typically cannot meet landlord demands for income, credit, or stability. Policing can clear a sidewalk for a day, yet without an exit path that person returns or relocates a few blocks away. Sweeps consume officer hours that should target violence and theft, while generating records that further block housing and work. The disorder reappears because the underlying scarcity and service gaps never closed.
[P1.3] A housing-and-services strategy supplies the missing foundation. Secure shelter makes treatment, case management, and job access actually stick. Street presence drops permanently for those housed, reclaiming public space without endless relocation. Public order improves as a consequence rather than a temporary theater of enforcement.
[P1.4] Resources once spent reacting to encampments can shift to prevention and rehousing, cutting the revolving costs of emergency rooms, jails, and repeated clearances.
[P1.5] Treating homelessness primarily as housing and access does not abandon policing of real crime. It simply refuses to make camping itself the dominant policy lens. The enforcement-first alternative locks cities into permanent crisis management: higher budgets, more trauma, and no durable reduction. Cities win when they fix the root shortage and service barriers first; order follows.

[C2.2] They call sweeps theatrical and temporary, but inaction is theatrical too—it pretends that unchecked encampments are sustainable. In Los Angeles after a reduction in clearance operations, encampments grew by over 50 percent in two years, with concomitant spikes in violent crime and overdose. Relocation would imply the problem moves elsewhere; instead, it concentrates where enforcement is weakest, trapping residents in worsening conditions.
[C2.3] Their timeline argument that interim tools suffice overlooks a critical dependency: outreach and shelter require a modicum of safety to function. Outreach workers decline to enter chaotic encampments; shelters operate under rules that exclude those with partners, pets, or possessions, leaving many street dwellers refusing entry. Without baseline public order, these services cannot access the people they aim to help. Policing provides that baseline, enabling services to operate rather than replacing them.
[C2.4] Finally, their model risks creating more chronic homelessness. When streets are allowed to become violent, unsanitary, and isolating, temporary hardship calcifies into entrenched patterns. Emergency room spikes, arrest records, and trauma push people further from housing, not closer. CON's proportional enforcement prevents that hardening; it keeps the population less damaged and more reachable when units do become available.
[C2.5] Policing and housing are not alternatives; they are sequential prerequisites. Order first, then housing, sustains both public safety and long-term exits. Prioritizing housing before order serves neither goal.
