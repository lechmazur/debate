[P1.1] A young child’s access to their accompanying adult is not a seat preference. It is part of providing usable, responsible transport. Airlines and rail operators should not sell a child a ticket and then charge extra to ensure that the adult responsible for that child can actually supervise them.
[P1.2] Consider a preschooler assigned several rows away from a parent. If the child becomes frightened, needs the toilet, or cannot understand an instruction, somebody must intervene. Separation transfers responsibility onto strangers and staff, while leaving the parent unable to provide ordinary care. Even without an emergency, that is an avoidable problem created by the operator’s seating system—not a luxury the family has declined to purchase.
[P1.3] The requirement should therefore be straightforward: seat young children beside an accompanying adult without a seating surcharge or requiring a more expensive fare. This does not entitle a family to its favorite row, extra legroom, or every relative sitting together. Operators can choose the seats; families can still pay for genuine preferences. What cannot become a premium feature is the minimum arrangement necessary for a dependent child to travel with their caregiver.
[P1.4] The obvious objection is that seating constraints have costs. But these passengers already occupy seats. Linking their assignments during booking and preserving suitable combinations before selling optional seat choices addresses the problem upstream. That is more orderly than leaving staff to negotiate swaps at departure.
[P1.5] Any administrative or opportunity cost must be weighed against the predictable burden of separating children—not treated as an automatic justification for charging families.
[P1.6] Nor is “just buy the higher fare” a defense. It concedes that the cheaper advertised ticket may exclude a basic condition of safe, manageable travel. Competition should reward better prices and service, not the ability to monetize parental anxiety. Operators control seat allocation; they should carry the responsibility for making that allocation compatible with the young passengers they agree to transport.
