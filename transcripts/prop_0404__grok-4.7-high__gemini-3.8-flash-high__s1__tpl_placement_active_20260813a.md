[P1.1] Identity documents should tell the truth about the person who carries them. For people who identify as neither male nor female, a mandatory binary marker does the opposite. States should recognize a third-gender or nonbinary option because accuracy, equal standing, and administration all require it.
[P1.2] These documents are practical instruments. A passport or driver's license lets officials, banks, and border agents match a person to a record. When the gender field is false, that match fails. Nonbinary people can be stopped, questioned, or turned away because their appearance or other records conflict with an M or F they cannot honestly claim.
[P1.3] The state manufactured that conflict by printing only two boxes. A third option removes the mismatch at its source.
[P1.4] Nothing in that change rewrites biology. Chromosomes, anatomy, and clinical needs stay what they are. The civil marker records legal gender, and states already treat that field as revisable when a transgender person moves between male and female. Drawing the line at a third category is arbitrary.
[P1.5] If a short administrative process is trusted to cross the binary, it can record that someone stands outside it.
[P1.6] Nor does recognition dissolve every sex-based rule. Prisons, competitive sport, and some medical protocols can rely on sex characteristics or separate criteria rather than one checkbox. Jurisdictions that already issue an X marker have updated forms and databases without losing the ability to count people or to keep single-sex settings where those settings are justified on their own terms.
[P1.7] What refusal buys is not clarity. It buys a standing falsehood at every checkpoint, counter, and application. Equal citizenship includes not being misdescribed by the state that claims to identify you. One added line costs almost nothing.
[P1.8] A forced binary costs the accuracy of the document itself. States should match the record to the person.

Conflating biological sex with internal gender identity erodes the foundational reliability that makes state identification useful in the first place.
[C1.2] PRO dismisses this reform as a harmless administrative update, claiming separate criteria can simply govern single-sex spaces [P1.6]. From correctional facilities and domestic abuse shelters to bodily search protocols and athletic categories, public institutions rely on state records to balance privacy, safety, and fairness. Introducing an indeterminate third marker forces these institutions into an unworkable dilemma: either abandon sex-segregated protections entirely, or institute intrusive physical examinations to determine placement.
[C1.3] A binary legal marker prevents this administrative chaos by maintaining a clear, objective standard.
[C1.4] Governance also depends on reliable, sex-disaggregated data. [C1.5] Moreover, official documents must function in a global system. Passports govern transit across nations with vastly different legal frameworks. Nonstandard markers generate cross-border friction, unpredictability, and administrative rejections at foreign checkpoints, undermining the primary purpose of international credentials.
[C1.6] Identity belongs to the individual, but identification belongs to the state. Subordinating objective physical records to self-declared internal states damages public safety, degrades vital public data, and compromises the legal coherence of official identification.
