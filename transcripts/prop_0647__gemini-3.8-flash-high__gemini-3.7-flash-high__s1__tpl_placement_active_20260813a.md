[P1.1] Urban freeways were the twentieth century’s most destructive planning blunder. Carved through dense urban cores, they severed vibrant communities, degraded public health, and sacrificed the life of the city to suburban commuters. Today, as this aging concrete reaches the end of its lifespan, cities face a fundamental choice: double down by widening, or reclaim their cores by tearing them down. Removal must become the far more frequent policy.
[P1.2] The case against widening rests on an inescapable physical reality: induced demand. Decades of transport data demonstrate that expanding highway capacity in dense areas does not eliminate congestion; it manufactures it. Adding lanes subsidizes further driving, rapidly filling new asphalt until gridlock returns, while dumping worse bottlenecks onto neighborhood streets. Widening is an exorbitant fiscal trap that consumes municipal wealth to perpetuate systemic failure.
[P1.3] Removal, by contrast, activates traffic evaporation. When cities decommission urban freeways, the catastrophic gridlock predicted by traffic models fails to materialize. As seen from San Francisco’s Embarcadero to Seoul’s Cheonggyecheon, significant portions of traffic simply disperse as travelers adjust their routes, shift departure times, or switch to public transit. Cities do not grind to a halt; they adapt.
[P1.4] Crucially, removal reclaims a city’s most scarce and valuable asset: land. While widening consumes urban ground for toxic exhaust corridors and physical barriers, removal heals the urban fabric. It reconnects severed neighborhoods and converts wasted space into housing, commerce, and public parks. Rochester’s removal of its Inner Loop proved that replacing a sunken highway with a walkable surface boulevard unlocks immediate private investment, generates municipal tax revenue, and builds desperately needed homes.
[P1.5] Widening pours billions into an obsolete, carbon-intensive status quo. Removal transforms expensive liabilities into flourishing neighborhoods. When urban freeways expire, tearing them down must be the rule, not the rare exception.
