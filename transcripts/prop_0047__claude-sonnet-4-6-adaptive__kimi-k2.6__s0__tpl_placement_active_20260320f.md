[C1.3] Vouchers and cross-subsidies are slow, stigmatizing, and rarely cover the full gap between a ten-dollar fast-fashion garment and its fifty-dollar domestic equivalent. By the time any rebate arrives, the damage to monthly budgets is done. The proposition’s "honest price" is, for millions, simply unaffordable honesty.
[C1.4] Next, consider what the tax is supposed to build. Local, sustainable textile manufacturing is welcome, but it cannot scale to replace fast fashion’s volume at anything close to the same price. Domestic labor costs, material traceability, and smaller-batch production are not frictions that subsidies erase; they are structural features. The likely result is not a mass transition to virtuous local garments.
[C1.5] It is a smaller, premium market for the affluent, while everyone else faces higher prices for scarcer goods.
[C1.6] Then there is the leakage problem. Tax production heavily in one jurisdiction, and the same garments enter through untaxed direct-to-consumer channels, secondhand overflow, or rerouted imports. The environmental harm does not stop; it merely evades the tax collector. Meanwhile, the revenue intended for domestic factories never materializes.
[C1.7] If the goal is less waste and cleaner supply chains, the tools are direct regulation—extended producer responsibility, mandatory recycling, fiber standards—not a consumption tax whose heaviest footprint lands on the very households least able to bear it.
