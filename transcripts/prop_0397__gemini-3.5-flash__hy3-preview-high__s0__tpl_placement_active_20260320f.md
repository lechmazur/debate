[P1.1] The fundamental promise of bankruptcy law is a "fresh start"—the recognition that in a dynamic economy, honest debtors should not be condemned to a lifetime of financial ruin. Yet, student loan borrowers are uniquely locked out of this relief. In most jurisdictions, they must meet an almost insurmountable legal standard of "undue hardship" to discharge their debts. This standard is so punitive that even borrowers facing permanent disability, chronic underemployment, or severe economic distress are routinely denied relief.
[P1.2] We must make it much easier to discharge student debt when a long-term inability to repay is established.
[P1.3] First, the current system creates a lifetime trap that stifles economic mobility. Unlike credit card debt, medical bills, or even business loans, student loans persist through decades of hardship. When borrowers face a long-term inability to repay, denying them discharge does not magically conjure money; it simply forces them into perpetual default, wage garnishment, and tax refund offsets. This drains the consumer economy, preventing millions of people from buying homes, saving for retirement, or starting small businesses.
[P1.4] Second, the current exemption shields lenders from the consequences of poor underwriting. When lenders—both public and private—know their debt can never be discharged in bankruptcy, they have zero incentive to assess a borrower’s actual capacity to repay. Restoring standard bankruptcy protections forces rational risk-sharing, encouraging lenders to be more responsible and holding institutions accountable for high tuition costs.
[P1.5] Bankruptcy is the safety valve of a market economy. If we allow corporations to discharge billions in failed business ventures, we cannot justify denying a financial reset to individuals whose only "mistake" was investing in their own education. Lowering the barrier to discharge restores equity, dignity, and economic utility to the legal system.
