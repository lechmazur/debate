[C1.3] A coerced denunciation, stamped and filed, looks identical to a voluntary one once it reaches a historian's desk. Wholesale opening hands totalitarian regimes posthumous victory: their poison becomes our public record.
[C1.4] Colonial and wartime records name victims, witnesses, collaborators, and bystanders indiscriminately. Declassified colonial files in Algeria, Kenya, or Indochina contain testimony from villagers who spoke under threat, medical details of torture survivors, names of local intermediaries whose descendants still live beside the families they were forced to inform on. The proposition treats communities as unified victims; reality is more fractured, and disclosure can fracture them further.
[C1.5] The motion's escape clause—"once immediate security concerns have passed"—is structurally inadequate. Security is the easy objection. [C1.6] An informant's grandchildren do not inherit collective guilt that justifies outing them.
[C1.7] Truth matters. But truth pursued by dumping classified records onto communities without individual review, context, or consent is not justice. It is a different kind of violence, performed in justice's name.
