[P1.1] At 11pm, hungry and tired, you open a delivery app. A red banner screams your cart expires in 2:47. On a travel app, it's worse: "Only 1 room left at this price!" with the seconds ticking. You don't choose.
[P1.2] You flinch. You click.
[P1.3] That flinch is the business model. Countdowns and scarcity prompts are not neutral information. They are engineered to short-circuit deliberation, raising heart rate and narrowing attention so comparison, budget-checking, and reading cancellation terms feel impossible. In travel and food — where hunger, fatigue, unfamiliar cities, and nonrefundable fares already cloud judgment — manufactured urgency turns normal vulnerability into overspending: pricier rooms, rushed add-ons, double fees, trips booked before checking dates.
[P1.4] Defaults decide everything. A tired user cannot meaningfully consent to a screen designed to panic them. Leaving these tricks on by default means millions face pressure they never asked for, with the poorest and most time-pressed hurt most. Turning the default off does not hide truth.
[P1.5] Apps can still state calmly: three seats remain, checkout closes at midnight. What disappears is only the blinking timer and the false now-or-never framing.
[P1.6] The efficiency defense fails. Rushed choices create more cancellations, angry support calls, wasted food, and broken trust — costs users and honest hotels and restaurants pay. Real scarcity does not need a countdown to be understood; fake scarcity deserves no protection. When information is genuinely needed, plain language works without manipulation.
[P1.7] Travel and delivery shape essentials: shelter and food. Defaults there should protect calm, informed choice, not auction it to whoever panics fastest. Take the timer away by default, and what remains is competition on price, quality, and service — not on fear.

[C1.1] That 2:47 cart timer at 11pm is not automatically a trick. If the kitchen closes soon, drivers are logging off, and another customer will take the slot, the countdown tells you how long that price and delivery window are actually held for you. Scarcity prompts do the same for a flight, table, or hotel room. Removing them by default does not create calm choice; it creates ambush — you compare leisurely, return, and find the fare raised or the room gone, with no clear warning you could have acted on.
[C1.2] The proposition turns on mind-reading: cues "mainly designed to pressure impulsive spending." But urgency is often the information. Travel and delivery sell perishable capacity — a seat tonight, a courier this hour, a cook's remaining orders cannot be stored for tomorrow. When only a few remain, saying so plainly but less visibly is not more honest; it just makes true, decision-relevant facts easier to miss, especially for users scanning quickly or in a second language, for whom a timer is clearer than a paragraph of terms.
[C1.3] A default ban also misallocates that scarce capacity. Timers let firms release unpurchased holds quickly, stop restaurants preparing food no one collects, and give the next customer a fair chance. Without them, indecisive carts linger, inventory looks unavailable, prices rise to hedge uncertainty, and the decisive shopper loses to someone who was never serious. The stressed late-night user is not helped by discovering at checkout that protection they assumed existed never did.
[C1.4] If a prompt is false, punish the lie. If it is true, do not treat adults as incapable because a real deadline feels stressful. People use urgency in ordinary life — expiring coupons, limited menus, last trains — and can close an app, compare elsewhere, or opt out. Accuracy rules, substantiation for "only one left," and easy settings target deception without hiding truthful deadlines from everyone who benefits from them.
[C1.5] Keep the signal; police the fabrication.
