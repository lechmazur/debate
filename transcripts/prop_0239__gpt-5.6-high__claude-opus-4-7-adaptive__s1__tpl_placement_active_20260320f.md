[P1.1] Consent answers one moral question: did the parties authorize the exchange? It does not answer the whole ethical question: what is being exchanged, under what conditions, and at whose expense? Consent is normally necessary, but it is not sufficient.
[P1.2] First, transactions can violate the rights of people who never had a seat at the table. A public official and a company may freely agree to exchange money for a favorable decision. Both understand the terms and both benefit. Yet their consent cannot authorize the misuse of public power or erase the harm imposed on citizens and competitors.
[P1.3] The same is true of paying someone to assault a rival or knowingly purchasing stolen property. Agreement between buyer and seller does not manufacture an entitlement to trade what belongs to, or harms, someone else.
[P1.4] Second, even informed and voluntary agreement can occur on grossly exploitative terms. Imagine the only nearby supplier after a disaster demanding a family’s life savings for essential drinking water. The family may rationally accept; the offer may even leave them better off than no offer.
[P1.5] But “better than the desperate alternative” is not the same as fair. When one party leverages another’s urgent vulnerability to extract extreme advantage, consent demonstrates choice within the available options—not that those options or terms are ethically acceptable.
[P1.6] Third, some goods and roles carry obligations that private agreement cannot cancel. Votes, judicial rulings, guardianship, and public authority are entrusted for particular purposes. Selling them corrupts those purposes even when every direct participant eagerly agrees. A judge cannot turn a ruling into an ethical commodity merely by finding a willing buyer.
[P1.7] The opposing principle mistakes permission for justification. Consent protects agency by establishing a boundary: without it, a transaction is ordinarily wrongful. But crossing that boundary does not grant immunity from every other moral duty. Ethical transactions must also respect third-party rights, avoid wrongful exploitation, and involve things the parties are entitled to exchange.
[P1.8] A signature can show “we agreed.” It cannot, by itself, show “we acted rightly.”
