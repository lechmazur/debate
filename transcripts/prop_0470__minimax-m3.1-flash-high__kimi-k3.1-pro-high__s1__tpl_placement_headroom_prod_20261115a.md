[P1.1] Hyper-realistic political deepfakes pose a different danger from ordinary satire: they manufacture apparently authentic evidence that a real person said or did something they never did. Their effect does not depend on whether the viewer spots the deception. A convincing fake can shift coverage, chill legitimate speakers, and weaken public trust before correction. A later correction cannot restore all the credibility lost during the decisive moment.
[P1.2] The proper response is a targeted ban on hyper-realistic political impersonation, not a general restriction on parody. Parody signals artificiality through absurdity, distortion, or incongruity. A deepfake engineered to pass as recorded reality deceives about the existence and provenance of the political statement. Satire should remain vigorous, including uncomfortable or anonymous satire.
[P1.3] But protecting expression is not a reason to permit media engineered to fool the public into mistaking invention for evidence.
[P1.4] That distinction also handles anonymous speech. The ban should prohibit making or distributing a hyper-realistic fake of a real political actor when used deceptively. Anonymous criticism and satire can remain protected. A speaker need not reveal identity merely because they use synthetic tools; the legal line runs between protected expression and false impersonation, not between open identity and secrecy.
[P1.5] This is narrower than universal labeling or banning every synthetic image.
[P1.6] Opponents will rightly ask about slippery slopes, so the rule needs clear elements: political subject matter, real-person impersonation, and a realistic presentation that could mislead a reasonable viewer as authentic. Obvious caricature, quotation, fictional characters, and apparently artificial media fall outside the ban. Enforcement should not require proving every viewer was deceived, any more than distributing forged currency requires proof someone was fooled. Nor should satire be excused simply by attaching a disclaimer after a realistic fabrication has circulated.

[C1.1] The proposition empowers governments to decide which political depictions are too convincing to exist. That is the decisive danger. “Hyper-realistic” has no stable boundary: realism changes with editing, compression, context, and viewer. A “reasonable viewer” test does not remove discretion; it invites officials, platforms, and courts to guess how audiences might react, often after a clip is stripped of its caption.
[C1.2] PRO’s promised satire exception mistakes an artistic technique for a legal safe harbor. Satire need not be absurd or immediately recognizable. It can imitate official formats, sustain an impersonation, and reveal its point only through context. Once realism itself becomes evidence of illegality, the safest course for a platform is removal and the safest course for a dissident is silence.
[C1.3] Anonymous creators are especially vulnerable: proving parodic intent, authorship, or original context creates pressure to identify themselves. These accepted costs fall most heavily on speakers with the least power to contest a takedown.
[C1.4] The forged-currency analogy collapses on inspection. Counterfeit money has no protected political message; fabricated depictions can criticize, dramatize, or expose. Making deceptive circulation sufficient, without concrete injury, punishes the artifact rather than demonstrated harm.
[C1.5] Malicious deepfakes are genuinely dangerous. They can justify narrowly drawn liability for fraud, threats, defamation, or interference with election procedures, alongside provenance tools, authentication, and rapid corrections. Those responses are imperfect, but a criminal or civil ban will not reliably stop foreign or anonymous propagandists either. It will, however, give domestic incumbents a powerful accusation against critics: not “this is false,” which can be answered, but “this looks real,” which invites suppression before the answer.
[C1.6] Democracies should target demonstrated harm, not convincing political expression. PRO draws the line at believability and hands control of that line to the very officials most likely to be satirized. Reject the ban.
