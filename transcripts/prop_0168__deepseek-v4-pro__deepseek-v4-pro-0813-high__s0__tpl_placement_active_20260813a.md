[P1.1] Exploration budgets are finite, so the relevant question is what new knowledge a marginal billion dollars buys. On that test, robotic probes and telescopes dominate. Crewed systems must launch, shelter, feed, water, and return fragile humans. Those subsystems dominate cost, mass, and engineering attention; instruments become a small afterthought.
[P1.2] A rover or orbiter spends almost all its payload on science, can operate for years, and does not require a return ticket.
[P1.3] That difference multiplies across the solar system. Robotic missions can survive years in Jupiter’s radiation belts, land on Venus’s crushing surface, fly through Enceladus plumes, or coast in deep space for decades. Crewed missions are restricted to the few places where humans can survive and return. Every human-rating requirement adds testing, redundancy, and abort modes, while squeezing out instruments and follow-up missions.
[P1.4] The scientific record already shows this. Mars rovers and orbiters have mapped the planet, found ancient water chemistry, and are collecting samples. Cassini revealed Enceladus as an ocean world. Space telescopes from Hubble to Webb have measured cosmic expansion, imaged exoplanet atmospheres, and looked back to the early universe.
[P1.5] None of these depended on an astronaut on board. The greatest recent breakthroughs in planetary science and astrophysics came from automated platforms built to maximize data, not to sustain a crew.
[P1.6] The human-adaptability argument is weaker than it looks. A geologist on Mars could make quick field decisions, but a rover team on Earth can plan, simulate, and rerun analyses over many sols. Autonomy, machine learning, and orbital relay links are narrowing the response gap, while robotic missions can visit far more sites for the same budget. A single crewed landing architecture costs as much as an entire fleet of landers, orbiters, and telescopes, so the true cost is not one human advantage; it is the many high-return robotic missions cancelled to pay for it.
[P1.7] Public agencies should therefore shift most exploration budgets toward robotic probes and telescopes, while allowing human spaceflight only a narrow role where it genuinely adds value. That is the science-per-dollar choice.

[C1.1] The proposition’s central claim is a cost-accounting illusion. It treats exploration as a search for the cheapest data point. But an agency is not buying journal articles; it is building long-lived capabilities, operational knowledge, and the ability to choose harder missions later. Once most budgets are shifted, human launch, EVA, closed-loop life support, and surface operations teams decay quickly and take decades to rebuild.
[C1.2] That lost option value is enormous, even on a purely scientific calculation, because future telescopes, sample-return assembly, and planetary outposts depend on those skills.
[C1.3] The science ledger is also not as one-sided as PRO suggests. Robotic platforms cover ground slowly and sample narrowly. Apollo’s astronauts returned hundreds of kilograms of geologically selected lunar material whose context still produces discoveries; robotic sample returns, where they succeed, recover grams from limited sites. In orbit, crewed laboratories answer the one set of questions telescopes cannot: how fluids, materials, and human physiology behave in space, which is essential data for long-duration exploration.
[C1.4] Even PRO’s telescope evidence cuts against them. Hubble became a world-class observatory only because crews could service it. After the mirror flaw, astronauts installed corrective optics; later they replaced gyros and instruments. A strict robotic budget would have abandoned Hubble or left it crippled.
[C1.5] Crewed servicing produced decades of high-return science, exactly the outcome PRO says is impossible.
[C1.6] Finally, robotic missions are not uniformly efficient. Flagship telescopes and orbiters suffer deep cost growth and failure as well; the choice is not one rover versus one crewed landing, but a diversified portfolio versus a fragile single-strategy program. The proposition would maximize near-term data while dismantling the only infrastructure that makes the next generation of ambitious exploration possible. That is not better science; it is a forced narrowing of science.
