[P1.1] An account suspension today is not a minor inconvenience. It can silence a journalist, cut off a small business from customers, isolate an activist community, or erase a person’s public record. Yet most platforms can impose these harms through opaque rules, automated enforcement, and mass-reporting raids, often with no specific explanation and no realistic chance to be heard. That is why the core issue is not whether platforms may moderate speech; it is whether they may do so arbitrarily.
[P1.2] The proposition requires a minimum procedural floor: notice of the alleged violation, a reason tied to the conduct or rule relied on, and a meaningful appeal before a user loses access. These are basic fairness tools, not luxury burdens. They force decisions to be intelligible and reviewable. If a user is banned for harassment, the platform should identify the offending conduct or content category and allow the user to contest mistaken facts, context, or impersonation.
[P1.3] If the account is suspended because of spam flags, the user should be able to show the flags were coordinated abuse.
[P1.4] This legal duty is necessary because market discipline is weak. Major platforms function as essential channels for modern speech, commerce, and civic life. Terms of service are take-it-or-leave-it contracts, and leaving one network often means losing audiences, archives, and communities that cannot be rebuilt elsewhere. Without law, users have only unilateral corporate policy, which can change overnight and is hardest to challenge when the platform is least accountable.
[P1.5] Due process here does not require platforms to keep every account online. They may still enforce rules against violence, fraud, harassment, or spam, and scalable notice or tiered appeals can handle volume. But when the penalty is suspension or banishment, the state should require platforms to explain, justify, and permit review before destroying a person’s access to the public square.
