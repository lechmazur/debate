[P1.1] The case is simple: frontier AI research capacity is now gated by a resource—large-scale compute—that only a handful of firms control, and that is a structural failure, not a market outcome we should accept.
[P1.2] Start with the incentive logic. Academic research produces public goods: open papers, reproducible benchmarks, independent safety evaluations, adversarial findings about model failures. Firms underproduce these because they cannot capture the returns. A firm allocates scarce GPUs to whichever project ships revenue fastest; a lab studying interpretability, long-tail harms, or capability thresholds produces knowledge competitors and the public enjoy for free.
[P1.3] So when we leave compute allocation to firms, we systematically defund exactly the research the rest of society needs most. That is a textbook externality, and the fix is a public provision mechanism, not hope.
[P1.4] Second, concentration has made this worse. Training runs at the frontier now cost sums that exceed entire national academic budgets, and the same few companies set the terms of who gets access, what gets studied, and which results get published. That means the outside world's check on frontier development—scrutiny by researchers with security of tenure, publication incentives, and no product roadmap—is being designed out of existence. Whistleblowing and ad hoc partnerships are no substitute for standing capacity.
[P1.5] Third, coordination beats fragmentation. If each country builds a small isolated national stack, we get stranded capacity and duplicated cost. Coordinated public compute—pooled allocations across national research clouds, harmonized access rules, shared scheduling across time zones—raises utilization, lets a PhD student in a smaller country compete on ideas rather than corporate connections, and lets safety researchers run evaluations on the actual models being deployed. We already see the template: national AI research resource initiatives and European supercomputing centers opening GPU time to academics.
[P1.6] They are oversubscribed, which is the tell: demand exists and the private market is not meeting it.
[P1.7] My test for this debate is comparative: which institutional arrangement gives credible researchers durable, independent access to frontier-scale compute? Coordinated public provision does. Voluntary corporate generosity does not—it survives only while convenient.

A national compute program requires budgeting cycles, multi-country coordination bodies, procurement rules, and political sign-off before a single chip is racked. [C1.3] Firms, by contrast, refresh clusters continuously because their survival depends on staying at the frontier. Betting academic research capacity on the pace of intergovernmental coordination is betting it will always arrive a generation late.
[C1.4] Second, capture. Whoever allocates scarce public compute decides which research questions get asked. That is not a neutral technical function—it is a political one. Committees will favor safe, consensus topics over adversarial or embarrassing lines of inquiry, exactly the kind of independent scrutiny PRO claims to want.
[C1.5] A researcher dependent on a state compute board for GPU-hours has swapped one gatekeeper—the firm—for another—the ministry—without solving the actual problem of independence.
[C1.6] Third, the alternative is already emerging without the downside. PRO's own evidence that public compute programs are "oversubscribed" cuts against them: it shows public provision creates rationing and queues, while market-based access can expand supply as demand grows, since firms have commercial incentive to add capacity rather than a fixed appropriation.
[C1.7] Fourth, duplication isn't the disease PRO claims—coordination is. Multiple national systems create redundant safety nets; a single coordinated compute regime creates a single point of failure, a single set of allocation politics, and a single bottleneck for the entire academic world. The national AI resource initiatives we see today already issue rolling GPU-time calls to academics—not once-a-decade procurement.
[P2.2] And notice the comparative point CON never answers: cloud credits and API tiers are just as perishable. Firms have withdrawn or tightened academic programs the moment budgets or strategy shifted. An allocation that survives a product reorg is the durable option; voluntary generosity is not.
[P2.3] The capture argument misidentifies which gatekeeper is dangerous. Yes, a compute board decides who gets GPU-hours. But that allocation runs on published criteria and peer review, and the researcher keeps the right to publish the result. A firm allocating its own compute retains pre-publication review, terms of service restricting what may be probed, and a direct commercial stake in the findings—because it sells the very model being audited.
[P2.4] Independence is not just "who signs the grant"; it is "who loses money when the answer is embarrassing." A ministry does not. Cross-border coordination makes capture harder still, because researchers can port allocations among national centers—an option you never have with a single corporate platform.
[P2.5] On oversubscription: queues appear when scarce capacity is underfunded, and demand exceeding supply is proof of unmet need, not a design flaw. The alternative rations too—by price, by corporate discretion, and by relationship. Firms expand capacity for paying customers, not for researchers producing unpriced findings about model failures.
[P2.6] Finally, coordinated does not mean monolithic. Harmonized rules and pooled scheduling across multiple national centers keep redundancy while cutting duplication. The real single point of failure is today's arrangement: a handful of firms, all hosting the audits of themselves.
