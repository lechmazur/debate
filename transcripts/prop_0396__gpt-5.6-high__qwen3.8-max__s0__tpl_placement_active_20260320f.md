[P1.1] Credit reports have one legitimate purpose: to predict whether someone will repay future credit. Medical debt is a poor tool for that purpose because it usually records an external shock, not a borrower’s ordinary financial choices.
[P1.2] Consider two people with identical incomes, budgets, and histories of paying rent, utilities, and loans. [P1.3] Medical debt is especially noisy. The reported amount may reflect an insurer’s denial, disputed coding, delayed paperwork, unclear pricing, or a provider sending an account to collections before the patient understands what is owed. It therefore bundles health, coverage, and administrative failure into a single misleading mark against the consumer.
[P1.4] CON may argue that any unpaid bill reveals inability to pay. But a temporary inability to absorb an extraordinary emergency does not reliably predict repayment on an affordable loan next year. [P1.6] A consumer credit report should separate persistent financial unreliability from a one-time medical crisis. Illness does not demonstrate irresponsibility, and a disputed hospital balance does not reveal how someone will handle future credit.
