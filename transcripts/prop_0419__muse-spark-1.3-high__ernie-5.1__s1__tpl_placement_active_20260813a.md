[P1.1] When a company bills you, locks you in, or puts you at risk, it should not get to hide behind software. A fast path to a human is basic accountability.
[P1.2] Consider billing disputes. A chatbot can apologize forever without fixing anything. It asks for the same account number three times, offers irrelevant help articles, closes the chat for inactivity while you search for a receipt. Meanwhile the erroneous charge stands, late fees pile up, your card is auto-debited again next month.
[P1.3] The harm is not inconvenience; it is money taken without consent. Most people give up over $20 or $40, which is exactly why the trap works at scale.
[P1.4] Cancellations are worse because friction is the business model. Signing up takes one click; canceling takes a forty-minute maze of ‘are you sure?’ prompts, retention offers, and dead-end bot loops that conveniently crash before confirmation. That asymmetry is intentional. A guaranteed human exit restores the simple principle: no company should profit from making it hard to leave.
[P1.5] For safety complaints, bots are dangerous. A script cannot judge urgency when a water heater leaks gas, a car rental’s brakes fail, a child chokes on a toy part, or a passenger faces threats from a driver. Those reports need triage, judgment, and power to act — dispatch, recall, refund, rebooking. Every minute spent arguing with a bot multiplies risk.
[P1.6] This mandate does not ban automation. Companies can still use chatbots for passwords, hours, tracking. It simply requires an obvious, fast human off-ramp for the three moments where stakes are highest and incentives to stonewall are strongest. That is light-touch, pro-consumer, and pro-competition: firms win by serving people, not exhausting them.
[P1.7] Freedom to pay, leave, and be safe means little if you cannot reach a person to make it real.

[C1.1] Mandating a fast‑track human off‑ramp sounds fair, but it creates a blunt instrument that hurts the very consumers it purports to protect. First, the cost of staffing 24/7 live agents for every billing or cancellation query would be enormous, especially for small and medium firms. Those costs are passed on as higher prices, fees, or reduced service elsewhere—so the rule would tax all customers to rescue a few.
[C1.2] Second, speed is not guaranteed by law; it is guaranteed by capacity. Requiring a “fast” human path forces companies to hire enough agents to meet a vague standard, or face penalties. In practice that means long hold times during spikes—think a holiday billing surge where callers wait hours, far slower than a well‑designed bot that resolves routine disputes in minutes.
[C1.3] Third, the proposition conflates three very different problem spaces. Billing disputes are often straightforward data look‑ups that bots handle efficiently; cancellations are already regulated by consumer‑protection statutes that outlaw deceptive dark‑patterns, making a separate human‑path rule redundant. Safety complaints are the one area where urgency matters, but there the bottleneck is usually information triage, not the presence of a human. Sophisticated bots can flag keywords, route to specialist teams, and even dispatch emergency services faster than a generic call‑center queue.
[C1.4] A rigid mandate would prevent companies from deploying smarter, tiered systems that combine automated triage with human escalation only when needed. Better policy targets outcomes—penalties for unresolved disputes, required disclosure of resolution times, and enforcement against deceptive design—rather than prescribing a single channel. By forcing a one‑size‑fits‑all human channel, the rule incentivizes compliance on paper while delivering slower, costlier, and less nuanced service. The real consumer protection lies in holding firms accountable for results, not for how many minutes they spend on a phone line.
