[P1.1] Math tracking doesn't identify talent; it sorts privilege. Advanced tracks in public schools remain stubbornly segregated by race and class, not because of differences in mathematical potential, but because the tests and teacher recommendations that gate entry measure access to enrichment, not capacity to learn. When we label a child "not advanced" in eighth grade, we convert a temporary gap in preparation into a permanent ceiling on opportunity, denying the very students who might catch up the chance to demonstrate their growth.
[P1.2] The alternative is not lowering standards. It is raising them for everyone through heterogeneous classrooms paired with intensive, targeted tutoring. In mixed-ability rooms, struggling students gain from peer modeling and from teachers who must explain concepts with precision to reach every learner. When a classroom includes students at different stages, the teacher cannot rely on a narrow script; they must make algebraic reasoning visible and accessible, which benefits everyone.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, advanced students are not held back; they deepen their understanding by articulating reasoning to classmates, a practice that solidifies mastery more reliably than racing ahead alone through worksheets. The tutoring component—whether delivered after school, during the day, or through embedded support—ensures that students who start behind receive the individualized acceleration they need to remain on the college-preparatory pipeline, rather than being warehoused in a remedial track that leads nowhere.
[P1.4] Districts that have dismantled rigid tracking have seen the share of students eventually reaching advanced coursework rise, not fall, precisely because the arbitrary gatekeeping was removed. The fear that heterogeneous classes drag everyone down to the middle ignores how mathematics actually develops: early gaps are narrow and closable with proper support, while the psychological damage of being labeled "basic" is durable. We face a choice between sorting children based on a snapshot of their past performance, or building the tutoring infrastructure that makes advanced math genuinely accessible to the students tracking was designed to exclude.
[P1.5] The proposition delivers both equity and excellence, proving that high standards and open doors are not rivals but partners.

[C1.2] That lowers the ceiling without raising the floor.
[C1.3] PRO claims mixed rooms “benefit everyone” [P1.2], but the benefit to advanced students is not learning new mathematics; it is peer tutoring dressed up as depth. Explaining slope to a confused classmate does not replace doing proofs, nonlinear systems, or earlier access to calculus. For a student ready for eighth-grade algebra, being placed in heterogeneous grade-level math is a lost year, and lost years in math cannot be recovered by liking the class.
[C1.4] The motion’s equity mechanism is not heterogeneous grouping; it is “intensive tutoring.” That is the part districts cannot deliver at scale. It requires trained math instructors, small ratios, and sustained time—the very resources public schools already lack. When tutoring is underfunded or staffed by untrained aides, the mixed classroom becomes an untracked class with the advanced curriculum stripped out. Struggling students get less direct remediation, not more, because one teacher is stretched across a wider span.
[C1.5] The admitted flaws in tracking are gatekeeping flaws. The alternative is fairer, multiple-entry placement: universal screening, retesting, summer bridge courses, and open pathways into advanced sections. Abolishing the track because the gate was biased is like closing the hospital because triage was unfair. Keep the advanced track; fix who gets in.
[C1.6] The proposition promises equity and excellence, but its likely result is mediocrity for the strongest students and disappointment for those promised tutoring that never arrives.
