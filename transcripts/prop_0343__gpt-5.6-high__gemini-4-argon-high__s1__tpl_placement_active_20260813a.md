AI-assisted drafting can help generate outlines, test counterarguments, revise clarity, or expose gaps. None of that removes responsibility if the student must disclose the assistance, verify every claim, and defend the final reasoning. The relevant authorship is intellectual ownership: Can the student explain, justify, and revise what bears their name?
[P1.2] First, guided use improves learning. A student can ask an AI system for three objections to a thesis, then evaluate which objections are valid and rebuild the argument. That cycle demands judgment rather than mere production. Faculty can require an AI-use statement, selected prompt history, annotated revisions, or a short reflection identifying errors the tool made.
[P1.3] Students thereby learn both the subject and the crucial skill of critically supervising unreliable output.
[P1.4] Second, permission with rules is more enforceable than blanket prohibition. AI access is widespread and difficult to detect reliably. [P1.5] Oral checks, staged drafts, and targeted follow-up questions can reveal whether understanding is real far better than guessing whether prose “sounds AI-generated.”
[P1.6] Third, universities should prepare students for environments where assisted writing will be ordinary. [P1.7] “Most coursework” wisely leaves exceptions. If unaided composition is itself the skill being tested, instructors may restrict AI; secure exams can remain unaided. When a student struggles to translate a vague intuition into a coherent paragraph, that friction is precisely what exposes logical gaps and forces analytical rigor. [C1.2] Claiming that intellectual ownership survives if students evaluate, revise, and defend machine output [P1.1, P1.2] confuses recognition with mastery. Reviewing a fluent, AI-generated draft requires far shallower cognitive processing than synthesizing raw evidence from scratch. A student who studies a machine-drafted history paper well enough to answer follow-up questions has learned how to brief a script, not how to do historical analysis [P1.7]. Moreover, novices cannot critically supervise a tool in a discipline they do not yet understand [P1.3].
[C1.3] AI failures in academic writing are rarely just obvious factual fabrications; they are subtle omissions, flattened nuances, and conventional framing. Students cannot spot what is missing from an argument when they bypass the struggle of building it.
[C1.4] Second, the proposed accountability safeguards are an administrative mirage. Auditing prompt histories, grading annotated revisions, and conducting oral checks [P1.2, P1.5] might work in a tiny seminar, but this motion covers *most* coursework. Faculty and teaching assistants grading dozens or hundreds of students cannot realistically audit multi-page prompt logs or hold individual oral defenses for routine assignments. In practice, disclosure plus accountability collapses into rubber-stamping polished prose.
[C1.5] Finally, disclosure does not solve enforcement [P1.4]. It merely shifts evasion from hiding AI use to misrepresenting its depth—claiming a model only "outlined" an argument it substantively generated. Instead of normalizing cognitive offloading across the curriculum, universities should confine AI training to dedicated modules while protecting unaided drafting as the irreplaceable anvil of student thought.
