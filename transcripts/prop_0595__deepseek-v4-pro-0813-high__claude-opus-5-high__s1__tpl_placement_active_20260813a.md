[P1.1] Workplace AI is not just a productivity tool. It assigns tasks, sets pace, records every pause, scores tone, and increasingly recommends discipline or dismissal. In other words, it is employment policy encoded in software. When a large employer deploys such a system unilaterally, it changes the real terms of work without the consent of the people governed by those terms.
[P1.2] A legal duty to negotiate with an elected worker council is the minimum response to that shift.
[P1.3] Negotiation is not ceremony. AI systems that look neutral in a vendor demo often fail on the floor. A warehouse worker knows when an algorithmic pace treats a safety stop as lost time. A call-center worker knows when sentiment scoring penalizes an accent, a disability, or a calm refusal to be abused.
[P1.4] A delivery worker knows when route optimization makes bathroom access impossible. Elected councils can surface this before deployment, reducing injuries, discrimination, turnover, and compliance failures. Individual workers cannot safely raise these issues because they fear retaliation and lack access to the system's logic. A council bargains with collective power, can demand information, and can secure enforceable safeguards.
[P1.5] Without legal compulsion, the default is not consultation. It is briefing after the decision, if at all. Large employers already have lawyers, data scientists, and consultants designing the rollout; the workforce has no comparable power. A negotiation requirement changes the default from "accept or quit" to "bargain before implementation."
[P1.6] It does not give workers a veto. Employers may still adopt AI, but they must negotiate over data limits, transparency, audits, appeal rights, retraining, and phased rollout. If an AI system is genuinely productive, it can survive that negotiation; if it only works by hiding its effects, that is exactly why scrutiny is needed.
[P1.7] The duty should apply to large employers because they have the capacity to bargain and their systems affect thousands at once. The result is not obstruction but a fairer, better-designed deployment.

Pace algorithms that treat a safety stop as theft; sentiment scoring that penalizes an accent or a disability; routing that makes a bathroom break impossible — those are safety violations and discrimination. The proposition's answer is to put them on a bargaining table. Mine is to take them off the table entirely.
[C1.2] That is the central clash. A duty to negotiate is a duty to reach an accommodation, and accommodations have a price. Once a council has bargained over the scoring system and won a retraining fund and a phased rollout, the system has been *legitimated*. The employer now says, truthfully, that worker representatives agreed.
[C1.3] Procedural consent is the cheapest liability shield ever invented, and this proposal hands it over by statute.
[C1.4] Consent is only meaningful if the consenting party understands what it is consenting to. Elected councils will be ordinary employees facing vendors' data scientists, evaluating models whose behavior their own designers often cannot fully explain, on a bargaining clock. They will not audit an error rate across demographic subgroups. They will trade what they can see — headcount, pace targets — for things they cannot.
[C1.5] And they will trade on behalf of the people in the room. The people most damaged by workplace AI are frequently not in the room: rejected job applicants screened by hiring models, agency temps, subcontracted cleaners and drivers. An incumbent council has every incentive to protect insiders and no incentive to protect the applicant who never got called. Restricting the duty to large employers makes this worse, giving firms one more reason to push algorithmically managed work down into the subcontracting layer where nobody negotiates anything.
[C1.6] There is a better instrument available and PRO has already named its contents: transparency, data limits, appeal rights, independent audits, a human decision-maker before dismissal. Legislate those as floors, enforced by a regulator with technical capacity, covering every worker regardless of employer size or council election results.
[C1.7] Rights, universally. Not chips, locally.

[P5.1] The decisive reason is baseline power. The opponent keeps judging a negotiated agreement against a regulator’s ideal rulebook. That comparison is illusory. In the lawful discretionary zone both sides agree exists, the actual alternative is not unconditional protection; it is the employer’s compliance and data staff writing the default before workers can object.

When a council accepts broader keystroke monitoring than some future regulator might choose, that is not selling a right. Disclosure and a regulator cannot sit inside every workplace or specify every pace, score, and monitoring boundary while the system is being configured; they mostly arrive after harm is locked in.
