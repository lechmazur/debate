[P1.1] The proposition is narrow and defensible: when a college degree isn't genuinely necessary for job performance, requiring one is bad policy. The qualifier matters — we're not discussing neurosurgery or structural engineering. We're talking about the vast middle band of office, administrative, technical, and managerial roles where employers use the diploma as a lazy filter rather than a genuine requirement.
[P1.2] Degree inflation is real. Jobs that mid-twentieth-century employers filled with high school graduates — entry-level analysts, project coordinators, HR staff, sales representatives, IT support — now routinely list bachelor's degrees as mandatory. The work didn't change. The credential creep did.
[P1.3] Three costs follow. First, employers exclude capable candidates who couldn't afford college or chose not to attend. A candidate with five years of relevant experience and demonstrated skill loses to a recent graduate with none of those things simply because one cleared an arbitrary hurdle. That's not meritocracy; it's credentialism masquerading as standards.
[P1.4] Second, degrees are weak proxies for what employers actually need. A marketing degree doesn't guarantee someone can run a campaign. A communications degree doesn't prove someone can write clearly. Work samples, skills assessments, and certifications test capability directly.
[P1.5] Employers who shift to skills-based hiring find broader applicant pools and no drop in hire quality — because they measure what matters instead of what's convenient.
[P1.6] Third, degree requirements reproduce inequality. College completion correlates strongly with family income. When employers gate non-specialized jobs behind degrees, they select for socioeconomic advantage, not talent. The result is a labor market where class background predicts access more than ability does.
[P1.7] The proposition asks employers to align requirements with actual job demands. If a degree isn't necessary for performance, demanding it anyway is exclusion without purpose. Employers should stop — for their own bottom line and for the candidates they're currently turning away.
