[P1.1] When a performer's paycheck rises or falls with how many people watched, but only the streaming service knows how many people watched, that is not a bargain. It is a trust exercise controlled by the payer.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the core defect this requirement fixes. In theatrical film, box office receipts were publicly reported. In broadcast television, ratings were measured by third parties and widely circulated. A writer, actor, or director could see whether a work succeeded, estimate what it was worth, and negotiate the next deal.
[P1.3] Streaming broke that shared reality. Platforms collect detailed data on starts, completions, rewatches, and abandonment, then release only selective highlights — a Top 10 list or a vague claim of millions of views. The people whose income depends on reach must accept the platform's summary of its own liability.
[P1.4] If compensation is tied to audience reach, the data defining that reach is not a courtesy. It is the pay stub. No other industry would tolerate an employer saying, "Your bonus depends on sales, but sales figures are proprietary, so take our word for it." Royalties, residuals, and profit participation have always carried an implied right to inspect the underlying numbers.
[P1.5] Streaming should not create a black-box exception simply because the measurement technology is internal.
[P1.6] Standardization is what makes disclosure meaningful rather than theatrical. Without common definitions, a platform can count a two-minute autoplay as a view, inflate hours by bundling trailers, or report only the best week in the best territory. A shared standard — for example, unique accounts reached, completed views, and total viewing time for that specific title over a defined period — allows filmmakers and performers to verify payment, compare offers across services, and spot errors or manipulation. It reveals performance of their own work, not personal information about viewers and not the recommendation algorithm.
[P1.7] Platforms remain free to pay flat fees and keep broader analytics private. But once they make reach the price, they must show the count.

[C1.1] This rule does not fix the power imbalance PRO describes. It will harm the very creators it claims to protect, in three unavoidable ways.
[C1.2] First: Streaming viewing data is not a neutral payroll number, as PRO pretends. It is the single most valuable competitive asset each platform holds. The relationship between viewing patterns, user retention, and cross-title audience pull is how small upstart platforms compete against the largest incumbents. Forcing universal standardized disclosure of per-title performance data hands the dominant platforms a permanent, unassailable advantage.
[C1.3] New entrants will not be able to protect the metrics that let them carve out a niche. Fewer competing buyers for creator work will drive down overall compensation far more than any residual underpayment ever could.
[C1.4] Second: There is no neutral standard. PRO presents standardization as a common sense fix, but every possible definition of "reach" picks winners and losers among creators. Count completed views, and you destroy residual value for background streamed performance, experimental short film, and children's content that runs on repeat. Count unique viewers, and you punish the beloved rewatchable comedies that keep subscribers paying month after month.
[C1.5] There is no fair one-size-fits-all rule. Writing one into law will not end manipulation—it will just lock in which types of creators get systematically shortchanged.
[C1.6] Third: This mandate will eliminate success-based pay entirely. If tying compensation to reach forces platforms to surrender their most sensitive competitive data, they will simply stop offering reach-based deals. Creators will lose the ability to negotiate upside bonuses for breakout hits. They will be left with only flat up-front buyouts, with no path to share in the success of work that takes off.
[C1.7] That is not fairness. That is throwing away the entire upside of performance pay to win a trivial argument about transparency.

[P2.1] The competitive-harm argument collapses on who receives the data. The requirement shares standardized viewing data with the filmmakers and performers whose pay depends on it — not publishes catalogue performance to rival platforms. A lead actor learning how many accounts completed her series learns nothing usable about another service's retention strategy, churn model, or recommendation system. If confidentiality limits are needed for audit files, impose them.
[P2.2] Secrecy toward the person being paid is not secrecy toward the market.
[P2.3] Nor does standardized measurement dictate standardized pay. That distinction answers the claim that any definition picks winners and losers. Report a common basket — unique accounts reached, completions, and total viewing time — and contracts remain free to reward whichever dimension they value. A rewatchable comedy can be paid on repeat hours; a prestige drama on completions; children's programming on total time.
[P2.4] Standardizing the thermometer does not force everyone to be paid for the same temperature. What it prevents is each platform privately redefining "view" after the work succeeds.
[P2.5] The prediction that platforms will abandon success-based pay is also overstated. They offer it because it attracts sought-after talent, shares risk on unproven projects, and lets them pay most when a title delivers most value. A service retreating to flat buyouts while a rival offers verified upside will lose bids, or must raise flat fees to compensate creators for surrendering that upside. Either preserves more bargaining power than unverifiable bonuses.
[P2.6] History points the same way. Public box-office reporting and shared television ratings did not end residuals or profit participation; they made those systems workable because both sides negotiated from visible success. Streaming's internal metrics deserve no special exemption.
[P2.7] At bottom, creators are asked to accept the worst of both worlds: pay exposed to audience performance, but proof of that performance treated as untouchable. If reach is commercial enough to set the paycheck, it is not too sacred to show on the paycheck.

[P4.1] A1: Yes, for anyone actually receiving it — and that is not every day performer. Access attaches only where compensation depends on reach, so a flat-fee day player gets no report at all. For a residual participant, enforceable confidentiality, access through guilds, representatives, or auditors, and penalties for deliberate commercial disclosure are workable. Payroll figures, screeners, and budgets already move on that basis; occasional leaks are not a reason to deny the payee the number itself.
[P4.2] A2: A public standard-setter — a regulator convening measurement experts, creator guilds, and platforms — defines the counting, not the pay. Because the standard is a basket, it does not crown one kind of work. It transparently defines what a start, a completion, and an account reached mean, then leaves each contract free to reward unique reach, completions, or total viewing time. Authority over vocabulary is not authority over value.
[P4.3] That distinction also defeats the leakage panic. Suppose a title's aggregate reach becomes widely discussed, as box-office grosses and television ratings routinely were. A rival still learns nothing about recommendation weights, subscriber churn, acquisition costs, or which viewers were retained. It learns that a competitor's show was watched — the same visible fact that once let creators price themselves across studios.
[P4.4] Calling that a surrender of the business confuses a pay stub with a playbook.
[P4.5] Compare the imperfect alternatives. A public, revisable standard may involve argument over autoplay or pauses, but today each platform settles those questions alone, invisibly, and potentially after success is known. A central definition can be challenged and corrected; a private definition can merely be suspected. Likewise, confidentiality may not be watertight, but watertight secrecy for the payer guarantees blindness for the person paid.
[P4.6] And the exit threat still founders on talent competition: a platform refusing verified upside must either lose sought-after creators to one offering it or pay more upfront for surrendering it. What cannot survive scrutiny is the current offer — trust our count, accept our definition, cash our check.
