[P1.1] When a 19‑year‑old sits in a therapist’s office, the last thing she should worry about is whether her insurance company will flag the appointment on a statement that lands in her parents’ mailbox. Privacy is not a luxury for young adults; it is a prerequisite for effective mental‑health care. The proposition grants them that prerequisite, and the stakes are measurable: research on adolescent help‑seeking consistently shows that fear of parental disclosure cuts treatment utilization in half for issues ranging from depression to substance use.
[P1.2] Consider a gay teenager who fears family rejection, or a college student who survived childhood abuse. If the visit becomes a public family matter, the immediate reaction is avoidance, not healing. Avoidance translates into crisis—higher rates of self‑harm, emergency‑room visits, and long‑term disability. Surveys of college counseling centers indicate that roughly one‑third of students who considered therapy never followed through because they feared a parent would learn about it.
[P1.3] The insurance‑billing problem compounds the barrier. Even when a young adult pays out‑of‑pocket, the default billing pathway sends a claim to a parent’s plan, creating a de‑facto notification system. Allowing private, unbilled or confidentially billed encounters removes the financial paperwork that otherwise chills help‑seeking. Autonomy matters because 18‑year‑olds are legally adults, trusted to vote, sign contracts, and serve in the military, yet we deny them control over the most intimate decision of seeking care.
[P1.4] Opponents will argue parental oversight protects minors, but the motion already applies to adults, and the real risk is not over‑protection—it is the well‑documented deterrent effect of forced disclosure. By guaranteeing private access, we align the law with the developmental reality that young adults need a safe, stigma‑free entry point to treatment, and we prevent the cascade of harms that follow when that entry point is blocked.
