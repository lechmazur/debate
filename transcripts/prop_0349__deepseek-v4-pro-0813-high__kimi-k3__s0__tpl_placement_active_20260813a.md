[P1.1] Severe treatment-resistant depression, PTSD, and addiction are not merely “difficult cases.” They kill. They disable. After years of failed antidepressants, therapy, or hospitalization, the standard options leave too many people cycling through suicide attempts or incapacity. A government that confines a promising therapy to narrow trials is deciding that those patients may keep dying while the evidence accumulates at academic pace.
[P1.2] The clinical logic is straightforward. Psychedelic-assisted therapy appears to work differently: a small number of supervised sessions, combined with preparation and integration, can produce rapid and sometimes lasting shifts for people who have failed multiple conventional treatments. It does not require indefinite daily medication, and its risks are concentrated around the dosing session itself—exactly where regulation can be strongest.
[P1.3] That is the key point against the safety objection. The motion does not legalize self-treatment or recreational use. It permits medical access with screening for psychosis or cardiac risk, trained therapists, monitored dosing, and follow-up. These safeguards directly address the main foreseeable harms: panic, transient blood-pressure changes, or psychological destabilization.
[P1.4] By contrast, refusing regulated access does not eliminate risk; it pushes some desperate patients toward unregulated black markets or unguided use, where no monitoring exists.
[P1.5] The trial-only status quo is also unjust, not merely cautious. Trials cluster around research centers, exclude many of the sickest or most complex patients, and enroll only a tiny fraction of those who qualify. That makes treatment a lottery. We do not restrict ECT, methadone, or ketamine to trial settings forever once a medical use is established; we license, monitor, and update the rules.
[P1.6] Psychedelic-assisted therapy can be treated the same way.
[P1.7] The central choice is between managed access for the severely ill and a purity of caution that protects the system more than the patient. A patient with twenty years of refractory PTSD should not have to enroll in a study to have a chance at recovery.
