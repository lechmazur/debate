[P1.1] The fundamental purpose of a statute is to guide public behavior. When a legislature drafts a law in impenetrable legalese, it abandons this purpose. [P1.2] First, the rule of law requires knowable law. Citizens cannot comply with rules they cannot read. When statutes are buried in nested clauses, archaic jargon, and cross-references that only a specialist can decipher, the law ceases to be a public guide and becomes a trap. When the law is a foreign language, justice becomes a luxury good available only to those who can afford elite translators.
[P1.3] Plain language ensures that a small business owner can understand their tax obligations, or a tenant can understand their housing rights, without having to pay a lawyer just to decode the government’s own rules.
[P1.4] Second, the CON side will heavily defend legal precision, but they mistake verbosity for precision. True precision means the reader accurately grasps the rule, not that the drafter successfully hid a loophole in a subordinate clause. Hyper-technical drafting often creates more ambiguity, not less. It invites endless litigation over semantic technicalities and hyper-literal interpretations that defy the statute's obvious intent.
[P1.5] Plain language focuses on clear, functional meaning. When a statute clearly states its purpose in everyday English, judges can apply the law as the public understands it, rather than getting bogged down in syntactic puzzles.
[P1.6] Finally, we must weigh the impacts. The cost of sacrificing some edge-case precision is mostly borne by rare scenarios that appellate courts are already equipped to resolve through statutory interpretation and established precedent. The benefit of plain language, however, is systemic. It restores the social contract.
[P1.7] A government that speaks to its citizens in a language they understand is a government that respects them. Accessibility must trump pedantic precision.
