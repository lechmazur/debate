[P1.1] Luxury and performance cars warrant tighter urban enforcement because their costs are not evenly scattered; they are exported from the driver’s seat to the nearest sidewalk. A family hatchback can be driven badly. A performance car is built to make the cost-producing act easy: abundant torque, instant throttle response, launch modes, and exhausts designed or tuned to crackle on downshift. In a city, a two-second burst becomes sleep interruption, startled pedestrians, cyclists recalculating risk, and a spike in crash exposure where people are crossing.
[P1.2] That is what “unusually concentrated” means. Hard streets and narrow blocks reflect sound; apartments put many non-consenting listeners in the same block; traffic lights generate repeat launches. Sound rises nonlinearly, so an aggressive exhaust is not a minor difference in taste. It is an involuntary sensory taking, often at night and impossible for the listener to trace to a plate.
[P1.3] The driver sits in a quiet premium cabin and receives acceleration as the benefit. Private gain, public noise and risk: self-restraint is weak.
[P1.4] Tighter enforcement follows the cost. Ordinary limits remain, but the high-capability tail should face lower urban decibel tolerance, checks on modified or valved exhausts, and swift penalties for repeated hard launches, racing-style starts, or abrupt acceleration in dense zones. Different vehicles already carry different duties when they create different risks—heavy trucks, commercial drivers, motorcycles. Regulating a machine by what it can repeatedly do in public is not punishing wealth.
[P1.5] The equality objection has the burden backwards. Identical enforcement is not neutral when the capacity to impose harm is unequal, and conduct-only rules are too slow for costs that are brief, localized, and suffered by people who cannot identify the offender. Waiting for complaints after every exhaust crack pretends concentrated harm is random. Tighter scrutiny keeps ordinary motorists under ordinary rules while making the street stop subsidizing a premium thrill.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to punish the machine because it occasionally enables the misconduct. That is a category error. A car does not crackle its exhaust or launch from a traffic light; a driver does. An elderly hatchback with a hole in its muffler and a teenager who floors a base-model sedan can impose exactly the concentrated costs PRO describes—startled pedestrians, sleep interruption, and crash exposure.
[C1.2] The harm comes from behavior, maintenance, and enforcement gaps, not from a vehicle's price tag or its factory torque curve.
[C1.3] PRO argues that performance cars make the cost-producing act easy. But ease is not culpability. A high-performance car driven legally is quieter and safer than an aggressive driver in a rusted-out compact. If the cost is unusually concentrated, then the penalty should track the actual act—hard acceleration, illegal exhaust modification, racing-style starts—not a broad class of vehicles.
[C1.4] We already know how to write conduct-based rules. They are fairer and harder to evade because they follow the wheel, not the window sticker.
[C1.5] Class-based enforcement is also administratively crude. It diverts police attention from profiling dangerous driving toward stopping every responsible owner of a fast car. It creates perverse incentives: ordinary drivers learn that aggressive acceleration carries less scrutiny simply because their car is less exotic, while a careful performance-car driver faces suspicion for doing nothing wrong. Wealth may be visible, but bad driving is not.
[C1.6] Targeting the former rewards the poor lawbreaker and punishes the rich law-abider.
[C1.7] Different vehicles already face different duties—trucks, buses, motorcycles—but those duties arise from structural capacities: size, braking, cargo, passenger safety. A performance car's capacity for speed is not a structural difference that inherently endangers others in the same way; a truck's blind spots are. The fair rule is simple: enforce existing noise and reckless-driving ordinances against everyone. If a two-second burst is harmful, ticket the burst.
[C1.8] If an exhaust is illegal, ticket the exhaust. Do not tax the badge.
