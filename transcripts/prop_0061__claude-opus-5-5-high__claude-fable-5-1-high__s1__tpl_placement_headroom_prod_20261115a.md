[P1.1] Japan is not facing a future problem; it is living one. Births have fallen to well under 800,000 a year while deaths far exceed them, so the country now loses roughly the population of a mid-sized city every year. The working-age population has been shrinking for three decades. The question is not whether Japan adapts, but who carries the cost of adapting.
[P1.2] My case rests on three claims.
[P1.3] First, automation cannot fill the gaps that matter most. Japan is a world leader in robotics, and still its nursing homes, hospitals, construction sites, farms and logistics depots report chronic shortages. Lifting a frail patient, comforting someone with dementia, repairing a burst pipe in an old house: these are tasks machines handle poorly and expensively. Robots also do not pay into the pension system, rent apartments, or buy groceries in a declining town.
[P1.4] A smaller workforce with better machines still leaves a tax base too thin to support an expanding elderly population.
[P1.5] Second, "shrinking expectations" is a polite phrase for a brutal reality. It means rural clinics closing, bus routes cancelled, schools merged out of existence, millions of abandoned homes, and elderly people waiting longer for care that may never arrive. Those who pay are the most vulnerable: the old, the rural, and the young workers asked to fund everyone else.
[P1.6] Third, immigration works, and Japan already knows it. Foreign workers now staff convenience stores, food factories and care facilities. Programs like the Specified Skilled Worker visa exist precisely because employers cannot find staff. The problem is that policy remains timid, temporary, and built to keep newcomers from settling.
[P1.7] Prioritizing immigration means pathways to permanence, family reunification, and language support, turning workers into residents and taxpayers who stay.
[P1.8] The comparison is simple: immigration plus automation can sustain Japan; automation plus decline merely manages its retreat.

[C1.2] Start with scale. Japan now loses well over half a million people a year net, and the gap widens annually. To offset that, Japan would need something like a Toronto's worth of net newcomers every year, permanently, because immigrants age too and their fertility converges toward the host's within a generation. It is a treadmill, not a solution.
[C1.3] Meanwhile the sending countries Japan depends on, Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia, are themselves aging fast, and Korea, Taiwan and the Gulf are bidding for the same workers. Building national strategy on a labor supply that is shrinking and contested is reckless.
[C1.4] Second, the fiscal story collapses on inspection. The workers Japan can realistically attract are low-wage care aides, farmhands and factory staff. They pay modest taxes, qualify for services, and under the family reunification the proposition demands, bring dependents. Low-wage immigration does not rescue a pension system; it widens the base of people the system must eventually support.
[C1.5] Third, cheap imported labor is a sedative. Japanese wages stagnated for thirty years partly because firms could always find someone to underpay. Scarcity is finally forcing investment, consolidation of hopelessly small firms, and real wage gains for young Japanese workers. Flooding the market with low-cost labor reverses exactly that correction.
[C1.6] Fourth, "shrinking expectations" means compact cities, merged municipalities, hub clinics, demand-responsive transit. A Japan of ninety million is still larger than Germany. Per-capita prosperity, not headcount, measures success.
[C1.7] Japan should take selective immigrants. It should not stake its social cohesion on a demographic bet that cannot pay off.
