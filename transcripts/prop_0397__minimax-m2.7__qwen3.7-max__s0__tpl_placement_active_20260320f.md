[P1.1] Student debt is the only debt in America where the borrower cannot discharge it through bankruptcy without proving "undue hardship"—a standard so vague and demanding that most courts deny relief even to the disabled, the chronically ill, and those whose incomes place repayment permanently out of reach. This is not an accident of history; it is a policy choice, and it is wrong.
[P1.2] The fresh start principle has been a cornerstone of American bankruptcy law since its founding. It exists because society recognized that punishing people forever for financial misfortune serves no one—not the debtor, not creditors, and not the economy at large. We apply this principle to credit card debt, medical bills, business loans, and mortgage defaults.
[P1.3] But for student loans, we have created a permanent underclass of borrowers whose wages can be garnished indefinitely, whose tax refunds can be seized, and whose Social Security benefits can be reduced to satisfy debts they incurred as teenagers with no credit history and no real understanding of what they were signing.
[P1.4] Consider the single mother working two jobs who still cannot escape $60,000 in debt because she earns too little to qualify for the narrow income-driven repayment plans, and courts refuse to find her situation "harsh enough." Consider the borrower who becomes disabled at 25 and spends 40 years unable to work before the government seizes their benefits. These are not hypothetical edge cases—they are the routine outcomes of a system designed to extract payment regardless of circumstance.
[P1.5] Making bankruptcy relief more accessible would not eliminate accountability. Borrowers would still face credit consequences, and colleges would still bear reputational costs. But the current regime treats student debt as uniquely unforgivable, while the evidence shows that graduates default at rates driven by factors—family wealth, field of study, labor market luck—that bankruptcy can distinguish between and other policies cannot.
[P1.6] The proposition does not demand total forgiveness. It demands fairness: the same second chance we extend to every other borrower. That is a modest request, and the case for it is overwhelming.

[C1.2] Because the vast majority of student loans are federally backed, discharging this debt simply transfers the cost to taxpayers. Lowering the bar invites massive moral hazard. Borrowers who could manage payments through existing income-driven plans will choose bankruptcy to wipe the slate clean. [C1.3] The PRO side’s "fresh start" for current borrowers becomes a financial penalty for future generations.

[C1.5] Finally, treating student loans like unsecured consumer debt ignores that they are issued without credit checks or collateral, based entirely on the promise of future earnings. We must reject this systemic risk in favor of targeted, sustainable relief.
