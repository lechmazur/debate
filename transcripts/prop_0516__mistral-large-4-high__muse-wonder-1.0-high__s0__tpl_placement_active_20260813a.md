[P1.1] The housing crises gripping university cities are not abstract—they are visceral. In Toronto, Vancouver, Sydney, and Amsterdam, rental vacancy rates have collapsed to historic lows while international student enrollment has surged past local infrastructure. Public universities, facing austerity and provincial funding cuts, have treated overseas tuition as a lifeline, recruiting aggressively into communities that cannot house them. This is not merely a university finance question; it is a question of who bears the cost of institutional growth, and whether public institutions should externalize their expansion onto local housing markets.
[P1.2] A public university exists to serve the public good. When a taxpayer-funded institution maximizes revenue by flooding a tight rental market with thousands of additional residents requiring immediate housing, it externalizes severe costs onto local families. Working-class students, newcomers, and long-term residents find themselves priced out, displaced, or pushed into precarious living situations. The university captures the tuition windfall; the community absorbs the rent spikes, housing insecurity, and social strain.
[P1.3] This is a market failure in the purest sense: the institution profits while others pay.
[P1.4] This proposition does not oppose international education or diversity. It recognizes that physical capacity is finite and that growth has limits. When local housing shortages become severe, unlimited recruitment becomes a form of institutional negligence. A cap is a targeted, conditional brake—not a permanent wall.
[P1.5] It insists that universities grow only as fast as their communities can sustainably absorb them without destroying the very neighborhoods that host them.
[P1.6] Leaving capacity unconstrained treats local housing markets as infinite resources. They are not. Until supply meaningfully catches up through construction or policy reform, public universities must choose between their balance sheets and their neighbors. The ethical and practical answer is clear: cap recruitment when the community is drowning.
