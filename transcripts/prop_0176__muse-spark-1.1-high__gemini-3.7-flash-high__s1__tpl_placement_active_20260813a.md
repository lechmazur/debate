[P1.1] Commodity booms are not income. They are a one-time sale of finite national wealth at a temporarily high price. The job of management is to solve three problems: price volatility, resource exhaustibility, and domestic absorption limits. Sovereign wealth funds solve all three; mailing checks solves none.
[P1.2] Inject a windfall directly into a poor economy with broken roads, thin supply chains and few factories and you do not create prosperity, you create inflation. Demand spikes, supply cannot respond, prices rise. The real exchange rate appreciates and farmers and manufacturers who must compete outside oil get crushed. That is Dutch Disease by design.
[P1.3] A fund that saves abroad and releases only a smoothed, sustainable amount to the budget sterilizes the inflow, dampens that appreciation, and keeps non-oil tradables alive. Chile and Botswana used funds to ride out copper and diamond cycles; Nigeria, Venezuela and Mongolia tried to spend or distribute booms as they arrived and collapsed when prices fell.
[P1.4] Second, the oil will end. The fundamental question is what you turn it into. A fund makes the conversion deliberate: subsoil wealth becomes productive assets - power grids, ports, schools, roads that allow a diversified economy to emerge. A thousand dollar check fragmented across millions is consumed.
[P1.5] It cannot finance a power plant or a highway network. When the well runs dry the country is left with memories of consumption, not higher capacity. That violates intergenerational equity in the most literal sense.
[P1.6] Cash sounds like an anti-corruption shortcut, but it bypasses state-building rather than improving it. It creates an immediate entitlement that is politically impossible to cut when prices crash, forcing debt or brutal service cuts. A rules-based fund - with a fiscal rule, independent board, published audits and parliamentary oversight - builds the fiscal institutions developing countries lack. It creates a defensible way to say no today to save for tomorrow.
[P1.7] Better management means turning a temporary spike into permanent development. Only a fund does that.

[C1.1] Sovereign wealth funds in developing countries rest on a fatal paradox: they require the very institutional integrity, transparency, and elite restraint that resource-rich developing nations structurally lack. By centralizing billions of dollars in off-budget accounts, funds create massive honey pots for political patronage, opacity, and outright looting—from Malaysia’s 1MDB to billions vanished in Angola and Equatorial Guinea. Handing a predatory or fragile state a centralized vault does not create Norway; it entrenches rent-seeking elites.
[C1.2] Direct cash distributions solve the fundamental political economy of the resource curse. First, cash transfers break elite capture by putting wealth directly beyond the reach of corrupt officials. More crucially, direct dividends build state capacity rather than bypassing it. When citizens directly receive resource revenues, governments are forced to tax their population to fund public administration.
[C1.3] This fiscal relationship is the bedrock of democracy: taxation transforms passive subjects into vigilant taxpayers who actively demand accountability, public goods, and institutional oversight.
[C1.4] Second, the claim that citizen cash is merely squandered on ephemeral consumption ignores the reality of developing economies. For impoverished households facing acute credit constraints, direct cash is productive capital. It is spent on child nutrition, schooling, preventative healthcare, and local micro-enterprises. These bottom-up investments in human capital yield far higher social and economic returns than state-directed "white elephant" infrastructure projects—the stalled highways, half-built ports, and inflated contracts that routinely devour centralized funds.
[C1.5] Third, direct distributions protect against volatility when indexed to actual revenues as variable dividends. Instead of trusting unaccountable fund managers to play global financial markets while domestic poverty festers, direct distributions alleviate immediate deprivation and build local economic resilience from the ground up.
[C1.6] For developing nations, the superior path is clear: do not trust centralized state vaults in fragile institutions. Empower citizens directly, build human capital, and create the taxpayer accountability necessary for genuine development.

[C2.1] PRO claims that distributing cash widens the surface area for theft [P2.2]. This gets the mechanics of corruption backward. [C2.2] In contrast, modern direct cash transfers utilize biometric identification and direct mobile money. Siphoning billions from millions of individual citizen wallets in broad daylight is impossible without instant public detection. Cash decentralizes wealth, putting it physically beyond elite reach.
[C2.3] PRO then paints a fantasy of benevolent state planning, asserting that funds finance essential infrastructure while cash only fuels fleeting consumption [P2.5]. But in fragile, resource-rich states, centralized capital rarely builds efficient infrastructure; it finances inflated procurement contracts, political patronage, and abandoned white elephant projects.
[C2.4] Meanwhile, impoverished households use cash transfers as productive capital: buying fertilizer, starting micro-enterprises, and paying for nutrition and schooling. These decentralized investments yield immense social returns. A highway or power plant generates little value if the population lacks the basic health and skills to participate in the economy.
[C2.5] PRO also warns that variable dividends expose the poor to volatility [P2.7]. Yet centralized funds fail that exact test: developing regimes routinely raid, deplete, or secretly borrow against their stabilization funds the moment politics demand it—as seen repeatedly with Nigeria's excess crude accounts. A formulaic dividend creates transparent expectations linked to market reality, rather than a hidden treasury buffer that elites quietly pillage.
[C2.6] Most critically, cash dividends dismantle the rentier state. When governments must ask dividend-earning citizens for tax revenue [P2.3], it activates genuine democratic accountability. Stashing windfalls in opaque state vaults simply perpetuates the institutional failure that direct distributions are designed to cure.
