[P1.1] Major beverage and packaging producers profit from selling millions of tons of single-use containers, yet when those items become waste, the disposal cost falls on local governments and, ultimately, taxpayers. This reverses basic fairness: those who profit from a product should bear the costs of managing its waste. The proposition champions Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), a policy already functioning across the European Union, Canada, and parts of the United States.
[P1.2] Consider British Columbia, where a full EPR system for packaging has operated since 2014. Producers finance and manage collection and recycling, lifting recycling rates while sparing municipal budgets hundreds of millions in avoided disposal costs. Germany’s Green Dot program does the same, making manufacturers responsible for take-back and spurring design innovations like lightweight bottles and concentrated detergents that cut material use at the source. These proven systems reduce landfill, lower public spending, and shift burdens from households to the supply chain.
[P1.3] Critics say producers will pass costs to consumers. Yet that is economically sound: if a product’s price includes waste management, it signals true cost and discourages overconsumption. Currently, taxpayers subsidize artificially cheap products. Moreover, EPR empowers producers to redesign packaging—eliminating unnecessary layers or switching to reusable formats—because they now control the entire lifecycle cost, incentivizing prevention over disposal.
[P1.4] Opponents argue government should manage all waste services, but that stance socializes private profit. Why should a resident’s property taxes fund the disposal of a multinational corporation’s aluminum can? The current system violates the polluter-pays principle and stifles sustainable innovation. Moving producer funding is not radical; it is overdue accountability.
[P1.5] We must end the corporate welfare of externalized waste costs and ensure those who put products into commerce shoulder responsibility for their end-of-life impact.

[P2.1] CON’s central charge that EPR is a regressive consumption tax collapses under the weight of the status quo it defends. Low-income households already bear a disproportionate share of current waste costs: property taxes and municipal waste fees are baked into rent, and low-income families spend a far larger share of their income on these embedded costs than wealthy households, with no rebate or exemption available. EPR systems can include targeted household rebates or exemptions for low-income residents, a flexibility the rigid tax system CON favors lacks.
[P2.2] CON’s claim that producer-run systems privatize accountability with no public recourse ignores the chronic underfunding of the public systems it upholds. Countless U.S. cities have slashed or eliminated curbside recycling in recent years due to municipal budget shortfalls, with residents left with no meaningful recourse to restore service. Regulated EPR programs have mandated performance standards, public reporting requirements, and financial penalties for producers who fail to meet recycling targets, with public oversight built into the framework.
[P2.3] CON’s assertion that shifting costs does not create recycling infrastructure misses the entire logic of Extended Producer Responsibility. When producers are financially responsible for end-of-life management, they have a direct, mandatory incentive to invest in collection systems, recycling facilities, and material markets to lower their own costs, rather than passing a flat fee onto consumers. Far from a permanent surcharge, this incentive drives redesign: producers will cut unnecessary packaging layers, switch to lighter, recyclable materials, or adopt reusable formats to reduce their EPR fees, passing long-term savings on to households.
[P2.4] British Columbia’s EPR system funded over 100 new recycling facilities in its first five years of operation, because producers had a financial stake in building the systems needed to process their own packaging.
