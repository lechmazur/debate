[P1.1] Export bans on staple foods are the single most destructive policy a government can deploy during a crisis. They turn a regional shortage into a continent‑wide famine, spike prices, and destroy the very market integration AfCFTA was created to deliver. The proposition is simple: AfCFTA must adopt enforceable, continent‑wide rules that forbid any member from imposing export bans on staples during declared emergencies.
[P1.2] Consider the 2007‑08 global food price shock. When several West African states banned rice and wheat exports, neighboring countries that relied on those imports saw price hikes of 30‑40 percent within weeks, pushing millions into acute food insecurity. The same pattern repeated in 2022 when the Russia‑Ukraine war prompted at least three African nations to restrict maize shipments; the result was a cascade of price spikes that hit the poorest households hardest. These are not isolated incidents; they are the predictable outcome of unrestricted export bans.
[P1.3] An enforceable rule changes the calculus. If a ban is prohibited unless an independent AfCFTA panel certifies an extraordinary, time‑limited emergency, governments lose the cheap political tool of scapegoating neighbours. The rule also creates a credible commitment: traders and investors know that supply chains will not be arbitrarily cut, encouraging cross‑border investment in storage and logistics that further stabilises markets.
[P1.4] The mechanism is already familiar. The EU’s internal market bans quantitative restrictions on essential goods, backed by the European Court of Justice. AfCFTA can adopt a comparable arbitration panel with binding sanctions—tariff penalties, loss of voting rights, or financial fines—that are proportionate and enforceable.
[P1.5] Adopting this rule does not cede sovereignty; it protects the sovereignty of food‑secure nations by preventing a single state’s panic from starving an entire region. The benefit is clear: fewer famines, lower prices, stronger integration, and a continent that can finally turn crises into opportunities rather than catastrophes.

[C1.5] A crisis demands flexibility, not a straitjacket. If the African free trade project is to survive, it must be built on mutual support and shared reserves, not on court orders forcing nations to empty their granaries when their own children are starving.
