[P1.1] Violent crime is not spread evenly across a city like butter on toast. It clusters—on a handful of corners, blocks, and addresses that generate a wildly disproportionate share of shootings and assaults. That single fact is the foundation of my case. [P1.2] Hot spot policing puts deterrence exactly where the violence is, before it happens.
[P1.3] The mechanism is certainty, not severity. Decades of criminological work point the same direction: would-be offenders respond far more to the perceived likelihood of being caught than to the length of a sentence they may never serve and rarely contemplate in the heat of a dispute. A statute adding years to a sentence changes nothing for someone who assumes they won't be caught at all.
[P1.4] Now weigh that against the proposition's alternative: longer sentences for repeat offenders. That policy works only through incapacitation and deterrence, and both are weak here. Incapacitation suffers brutal diminishing returns—people age out of violent crime, so we spend enormous sums caging men well past their high-risk years while the next cohort takes the corner untouched.
[P1.5] Longer sentences act after the body is on the ground. Hot spot policing acts before.
[P1.6] The word "accountability" in this motion is doing real work, and I embrace it. The legitimate worry about saturation policing is abuse, harassment, and the erosion of community trust. Tight accountability—body cameras, civilian oversight, data transparency, focusing on places rather than profiling people—is precisely what converts a blunt instrument into a surgical one. It preserves the deterrent benefit while stripping out the harms that make heavy-handed policing backfire.
[P1.7] So the comparison is clean: a targeted, immediate, place-based intervention with built-in safeguards, versus a delayed, expensive, back-end response that the offender never weighs. Prevention beats punishment. That is why PRO prevails.
