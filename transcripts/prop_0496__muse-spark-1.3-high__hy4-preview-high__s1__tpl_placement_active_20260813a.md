[P1.1] War is the gravest power a democracy exercises. It is legitimate only when citizens can see it, authorize it, limit it, and punish its abuses. Private military contractors hired for combat break each of those links, which is why combat must be reserved to uniformed forces under public command.
[P1.2] First, contractors let leaders wage war while pretending not to. When legislatures cap troops, require approval, or public opinion turns against casualties, executives can simply hire guns instead. Contractor deployments, deaths, and injuries are often not counted in official tolls, not debated in parliaments, and shielded by commercial confidentiality. The public cannot restrain a war it cannot see.
[P1.3] That is evasion of democratic control, not efficiency.
[P1.4] Second, contractors shatter the chain of responsibility. Soldiers operate under a public chain of command, military law, and open rules of engagement. Contractors answer ultimately to a contract and a corporation. When civilians are killed — as in Baghdad’s Nisour Square shooting by Blackwater guards — prosecution becomes a jurisdictional maze across hiring state, host state, and home state, investigations stall, and victims wait years for justice.
[P1.5] Voters cannot hold a company accountable at the ballot box; they can only hope a future contract is not renewed.
[P1.6] Third, profit corrupts the decision to use force. A national army bears costs and wants wars to end. A firm paid for deployment, security convoys, and firefights has a financial interest in continued danger and permissive rules. That misalignment encourages escalation, reckless shooting, and mission creep beyond what elected leaders publicly authorized.
[P1.7] Logistics, construction, and training can be contracted. Pulling a trigger in someone else’s country cannot. Combat is inherently sovereign: killing in the name of the people must be done by those answerable to the people. Ban contractors from combat roles, and force democracies to fight in the open or not at all.

[C1.1] This debate is not about whether contractor abuse has occurred; Nisour Square disproves any denial. The real question is whether a categorical ban on private combat roles is the right remedy for democratic accountability. It is not, because accountability follows the state’s decision to use force, not the payroll status of the person who carries it out. When elected leaders authorize, fund, embed, and command contracted defenders, they remain politically and legally answerable.
[C1.2] Parliament can cap numbers, publish contracts, mandate rules of engagement, and cancel funding. If oversight has lagged, the fix is stronger reporting and jurisdiction, the very opposite of pushing the activity out of statutory reach. [P1.2] warns that the public 'cannot restrain a war it cannot see,' but visibility is a reporting requirement; the state can simply be forced to see it.
[C1.3] A ban would perversely reduce that visibility. Force does not disappear when one instrument is removed. Facing a siege on an embassy, attacks on aid convoys, or a fragile partner’s collapse, a democracy must either retreat or substitute another instrument. Too often the substitute is less accountable: classified special-operations raids, drone strikes, or proxy militias.
[C1.4] Those have no public contract, no line-item budget, and often no acknowledged casualty count. The jurisdictional maze PRO laments [P1.4] is a problem of overlapping law; a uniformed or covert alternative can hide behind classification or status-of-forces shields and be harder to punish.
[C1.5] The profit argument [P1.6] also proves too much. Contracted guards do not choose wars; elected leaders do, and they can terminate a contract faster than they can demobilize a battalion. The remedy for bad incentives is fixed-price, tightly scoped, publicly audited contracts, not prohibition. Banning combat contractors would not return war to the demos; it would drive needed force into darker places where voters can see even less.
[C1.6] Keep contractor combat rare, regulated, and reported, and democratic accountability is strengthened, not undermined.
