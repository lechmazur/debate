[P1.1] States that keep colonial, wartime, and secret-police archives sealed long after the fighting and the regime are gone are not guarding security. They are prolonging a silence that still injures the living. Families searching for the disappeared, communities marked by occupation or repression, and historians reconstructing what was done in the state’s name all have claims that strengthen with time, not fade.
[P1.2] The motion is bounded: open these archives much more broadly once immediate security concerns have passed. That is not a call to expose live operations or active sources. It is a refusal to treat historical violence as a permanent state secret. Decades after a war ends, a colony is relinquished, or a dictatorship falls, the names of victims, the chains of command, and the paper trail of coercion belong to those who suffered and to the public record.
[P1.3] A daughter seeking her father’s fate in a secret-police file is not an intelligence risk. A historian tracing forced labor, village clearances, or extrajudicial killings is not an enemy agent. An affected community mapping mass graves or stolen land is exercising a right the state itself created by recording the facts and then hiding them. Where such files have been opened more widely—Stasi records after German reunification, portions of Latin American dictatorship archives, declassified wartime files on civilian atrocities—the result has been painful and clarifying: better history, fewer official myths, and a narrower space for impunity.
[P1.4] Prolonged closure produces the opposite: rumor, denial, and an injury that outlives the original perpetrators.
[P1.5] Residual privacy and sensitivity do not require default secrecy. Redaction where a living person faces genuine harm, controlled access, and distinctions between family inquiries and scholarly use are ordinary safeguards. What cannot be defended is indefinite, unilateral closure of the state’s own record of abuse because openness is politically inconvenient. Truth delayed is not neutral.
[P1.6] Once the immediate threat has passed, these archives should be opened far more broadly to the people with the strongest claim on them.

[C1.1] The motion treats sealed archives as leftover cruelty. That misreads what these files still are. Colonial, wartime, and secret-police records are not a library of finished crimes. They still name living sources, still describe methods successor agencies use, and still sit inside diplomatic bargains that did not expire when the colony, the war, or the dictatorship ended.
[C1.2] “Once immediate security concerns have passed” is a slogan, not a threshold. Immediate danger is not residual danger. A wartime informant’s grandchildren can still be hunted. Counterinsurgency files can teach current militants how the state found people last time.
[C1.3] Interrogation logs and village maps can rearm old feuds. Security does not switch off with the last shot.
[C1.4] Broad release also harms people who never asked to be listed. These dossiers mix coerced statements, neighbor denunciations, and bureaucratic inventions. Opening them much more broadly to families, historians, and affected communities converts suspicion into official stain. A daughter may learn a fate.
[C1.5] A neighbor may be branded an informer by a coerced, unsigned note. The living informant, the wrongly named accomplice, and the victim who wanted silence all lose the only protection they had: that the file stayed closed. Privacy here is not political inconvenience. It is the right of people who cannot cross-examine a dead clerk.
[C1.6] Affected communities do not speak with one voice. Some demand excavation. Others demand a settlement that holds because yesterday’s paper stays buried. Post-conflict and post-colonial archives become weapons: land claims dressed as history, ethnic score-settling, partisan leaks.
[C1.7] Painful clarity is one possible result. Blackmail, rumor made official, and renewed violence are others.
[C1.8] Redaction and controlled access cannot carry a presumption of broad opening. Once a name is out, it cannot be recalled. Incomplete files then masquerade as verdicts. Delayed truth can be unfair.
[C1.9] Indiscriminate disclosure without process or consent is not the end of state power over the past. It is a new round of it.
