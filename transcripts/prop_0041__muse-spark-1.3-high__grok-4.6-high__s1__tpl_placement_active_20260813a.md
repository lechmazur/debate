[P1.1] A fair price reflects the product, not a profile of the buyer. Personalized algorithmic pricing flips that.
[P1.2] Retailers now harvest location, device type, purchase history, browsing habits, and feed them to models that guess how desperate or wealthy you are, then quietly change the price for the exact same item at the exact same time. Two neighbors ordering the same groceries from the same store minutes apart can pay different amounts, with no difference in cost, service, or demand — only in what the algorithm thinks they will tolerate.
[P1.3] That is extraction, not competition. It rewards surveillance, not better products or lower costs.
[P1.4] Second, it systematically hurts the vulnerable. Algorithms do not need race or income on file to redline; zip code, phone model, shopping time, and click patterns are powerful proxies. The single mother searching at midnight for emergency baby formula, the patient refilling a prescription, the renter with few options nearby — these signals of need become signals to charge more. Ability to comparison-shop becomes another luxury, as those with less time, data literacy, or alternatives pay the ignorance penalty.
[P1.5] Third, it corrupts the market itself. Prices are supposed to be public information that lets buyers compare and sellers compete. Secret individualized prices make comparison impossible. You cannot shop around when the price follows you.
[P1.6] A ban is narrow and workable. It does not touch honest variation: sales, coupons, student discounts, bulk buying, or higher prices when inventory is scarce. All of those are transparent and open to anyone who qualifies. What must go is the secret surcharge based on who you are.
[P1.7] Ban the profile price, keep the price tag honest.

[C1.1] Personalized pricing is not a scandal; it is bargaining with better memory. For as long as commerce has existed, sellers have charged what the traffic will bear: the hurried buyer more, the patient bargainer less, the tourist more than the regular. Coupons, student rates, loyalty points, and last-minute markdowns already sort people by willingness to pay. An algorithm that infers the same thing from cart abandonment or device type is a faster coupon, not a secret tax.
[C1.2] A ban would not restore a golden age of honest tags. Uniform prices are not fairer; they are blunter. When one number must fit everyone, sellers aim at the customers who will still buy. Price-sensitive shoppers lose the quiet discounts that keep them in the market.
[C1.3] The midnight shopper does not magically get cheaper formula under a posted price; she may face a higher floor because the store can no longer shade offers toward those who hesitate. Charging less to those who will walk away is how more goods get sold.
[C1.4] Competition already punishes overreach. If a grocer charges extra for an iPhone search at midnight, another grocer, another app, or another tab can offer less. Personalized prices only stick where rivals cannot. Most retail is not that world.
[C1.5] The so-called ignorance penalty is unread circulars and unused coupons, not a uniquely digital crime. Forbidding the practice does not create public comparable prices so much as it reduces targeted deals and leaves posted prices higher on average.
[C1.6] Keeping coupons and student discounts while banning the model that estimates the same facts is a distinction without a principle. The ban would then require regulators to mind-read every pricing engine: inventory versus inferred wealth, A/B test versus profile. That is unworkable. Ban illegal discrimination and deception.
[C1.7] Do not ban sellers from guessing what a customer will pay.

[P2.1] Bargaining with better memory is a pretty name for a one-sided negotiation. In a bazaar you can see the seller, haggle, walk away, and know you were sized up. The algorithm sizes you up in secret, from your phone, your zip code, your midnight click, and hands you a take-it-or-leave-it number you cannot counter. A coupon is an offer you can clip; a profile price is a judgment you cannot see.
[P2.2] That is not a faster coupon, it is the opposite: one is transparent and claimable, the other invisible and inescapable.
[P2.3] The claim that uniform prices hurt the poor gets the economics backwards. When sellers must post one price, that price has to attract enough buyers to move volume. When they can charge each person their maximum, they have no reason to lower the floor — they raise the ceiling shopper by shopper while keeping discounts minimal and targeted only to those who were about to leave anyway. The midnight mother does not get cheaper formula from profiling; she gets flagged as desperate.
[P2.4] Nor does competition save her. Competition only works when buyers can compare. How do you comparison-shop when the price follows you? Open another tab and the tracker follows; drive to another store and you have spent time and gas the algorithm already priced in.
[P2.5] Rival grocers using the same data brokers and pricing software are not checking each other, they are copying each other.
[P2.6] And the workability objection collapses on its own example. We already distinguish a student discount anyone can claim by showing an ID from charging more because your browsing history says you will pay it. One uses a disclosed category and an open offer; the other uses surveillance to personalize a surcharge. Ban the second input, audit the data fields, keep sales, coupons, and scarcity pricing legal.
[P2.7] What remains is guessing what you will pay by reading who you are. That should be illegal.
