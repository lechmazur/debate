[P1.1] Science is facing a systemic crisis of credibility. Public funders pour billions into "novel" studies, yet across fields from oncology to psychology, independent researchers routinely fail to replicate landmark findings. When we fund novelty at the expense of verification, we are building a house of cards. Piling new projects onto an unverified foundation is not progress; it is a massive waste of public resources.
[P1.2] Reserving a meaningful, fixed share of grant budgets specifically for replication corrects this broken incentive structure. Currently, academic systems reward publishing flashy, novel results, while replication is dismissed as low-status, unfunded grunt work. A ring-fenced budget for replication guarantees that verification becomes a viable, prestigious scientific path.
[P1.3] Crucially, it introduces a powerful deterrent against sloppy methodologies and selective reporting. When researchers know their work faces a real, funded probability of being audited, the baseline quality of all primary research will rise.
[P1.4] Opponents will argue that this policy diverts precious capital from groundbreaking discoveries. But a discovery is only groundbreaking if it is true. Funding five robust, verified breakthroughs is infinitely more valuable to society than funding ten flashy claims that lead entire industries down expensive dead ends. When clinical trials or public policies are built on unreplicable academic science, the cost is measured in wasted decades and human lives.
[P1.5] Replication is not an optional luxury to be funded only when there is surplus cash; it is the fundamental mechanism of the scientific method. Just as financial markets rely on independent audits to maintain trust, public science requires dedicated verification to maintain its integrity. Reserving a fixed share of funding for replication is a necessary systemic shift. It ensures we spend public money on building a bedrock of reliable knowledge, rather than a mountain of exciting but ultimately useless errors.

[C1.1] Nobody here defends unreliable science. The question is narrower and harder: should funders buy reliability by ring-fencing a fixed slice of money for one particular activity? That's not a defence of rigour; it's a bet that a quota beats judgment. It doesn't.
[C1.2] Start with what a fixed share actually does to incentives. Ring-fence money and you create a protected pot that must be spent, whether or not this year's replication proposals are worth funding. Money that must be spent gets spent. You get cheap, underpowered replications of claims nobody was relying on anyway, because the fund's survival depends on volume.
[C1.3] The pathology PRO diagnoses in novelty-chasing — status and money attached to a category rather than to value — is exactly what a quota reintroduces on the other side of the ledger.
[C1.4] Then the deterrence argument. Millions of papers are published annually. Even a generous fixed share can audit a vanishing fraction, and a lottery with near-zero odds does not discipline anyone. Meanwhile the cheap interventions that actually bite are structural and nearly free: registered reports, preregistration, mandatory data and code deposit, powering requirements written into the grant, funding internal replication *inside* the original project.
[C1.5] A funder that demands a confirmatory arm in the initial award buys verification at marginal cost, from the team that already has the reagents and the protocol, instead of paying a second lab full price to rebuild it years later. Reliability is best bought at the point of production, not the point of audit.
[C1.6] And a fixed share is field-blind by construction. What does replication mean for a mathematical proof, a fifty-year climate record, a rare palaeontological find, a one-off epidemiological event? Uniform quotas force funders to spend replication money where replication is conceptually weak, while genuinely high-stakes claims — the drug target that industry is about to sink a billion into — get one slot among many.
[C1.7] PRO's own framing concedes the cost. Reduced novel funding is certain. The reliability gain is the thing we should design for directly.

Funding the audit does not create the consequence.
[C2.5] What actually disciplines behaviour is having your analysis plan locked before you see the data, and your data and code open to anyone. Deterrence needs teeth, not a bigger budget for polite disagreement.
[C2.6] "Grading your own exam" [P2.1] misreads the mechanism. A registered report is graded by reviewers *before* results exist — the author cannot mark themselves, because the marking scheme is fixed in advance. A funder-mandated confirmatory arm can require blinding, an outside analyst, an adversarial partner. That is external adjudication at the point of production.
[C2.7] The real comparison stands: PRO pays full price, years late, to re-litigate errors after they enter the literature. Prevention keeps them out. And PRO still has not named the novel research it is willing to cancel to buy that slower option.
