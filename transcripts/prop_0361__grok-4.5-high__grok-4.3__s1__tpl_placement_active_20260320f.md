[P1.1] Theatrical exclusivity is not nostalgia. It is the mechanism that turns a major movie into a cultural and commercial event rather than disposable content. Studios that protect a meaningful window—long enough for audiences to gather, for word-of-mouth to build, and for exhibitors to recover costs—maximize the film’s total value across every subsequent window. Those that rush major titles to streaming for short-term subscriber spikes trade that value away.
[P1.2] A theatrical window creates scarcity and urgency. Scarcity drives premium pricing: tickets, IMAX surcharges, concessions, merchandising. It also concentrates attention. Opening-weekend numbers become news; water-cooler conversation becomes free marketing.
[P1.3] Once that heat is generated, later streaming and transactional VOD rides a higher price point because the film has already proved it is appointment viewing. Collapse the window and you flatten demand into a convenience stream. The same IP is watched, but fewer people pay peak prices and fewer people talk about it. The lifetime value falls even if one platform’s sign-ups tick up for a quarter.
[P1.4] Exhibitors and filmmakers both depend on this structure. Theater chains invest in premium screens and staffing precisely because exclusivity gives them a protected runway. Remove it and screens darken; the remaining ones demand worse terms. Filmmakers of scale design for the big image and shared audience reaction.
[P1.5] When the economic incentive shifts toward same-month streaming, budgets shrink for the very spectacle that once differentiated cinema from television. The result is a feedback loop: less theatrical success, less investment in theatrical craft, weaker product for every platform.
[P1.6] Subscriber growth is real, but it is not free. It is purchased by eroding the uniqueness of the product that made the subscriber base valuable in the first place. A studio that treats major films as mere churn reducers will eventually discover it has less valuable IP to feed any platform. Preserve the window, protect the event, and the downstream rights stay expensive.
[P1.7] That is the only durable path.

[C1.1] Studios cannot afford to treat theatrical exclusivity as a fixed requirement when subscriber platforms now determine long-term valuation. Every month a major title stays off streaming, potential subscribers sign up elsewhere or remain on free ad-supported tiers. The revenue from one theatrical run rarely offsets the lifetime loss of converting those households into recurring payers who watch the studio’s entire library.
[C1.2] The old event model relied on audiences having few alternatives. Today the same film competes with dozens of other new releases plus back catalogs already available at home. Forcing viewers to choose between a theater ticket or nothing often produces nothing. Shortening or removing the window lets the title reach the larger population that prefers convenience, generates immediate data on engagement, and feeds algorithmic recommendations that drive further views across the catalog.
[C1.3] Marketing costs stay largely the same whether the film opens in theaters or on a platform. The difference is that streaming captures that spend directly as sign-ups and retention rather than splitting it with exhibitors who take the majority of ticket revenue. Studios that moved titles quickly during recent disruptions retained ownership of both the first and all subsequent viewings without additional distribution fees.
[C1.4] Exhibitor health matters only if it supports the studio’s overall economics. When chains demand longer protections or higher revenue shares, they raise the cost of the theatrical leg without guaranteeing higher total earnings. A studio that instead funnels major releases straight into its own service controls pricing, bundling, and cross-promotion, turning each title into an acquisition tool rather than a one-time event.
[C1.5] The proposition therefore asks studios to subordinate their primary growth engine to a secondary, declining channel. That choice trades measurable subscriber gains for uncertain theatrical upside that fewer households still select.
