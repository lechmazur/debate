[P1.1] Critical minerals are the skeleton of modern defense and clean-energy infrastructure. From rare-earth magnets in fighter jets to lithium batteries in naval vessels, control over these supply chains determines whether a military can fly and an economy can transition. When firms intimately tethered to rival states acquire these assets, they do not merely seek profit; they acquire strategic leverage that can be weaponized in hours, not months.
[P1.2] The presumption to block is necessary because the threat is structural, not incidental. State-directed enterprises operate under legal regimes where commercial secrecy bows to strategic command. An acquisition today creates a dependency tomorrow that cannot be undone by emergency decree. Once a rival controls extraction sites or processing facilities, they can throttle supply during a Taiwan Strait crisis or an Arctic dispute, turning a trade tool into a coercive weapon before regulators finish reading the fine print.
[P1.3] History demonstrates this vulnerability. When Beijing consolidated effective control over global rare-earth refining, Washington discovered that decades of outsourcing had left Pentagon contractors scrambling for basic components. The leverage was not theoretical; it was exercised in diplomatic standoffs. A presumptive block reverses this dynamic.
[P1.4] It forces acquirers to prove the negative—that no hidden state directive, no backdoor technology transfer, and no latent supply chokehold exists—rather than forcing governments to race against closing deadlines to prove a risk they cannot fully audit in time.
[P1.5] Economic efficiency is a poor shield against strategic exposure. Markets price quarterly returns; adversaries price decades of coercion. Without the presumption, regulators face asymmetric information: classified intelligence cannot be shared in commercial proceedings, while state-backed firms hide behind shell companies and sovereign wealth structures. A presumptive block is not protectionism; it is the minimum insurance premium against holding the next war or energy transition hostage to a rival state's geological grip.

[C1.3] Regulators lose the opportunity to negotiate protective terms, such as supply commitments to domestic defense contractors or restrictions on data sharing with foreign governments.

[C1.5] Retaliation compounds the damage. Sustainable security comes from increasing overall supply through pragmatic partnerships, monitored for risks, rather than shrinking options in the name of absolute separation.
