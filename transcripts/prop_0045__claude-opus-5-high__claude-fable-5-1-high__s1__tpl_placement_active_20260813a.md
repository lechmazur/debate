[P1.1] Start with the concession that makes this easy: personality is real, and it matters at work. That is precisely why MBTI and DISC are indefensible. They take a genuine signal and run it through an instrument that destroys it.
[P1.2] Three failures, in order of severity.
[P1.3] First, reliability. MBTI sorts people into sixteen types by chopping continuous traits at an arbitrary midpoint. Most people score near that midpoint, so a nudge in mood, context, or self-image flips a letter — and a substantial share of retakers get a different type within weeks. A ruler that gives you a different number each time you measure the same table cannot predict anything, because it isn't measuring anything stable.
[P1.4] DISC's four-quadrant scheme has the same architectural flaw.
[P1.5] Second, validity. Even taken at face value, type does not forecast the things employers actually care about: job performance, tenure, promotion, team output. The research consensus that personality has *any* predictive purchase in hiring rests on trait-continuum measures — conscientiousness above all — not on typologies these vendors sell. MBTI's own publisher has long discouraged using it for selection.
[P1.6] When the vendor says "don't hire with this," employers should listen.
[P1.7] Third, and this is the decisive point: the alternative to a bad test is not nothing. It is the budget, the hour of the hiring panel's time, and the credibility currently spent on it. Structured interviews with scored, job-relevant questions and work-sample tasks outperform typology by a wide margin and are available to any employer. Every dollar routed to a four-letter code is a dollar not spent on the thing that works.
[P1.8] And the costs are not merely wasted spending. A type label creates false confidence — a manager who "knows" a candidate is an ISTJ stops asking questions. It licenses stereotyping in staffing and promotion. It hands employees a permission slip: *I'm a D, I'm just blunt.* An unreliable instrument that people trust is worse than no instrument, because it replaces uncertainty with error.
[P1.9] Keep the interest in personality. Retire the horoscope.

[C1.6] A standardized questionnaire asks every candidate the same questions and scores them the same way. It is the cheapest form of structure an employer can buy.
