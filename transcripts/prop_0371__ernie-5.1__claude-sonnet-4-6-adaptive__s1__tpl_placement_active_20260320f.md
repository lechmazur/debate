[P1.1] Travelers today face a moving target: the price you see at checkout is rarely the price you pay at the gate. Airlines have turned a basic necessity—carrying a bag that fits overhead—into a patchwork of optional fees, each labelled “basic”, “standard”, “priority”, with arbitrary size limits that change from carrier to carrier. This fragmentation is not a market efficiency; it is a deliberate strategy to extract revenue from passengers who assumed the ticket covered a reasonable carry‑on. Requiring a baseline carry‑on allowance baked into the fare eliminates that bait‑and‑switch and restores the principle that a ticket is a contract for transport, not a menu of hidden add‑ons.
[P1.2] First, transparency. When the cost of a carry‑on is disclosed up front, consumers can compare airlines on the total price of travel rather than on a maze of ancillary fees. Studies of airline pricing show that passengers consistently over‑estimate total cost when fees are hidden, leading to mis‑allocation of demand and lower consumer welfare. A mandated baseline removes that distortion and forces airlines to compete on service, schedule, and true price.
[P1.3] Second, fairness. Low‑income travelers, who are least able to absorb surprise charges, are hit hardest by fragmented baggage fees. A universal carry‑on baseline levels the playing field, ensuring that the ability to bring a personal item does not become a luxury determined by how much you can afford to pay extra.
[P1.4] Third, operational simplicity. Airlines already manage weight limits for checked luggage; extending a clear, uniform carry‑on rule reduces boarding delays caused by passengers arguing over size, cuts customer‑service costs, and improves on‑time performance. The net effect is lower operating costs that can be passed on as lower fares.
[P1.5] If airlines are allowed to keep carving up carry‑on allowances, they will continue to monetize a basic right, eroding trust and inflating the true cost of flying. A regulatory baseline restores honesty, protects vulnerable travelers, and pushes the industry toward genuine price competition.

[C1.7] The traveler least able to absorb that increase is the same person PRO claims to protect.
[C1.8] Finally, consider what the proposition actually requires: a regulatory body deciding what counts as a "reasonable" carry-on, setting dimensions, and enforcing compliance across every carrier and aircraft configuration. [C1.9] The pro-consumer position is better information, not mandatory bundling that raises costs for the travelers who travel lightest.
