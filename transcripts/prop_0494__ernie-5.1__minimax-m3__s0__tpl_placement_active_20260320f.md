[P1.1] Paying reviewers and publishing their reports solves the two fatal flaws of today’s system: chronic under‑investment in review quality and the opacity that lets low‑effort or biased work slip through.
[P1.2] First, compensation turns reviewing from a volunteer chore into a professional task. When reviewers are paid, journals can set clear standards, expect timely turnaround, and hold reviewers accountable for the depth of their critique. The current model relies on goodwill; most academics review only a handful of papers a year because the opportunity cost is high. Paying even modest fees—comparable to a conference travel grant—creates a labor market where competent scholars can allocate dedicated time, reducing the “quick‑glance” reviews that delay publication and let errors propagate.
[P1.3] Evidence from open‑review platforms that reward reviewers with credit or modest stipends shows higher reviewer engagement and more detailed feedback.
[P1.4] Second, publishing the reports alongside the article eliminates the black‑box that anonymity creates. When a review is hidden, authors cannot see why a paper was rejected, and readers cannot assess whether the evaluation was rigorous. Public reports make bias visible: a reviewer who consistently down‑plays methods from a rival subfield can be identified, and the community can weigh the criticism on its merits. Journals that already publish reviewer comments—such as eLife’s transparent review—report higher author satisfaction and lower rates of post‑publication controversy.
[P1.5] Together, payment and transparency convert peer review from a privileged, unaccountable gate into a visible, incentivized quality‑control process. The result is faster, more thorough assessments and greater trust in the literature. Any argument that anonymity protects reviewers must confront the fact that it also protects sloppiness; any claim that payment is unnecessary must explain why the system continues to produce delayed, shallow reviews despite decades of unpaid labor. The status quo wastes thousands of researcher‑hours each year on superficial checks that could be replaced by a professional, transparent service.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like a clean reform, but it mistakes which problem peer review is meant to solve and which features actually keep it honest.
[C1.2] Anonymity in peer review is not a flaw to be patched—it is a shield that lets junior scholars, women, and researchers in small or dissenting fields criticize powerful authors without retaliation. Call it opacity if you like, but identifiable reviewers become participants in reputation markets: they self-censor to avoid offending authors whose grants they may one day need, or whose collaborators they meet at conferences. The same feature that may protect sloppiness also protects the lone postdoc who tells a senior author their model is wrong.
[C1.3] Strip it out, and the people most likely to push back on shaky work are the ones most exposed.
[C1.4] Payment does not create a neutral labor market; it creates a new conflict of interest. When journals pay reviewers, reviewers become quasi-contractors of the journal, and journals have revenue motives—to keep authors publishing and paying article charges—that no longer align with saying "reject." If funders pay, then reviewers are paid by the same agencies whose grantees they judge, which is worse than the current arrangement. Either way, paid review invites the capture that already distorts consulting and sponsored research.
[C1.5] Publishing raw reports does not help readers either. Most reviews contain hedging, off-topic tangents, and contradictory recommendations; readers would have to wade through three to five of them per paper to extract signal. The published article, methods, and replication record already carry the testable claims; a stream of referees' notes is noise dressed up as accountability.
[C1.6] The bottleneck is not opacity. It is reviewer time, which payment can buy but cannot create, and editorial bandwidth, which publishing reports does nothing to expand. The reform on offer would raise costs, weaken dissent, and leave the actual delays untouched.
