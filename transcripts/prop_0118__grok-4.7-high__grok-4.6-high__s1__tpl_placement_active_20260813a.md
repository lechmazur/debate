[P1.1] Public-school meals should be free for every student, not a benefit earned by proving poverty. Means-testing promises precision and delivers the opposite. Forms are long, humiliating, and easy to miss. Language barriers, unstable housing, and fear of sharing family information keep eligible children off the rolls.
[P1.2] Cutoffs are blunt: a family just over the line can still be choosing between rent and groceries. Children who qualify often skip the meal rather than be marked as the free-lunch kid. Targeting does not fail only at the margins. It builds shame and paperwork into the program meant to end hunger.
[P1.3] Universal provision removes those traps. When the meal is simply part of the school day, take-up rises because nothing must be applied for and nothing must be hidden. A full cafeteria changes the room: no one scans the line to see who pays, and no child sits out lunch to avoid a question. Staff time shifts from verifying incomes to serving food.
[P1.4] Hunger does not respect an eligibility chart. It shows up as distraction, absenteeism, and worse health for any child who arrives empty. Feeding everyone protects the students targeting misses and stops turning lunch into a public ranking of family income.
[P1.5] The efficiency objection gets the costs backward. Chasing means-tests spends real money to exclude people, then still leaves gaps. Schools already treat instruction, and often transportation, as shared goods rather than income-tested privileges. Meals belong in that category because they happen inside the school day and determine whether that day can succeed.
[P1.6] Universality is not a gift to families who could pay. It is the design that actually reaches hungry children, preserves their dignity, and stops wasting the program on bureaucracy. That is the system public schools should run.

[C1.1] Public schools exist to teach every child, not to underwrite lunch for families that already eat well. The motion collapses that distinction. Instruction is a shared public good every student needs in the same way. A meal is food a household can, and usually does, provide.
[C1.2] When a district feeds children whose parents can pack a lunch or pay the till, it spends less on the children who cannot.
[C1.3] Targeting is not cruelty. It is the only honest answer to scarcity. Kitchens, labor, and food cost money. Universal free meals invent none of it; they merely shift the subsidy from the poorest students toward everyone else.
[C1.4] A family that can afford after-school sports can afford a sandwich. A family choosing between rent and groceries cannot. Means-testing exists because those two families are not the same, and pretending otherwise is not compassion. It is leakage.
[C1.5] Stigma and paperwork are design problems, not laws of nature. Direct certification, community eligibility in high-poverty schools, and cashless lines already feed eligible children without a public ranking of income. The remedy for a humiliating form is a better form, not a blank check for households that never needed the program. Spreading a free tray to every student does not magically reach the child targeting misses; it merely conceals the miss behind a larger bill.
[C1.6] Universality also buys a quality tradeoff. Stretch the same kitchen across every plate and the students who actually arrive hungry get thinner food so that students who do not need the meal can take it for convenience. That is not dignity. That is dilution of the very resource hunger requires.
[C1.7] The duty is to end childhood hunger among those who face it, not to recast lunch as a universal entitlement. Name poorer students as the purpose, fund them first, and stop treating an affluent child's free pizza as if it were the same as feeding a child who would otherwise go without.
