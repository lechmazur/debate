[P1.1] A university is the rare seller that gets paid in full, up front, with borrowed public money, and owes nothing if the product fails. The student carries the debt. The taxpayer carries the default. The institution keeps the tuition.
[P1.2] That arrangement is the problem, and the motion fixes it.
[P1.3] An eighteen-year-old cannot audit a program's earnings record. The university can. It sets the price, decides how many seats to fill, designs the curriculum, and knows what its graduates actually earn. When a master's program charges a price comparable to a house deposit and sends cohort after cohort into jobs that cannot service the debt, that is not bad luck.
[P1.4] It is a business decision made by the one party with the data and the power to change it.
[P1.5] Federal loans flow with almost no regard to whether a program pays off, so expanding a weak program is pure revenue. We do not tolerate this elsewhere. Lenders who originate bad mortgages are expected to hold some risk. Putting a share of losses back on the institution makes it ask the question it currently has no reason to ask: is this program worth what we charge?
[P1.6] Notice how narrow the motion is. "Part" of the cost, not all. "Repeatedly," not one bad year or a recession cohort. "Poor earnings relative to debt," which a school can fix two ways: improve outcomes or cut price.
[P1.7] Either is a victory for students.
[P1.8] Expect the objection that schools will abandon low-paid vocations. But the measure is earnings relative to debt, so an affordable teaching or social-work program passes easily. What fails is expensive programs with weak returns, exactly the ones that should shrink, reprice, or reform.
[P1.9] So the question is simple: when a program fails repeatedly, should the one party that profited pay nothing?

[C1.1] PRO treats student debt as a simple receipt for tuition [P1.6], and universities as mortgage lenders who underwrite each borrower [P1.5]. Both premises are false, and that error turns this policy into a direct penalty for educating low-income students.
[C1.2] First, universities do not originate loans. The government sets the lending terms, and institutions generally cannot stop an eligible student from borrowing up to federal limits, including for rent, food, and transportation. Consequently, a program’s debt-to-earnings ratio tracks student family wealth far more than institutional greed. When two colleges charge the exact same modest tuition for a teaching or social-work degree, the school serving affluent students will show low debt and minimal defaults, because parents pay the bills.
[C1.3] The open-access college serving working-class students, who must borrow for living expenses and lack a family safety net when financial shocks hit, will show high debt and repeated defaults.
[C1.4] That destroys PRO’s reassurance about low-paid vocations [P1.8]. Cutting tuition cannot eliminate living-cost borrowing, and universities cannot dictate what rural school districts or community clinics pay their graduates. Faced with default penalties they cannot fix by cutting price [P1.6], colleges have one rational escape hatch: change who they admit and which communities they serve.
[C1.5] When you fine a school for its graduates' defaults, you give institutions a powerful financial incentive to screen out lower-income, first-generation, and older students: anyone likely to borrow heavily or struggle without family wealth. Worse, when an under-resourced school is hit with clawbacks, the money does not come from some hidden profit vault [P1.9]; at public and nonprofit colleges, it is raided directly from instruction, advising, and aid for current students.
[C1.6] If the government is originating bad loans, it should reform its own lending caps or accreditation standards, not fine colleges in a way that locks out the disadvantaged.
