[P1.1] Car owners buy hardware that is physically present at delivery. Once the sensors, motors, and processors sit inside the vehicle, any later demand for recurring payments simply withholds functions the buyer already purchased. This converts outright ownership into a revocable license without adding new hardware or capability.
[P1.2] The subscription model creates an artificial scarcity after the transaction closes. Manufacturers can disable heated seats, navigation, or driver-assistance features through over-the-air changes, even though the components remain unchanged. The buyer loses utility while the company continues to profit from hardware it has already sold and been paid for. This mismatch between possession and control contradicts ordinary expectations of property.
[P1.3] Resale value also declines when future owners inherit the same paywall. A car whose features can be turned off at any time becomes less transferable and less reliable as an asset. Buyers who anticipated permanent access discover they effectively leased part of the vehicle, a condition rarely disclosed with the same clarity as the initial price.
[P1.4] Permanent access after purchase keeps the exchange honest: one payment for the hardware and its embedded functions. Anything less lets sellers retroactively alter the terms of ownership and extract ongoing rent for static equipment.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds intuitive until you ask a simple question: who pays for what happens after the car leaves the lot? Heated seats and unlocked horsepower are not free just because the wires are already there. Someone still has to maintain the software, patch security vulnerabilities, and keep servers running for connectivity features. A rule mandating permanent access ignores that ongoing cost and pretends software is a one-time good like a steering wheel.
[C1.2] Consider how this actually plays out in pricing. If manufacturers cannot recover costs through optional subscriptions, they build fewer optional features into the base hardware at all, or they raise the sticker price for everyone, including buyers who never wanted heated seats or advanced driver assistance in the first place. Subscription unlocking is a form of price discrimination that lets budget buyers pay less upfront while giving flexibility to those who want premium capability later, seasonally, or temporarily. Banning that model doesn't create more ownership rights; it just removes the cheaper option and forces uniform higher prices onto a market that currently offers choice.
[C1.3] The "property" framing in the opening also proves too much. Buying a phone doesn't entitle you to permanent free cloud storage or app updates forever. Buying a printer doesn't guarantee free ink. Consumers already navigate hardware-plus-service bundles across nearly every category of consumer electronics, and markets have not collapsed under claims of stolen ownership.
[C1.4] What matters is disclosure at time of sale, not a blanket rule that treats every subscription as theft.
[C1.5] Finally, subscriptions fund the very safety and security updates that keep connected cars viable over a ten- or fifteen-year lifespan. Cybersecurity patching, mapping data, and driver-assistance calibration all require continuous engineering investment. A model that eliminates recurring revenue removes the incentive to keep supporting older vehicles at all, pushing manufacturers toward planned obsolescence instead of long-term software support.
[C1.6] Permanent access sounds fair in the abstract. In practice, it raises prices, discourages optional features, and starves the ongoing maintenance that keeps installed hardware useful for the life of the car.
