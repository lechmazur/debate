[P1.1] A resale platform is not merely a noticeboard when it selects sellers, ranks listings, processes payment, and takes a fee for facilitating the sale. It designs the marketplace; it should therefore carry more of the risk created by that design. The question is not whether counterfeits can be eliminated, but who is best placed to prevent them and absorb the losses when prevention fails. Platforms clearly outperform individual buyers on both.
[P1.2] First, authentication belongs where information and expertise can be pooled. A buyer often purchases from photographs, cannot inspect the item before paying, and may encounter a sophisticated fake only once. A platform can examine physical goods, train specialists, compare seller histories, identify repeat suspicious listings, and learn from disputed transactions. Requiring more authentication—especially for high-value or frequently counterfeited goods—turns scattered buyer guesswork into a repeatable screening system.
[P1.3] Second, platform risk-bearing creates the right incentives. If buyers mainly bear the loss, a platform can profit from transaction volume while treating counterfeits as an unfortunate dispute between users. If it must provide meaningful refunds or guarantees, prevention becomes financially rational: better listing checks, seller penalties, inspection processes, and faster removal of repeat offenders. The party able to reduce the risk should have a reason to do so.
[P1.4] Consider a buyer who pays hundreds of dollars for a handbag advertised as genuine. Under a buyer-burden model, the buyer must detect the problem, gather proof, navigate a dispute, and possibly absorb the loss. Under platform-backed authentication, that same fee finances screening and a credible remedy. The buyer is paying for a functioning market, not merely access to a listing.
[P1.5] The predictable objection is cost. But authentication and guarantees can be reflected transparently in commissions, targeted by risk, and supported by seller reserves or insurance. Higher prices for trustworthy transactions are preferable to apparently cheap transactions whose hidden price is fraud. This does not require authenticating every inexpensive item or promising perfection; it requires platforms to do substantially more screening and stop leaving residual losses mainly with buyers.
[P1.6] A market built on “buyer beware” [clipped]

Cheap, casual resale will vanish, leaving only high-end luxury goods where authentication is cost-justified.
[C1.6] You do not make a market fairer by making it only usable for rich people buying expensive items. You do not fix fraud by punishing every honest user first.

[P2.2] The supposed jump from $12 to $22 in [C1.5] attacks an invented mandate, complete with invented arithmetic.
[P2.3] Nor does imperfect authentication imply worthless authentication. Screening can reduce fraud without achieving perfection, especially when a platform learns from repeat sellers, disputed transactions, and patterns no one-off buyer can see. CON’s handbag anecdote shows, at most, that residual error remains. It does not show that buyers detect counterfeits better from photographs than platforms do with greater information and, where justified, physical inspection.
[P2.4] False positives also deserve remedies such as review and appeal; under buyer-burden resale, the opposite error—a fake passing as genuine—leaves the buyer fighting for redress.
[P2.5] The “universal tax” argument then conceals the crucial comparison. Counterfeit losses already cost money; they are simply dumped unpredictably on the unlucky buyer. A transparent fee, targeted reserve, insurance mechanism, or platform guarantee converts that catastrophic individual loss into a manageable market cost. It need not be an identical surcharge on every user.
[P2.6] More importantly, platform risk-bearing funds its own reduction: when refunds come from the platform’s bottom line, tolerating suspicious sellers for transaction volume stops being profitable.
[P2.7] Finally, protecting the sticker price of resale is not protecting access if buyers must silently add the expected cost of fraud, disputes, and abandoned purchases. Low-risk goods can retain lightweight processes; higher-risk goods deserve stronger screening and credible remedies. CON treats every additional safeguard as market destruction because it assumes the clumsiest possible implementation. The actual choice is simpler: residual risk should move toward the repeat participant that can prevent, pool, and correct it—not remain mainly with the buyer least equipped to bear it.
