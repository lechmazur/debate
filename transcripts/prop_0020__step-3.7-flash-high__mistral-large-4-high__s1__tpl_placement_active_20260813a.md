[P1.5] Without the common currency, the EU could have preserved flexible exchange rate adjustment for member states while deepening cooperation in areas where economic convergence is actually feasible, building a stronger, more cohesive union rather than a fractured bloc held together by coercive bailouts.

The alternative was not prosperity but chaos.
[C1.2] The single currency eliminated transaction costs and exchange rate uncertainty across the world's largest trading bloc. Before 1999, businesses paid billions annually in conversion fees and hedged against volatile swings between the franc, mark, lira, and peseta. That friction suppressed cross-border investment and kept markets fragmented. The euro removed these barriers, accelerating the single market's depth and making Europe a genuine economic space rather than a collection of protected national markets.
[C1.3] More critically, the euro prevents the competitive devaluations that historically plagued Europe. Without it, Italy might have devalued against Germany during the 2008 crisis, triggering retaliatory spirals that would have shattered the single market from within. The euro forced adjustment through structural reform—painful, yes—rather than beggar-thy-neighbor currency wars that would have impoverished everyone.
[C1.4] Geopolitically, the euro gives the EU weight no single national currency could command. As the world's second reserve currency, it grants the bloc influence in global finance, the capacity to impose effective sanctions, and a voice in international monetary policy that fragmented currencies never could. A Europe of floating marks, francs, and lire would be economically weaker, politically divided, and geopolitically irrelevant—a loose trade agreement rather than a union capable of acting as a global power.
[C1.5] The euro is not the EU's weakness; it is the foundation upon which deeper integration—banking union, capital markets union, and joint fiscal capacity—is being built. Remove it, and you remove the gravity that holds Europe together, leaving a continent of rival currencies competing to devalue against one another.
