[P1.1] More than fifteen consecutive days in solitary confinement is not a stricter form of ordinary punishment; it is a predictable machine for breaking a mind. Human beings need social contact, daylight, activity, and some measure of control over their environment. Strip those away for weeks, and the damage is not speculative. People in prolonged isolation commonly report panic, paranoia, hallucinations, memory problems, rage, and despair.
[P1.2] They may cut themselves, lose contact with reality, or become unable to function in ordinary settings afterward. The harm does not begin neatly at day sixteen, but by that point the risk of severe and lasting psychological injury is high enough that a decent society should not permit it as routine punishment.
[P1.3] That is why the practice fits the core idea of psychological torture. Torture is not only physical pain. It is the deliberate or foreseeable infliction of severe mental suffering on someone who cannot escape. A prisoner in solitary is entirely under state control: the door, the light, the food, the noise, and the duration are decided by others.
[P1.4] When officials keep a person in a cell for weeks with minimal human contact, they are not merely restricting liberty; they are imposing a psychological assault that can outlast the sentence.
[P1.5] The fifteen-day line is also a practical necessity. Without a bright rule, prison systems can call any long isolation “administrative,” “protective,” or “therapeutic” while doing the same damage. Short emergency separation may sometimes be needed to stop immediate violence, but that can be limited, reviewed, and kept under the threshold. For longer periods, there are safer alternatives: step-down housing, mental-health units, structured out-of-cell time, and conflict-resolution programs.
[P1.6] These can manage risk without manufacturing psychosis.
[P1.7] Abolishing solitary confinement beyond fifteen days would therefore draw a moral and operational boundary: the state may punish, restrain, and protect, but it may not deliberately corrode a person’s mind.
