[P1.1] When an automated system denies a family an apartment, discards a job applicant, cancels a credit line, or denies medical care, it exercises profound power over human survival. Handing down these decisions through an unexaminable black box strips individuals of dignity, agency, and legal recourse. Courts must recognize a limited right to explanation because reason-giving is the indispensable prerequisite to justice.
[P1.2] This right is deliberately bounded and workable. It does not demand proprietary source code or an impossible unraveling of complex neural networks. Instead, it requires actionable transparency: disclosing the principal variables that drove the adverse decision and the counterfactual criteria needed to alter the outcome. Just as legal systems already require adverse action notices in conventional credit lending, courts can require algorithmic gatekeepers in vital sectors to provide plain-language justifications.
[P1.3] This allows individuals to identify and correct basic, catastrophic data errors—such as mistaken identities or miscoded medical files—before their lives are derailed.
[P1.4] Crucially, algorithmic opacity renders substantive civil rights unenforceable. Protections against racial redlining in housing, gender bias in hiring, and disability discrimination in healthcare become dead letters if institutional actors can launder unlawful prejudice through automated models and disclaim liability behind a proprietary shield. Explanation is the evidentiary bridge between an injury and a remedy. Without it, victims of algorithmic bias cannot even state a claim, effectively placing automated systems beyond judicial review.
[P1.5] Courts have a duty to prevent automated decision-making from becoming an accountability loophole. Recognizing a limited right to explanation does not stall innovation; it guarantees that efficiency is not bought at the price of arbitrary power. When basic human survival is on the line, the law must insist on an answer to the most fundamental question: why?

[C1.1] The question is not whether people deserve answers. It is whether a judge-made "right to explanation" actually delivers them. It does not, and it crowds out the tools that do.
[C1.2] Start with what an explanation of a modern model actually is. When a lender's gradient-boosted model scores an applicant, there is no "principal variable" waiting to be disclosed. The reasons handed to the applicant are generated afterward by a second, simpler approximation of the first system. Two vendors can produce two different, equally plausible stories for the same denial.
[C1.3] Courts would be mandating a genre of document—confident, plain-language, and unverifiable—and calling it accountability. Worse, a fluent explanation legitimizes the decision. "Your debt-to-income ratio was too high" ends the conversation, even when the true driver was a proxy for neighborhood. Explanation is the cheapest way for an institution to look accountable while changing nothing.
[C1.4] Second, the remedy misfires. PRO's core harm is discrimination and data error. Neither is cured by narrating a decision to one person. Discrimination is proven statistically, through outcome audits and discovery of denial rates across groups—evidence a court can compel today without inventing a new right.
[C1.5] Data errors are cured by a right to see and correct one's file, which credit law already provides. Individual explanation is the least effective link in a chain PRO could strengthen anywhere else.
[C1.6] Third, incentives. Once explanation becomes a litigable duty, firms rationally retreat to models that are easy to explain rather than accurate or fair: coarse rules keyed to income, address, and tenure. Those crude models are precisely the ones that historically excluded thin-file borrowers and immigrant families whom richer models finally admitted. The cost of "right to explanation" is paid by the people PRO claims to protect.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO's own example concedes the point: adverse action notices came from legislatures, with defined triggers and safe harbors. "Significantly affect," "limited," and "explanation" are not standards a court can administer consistently across four industries. Demand audits and correction rights from lawmakers. Do not ask judges to certify stories.

When an institution provides an explanation, it commits to a factual position on the record. If a lender claims a denial was driven by debt-to-income, but the true driver was a racial proxy, that explanation becomes actionable evidence of pretext.
[P2.5] Concealment, not disclosure, is what allows institutions to hide behind black boxes while changing nothing.
[P2.6] Third, CON’s institutional competence argument [C1.7] mistakes judicial modesty for judicial abdication. Courts routinely operationalize standards like "materiality," "reasonableness," and "significant impact" across disparate industries. Waiting for comprehensive legislative safe harbors abandons victims today.
[P2.7] Finally, if an algorithm cannot be explained without retreating to coarse exclusion [C1.6], that model is fundamentally unready for high-stakes deployment. Denying explanations does not protect vulnerable applicants; it licenses unaccountable power. Courts must hold automated gatekeepers to the basic demands of justice.
