[P1.1] A country is not merely a territory with a market attached. It is a shared civic project: laws that bind everyone equally, institutions that require trust to function, and a public language in which strangers can deliberate, work, and hold each other accountable. [P1.2] Start with the common language. Speaking the public language is what turns an immigrant from a protected outsider into a full participant. Without it, you cannot read your own lease, argue with your employer, testify in court, or understand the political debate that decides your children's schooling. Communities where large groups cannot speak the common language become dependent on intermediaries — often exploitative ones — and vulnerable to segregation that locks in poverty across generations.
[P1.3] Requiring language acquisition is not hostility to immigrants' heritage; it is the single most empowering expectation a society can set, and every serious integration success story, from Canada's points-and-language system to mandatory language instruction in Nordic countries, treats it as a floor, not an option.

[P1.5] A society that says "these norms are optional" has no principled ground to refuse anyone who rejects them — including rejection of the very tolerance that immigrants came seeking.
[P1.6] Finally, expectations are what make welcome credible. Public support for immigration collapses precisely where integration is visibly abandoned; it holds where newcomers visibly join. [C1.4] Civic norms are the sharper danger. Democracy, equal treatment, rule of law, and free speech are legal settlements. You can comply with them while remaining a skeptic or a critic. "Adopt" means more than compliance: it means internalizing a story about who you must become.
[C1.5] Native citizens are not asked to adopt anything of the kind. They vote, they rant, they live in subcultures that would fail any honest loyalty test. If immigrants alone must pass that test, membership is probation, not equality.
[C1.6] Optional integration does not arm xenophobes; mandatory cultural uptake does. [C1.7] A free society expects the law. It does not conscript identities. Culture, conscience, and the language spoken at home remain optional — for newcomers for the same reason they remain optional for everyone else.
