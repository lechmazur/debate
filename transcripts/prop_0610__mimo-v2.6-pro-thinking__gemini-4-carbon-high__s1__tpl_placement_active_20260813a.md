[P1.1] The EU prices industrial carbon through the Emissions Trading System. Every tonne of steel, cement, aluminium, or fertilizer made in Europe carries that cost; every tonne imported from a jurisdiction with no carbon price does not. That gap is a subsidy to foreign production, a penalty on European industry, and a way of exporting emissions rather than cutting them. A border adjustment closes it.
[P1.2] The motion already concedes retaliation and asks whether to proceed anyway. Yes — because the comparator is not "adjustment plus retaliation" against "harmony." It is adjustment against the status quo, in which the EU either dilutes its climate ambition or watches energy-intensive industry relocate to the dirtiest jurisdictions. That is the real choice on the table.
[P1.3] Retaliation is bounded and reciprocal. Partners who tax EU exports impose costs that are visible, negotiable, and answerable in trade talks. Inaction is unbounded: leakage compounds year after year, EU plants close, and the emissions Europe avoids are simply emitted elsewhere on a dirtier grid. A tariff dispute hurts both sides.
[P1.4] A leakage regime quietly hollows out only one.
[P1.5] The design also blunts the threat. Because exporters can claim credit for carbon prices already paid at home, a trading partner can keep its exporters whole by pricing carbon itself and retaining the revenue domestically. Retaliation is not the only rational response; adopting a domestic carbon price is an option the adjustment deliberately offers. Some partners will choose tariffs anyway.
[P1.6] That is the cost of insisting the atmosphere is a shared sink, not a licence for regulatory arbitrage.
[P1.7] And the EU is not a small open economy importing at the world's pleasure. It is the largest single market on earth. Retaliation against it is expensive for the retaliator too, which is precisely why a credible price on carbon-embedded imports pulls major emitters toward sectoral arrangements instead of permanent conflict.

Nobody taxes a circuit board at the smokestack. Sector-average and product-intensity benchmarks handle complex manufactures today; they are the same blunt-but-fair instrument already used to allocate free allowances under the ETS. Where actual data is unavailable, default values are a calibration problem solvable by tightening the rule, not a reason to leave the border open.
[P2.2] And the claim that this "inflates input costs across Europe's entire downstream manufacturing base" proves too much: European steel and cement already bear the carbon price domestically. The adjustment does not add a new cost to the EU economy — it stops foreign inputs from undercutting producers who are already paying it. CON's complaint is that the carbon price exists at all, which is not what this motion adjudicates.
[P2.3] The "atmospheric gain approaches zero" claim in [C1.4] is the clearest overreach. Resource shuffling happens under *any* carbon constraint, including the ETS CON does not propose abolishing; it is a leakage channel to be monitored and adjusted for, not proof the whole instrument is inert.
[P2.4] Meanwhile the alternative channels CON offers in [C1.5] — clean-industry subsidies, carbon contracts for difference, sectoral deals — do nothing about imports. Contracts for difference pay European producers out of public budgets while untaxed foreign steel still undercuts them in the European market and abroad. That is the leakage regime, financed by taxpayers instead of polluters, and it still hollows out the plants.
[P2.5] Which leaves retaliation. CON never engages the motion's own premise: we proceed *even if* partners retaliate. Counter-tariffs on autos and aerospace are painful, but they are paid by both sides and are reversible in negotiation; competitive ruin at home and emissions exported to dirtier grids are neither.
