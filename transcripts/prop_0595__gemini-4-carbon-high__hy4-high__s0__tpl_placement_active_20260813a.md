[P1.1] Workplace AI is not just another software upgrade; it is automated management. Across warehouses, hospitals, call centers, and offices, AI systems now dictate work pace, monitor behavior, score performance, and restructure jobs overnight. Allowing large corporations to deploy automated management by unilateral decree concentrates unaccountable power inside opaque code. Requiring negotiation with elected worker councils places governance directly where technology meets human labor.
[P1.2] First, mandatory negotiation prevents concrete harms that external laws and voluntary policies miss. Legislatures move far too slowly to police thousands of bespoke workplace algorithms, and traditional collective bargaining leaves out the vast majority of private-sector workers who lack a union. Meanwhile, voluntary "consultation" lets executives hear objections and ignore them. Without a legal duty to negotiate, firms routinely deploy black-box trackers that penalize warehouse workers for bathroom breaks, or clinical tools that pressure nurses to rush care while leaving those nurses bearing the liability when software fails.
[P1.3] Elected worker councils empower the people governed by an algorithm to negotiate transparent metrics, data boundaries, retraining, and human appeals before harm is locked in.
[P1.4] Second, bargaining improves deployment rather than paralyzing innovation. Executives and vendors sell idealized systems; frontline workers know where workflows actually break, where data is flawed, and where automation creates safety hazards. Negotiating implementation frameworks upfront replaces quiet sabotage, turnover, and costly rollout failures with worker trust and smarter tools.
[P1.5] Crucially, this requirement applies only to large employers that already possess the administrative scale to bargain efficiently, and it governs internal workplace deployment while leaving external product R&D untouched. Major industrial economies with established works councils already prove that co-determining workplace technology sustains global competitiveness alongside worker dignity.
[P1.6] Workplace efficiency does not require executive autocracy. When large employers use algorithms to rewrite the daily rules, risks, and value of work, the people subject to those systems deserve a legally enforceable seat at the table.

[C1.5] The bathroom-break tracker and nurse-pressuring software in [P1.2] prove misconduct, not a universal duty to bargain.

Statutes are necessary floors, yet too blunt to govern AI alone: legislators cannot write a single formula defining a humane warehouse pace, a workable clinical override workflow, or a fair retraining plan for automated clerical jobs.
[P2.2] Legislation bans extreme misconduct; negotiation governs daily operational design. Nor does bargaining launder accountability [C1.4]. That critique confuses binding negotiation with toothless consultation; written deployment agreements create enforceable obligations without stripping individual workers of statutory remedies.
[P2.3] The "legal dragnet" [C1.1] and "chokepoint" [C1.2] objections likewise attack a strawman of haggling over every caption tweak or security patch. Negotiating AI deployment means establishing standing framework agreements, not litigating every model update. Councils have zero incentive to block captions or fraud filters; a single framework agreement can automatically clear low-risk tools and urgent safety or security patches, reserving active bargaining for deployments and updates that materially affect pay, schedules, staffing, or surveillance. Moreover, statutory notice, audit, and safety rules [C1.6] must draw the exact same legal lines around updates, only through slower bureaucracies.
[P2.4] Meanwhile, the supposedly harmless tools in [C1.1] prove why narrow definitions fail: "scheduling forecasts" routinely generate punitive, erratic shifts, and "speech-to-text notes" readily morph into covert worker scoring. Precisely because AI evolves continuously [C1.2], static external audits lag behind function creep, whereas an on-site council catches when mundane software turns coercive.
[P2.5] Finally, workers need operational insight, not coding degrees, to bargain effectively [C1.3]. Councils at large firms can retain technical specialists, and frontline nurses, unlike cost-focused executives, immediately recognize when a faulty triage alert floods wards with dangerous false alarms [C1.2], protecting incumbents, new hires, and patients alike.
