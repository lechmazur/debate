[P1.1] Shipping is the last great unpriced smokestack. It moves roughly 80% of world trade on heavy fuel oil, and it emits on the order of a billion tonnes of CO2 a year — comparable to a major industrialized nation. No country owns it, so no country can fix it. That is the whole case in one sentence: a fleet that flies flags of convenience and refuels wherever fuel is cheapest cannot be decarbonized by voluntary pledges or national rules.
[P1.2] It can only be decarbonized by a binding global timetable, and the IMO is the only body with the legal authority to set one.
[P1.3] Consider the shipowner's actual choice today. Ammonia or e-methanol costs more than residual fuel oil. If she buys the green ship, she loses charters to the competitor who didn't. If she buys the dirty ship, she pays nothing for the atmosphere she uses.
[P1.4] Rational behavior produces a fleet locked into fossil fuel for the 25-year life of every hull ordered this decade. A mandate doesn't distort that market; it fixes a market that is currently rigged against the cleaner ship.
[P1.5] The rising freight cost is not an embarrassment to concede — it is the mechanism. Green fuel plants are billion-dollar bets that no one finances against a hoped-for demand. A binding date creates guaranteed offtake, which unlocks supply, which drives cost down the learning curve, exactly as it did for solar and offshore wind. Voluntarism gives you pilot projects forever.
[P1.6] And weigh the magnitude honestly. Ocean freight is typically a small share of a manufactured good's retail price — a container crossing the Pacific carries thousands of pairs of shoes. Even a doubling of fuel cost lands as cents on the shelf. Against that: sea-level rise threatening the very port cities and island states that trade depends on.
[P1.7] Revenues from a shipping levy can be recycled to vulnerable and developing economies, so the burden is manageable and the transition is funded.
[P1.8] Cents on a shoebox, or a coastline. That is the trade.
