[P1.1] The human voice is as unique and identifiable as a face. When platforms distribute AI voice clones for profit, they are commodifying a core part of someone’s identity without permission. This violates personal autonomy: a person’s voice is not public domain just because it can be heard. Affirmative consent is the minimum standard to ensure individuals control how their likeness is used, from ads to synthetic media.
[P1.2] Without it, anyone’s voice can be co-opted for commercial gain, turning people into products against their will. Voice deserves the same legal protection as images and other likenesses.
[P1.3] The harms are not theoretical. Voice clones have already been used in scams—like a mother receiving a frantic call from her ‘kidnapped’ child, the voice a convincing AI clone. Even when no fraud occurs, the unauthorized use of a person's voice can cause lasting distress, as seen in cases of harassment via deepfake audio. Reputational damage is equally severe: a cloned voice could spread lies, damage careers, or manipulate public opinion.
[P1.4] Current law offers little recourse, and platforms face no consequences for enabling this without consent. As voice cloning improves, the risk of abuse will grow.
[P1.5] Commercially, this is exploitation. Media companies stand to profit from AI-generated content featuring cloned voices, while the original speakers bear all the risk and receive none of the benefit. The economic asymmetry is stark: corporations gain new revenue streams, while individuals are left with the burden of monitoring misuse. Consent ensures that individuals can negotiate fair terms, demand compensation, or opt out entirely.
[P1.6] Without it, we risk a future where identity is treated as raw material for corporate profit, eroding both economic fairness and the fundamental right to control one’s own image and voice. This is a basic dignity issue: no one should have their voice stolen and sold without their say. This requirement respects rights without stifling innovation.
