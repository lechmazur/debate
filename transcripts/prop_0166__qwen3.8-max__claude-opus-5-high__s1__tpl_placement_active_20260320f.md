[P1.1] De-extinction is not a vanity project; it is the high-risk frontier of conservation. Governments should fund and permit it even at some cost to present-day programs because the central failure of conservation politics is not too much spending, but too narrow an imagination and too little capacity. A mammoth-style project or thylacine revival forces advances in genomics, reproductive biology, habitat restoration, and biosecurity that ordinary species protection cannot demand. Those tools spill over to endangered species: better genetic rescue, disease resistance, captive breeding, and habitat modeling.
[P1.2] Restored mammoth-like grazers could help maintain cold grasslands; a thylacine-like predator could rebalance prey pressure in Tasmania. These are not substitutes for saving living species; they are attempts to restore missing ecological functions.
[P1.3] The opportunity-cost objection treats conservation budgets as fixed and attention as a finite pie. But governments do not merely allocate existing demand; they create it. Charismatic restoration projects can draw new taxpayers, private partners, and volunteers who would never fund routine amphibian or invertebrate protection. The thylacine or mammoth becomes a gateway: it makes the public care about whole ecosystems—Tasmanian forests, Arctic grasslands—and the species living in them.
[P1.4] If a headline project diverts a small share of money but enlarges the political coalition for restoration, the net effect can be more protection, not less.
[P1.5] There is also a distinct governmental duty: to correct irreversible harms. Extinction caused by human action is not a natural limit; if science can restore a functional analogue or close relative, refusing to do so preserves our mistake as policy. Governments are the only actors with the legitimacy, timescale, and regulatory power to permit such work safely. We should not wait until every current species is perfectly protected; that standard would forbid every major innovation.
[P1.6] The right test is whether de-extinction builds ecological and scientific capacity beyond incremental spending alone. It does.
