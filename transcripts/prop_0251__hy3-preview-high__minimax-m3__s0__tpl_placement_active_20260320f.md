[P1.1] Extreme heat is the deadliest weather-related hazard across most populated regions, and working people face the highest exposure. Outdoor construction, agricultural, delivery, and warehouse roles force staff into direct, prolonged heat contact with no escape, and current labor laws in nearly all jurisdictions treat heat as an exceptional, optional condition rather than a mandatory safety trigger. This gap lets employers force workers to labor through dangerous temperatures with zero legal penalty, stripping them of the core labor right to a safe workplace.
[P1.2] Heat does not only cause fatal heatstroke: it impairs cognitive function and motor skills at far lower thresholds, spiking rates of falls, machinery accidents, and vehicle collisions that injure and kill workers across industries.
[P1.3] Voluntary employer measures and weak local regulations consistently fail. Profit incentives drive firms to prioritize short-term output over worker safety, and industry lobbying regularly blocks even modest heat protection bills. For example, multiple U.S. state legislatures have rejected mandatory cooling breaks for outdoor workers even after repeated fatal heatstroke incidents on job sites, while federal OSHA has no nationwide heat standard, leaving staff with no guaranteed recourse when temperatures turn lethal. Heat-related illness already imposes massive, uncompensated costs on public health systems and workers themselves, while employers pocket the short-term gains of uninterrupted operations.
[P1.4] Mandatory stop-work protections are narrowly tailored to trigger only at extreme, scientifically defined heat index thresholds, not mild warm days. The minimal cost of short, targeted stoppages pales against the human and economic toll of heat-related workplace deaths, medical emergencies, and accident-linked productivity loss. Claims that such rules would devastate local economies are a false tradeoff: a workforce decimated by preventable heat injury cannot sustain any economic activity, and the long-term cost of replacing skilled workers lost to heat disability far exceeds the expense of brief work pauses.

[C1.5] PRO's [P1.4] claim that stoppages are "narrowly tailored" cannot survive contact with a Delhi construction site in May.
[C1.6] Third, enforcement is a fantasy. Who measures the heat index at which of tens of thousands of work sites? Where the inspector is absent, the rule is either ignored or weaponized — used by bad employers to cut hours or extracted as leverage in disputes. The proposition offers no answer.
