[P1.1] The curb is the most contested and most undervalued real estate in any city. Yet most urban policy still treats it as a public subsidy for private car storage. That is backwards. Every square foot of curb space should be measured by one standard: how many people does it move, and how much commerce does it enable?
[P1.2] By that measure, on-street parking for private cars is the worst possible use.
[P1.3] A single metered parking space stores one vehicle, often idle for hours. The same twenty feet of curb, reallocated to a bus lane, can move hundreds of passengers per hour. Reallocate it to a protected bike lane, and it serves thousands of daily trips at near-zero public cost. Reallocate it to a commercial loading zone, and it keeps storefronts stocked and courier businesses running.
[P1.4] The trade is not close. Protecting most on-street parking for private cars privileges the least efficient, least equitable mode while choking the transit, delivery, and cycling networks that actually keep a dense city functioning. Free or underpriced curbside storage is a regressive transfer: it taxes everyone through congestion and road danger while delivering a private subsidy to a minority of drivers.
[P1.5] Consider the cascade. Double-parked delivery trucks block bike lanes; circling cars hunting for cheap curbside parking slow buses; and each lane of traffic devoted to vehicle storage induces more driving, creating the very congestion that parking was supposed to solve. This is not anti-car. It is pro-access.
[P1.6] A bus lane gets a nurse to a hospital shift, a delivery zone gets a restaurant its supplies, and a bike lane gets a parent to school without a parking hunt. Private car parking gets a driver a storage unit. When the curb serves the most people, traffic drops, small businesses stabilize, and streets become safer. The question is not whether drivers deserve convenience.
[P1.7] It is whether the public should subsidize the least productive use of public space. It should not. Cities should reallocate curb space to move people and goods, not to park metal.

[C1.6] The equity claim is backwards. In dense cores, transit and cycling are often substitutes.
