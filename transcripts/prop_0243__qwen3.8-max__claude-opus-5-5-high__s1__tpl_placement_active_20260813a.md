[P1.1] Ride-hail platforms worsened congestion because they put more vehicles on the busiest roads at the busiest times, not merely more passengers. The decisive question is net change in vehicle-miles and curb conflicts in dense city cores, not whether some households own fewer cars.
[P1.2] First, ride-hail induces car travel. Before apps, many city trips were made by subway, bus, walking, biking, or not taken at all. The app made door-to-door car service frictionless and psychologically cheap, especially for short trips in dense areas. When a rider switches from a bus to an Uber, the person may be better served, but the road now carries a private vehicle where a high-capacity vehicle once carried them.
[P1.3] That is congestion-worsening even if it is convenience-improving. Pooling does not erase this: shared rides often add detours, and a pooled car still carries far fewer people per lane than a bus or train.
[P1.4] Second, ride-hail adds empty driving. A private car usually travels from origin to destination and parks. A ride-hail vehicle drops a rider off, then circles, waits in a travel lane, or drives toward demand. Between pickups, it is consuming road space while producing no passenger movement.
[P1.5] In major cities, this deadheading can account for a large share of ride-hail mileage, and it occurs precisely where roads are already scarce.
[P1.6] Third, ride-hail creates curb friction. Double-parked pickups, sudden stops, and drivers waiting near airports, stadiums, or nightlife districts interrupt the flow of buses, trucks, and other cars. Even a few seconds of blocking a lane in dense traffic cascades through following vehicles.
[P1.7] CON may say ride-hail replaces private car trips or enables car-free living. But that benefit is speculative and spread out, while the congestion costs are direct, repeated, and concentrated where traffic is worst. On net, by converting transit trips into car trips, adding empty miles, and clogging curbs, ride-hail has made major-city traffic worse overall.
