[P1.1] Low Earth orbit is a commons we cannot clean. A piece of debris the size of a paint chip travels at roughly seven kilometers per second—fast enough to disable a satellite. When two large objects collide, they don't disappear; they shatter into thousands of new projectiles, each capable of causing the next collision. That cascade—the Kessler scenario—is not science fiction; it is the orbital mechanics consensus, and it is irreversible on any human timescale.
[P1.2] There is no fleet of garbage trucks in orbit. Once a shell of LEO becomes a shooting gallery, it stays that way for decades or centuries.
[P1.3] Against that backdrop, the current trajectory is reckless. A handful of companies and states are filing for tens of thousands of satellites under rules written in the 1960s and 70s, when the entire orbital population was a few thousand objects. There is no binding international standard for collision avoidance, no mandatory data-sharing on satellite positions, no enforceable deorbit requirements, no liability regime that actually deters negligence, and no protection for ground-based astronomy, which mega-constellations are already streaking across. Regulators are approving constellations faster than anyone can model their aggregate risk.
[P1.4] The motion asks for something modest: a temporary pause on *new* mega-constellations until those rules are materially stronger. Existing services keep operating. Research, small constellations, and replacement launches continue. What stops is the headlong expansion into a governance vacuum.
[P1.5] The decision principle is simple: when an action is irreversible and the harm is collective, you fix the rules before you scale, not after. We accept this everywhere else—we don't license new drugs first and run safety trials later.
[P1.6] So the burden on the other side is steep. They must show that the benefits of launching the *next* fifty thousand satellites in the next few years—not satellites generally, but these marginal ones, now—outweigh the risk of permanently degrading humanity's access to orbit. A short pause costs delay. The status quo gambles a commons we can never get back.
