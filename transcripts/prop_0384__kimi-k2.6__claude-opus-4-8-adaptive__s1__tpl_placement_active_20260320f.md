[P1.1] Public schools exist to serve every family equally, yet current policy frequently forces parents into a false binary: accept the default secular program on campus or arrange costly, logistically demanding off-campus religious instruction yourself. The proposition before you resolves this inequity by permitting optional on-campus religious instruction only under two strict conditions: a genuinely comparable secular alternative must be offered, and participation must be unmistakably voluntary. That framework is fair, constitutionally sound, and long overdue.
[P1.2] "Optional" and "comparable" are the twin pillars that neutralize any concern about coercion. No child is compelled to attend; no teacher is required to instruct; no public funds are diverted to devotional materials. The secular alternative ensures the school does not privilege faith over non-faith, but simply accommodates both worldviews on equal terms. Picture a midweek elective block where one room hosts religious study while another hosts a comparative ethics or philosophy seminar—students choose freely, parents provide clear written consent, and the core public curriculum remains entirely untouched.
[P1.3] The school does not promote religion; it permits it alongside a non-religious equivalent.
[P1.4] The deepest inequity in the status quo is logistical, not theological. Off-campus release-time programs implicitly favor families with private transportation, flexible work schedules, and safe routes to nearby churches or synagogues, disproportionately excluding low-income, single-parent, and rural households. On-campus accommodation under these strict conditions removes that barrier without spending extra instructional minutes or risking public subsidy of worship. It simply levels the playing field during time already allocated for electives.
[P1.5] Opponents will insist that any religious presence inside a public building breaches the wall between church and state. They mistake neutrality for endorsement. A school that permits religious instruction under identical logistical rules as a secular ethics club or study hall does not establish religion; it respects the constitutional pluralism that public education is supposed to embody.
[P1.6] By contrast, an outright ban does not protect conscience—it imposes a secular orthodoxy by default.
[P1.7] Adopt this measured accommodation. It safeguards the non-religious, empowers families of faith, and restores genuine equity to public education.
