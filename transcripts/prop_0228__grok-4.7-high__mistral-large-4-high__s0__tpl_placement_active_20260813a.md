[P1.1] The gig economy’s central trick is simple: platforms sell control and call it freedom. A rider who cannot set the price, cannot refuse too many jobs without penalty, cannot see the system that ranks them, and can be cut off overnight is not running a business. They are working for one. European and Asian governments should stop relitigating that fact and reclassify most platform workers as employees by default, with narrow exemptions only for genuine freelancers.
[P1.2] Dependency is the right test, and the default should follow it. Real freelancers choose clients, set rates, and manage risk they can actually bear. Most app-based delivery, ride-hail, and task workers do none of that. The platform is the market, the manager, and the discipline system.
[P1.3] Calling the arrangement a contract merely shifts sickness, injury, idle time, and old age onto the worker and, eventually, onto public budgets. Employment status puts those costs where the control already sits.
[P1.4] A default matters because the alternative is a classification arms race. Platforms rewrite terms, insert sham substitution clauses, and outlast workers in court. Europe has already watched this in ride-hail and food delivery; across much of Asia the same model is scaling faster than inspection. Case-by-case tests reward the side with lawyers.
[P1.5] A presumption of employment, rebuttable only where independence is real—multiple clients, genuine price-setting, no subordination—closes the loophole without abolishing freelancing.
[P1.6] The fear that jobs will vanish confuses a subsidy with a business model. What disappears is the practice of dumping labor risk onto people who cannot insure it. Firms that coordinate millions of trips can fund contributions and basic protections. Leaving misclassification intact does not protect innovation; it protects a race to the bottom against employers who hire properly.
[P1.7] Narrow exemptions keep true independents free. Everyone else should receive the status their working life already is.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to solve a twenty-first-century labor market with a twentieth-century binary. It treats Europe and Asia as a single regulatory zone, and it assumes the only choice is between employee and genuine freelancer—ignoring the third option that actually fits modern work: portable benefits decoupled from employment status.
[C1.2] Start with who these workers actually are. Across Asia and Southern Europe, millions use platforms not as a career replacement, but as a supplement—students fitting shifts around classes, caregivers earning between responsibilities, rural workers reaching urban demand, second earners topping up household income. They choose gig work precisely because employee status would chain them to schedules, seniority rules, and rigid firing protections they neither want nor need. A default-employee rule does not protect these workers; it prices them out of the market.
[C1.3] Platforms suddenly facing mandatory social contributions, minimum wage floors, and dismissal procedures will reduce headcount or exit entirely—leaving those workers with no income at all.
[C1.4] The motion’s “narrow exemptions” for genuine freelancers are a bureaucratic fantasy. Who draws the line between a genuine freelancer and a disguised employee? Courts? Labor inspectors?
[C1.5] In economies with weak administrative capacity across much of Asia, this invites corruption, arbitrary enforcement, and endless litigation—creating the very legal uncertainty the proposition claims to eliminate while chilling legitimate flexible work.
[C1.6] The economic stakes are highest where the motion bites hardest. In developing Asian economies, formal employment is already scarce because the regulatory cost of hiring is crushing. Forcing platforms into that model does not create good jobs; it destroys accessible ones. The delivery rider who could work evenings around family obligations loses the opportunity entirely.
[C1.7] We do not need to choose between exploitation and flexibility. Portable health coverage, portable pensions, and pro-rated protections that follow the worker across platforms deliver security without destroying the independence that makes gig work viable. That is the twenty-first-century answer. A default-employee rule is a regression disguised as progress—one that enriches lawyers and unions while hurting the very workers it claims to save.

Conflating "providing a benefit" with "exercising control" is a rhetorical trap designed to eliminate the middle ground. The legal test for employment is subordination, not generosity; a stipend changes neither who sets the price nor who bears the risk.
[C2.2] More damaging is the assertion that reclassification preserves flexibility [P2.3], [P2.4]. Tell that to the nursing student who can only work Tuesday and Thursday evenings, or the caregiver who dashes between clients. Employment status triggers minimum-hour commitments, scheduling mandates, and dismissal protections that make sporadic, self-directed work economically irrational for the platform. When a platform faces employer liabilities, it does not hire "part-time employees" for unpredictable evening shifts; it cuts headcount, automates, or exits the market entirely.
[C2.3] The evening shift does not survive with a mandatory wage floor and firing procedure attached [C1.3], [C1.6].
[C2.4] The presumption of employment [P2.7] sounds efficient until you place it in a weak-rule-of-law environment. Shifting the burden to the platform does not create clarity when inspectors in Manila or Jakarta can threaten reclassification to extract settlements from startups. A default employee status in a corruptible bureaucracy is not a shield for workers—it is a protection racket against the very platforms providing income in economies where formal jobs are scarce [C1.5], [C1.6].
[C2.5] Finally, Europe and Asia are not one labor market. Brussels can absorb compliance costs; a delivery startup in Hanoi or a task platform in Bangalore cannot.
