[P1.1] Carbon offsets have become corporate America's favorite accounting trick. A company can keep burning fossil fuels, keep emitting carbon, and still claim to be "net-zero" simply by buying pieces of paper representing someone else's trees or someone else's solar panels. The proposition before us says this should stop: the law should cap how much of a company's emissions can be offset, forcing the bulk of reductions to happen within the company's own operations. I will argue this is both necessary and urgent.
[P1.2] The fundamental problem is that offsets do not reliably reduce atmospheric carbon. Consider what an offset actually is: a forest in one country, a methane capture project in another, a cookstove initiative in a third. These projects carry chronic, well-documented risks—permanence failures when forests burn, additionality failures when projects would have happened anyway, and leakage when production simply moves elsewhere. A company buying an offset gets credit immediately; the carbon may re-emerge within years.
[P1.3] This is not a reduction—it is a bet on the future that rarely pays off.
[P1.4] Yet unlimited offsets create perverse incentives. Companies facing real decarbonization costs simply purchase offsets instead. They announce net-zero targets, enjoy the marketing benefit, and defer the harder structural changes that actual climate stability requires. This is greenwashing with a carbon label.
[P1.5] Public trust in corporate sustainability claims has already eroded sharply as watchdogs document the gap between net-zero rhetoric and actual emissions trajectories.
[P1.6] Legal limits solve three problems simultaneously. First, they force companies to make genuine in-value-chain reductions—the only kind that are permanent, measurable, and within their control. Second, they prevent the worst abuses of offset markets without eliminating them entirely for residual emissions. Third, they restore meaning to "net-zero" by ensuring it describes actual carbon math rather than creative accounting.
[P1.7] My opponents will argue that offsets are a useful tool and that limiting them harms climate ambition. I will show this gets the causality backwards: it is the unlimited offset loophole that has hollowed out corporate climate commitments and made "net-zero" a punchline.

[C1.5] Fourth, hard-to-abate sectors—cement, aviation, steel, agriculture—physically cannot decarbonize most operations today. Forcing "most" reductions internally isn't ambition; it's a mandate to miss targets, because the technology doesn't yet exist. Offsets bridge that gap while it matures.
[C1.6] So here is the clash I want decided: the proposition trades real, immediate, low-cost global tons for expensive symbolism about *where* reductions happen. The honest fix is integrity, transparency, and verification—not a number pulled from the air and frozen into statute.
