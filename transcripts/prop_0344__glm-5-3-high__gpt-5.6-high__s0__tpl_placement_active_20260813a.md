[P1.1] Early secondary school — roughly ages eleven to fourteen — is a narrow developmental window. Students are building the executive function to sustain focus, and the social skills to read faces, negotiate conflict, and form identity in groups. These are the exact capacities that one-to-one device programs strain hardest, and they are far harder to remediate later than any technology skill, which students reliably pick up whenever they need it. That asymmetry is the core of this case: what's scarce and fragile should get priority, and what's abundant and easily learned should not.
[P1.2] Consider attention first. A laptop or tablet on every desk is not a neutral tool; it is a competition for a child's working memory against a trained adult teacher. Even university students — with fully mature prefrontal cortices — struggle to take meaningful notes on devices, multitask during class, and remember less. Expecting a twelve-year-old to self-regulate against apps engineered by armies of behavioral designers is not empowerment; it is an unfair fight.
[P1.3] Shared device carts, computer labs, and teacher-led screen time deliver the digital skills without installing the distraction in every backpack and bedroom.
[P1.4] Now consider the social side. Middle school is when peer relationships become the primary engine of development. Device-mediated interaction — even collaborative online work — strips away tone, body language, and the productive discomfort of face-to-face disagreement. Lunchrooms full of students on individual screens are a familiar image precisely because they are common.
[P1.5] One-to-one programs don't just fail to help social growth; in practice, they cannibalize the unstructured and structured peer time that drives it.
[P1.6] Notice the burden here. This is not a debate about whether technology matters — it obviously does, and nothing in the proposition bans screens. [C1.1] The proposition mistakes an ownership model for a teaching model. One-to-one means every student has reliable access to the same educational infrastructure; it does not mean every lesson, lunch, or conversation occurs behind a screen. Teachers can close lids during discussion, restrict applications, and design device-free periods. Rejecting dependable access because devices can be misused is like rejecting textbooks because students can daydream behind them.
[C1.2] Used deliberately, one-to-one devices strengthen attention rather than undermine it. A teacher can check every student’s response at once, give immediate feedback, break complex tasks into visible steps, and redirect confusion before disengagement spreads. Text-to-speech, enlarged print, translation, captions, and organized digital materials help students who would otherwise spend their limited attention decoding instructions or searching through papers. For a child with dyslexia, weak vision, language barriers, or executive-function difficulties, the device is not the distraction; it is often the route back into the lesson.
[C1.3] One-to-one access also serves equity. Shared carts and occasional computer labs create delays, scheduling bottlenecks, and unequal continuity between class and home. More affluent students still enjoy personal devices and private support; less affluent students are left learning unfamiliar tools only during scarce booked sessions. Early secondary school is precisely when schools can teach safe research, source evaluation, privacy, online collaboration, and self-regulation under adult supervision.
[C1.4] Waiting does not protect children from digital life; it leaves them to learn its habits elsewhere, without guidance.
[C1.5] Nor are devices inherently antisocial. Students can jointly annotate a text, produce a presentation, peer-edit writing, or divide roles in a group investigation. The social outcome depends on whether teachers structure collaboration, not whether each group has to crowd around one scarce machine.
[C1.6] Attention and social development are goals; one-to-one access is a tool. Schools should prioritize effective, inclusive learning and choose when that tool is open or closed.
