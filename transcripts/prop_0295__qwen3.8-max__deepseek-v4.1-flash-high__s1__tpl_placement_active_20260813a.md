[P1.1] Restitution is not complete when a crate crosses a border; it is complete when a community can safely keep, understand, and display what was taken. The proposition makes that standard concrete: former colonial powers should fund conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity in receiving countries. The test is simple: shipping objects back without support transfers the costs of colonial wrongs onto the very people most harmed by them.
[P1.2] First, many returned objects are not stable commodities. Textiles, manuscripts, metalwork, photographs, and ritual objects may have suffered from poor storage, humid climates, or decades of neglect while held abroad. Conservation funding prevents the tragic outcome in which restitution becomes loss: a fragile mask corrodes, a banner disintegrates, an archive becomes unusable. If former powers genuinely acknowledge wrongful taking, they should ensure the object survives the return.
[P1.3] Second, provenance research is part of restitution, not an optional academic luxury. Objects often return with incomplete records: colonial administrators, dealers, and museums may have obscured how items were acquired. Research recovers names, places, rituals, and chains of custody; it helps receiving institutions explain the object to their own public, resist fraud, and reconnect fragments dispersed across collections. Former colonial powers usually hold the relevant archives, catalogues, and technical expertise, so funding this work is a direct repair, not charity.
[P1.4] Third, museum capacity is about sovereignty, not dependency. Secure storage, climate control, insurance, conservation labs, digitization, and trained staff determine whether returned heritage is accessible or disappears into under-resourced vaults. If returns are underfunded, opponents of restitution can point to damage or poor display as proof that objects “belong” in Western museums. Funding removes that excuse and makes restitution durable.
[P1.5] This is not paternalism if designed properly: recipient institutions set priorities, control narratives, and receive grants rather than loans. The alternative—shipping and shrugging—lets former powers claim moral closure while leaving the hardest burdens behind.

[C1.1] Restitution is complete when legal title, physical custody, and interpretive authority return. The proposition swaps that clear act for an open-ended donor relationship, and that is precisely why it fails. It does not strengthen return; it makes return conditional on the former colonizer’s continuing money and approval.
[C1.2] Once former powers fund conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity, they gain leverage. Grants come with priorities: which objects deserve climate control, whose provenance account is credible, which staff are trained, which institutions are deemed responsible enough. A receiving country that rejects the strings is told it does not care about its own heritage. That is colonial logic in new form: the metropole still decides what proper stewardship looks like.
[C1.3] The proposition is also unworkable. Which former power owes for which object? For how long—one grant, a decade, forever? If a returned textile fades or a manuscript is damaged, does the original taker owe again?
[C1.4] Open-ended liability gives museums and governments a reason to delay returns, demand indemnities, or hand back only the easy cases. PRO worries that shipping without support leaves burdens behind; the greater danger is that making support the definition of completion leaves objects in storage abroad while funding terms are negotiated.
[C1.5] Provenance research especially should not be donor-controlled. Colonial archives, catalogues, and collecting records were often created by the very powers being asked to fund the correction. Letting them set research questions can launder colonial categories and extend intellectual custody. Recipient institutions, source communities, and regional experts must define the work.
[C1.6] The just model is unconditional return first, then recipient-led support if desired—pooled through multilateral heritage funds, reparations settlements, or equal partnerships. Conservation is urgent, but it is not a condition of sovereignty. The proposition turns restitution into permanent dependency and gives former powers a veto over when return counts. That is not repair; it is managed return.
