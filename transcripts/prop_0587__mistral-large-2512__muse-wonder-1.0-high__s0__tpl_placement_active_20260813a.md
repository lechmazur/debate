[P1.1] A carbon border adjustment—taxing imports based on their embedded emissions—is the single most powerful tool the U.S. can deploy to cut global emissions in the next five years. Domestic subsidies and regulations alone cannot match its reach because they leave a fatal gap: carbon leakage. When U.S. factories face stricter rules or higher costs, production simply shifts to countries with weaker standards, and those emissions continue unchecked. A border adjustment closes that gap by ensuring foreign producers face the same carbon price as domestic ones, eliminating the incentive to offshore pollution.
[P1.2] Consider steel. The U.S. steel industry, already cleaner than China’s, risks losing market share to dirtier imports if we act alone. A border adjustment levels the playing field, forcing Chinese steelmakers to either adopt cleaner technology or pay a penalty. That’s not protectionism—it’s a race to the top.
[P1.3] The European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is already pushing trading partners like Turkey and India to accelerate their decarbonization plans. The U.S. can do the same, but with greater leverage: our market is the world’s largest, and our trading partners will adapt rather than lose access.
[P1.4] Critics claim border adjustments are too complex or slow. But the EU’s system took just three years from proposal to implementation, and the U.S. can move faster with streamlined rules. Meanwhile, domestic subsidies—like those for electric vehicles—often subsidize foreign production if supply chains aren’t fully domestic. A border adjustment ensures that every dollar spent on decarbonization actually reduces global emissions, not just shifts them overseas.
[P1.5] Finally, this isn’t an either-or choice. A border adjustment works *with* domestic policies, not against them. Subsidies can accelerate innovation at home, but without a border adjustment, that innovation will be undercut by cheaper, dirtier imports. The next five years are critical for bending the global emissions curve.
[P1.6] We can’t afford to leave the back door open.
