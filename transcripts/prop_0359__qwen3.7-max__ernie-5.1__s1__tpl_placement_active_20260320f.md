[P1.1] Ride-hail platforms classify drivers as independent contractors, yet deny them the fundamental market information required to operate as one: the actual price of the service they are providing.
[P1.2] Currently, platforms rely on opaque, algorithmic pricing. A rider might pay $40 for a trip, while the driver receives $15. The platform keeps $25, but neither party sees the exact split. This deliberate information asymmetry allows platforms to engage in extreme price discrimination.
[P1.3] They surge-price riders based on their immediate willingness to pay, while simultaneously suppressing driver wages based on their desperation to work. Because neither side sees the true clearing price of the ride, the platform captures the entire economic surplus, hiding behind a proprietary black box.
[P1.4] Mandating a default, transparent fare split corrects this market failure. When a rider sees exactly how much of their fare goes to the platform versus the driver, they can make informed choices, potentially favoring services with fairer compensation models. When a driver sees the full gross fare, they can accurately assess their true margin, factoring in wear-and-tear, and decide whether to accept the ride or log off. This is how actual independent businesses operate.
[P1.5] The opposition will likely argue that dynamic pricing is too complex to display, or that transparency introduces market friction. But algorithmic complexity is a smokescreen for rent-seeking. Displaying a simple dollar split or percentage breakdown on the final receipt is technically trivial. If a platform truly offers a superior matching service, it should be proud of its take rate, not terrified of it.
[P1.6] This debate ultimately comes down to whether a multi-billion-dollar intermediary should be legally permitted to hide its profit margins from the two parties generating its revenue. Opacity only serves the middleman. By requiring full fare transparency, we restore basic market efficiency, empower workers to act like true contractors, and allow consumers to vote with their wallets.

[C1.2] Lower demand forces platforms to raise base fares or cut driver payouts, harming the workers the rule is supposed to protect.
[C1.3] Drivers, meanwhile, will be demoralised by a permanent view of a shrinking margin. The result is fewer drivers on the road, longer wait times, and higher surge spikes for everyone.
[C1.4] The technical argument that showing a split is “trivial” ignores that the split is not a static number. It fluctuates with promos, taxes, insurance, and dynamic multipliers. A default display would present a misleading snapshot, inviting litigation and confusing users.

Removing that tool would push costs onto riders and drivers alike. Moreover, platforms rely on the opacity to fund safety nets, insurance, and driver incentives. If riders see a large platform cut, they may switch to cheaper alternatives that lack those protections, exposing drivers to higher risk.
[C1.6] The net effect is a race to the bottom, not empowerment. The proposition trades a clean headline for real‑world inefficiency, and the net welfare loss outweighs any symbolic gain in transparency.
