[P1.1] Fundamental science fails when it's managed like a procurement contract. Short-cycle competitive grants ask a scientist to promise, in advance, what they will discover—on a three-year clock, with annual reports, preliminary data, and a bureaucracy that rewards confident storytelling over honest uncertainty. That is precisely backwards for research whose whole point is that nobody knows the answer yet.
[P1.2] Three mechanisms make this case. First, time horizons. The distance from a fundamental insight to a Nobel Prize is typically measured in decades. Grant cycles of three to five years systematically starve long problems—the hard ones.
[P1.3] A researcher who spends two years building tools and the third year writing the renewal application has produced science shaped by the renewal, not by the question.
[P1.4] Second, the overhead of competition. Competitive granting converts a growing share of the scientific workforce into professional proposal-writers. Senior investigators routinely spend weeks per proposal on applications with funding rates that leave most work unfunded, and labs live in permanent boom-and-bust, unable to hire postdocs on a two-year runway. Block grants to institutions or research groups slash that deadweight: one decision, stable funding, scientists spending their time doing science.
[P1.5] Third, the distortion of research agendas. When funding is scarce and short, reviewers favor safe, incremental work with predictable publications. Risky, paradigm-threatening ideas—the ones that occasionally change everything—cannot survive a process designed to minimize reviewer risk. The Pentagon's early computing investments, the decades of patient support behind CRISPR's foundational biology, the curiosity-driven work that later became MRI: none of it fits a three-year deliverable.
[P1.6] They came from patient, trusting money.
[P1.7] Now the honest cost: block grants buy less accountability per dollar. Some funds will sit inert; some researchers will coast. Accepted. But accountability is not the goal—discovery is.
[P1.8] An accountability system that optimizes for legible quarterly outputs will reliably fund legible, quarterly science. If we want the illegible, decade-scale breakthroughs, we must pay for them the only way they can be bought: with trust, at scale, over time.

[C1.1] The proposition treats accountability as a tax on discovery, but in public science accountability is the system’s immune defense. Fundamental science is expensive, uncertain, and politically vulnerable. If governments abandon the ability to ask what public money is doing, they do not buy trust; they invite capture. The strongest case for competitive project grants is not that every dollar can be audited into a breakthrough.
[C1.2] It is that recurring peer competition keeps the system open, corrigible, and responsive to new ideas rather than entrenched incumbents.
[C1.3] Short-cycle grants are often badly designed, but the proposition replaces bad design with no exit. Long-term block grants to institutions or groups freeze today’s reputations into tomorrow’s budgets. That helps established labs, but it hurts the mobile, the early-career, and the outsider whose idea has not yet earned institutional prestige. Science advances partly by turnover: by letting unsuccessful programs shrink and unexpected ones begin.
[C1.4] A portfolio that cannot be rewon cannot be renewed.
[C1.5] The proposition also misdescribes accountability. It need not mean quarterly deliverables or pretending uncertainty away. It can mean five-year reviews, independent panels, transparent criteria, and renewal decisions based on what was learned, not merely what was promised. Competitive project grants already allow long horizons when agencies choose multi-year awards or fellowships.
[C1.6] The remedy for short-termism is better cycles, not abolishing cycles.
[C1.7] Consider the political economy. If funders cannot point to projects, teams, or results, they cannot defend budgets when austerity comes. Less accountability per dollar weakens the coalition that pays for fundamental science in the first place. The public funds curiosity because it can see competence, fairness, and review—not because it writes blank checks.
[C1.8] For fundamental science, patience matters. But so does contestability. The proposition buys stability by surrendering the mechanisms that keep science honest, open, and publicly legitimate.
