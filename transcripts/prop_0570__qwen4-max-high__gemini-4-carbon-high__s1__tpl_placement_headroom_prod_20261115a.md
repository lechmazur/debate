[P1.1] Mastery practice has a fixed target. There is a right way to factor a quadratic, a right case ending, a right mechanism for osmosis, and a student who gets it wrong can be told so and try again immediately. That is exactly the loop a machine runs well: infinite patience, endless variants, hints before answers, no embarrassment about asking the same question a fourth time. Give it that job.
[P1.2] Writing feedback is a different job. Its object is not correctness but judgment — whether this argument earns its conclusion, whether this evidence is actually evidence, whether the writer's voice survived the draft. An AI comments on the text in front of it. A teacher comments on the writer who produced it: the student who has dodged the counterargument in three consecutive essays, the one whose real point is buried in the last paragraph, the one who is finally taking a risk.
[P1.3] That memory is not sentiment; it is the diagnostic data.
[P1.4] And AI feedback has a characteristic failure: it improves the surface. It smooths sentences, inserts a transition, suggests "consider a counterargument," and praises structure that is hollow underneath. A student who follows it learns to produce prose that sounds like good writing without having done the thinking that makes writing good. Worse, the model will fluently affirm a claim that is simply false, and a fifteen-year-old often cannot tell the difference between confident phrasing and a checked fact.
[P1.5] The motion says *main source*. That is about authority — whose judgment a student treats as the standard when the two disagree, and who they learn to write for. Use AI to drill vocabulary and check algebra all day; nothing here forbids a student asking it where a comma goes.
[P1.6] But the voice that tells a teenager what counts as good writing should be a human who knows them, is accountable for the grade, and can be argued with.
