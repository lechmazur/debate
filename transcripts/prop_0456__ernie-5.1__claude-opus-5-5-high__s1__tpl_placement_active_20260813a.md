[P1.1] The core tension here is simple: generative AI needs vast amounts of copyrighted material to function, and creators deserve to be paid when their work generates value. Compulsory licensing resolves that tension with a mechanism we already trust in music, broadcast, and publishing. Why would we invent something worse from scratch?
[P1.2] Consider mechanical licensing for cover songs. Songwriters don't get to veto every karaoke performance, yet they collect royalties through a statutory rate set by a body like the Copyright Royalty Board. The system works because it is administrable, predictable, and fair. Extending that logic to AI training doesn't betray creators — it protects them from a far worse fate: either total exclusion from a transformative technology, or a patchwork of litigation that enriches lawyers and starves everyone else.
[P1.3] Requiring individual permission for training datasets is functionally impossible. A single large language model may ingest billions of text fragments. Negotiating licenses with millions of rights holders is not a legal framework — it's a logistical fantasy that would concentrate power in the hands of a few gatekeepers who can afford the transactions, while smaller creators get squeezed out entirely.
[P1.4] A compulsory scheme flips that dynamic. It guarantees payment flows to rights holders through a collective or centralized mechanism, lowers transaction costs for developers, and creates legal certainty that attracts investment into AI tools that benefit society — from medical research to education to accessibility.
[P1.5] The opponent will say creators lose control. But control without compensation is already what happens when works sit unlicensed and unread. Control with guaranteed payment is the real improvement. The proposition does not abandon copyright — it modernizes it, ensuring that the enormous commercial value AI extracts from creative works returns to the people who made those works possible.
