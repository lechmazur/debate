[C1.1] The motion, as [P1.1] frames it, sells a false trade: evidence or elections. School history is already a hybrid. Scholars write and critique textbooks; public bodies adopt them. The real question is where final authority over contested civic narrative should sit.
[C1.2] I contend it should remain politically accountable, with scholars as strong advisors, not handed to a stronger independent scholarly review posing as neutral. Such review would not remove politics; it would relocate it to a less visible, less accountable body.
[C1.3] History textbooks are not laboratory reports. They tell children what their community has done and whom it has wronged. Peer review can catch a wrong date or a misread treaty. It cannot decide whether a war was a tragedy of empire or a burden of civilization, or how central colonialism should be to national identity.
[C1.4] Those are civic judgments, not footnotes. Ask not who checks the footnote, but who chooses the frame. To treat them as technical questions is to smuggle in values while denying responsibility.
[C1.5] Nor is scholarly review stable or disinterested. Historians divide by school, generation, and moral conviction. West Germany’s Historikerstreit showed leading scholars split over Nazi crimes; no independent panel could resolve it without choosing a side. [C1.6] It is unelected, slow to correct, and can dismiss religious, local, and dissenting views as “unscholarly.”
[C1.7] Political control is messy, but it is revisable. Hearings, courts, teachers, and elections let citizens contest the story. A bad narrative can be voted out. A bad scholarly consensus can take a generation to dislodge.
[C1.8] The answer to bad politics is more democracy, not less: multiple textbooks, teacher autonomy, and public justification.
[C1.9] The choice is not accuracy versus propaganda, but accountable pluralism versus expert closure. National identity is something citizens must argue over, not receive as settled expertise. This motion should be rejected.
