[P1.1] The motion asks a comparative question, and the answer turns on what we count. Count only this month's enrollment figures and work requirements look harsh. Count years of eroded skills, stalled wages, and children raised in households where nobody works, and the calculation flips.
[P1.2] Dependency is not a metaphor; it is a trap. Means-tested benefits phase out as earnings rise, so a pay raise can leave a family worse off. Work requirements push against that gravity, keeping recipients attached to the labor market where wages compound instead of parked outside it. The clearest test came with the 1996 welfare reform: once cash assistance carried work requirements and time limits, caseloads fell by more than half, employment among single mothers rose substantially, and child poverty dropped.
[P1.3] That is dependency reduction at scale — not people cast aside, but people moved into work who otherwise would have stayed on the rolls for years.
[P1.4] Now weigh the other side: those who genuinely cannot comply. They are exempt by design. These requirements target able-bodied adults; disability, pregnancy, caregiving for young children, and age carve out precisely the vulnerable people the opposition will invoke. The obligation itself is modest — typically around twenty hours a week of work, training, job search, or volunteering.
[P1.5] And when people do lose benefits, as under Arkansas's Medicaid reporting rule, the losses are largely procedural: forms not filed, not work not done. That is an implementation flaw to be repaired with better notice and automatic verification, not evidence that the requirement injures the truly unable.
[P1.6] So compare. The harms alleged against these policies are short-term, administrative, and fixable. The harm they attack — entrenched, intergenerational non-work — compounds for decades. A child who grows up watching a parent hold a job gains something no check delivers.
[P1.7] The genuine hardship cases are already carved out; what remains is a firm nudge toward self-sufficiency for those who can. On any honest ledger, that trade favors the proposition.

[C1.2] When an eligible person loses Medicaid because a form went to an old address, the policy has injured someone who could not comply with the administrative requirement, even if they could theoretically work.
[C1.3] Exemptions exist on paper, not automatically in practice. [C1.4] Caseload decline is not dependency reduction. People leave SNAP and Medicaid for many reasons: they find work, but also they move, lose paperwork, become homeless, or shift to emergency care. If a requirement pushes someone off coverage and into uncompensated hospital debt, dependency has not fallen; it has become more expensive and less visible. The 1996 analogy is weak: TANF was cash assistance with time limits; Medicaid and SNAP are health and food supports, and work requirements there mostly police eligibility rather than build skills.
[C1.5] Finally, “more than” is doing all the work.
