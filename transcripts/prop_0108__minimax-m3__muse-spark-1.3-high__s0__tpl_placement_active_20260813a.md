[P1.1] Winner-take-all elections in single-member districts do not simply produce two parties; the rules themselves manufacture that outcome. When only one candidate can win and a plurality is enough, voters drift toward the perceived frontrunner, viable third parties are punished as spoilers, and ambitious politicians have every reason to mobilize the base rather than persuade the median voter. That is the structural engine of the polarization the motion targets, and it will keep running as long as the rules stay the same.
[P1.2] Ranked-choice voting attacks that engine directly. In single-member districts with RCV, a winner needs majority support after lower rankings are redistributed. Candidates can no longer survive on a hostile 35% when a broad field is waiting; they need second-choice support from rivals' backers. That changes the math of campaigning.
[P1.3] Attack ads aimed at one opponent alienate the voters whose next ranking decides the race. The result, already visible in Alaska and Maine, is more civil competition and survival of candidates who can bridge coalitions rather than purify them. Voters, freed from the spoiler trap, finally get to express honest preferences.
[P1.4] Proportional representation goes further for legislative chambers. If a quarter of voters prefer parties that currently hold zero seats, the system has told those ballots they are decorative. Under PR, those voters get representation, parties must negotiate to govern, and policy outcomes reflect actual public opinion. Multiparty parliaments across northern Europe produce stable coalition governments while accommodating ideological diversity our two-party system simply cannot.
[P1.5] The opposition will warn that reform risks instability. But the status quo is not stability; it is paralysis, where lopsided majorities oppose the country's direction yet nothing budges, and the median voter is repeatedly asked to choose the lesser of two polarized extremes. Reform is not the gamble. Staying put is.

[C1.1] Polarization in America is not printed on the ballot. It lives in sorted neighborhoods, nationalized media, primaries, and social feeds that reward outrage. Changing how we count votes will not change why neighbors distrust each other, which is why electoral rewiring is an elegant distraction from the harder cultural work.
[C1.2] Proportional representation would not defuse polarization; it would house it. America's two big parties are messy coalitions forced to moderate before an election to assemble a majority. Shatter them into four or five disciplined ideological factions and compromise moves from voters to smoke-filled coalition talks afterward. The fringe becomes the kingmaker.
[C1.3] We have seen this movie elsewhere. Parliamentary systems frequently produce revolving cabinets, fragile majorities, and tiny extremist parties extracting outsized concessions for their support. Import that model to a closely divided House and every speakership fight, every debt ceiling, becomes a hostage negotiation where 5 percent of seats dictates to 95 percent. That is not broader representation; it is minority rule.
[C1.4] Ranked-choice voting promises majority winners and kinder campaigns but delivers complexity and distrust. Longer ballots, exhausted ballots that stop counting, days of algorithmic transfers, winners declared after second- and third-place math — that is a recipe for suspicion in a country already doubting election administration. Where tried, it has not ended bitter politics but added a bitter argument about the system itself.
[C1.5] Winner-take-all single-member districts have a democratic virtue reformers ignore: a named representative for a real place, a clear majority to govern, and a clear address for blame when it fails. Divided government is not proof the system is broken; it is proof voters are ambivalent and want brakes. Give clear winners, not permanent coalitions no one voted for.
