[P1.1] When you buy a phone or a laptop, you should own the right to keep it working—not merely the right to beg the manufacturer for permission. India should adopt a national right-to-repair law that requires makers of consumer electronics to supply parts, tools, and documentation. That reform restores consumer sovereignty, cuts waste, and opens a repair economy now forced to operate in the shadows.
[P1.2] The status quo is quiet extraction. Batteries are glued shut, screws are proprietary, diagnostics are locked, and spare parts are withheld or priced so replacement beats repair. A student whose laptop fails before exams, or a small trader whose phone dies in the middle of festival sales, faces a false choice: pay an authorized premium or buy new. Where device costs still strain household budgets, that outcome is corporate design, not consumer preference.
[P1.3] A statutory right to parts and manuals ends the lockout without banning innovation or authorized service.
[P1.4] The waste case is equally concrete. Devices discarded early become e-waste India already struggles to process. Repair is the highest-value form of reuse: it saves the energy, metals, and emissions embedded in a new unit, at the moment of failure rather than after the landfill. Access to documentation and tools makes that reuse reliable instead of improvised.
[P1.5] Independent repairers are not a threat to industry. They are the service network manufacturers have declined to build outside big cities. Legal parts and guides formalize skills that already exist, raise safety, and create jobs that cannot be offshored. Manufacturers keep the market for new devices and premium support.
[P1.6] They lose only the aftermarket monopoly.
[P1.7] Voluntary codes will not deliver this. Profit favors shorter replacement cycles. Only a national law, with clear duties on parts, tools, and documentation, can make repair a real option across states and product lines. Ownership should mean the ability to maintain what you paid for.

[C1.1] A national right-to-repair mandate does not restore ownership. It conscripts manufacturers into a nationwide spare-parts and secrets-distribution system. Buying a phone already gives you title to the device. It does not give you a legal claim on the factory’s inventory, proprietary tools, and internal documentation.
[C1.2] That distinction is the whole case.
[C1.3] The safety problem is not theoretical. Phones and laptops pack lithium cells, dense boards, and software that gates radios and personal data. Independent repair in India already runs on improvised parts. Forcing makers to feed that ecosystem with official tools and manuals does not create trained, insured technicians in every district.
[C1.4] It multiplies the ways a counterfeit battery or flashed firmware can fail, then leaves the brand holding blame when something burns or a device is bricked.
[C1.5] Documentation is worse than parts. Service manuals and diagnostic software protect designs and close security holes. A statutory duty to supply them is an invitation to cloners and attackers. Those files will not stay with a neighborhood shop; they circulate.
[C1.6] Costs follow at once. Long support windows for thousands of models mean warehouses, working capital, and liability. First-time buyers of budget devices pay those costs, not the minority seeking an independent battery swap. Smaller brands and importers raise prices, shrink catalogs, or withhold the latest hardware from India.
[C1.7] Authorized networks are how firms police fakes, ship firmware, and absorb warranty risk. Strip the aftermarket and still demand cheap Indian devices plus security patches, and you have wished away the model that makes those products affordable.
[C1.8] E-waste is not solved by a parts catalog. A new entry-level phone is often cheaper than skilled labor plus genuine parts, so grey components still win. India needs collection, recycling capacity, and real warranty enforcement—not a national law pretending a supply chain can be legislated into being. This mandate would raise prices, leak designs, and punish the very products households can actually afford.
