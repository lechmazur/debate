[P1.1] Japan and South Korea are effectively energy islands. Japan is an archipelago; South Korea is severed from continental grids by a hostile northern border. Neither possesses meaningful domestic fossil fuel reserves, forcing both to import the vast majority of their energy through vulnerable maritime choke points. When global fuel markets convulse, their trade balances crater, utility debt balloons, and households absorb painful price shocks.
[P1.2] Restarting idle reactors and building new capacity directly replaces volatile coal and liquefied natural gas shipments with energy-dense uranium that can be safely stockpiled on domestic soil for years.
[P1.3] Decarbonizing industrial titans also requires confronting physical geography. Both nations are densely populated and mountainous, with limited flat land for utility-scale solar and deep surrounding waters that complicate offshore wind. More importantly, semiconductor foundries, steelmakers, and precision manufacturers demand uninterrupted, around-the-clock baseload power that intermittent renewables cannot guarantee without prohibitively expensive storage and grid overhauls. [P1.4] Crucially, both countries already possess the reactors, engineering talent, and regulatory upgrades to execute this pivot safely. Japan has a large fleet of existing reactors that have undergone stringent post-Fukushima safety overhauls; bringing those paid-for assets back online is the fastest, cheapest carbon-reduction lever available.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, South Korea boasts one of the world’s most capable nuclear supply chains, routinely building advanced reactors on time and on budget. Leaving existing plants idle and halting new construction squanders immense sunk capital while tethering national survival to foreign fossil exporters. Restarting and expanding nuclear power is the only realistic path to secure, clean energy.
