[P1.1] Your birth certificate should not be a secret kept from you.
[P1.2] An adopted adult is the only person in society denied access to the basic facts of her own birth — her original name, where she was born, and who her parents are. That is not protection. That is state-imposed ignorance about one's own life, continuing long after any justification of child welfare expires at 18.
[P1.3] First, identity is foundational. Knowing where you come from shapes medical decisions, cultural belonging, and psychological wholeness. Adoptees describe years of blank forms at the doctor's office: heart disease? cancer? mental illness? Unknown.
[P1.4] Unknown. Unknown. No parent's desire for privacy can justify forcing another adult to make medical choices blind, or to wonder whether a chance encounter is with a sibling.
[P1.5] Second, no one owns anonymity against their own child. A birth parent made a difficult decision decades ago, often under pressure, poverty, or stigma. That deserves compassion, not a lifetime gag order enforced by the state. Learning a name does not compel a relationship.
[P1.6] Modern laws can pair disclosure with contact preferences — you may be told who I am, but please do not contact me — protecting safety without erasing truth.
[P1.7] Third, secrecy is already fiction. With affordable DNA testing and searchable databases, any determined adult can likely find biological relatives. The only question is whether the law forces that search to happen through unregulated surprises and traumatic ambushes, or through accurate, supported, truthful records.
[P1.8] Adulthood must mean informational adulthood. At 18 we grant the right to vote, marry, serve, and consent to surgery. It is perverse to say: you are mature enough to be responsible for your life, but not mature enough to know how it began.

[C1.3] A contact-preference form cannot restore privacy once a name is out. Social media, relatives, and a determined searcher do the rest. The person exposed does not get a vote.
[C1.4] Medical need does not require identity. Family history of heart disease, ancestry, and age can move in a record without a street address. Blank clinic forms are an argument for better non-identifying summaries, not for abolishing another adult's anonymity. A name does not treat cancer.
[C1.5] That DNA testing exists does not justify a legal right. This motion makes the state unmask the parent even over objection. Official records are more accurate—and more inescapable.
[C1.6] The claim that no one owns anonymity against their own child erases the legal bargain. Adoption is not delayed custody. It is a permanent transfer of parental rights, often given in exchange for secrecy. Learning a name does not force a relationship, but it does force an exposure.
[C1.7] An unconditional right also reshapes the next placement. If confidentiality cannot be trusted, some women will not use legal adoption at all. The people with the most to lose—young, poor, coerced, or in danger—are exactly those sealed records were built to protect.
[C1.8] Adulthood means you govern your own life. It does not mean the government must unmask another adult who kept a lawful promise you did not make. Identity hunger, however genuine, is not a superior legal claim.
