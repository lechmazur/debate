[P1.1] The crucial distinction is not between genetic medicine and genetic engineering, but between a change confined to one consenting patient and a change incorporated into the human reproductive lineage. Somatic editing can repair a serious disease in an adult who has knowingly consented. Heritable editing deliberately transmits the alteration to descendants who cannot consent and whose identities and opportunities are determined in substantial part by an irreversible genomic decision.
[P1.2] That asymmetry justifies a targeted ban on creating or reproductively using heritable edited embryos while leaving non-heritable therapies legal. Countries can still permit tightly reviewed laboratory study of embryo editing; what must be prohibited is turning an experimental intervention into a reproductive fait accompli. The issue is not that every future germline innovation is necessarily harmful. It is that the burden of proof is reversed: society should not expose children and later generations to a novel, permanent alteration unless the benefit is compelling, the risk is demonstrably manageable, and the intervention can meet a high medical-need threshold.
[P1.3] Such proof is especially demanding because germline editing is not a one-time dose. An unintended edit may be harmless in the first affected person yet become a population-level inheritance. Mosaicism, off-target changes, uncertain developmental effects, and differences in how variants affect people across environments make “probably safe” an inadequate standard. A child cannot opt out, and later parents cannot restore the family’s original genetic sequence.
[P1.4] Opponents will say a ban makes the technology less safe by driving it underground. But regulation does not require silence; it requires enforceable rules, registries, clinical oversight, and penalties for unauthorized reproductive use. A narrow ban also avoids collapsing into a broader prohibition on all genetic medicine, because patients seeking somatic treatment remain free to obtain it. That protects present patients without sacrificing future generations to experimentation.

A prohibition on creating edited embryos for reproduction doesn't eliminate the capability—it eliminates transparency. [C1.3] The families most affected are those carrying genes for devastating conditions like Huntington's disease, Tay-Sachs, or severe cystic fibrosis, where no somatic alternative exists and every child faces a coin flip of suffering or death. [C1.4] PRO frames this as consent—future generations can't consent. But we routinely make irreversible decisions for children: vaccination, surgery, dietary regulation, education. [C1.5] The real problem is not the science. It's that unilateral bans fragment oversight globally. If one country prohibits, others won't, and the result is exactly the regulatory vacuum PRO fears—just one PRO helped construct. International coordination on something this complex takes decades; meanwhile, patients with no options wait.
[C1.6] A ban treats the symptom while worsening the disease: less visibility, less accountability, and more inequality between nations that can offer the technology and those that cannot. The responsible path is rigorous, tiered regulation with international cooperation—not a prohibition that guarantees the worst of all worlds.

If the law cannot reliably separate “study” from “use,” the ban becomes a façade that legitimises secrecy rather than safety.
[C2.2] PRO also treats Huntington’s and Tay‑Sachs as outliers, arguing that few diseases justify the risk [P2.3]. But the number of families facing monogenic, lethal disorders is not negligible, and for many of them pre‑implantation genetic diagnosis is not an option because both parents are homozygous or because the mutation is de‑novo. That is a form of germline alteration performed without the future person’s consent, yet it is lawful. [C2.4] Finally, PRO says fragmentation does not vindicate unrestricted use [P2.5]; but a patchwork of bans creates precisely the vacuum they fear—nations that prohibit while others permit generate a marketplace of the desperate, deepening inequality. The responsible path is coordinated, transparent research, not a prohibition that guarantees the worst outcomes it claims to prevent.

Countries therefore need not identify the first embryo for which uncertainty becomes acceptable; they should prohibit reproductive creation until the evidence actually changes.
[P4.2] A2: Embryry selection and genome editing differ in the decisive respect: selection chooses among embryos with naturally occurring genomes, while editing deliberately introduces a new sequence into the reproductive lineage. Selection does not demonstrate that the embryo survives implantation, that the intended alteration has no unintended consequences, or that descendants will be unaffected. The fact that selection is lawful may expose inconsistency in current regulation, but it does not establish that experimental alteration is equally justified. Both involve absent consent; only editing asks society to permit an unvalidated genomic intervention.

It establishes that clinics must not create or implant edited embryos, while research can remain lawful, reviewable, and transparent.
[P4.4] The harder question is who bears the uncertainty. With somatic therapy, the patient may knowingly accept risk for an immediate benefit. With germline editing, an experimental burden is placed on a future person and later generations who receive neither benefit nor choice. If safety is unproven and alternatives remain available, a targeted reproductive ban is the more defensible allocation of that burden.
[P4.5] Allowing the procedure would not make the world safer merely because some clinics might operate illegally; it would give them lawful permission to experiment on descendants.
