[P1.1] Judging infrastructure by the upfront price of a tunnel-boring machine mistakes the visible invoice for the true cost. Across both mature metropolises and rapidly urbanizing nations, surface space is hitting a hard physical wall. Cities cannot keep widening roads, stringing high-voltage pylons through dense neighborhoods, or tearing open the same streets every few years for water, sewage, and power upgrades. Even while boring remains capital-intensive, heavy investment in underground transport and utility tunneling is increasingly indispensable, because on the surface, we have run out of cheap room and tolerable disruption.
[P1.2] First, land scarcity makes above-ground expansion either physically impossible or politically paralyzed. Building new rail lines, highways, or high-capacity transmission corridors through built-up areas requires costly property acquisition, forced displacements, and years of litigation that routinely stall projects or inflate budgets past underground alternatives. Worse, wide rights-of-way permanently sever neighborhoods and consume prime urban land better used for housing, parks, and commerce. Grade-separated subways and road tunnels move millions cleanly beneath existing street grids, delivering capacity that buses and streetcars, trapped in intersection traffic, simply cannot match as cities densify.
[P1.3] Second, staying at street level inflicts relentless economic and civic damage. Traditional open-trench utility work turns commercial arteries into construction zones, shuttering storefronts, snarling traffic, and forcing repeated excavation every time a pipe bursts or an electrifying economy demands heavier cables. Deep-bored lines and shared utility tunnels let cities install, inspect, and upgrade vital lifelines while daily life continues undisturbed above. Going underground also shields power and drainage trunks from extreme storms, heat, and wildfires that routinely cripple exposed networks.
[P1.4] Finally, treating tunneling as a rare luxury is precisely what keeps boring expensive. Sustained, heavy investment standardizes designs, builds skilled domestic workforces, and keeps machines digging rather than idling between bespoke megaprojects. Countries pay for growth and density one way or another: either upfront through disciplined underground investment, or forever through gridlock, torn-up streets, and strangled cities.

[C1.1] Surface scarcity is real, but it is not a mandate for heavy underground spending. If land is expensive and streets are congested, that makes capital discipline and sequencing more urgent, not less.
[C1.2] The core clash is opportunity cost. Tunneling is among the least reversible, least flexible infrastructure a society can buy. It concentrates geological, hydrological, and safety risk into a single corridor. Deep assets are impressive to open and punishing to alter: a weak seam, a misplaced utility, or a flood-prone aquifer can turn a project into a century of maintenance liability.
[C1.3] High boring costs are not simply a procurement problem to be absorbed by political will; they reflect scarce machinery, scarce specialist labor, and genuinely hostile uncertainty. PRO’s claim that sustained volume will make this cheap [P1.4] assumes learning curves where geology, station design, and utility interfaces remain stubbornly bespoke.
[C1.4] “Heavy” investment also means choosing tunnels by default. Many transport problems can be relieved far more cheaply through signal priority, dedicated bus corridors, fare integration, land-use reform, and better use of existing rail alignments. Many utility upgrades need targeted microtunnels or coordinated shallow work, not a national program of deep shared galleries. Boston’s buried Central Artery shows the danger: relief was needed, but the chosen scale left debt, leaks, and decades of upkeep rather than a template every city should copy.
[C1.5] Burying lifelines can help, but it also creates new exposure—flooding, fire, evacuation, pumping, and ventilation—that surface systems do not require in the same form.
[C1.6] The right rule is surgical, not heavy: bore where surface alternatives are physically or politically impossible and lifecycle costs still win. Underground space is a precision instrument. PRO wants it as the default foundation of urban policy—expensive to pour, and even more expensive to repair.
