[P1.1] The fundamental promise of a competitive housing market is that rival landlords must compete on price. When competitors outsource their pricing to a shared algorithmic tool, that promise is broken. This software does not merely analyze public trends; it pools sensitive, non-public data—like real-time occupancy and actual lease transactions—from direct competitors. By feeding private data into a single, shared engine, landlords achieve the exact same economic outcome as a traditional price-fixing cartel.
[P1.2] Under established antitrust principles, if these competitors met in a backroom and hired a human intermediary to collect their private data and recommend uniform pricing, it would be prosecuted as an illegal "hub-and-spoke" conspiracy. Substituting a human with an algorithm does not change the economic reality. It merely automates the collusion.
[P1.3] Treating algorithmic rent-setting with the same scrutiny as direct price-fixing is essential for three reasons. First, it closes a dangerous technological loophole. If the law fails to adapt, competitors in any industry could legally coordinate prices simply by licensing the same software. Second, the shared algorithm actively suppresses competition.
[P1.4] It instructs landlords to accept lower occupancy rates and keep units vacant rather than discounting rents, artificially inflating housing costs across entire markets. Third, it restores the legal focus to substance over form.
[P1.5] When competitors share a common pricing brain, they are no longer competing. If an action uses the same shared-agent mechanism and inflicts the same collusive harm on consumers as a traditional cartel, it must face the exact same level of antitrust scrutiny. We must not allow software to shield collusion.

[C1.2] PRO's entire case rests on one move: treating independent adoption of a common tool as an agreement to fix prices. Antitrust law has always distinguished the two. Landlords who separately decide the same software makes them money have agreed on nothing. They don't know whose data trained the model, and they override its recommendations daily.
[C1.3] That is parallel conduct plus a vendor—never per se illegal, because punishing it punishes every firm that watches its competitors. Even the vacancy claim proves too much: holding out for a higher rent is unilateral revenue management. Hotels and airlines do it with no competitor involved at all.
[C1.4] The hub-and-spoke analogy collapses too. A rimless wheel is not a conspiracy: prosecutions require proof that each spoke agreed with the hub knowing the others did likewise—a commitment to adhere, not a subscription. PRO says the backroom version "would be prosecuted," but the agreement is what makes that true, not the intermediary's species. An algorithm cannot supply a meeting of minds.
[C1.5] Now weigh what "as if coordinating directly" actually means: per se condemnation, no efficiency defense, no inquiry into whether rents even rose. That net catches ordinary benchmarking across the economy—airlines, hotels, and grocers all run on shared analytics. [C1.6] If the genuine concern is pooling rivals' real-time lease data, regulate exactly that—case-by-case, on evidence of agreement or demonstrated harm. Existing antitrust already reaches true conspiracies conducted through software. It must never declare that renting a calculator is the same crime as shaking hands.
