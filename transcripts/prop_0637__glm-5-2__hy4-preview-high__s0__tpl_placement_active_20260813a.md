[P1.1] The strongest argument for by-right densification near transit is that the alternative—the status quo—doesn't prevent displacement. It just makes displacement invisible, slow, and permanent.
[P1.2] When municipalities require discretionary review for every new housing project, the practical result is that very little gets built. Supply stays constricted. Rents rise. People get displaced—not through demolition or eviction, but through the quiet grind of rising market rents that no headline captures.
[P1.3] This is displacement without a paper trail, and it is already happening in every major metro with restrictive zoning near transit.
[P1.4] By-right densification changes the equation. When builders can add housing as-of-right, supply responds to demand. More units near transit mean more people can live where the jobs and services are without a car, without an hour-long commute, without paying a premium for artificial scarcity. The evidence from cities that have allowed meaningful density near transit is clear: rents in the broader market soften relative to what they would otherwise be.
[P1.5] That is the structural anti-displacement mechanism.
[P1.6] Yes, specific buildings may be redeveloped. Specific tenants may face relocation. That risk is real and deserves mitigation—through tenant protections, relocation assistance, and inclusionary requirements layered onto by-right approval. But the question isn't whether short-term displacement risks exist.
[P1.7] It's whether municipalities should perpetuate a system that guarantees long-term, systemic displacement of far more people in order to protect the few who happen to live in buildings that might be redeveloped.
[P1.8] The opposition's case depends on a false stability. Restrictive zoning doesn't freeze communities in place; it freezes them out. It turns transit-adjacent land into low-density enclaves for those who arrived first, while everyone else gets pushed to the periphery—far from transit, far from opportunity, and paying more for the privilege.
[P1.9] By-right densification near transit is the single most effective tool municipalities have to make housing abundance a rule rather than an exception. The short-term risks are manageable. The long-term cost of inaction is not.

[C1.1] PRO offers a trade: accept short-term displacement now to unlock long-term regional affordability. Reject it. The displacement is concrete and immediate; the affordability is diffuse and speculative.
[C1.2] Start with what 'by-right' does: it removes discretionary review. PRO says mitigation can be 'layered onto by-right approval' ([P1.6]). But once you condition approval on relocation help, deep affordability, or one-for-one replacement, you reintroduce the discretion and delay PRO's supply argument depends on escaping. The motion's version is genuinely by-right: fast, predictable, and stripped of local leverage that could protect tenants.
[C1.3] Short-term displacement is not a side effect; it is the output.
[C1.4] And the risk is not minor. Transit-adjacent blocks often hold the last naturally affordable, car-free housing in a region. When zoning flips by-right, land values jump, small landlords sell, and tenants face rent spikes or demolition before new units open. Picture an aging 12-unit building near a station: modest rents, a home health aide and a retired couple inside.
[C1.5] By-right rules let a developer replace it with 60 market-rate apartments. Even with a modest inclusionary set-aside, the city loses twelve affordable homes to gain about six. The tenants are pushed to the periphery; the regional rent effect is a rounding error. PRO calls this a 'structural anti-displacement mechanism' ([P1.5]), but for those households it is expulsion.
[C1.6] The wider supply argument is oversold. New market-rate units near transit may modestly slow regional rent growth at the margin, but filtering takes decades, and rising amenity value can push local rents up in the station area itself. A displaced family cannot be compensated with a statistical future dip in the metro rent index.
[C1.7] So the clash is not nostalgia versus abundance. It is this: municipalities should legalize more housing, but not by-right when that machinery knowingly sacrifices existing vulnerable residents first. Densification near transit is worth pursuing; sacrificing those communities to get it is not.

[P2.2] Builders know the rules before they file. No hearing, no veto, no delay. The opponent presents a false binary: unchecked demolition or discretionary gridlock. Municipalities can and do write binding tenant protections into by-right frameworks without surrendering predictability.

Land values rise, owners raise rents or convert, and tenants leave one by one—no demolition required, no headline, no displacement count. The status quo displaces the same households, just slowly enough that no one tallies it.
[P2.4] That is the displacement PRO identified in the opening, and CON still hasn't answered it.
[P2.5] The supply skepticism ([C1.6]) misframes the mechanism. New transit-adjacent units don't only filter down decades later—they absorb demand that would otherwise bid up existing rents throughout the station area. Every new unit near transit is a household not competing for the aging building two blocks away. The regional effect isn't a rounding error when multiplied across every station in a network.
[P2.6] The real question isn't whether displacement occurs under by-right rules. It's whether municipalities prefer visible, addressable displacement they can mitigate through objective code requirements—or invisible, unmitigated displacement baked into scarcity. CON calls the alternative "legalizing more housing" without by-right, but that path preserves the discretionary system that produced the crisis. You cannot fix exclusion with the machinery that built it.
