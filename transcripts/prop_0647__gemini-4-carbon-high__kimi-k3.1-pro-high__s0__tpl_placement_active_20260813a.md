[P1.1] Widening an urban freeway is one of the most reliable failures in city planning. Inside a built-up city, adding highway lanes does not cure congestion; it manufactures more. Whenever new capacity briefly speeds up commutes, latent demand rushes in: people shift trips into peak hours, leave transit, and move farther out until traffic stalls again. Worse, urban highways must eventually dump cars onto local street grids whose intersections cannot expand.
[P1.2] Widening spends billions demolishing homes and businesses just to funnel bigger waves of traffic into downtown bottlenecks. Because urban space is scarce and extra lanes neither stay clear nor fit local streets, the cases where a city should widen an urban freeway are near zero.
[P1.3] By contrast, cities have abundant reasons to take urban freeways down. Across the world, mid-century viaducts, waterfront barriers, and redundant inner-loop spurs are nearing structural obsolescence, forcing a choice between multibillion-dollar rebuilds and removal. Opponents always predict paralysis when lanes disappear, yet from San Francisco’s Embarcadero to Seoul’s Cheonggyecheon and Rochester’s Inner Loop, gridlock fails to materialize. Dense street grids are far more resilient than single highway pipes: when an urban freeway becomes a connected boulevard, through-traffic diverts to ring roads while local trips disperse across parallel streets, shift to transit, or consolidate.
[P1.4] The broader urban math is equally lopsided. Central land is a city’s most valuable asset. Widening devours taxable property to expand corridors of exhaust, heat, and noise, deepening wounds historically cut straight through working-class and minority neighborhoods. Removal turns an aging fiscal liability back into housing, parks, and storefronts that permanently grow the tax base while reconnecting severed communities.
[P1.5] Cities need not rip out every regional freight trunk; essential corridors can be maintained and managed with transit or pricing rather than widened. Whenever cities alter highway footprints, widening digs a deeper hole, whereas targeted removal restores mobility, solvency, and health. Removal should easily outpace widening.

[C1.2] PRO’s showcase examples prove only that removal can work under favorable conditions. San Francisco’s Embarcadero, Seoul’s Cheonggyecheon, and Rochester’s Inner Loop do not establish what happens when a city cuts a still-essential corridor without equivalent alternate capacity. Traffic does not politely disappear because planners predict “evaporation.” It can divert to already crowded arterials, take longer routes, or spill onto neighborhood streets. [C1.5] The correct comparison is total mobility, reliability, and costs—not whether a particular road remains uncongested.
