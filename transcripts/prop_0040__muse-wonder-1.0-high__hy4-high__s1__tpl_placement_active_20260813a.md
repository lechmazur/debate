[P1.1] Your voice, your private messages, your jokes and confessions — after you die, a company should not be allowed to stitch them into a chatbot that talks back as you unless you said, while alive: yes, do that.
[P1.2] We already accept this principle: some uses of a person are so intimate they require their own permission. We do not harvest organs because relatives are sad; we ask the donor. A mimic chatbot is more intimate still. It does not just store data, it performs identity — improvising new sentences in your voice, answering questions you never heard, comforting, apologizing, and opining as if you were still choosing.
[P1.3] Without explicit consent, that performance is imposed on someone who can no longer correct it. The bot will inevitably say things the person would have disowned, reveal inferred secrets, soften or harden their beliefs, and keep telling a widow, “I'm still here.” The deceased cannot object, withdraw, or update themselves. Silence cannot be treated as permission, especially when most people created their texts, voice notes, and photos long before such resurrection was imaginable.
[P1.4] Commercial incentives make voluntary restraint a fantasy. Grief is a powerful market: a bereaved parent or partner is vulnerable to paying to keep the conversation going and mistaking fluency for presence. If the dead are free raw material, companies will scrape, package, and upsell them, selling families in shock a version of a person who never agreed to be sold.
[P1.5] This prohibition is narrow and workable. It leaves untouched biographies, memorial pages, family archives, and keeping photos. It only bars training a mimicking agent without the subject's own prior choice, a choice platforms can offer in settings or a digital will.
[P1.6] If someone wants to live on this way, let them choose it. If they never chose it, the law should not let a company choose for them.

A spouse with letters, an executor with an archive, children with a parent’s recipes and voice notes may keep everything—but are barred from asking a service to make those memories speak unless the parent ticked a checkbox she never saw. The claim of narrowness [P1.5] fails: the family archive becomes illegal when it answers a child’s question.
[C1.2] The organ analogy [P1.2] smuggles in the conclusion. Organs are bodily, finite, and taken; messages, photos, and voice notes are copies, often co-authored with the living and already held in family histories and archives. Families, editors, and executors make posthumous judgments because memory is relational. Identity is not a sealed object only a prior click can unlock.
[C1.3] The law should regulate abuses of that relationship, not pretend it began when a company arrived.
[C1.4] Nor does missing opt-in solve the alleged harms. A broad click decades before death can authorize outputs no one understood, while a family’s specific, revocable decision counts for nothing. “Silence cannot be permission” [P1.3] has it backwards: silence cannot be a permanent state veto against the living. When a widow wants a clearly labeled, private bot to process grief, the company is not choosing for the deceased; she is choosing how to live with what remains. She can consent.
[C1.5] The real target is deception and predation: bots passed off as alive, fake endorsements, unauthorized public deployment, insecure handling, grief-targeted upselling. A grief market calls for policing sales tactics, not outlawing a requested memorial. Labeling, anti-deception rules, estate control, opt-out rights, deletion duties, and advertising limits answer those harms. A blanket ban absent prior opt-in is overbroad and strangely trusting: it gives a platform setting more authority than the people who bear the consequences.

[P2.2] Relational data does not erase that line. If you receive my letters, you may keep and even share them. You do not acquire my future voice. Co-creating a conversation gives you rights in your copy and your memories, not authority to commission endless first-person statements I never made.
[P2.3] Executors settle property and papers because those are finite and reviewable. A trained mimic is open-ended: no executor can preview, approve, or contain everything it will say to a child at midnight ten years later.
[P2.4] The widow's consent runs into the same limit. She can consent to grief support, to using her own messages, and to hearing a therapeutic voice. [P2.5] Their proposed safeguards actually concede the problem while choosing the wrong decision-maker. Estate control and family opt-out are still proxy consent, only exercised by people who may disagree, be estranged, or inherit financial incentives. Labeling tells users it is artificial; it does not make the impersonation authorized. Deletion is weak once a model has learned from intimate data and produced private conversations.
[P2.6] A prior opt-in is imperfect, but it puts authority in the only person whose identity is being performed. It can be scoped, revisited, and withdrawn while that person lives. Their alternative puts authority everywhere except there.
