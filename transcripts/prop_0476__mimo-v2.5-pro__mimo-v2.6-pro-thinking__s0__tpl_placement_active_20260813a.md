[P1.1] Low Earth orbit is becoming a junkyard, and the people filling it have no financial incentive to clean up after themselves.
[P1.2] When a satellite operator launches a spacecraft and then fails to deorbit it safely at end of life, the resulting debris threatens every other user of that orbital regime. [P1.3] Deorbit bonds fix this misalignment. By requiring operators to post a financial guarantee that is forfeited if their spacecraft is not properly retired, we force each company to internalize the true lifecycle cost of their asset. [P1.4] Construction firms post performance bonds. The principle is identical: if your project creates a future liability, you secure funds against that liability before you begin.
[P1.5] The current regulatory landscape is failing. Guidelines suggest deorbiting within twenty-five years of end of life, but compliance is inconsistent and enforcement is weak, especially across jurisdictions. [P1.6] Companies that plan to deorbit properly face minimal cost—they simply recover their bond. Companies that cut corners on propulsion or passivation systems face a real financial deterrent.
[P1.7] Critically, this is the least intrusive approach available. It does not ban launches. It does not impose prescriptive engineering requirements. It lets operators choose their own deorbit methods and timeline, as long as they succeed.

[C1.2] Start with the sizing problem. A deorbit bond must be large enough to fund remediation decades in the future, in an industry where launch costs, removal technology, and even currency values shift dramatically over a satellite's lifetime. Set it too low and it is a meaningless speed bump—cheaper to forfeit than to build reliable disposal systems. Set it too high and you lock capital out of the market for a fifteen-year asset life.
[C1.3] There is no correct number, and PRO has not offered one. That is not a minor drafting detail; it is the mechanism's core, and it does not work.
[C1.4] Second, forfeiture does not clean up orbit. Money paid to a government after a satellite is stranded does not conjure a servicer vehicle. Active debris removal remains technically hard and expensive; a treasury deposit does not deorbit anything. Compare this to the alternative the motion does not require us to reject: launch licensing that mandates disposal capability as a condition of approval, backed by mission-extension or removal-service contingency plans.
[C1.5] Prevent failure ex ante rather than fine it ex post. PRO calls bonds "least intrusive," but a rule that simply refuses licenses to operators without a credible disposal plan is both simpler and actually preventive.
[C1.6] Third, bonds punish the wrong actors and freeze the market. A startup constellation operator must post large sums upfront, while well-capitalized incumbents barely notice. That is a barrier to entry dressed up as environmental policy. Worse, most deorbit failures are technical or accidental—thruster failures, premature end-of-life, collisions not caused by the operator.
[C1.7] A blanket forfeiture rule taxes misfortune and negligence identically, which is both unjust and a poor incentive signal.
[C1.8] Finally, PRO's enforcement argument collapses across borders. A bond required by one licensing state is avoidable by registering elsewhere—exactly the flag-shopping problem PRO admits exists today. The bond does not fix jurisdiction; it just adds cost where compliance was already likely.
