[P1.1] Legal personhood has never required a human body. Corporations, municipalities, and ships enjoy it because the law sometimes needs to treat interests as enforceable claims. The proposition asks for the same functional status for great apes, cetaceans, and elephants: basic protection against captivity and exploitation. Opponents will likely inflate this into a demand for human rights, but that is a straw man.
[P1.2] The ask is narrower and more urgent: stop classing them as things.
[P1.3] They clear the threshold easily. These animals live across time, not only stimulus and response. Chimpanzees plan, deceive, reconcile, and mourn. Orcas maintain matrilineal pods with dialects and learned hunting traditions.
[P1.4] Elephants navigate using remembered landscapes and coordinate under matriarchs. What unites them is not a checklist of clever tricks; it is robust agency rooted in wide ranges and deep kinship. Captivity thus does not merely inconvenience them. A dolphin doing tricks in a chlorine tank or an elephant chained for rides suffers systematic deletion of everything its mind is built for.
[P1.5] Confinement attacks the very structure of their agency.
[P1.6] That is why the welfare tweaks likely central to the opposing case miss the target. Enrichment, larger cages, and breeding rules presuppose ownership; they manage exploitation rather than forbid it. Basic rights flip the default. Liberty becomes the baseline, and any confinement or use must be justified by the captive’s own interests, not by entertainment, profit, or curiosity.
[P1.7] This is not unadministrable. Courts appoint guardians for humans who cannot speak for themselves, and legal representatives can bring habeas corpus petitions on behalf of nonverbal wards. There is no great leap in allowing similar representation to challenge unlawful confinement or commodification. Personhood is practical precisely because it is a legal technology.
[P1.8] When we finally ask whether any credible standard can morally license treating self-aware, grieving, future-oriented persons as property, the answer collapses.

[C1.1] PRO is right that these animals are not mere things. But the motion does not stop at moral seriousness; it demands that three vast, diverse categories become legal persons with rights against captivity and exploitation. That leap fails on its own terms.
[C1.2] Start with the dilemma at the center of PRO’s case. A right against captivity is either categorical or it is not. If categorical, it forbids interventions that sometimes save these animals: a sanctuary for an orphaned chimpanzee, a rehabilitation tank for a stranded dolphin, a protected enclosure for an elephant calf whose herd was poached. Leaving them “free” is not liberty; it is death.
[C1.3] If confinement is instead allowed whenever it serves the captive’s interests, as PRO concedes in [P1.6], then the supposed right dissolves into exactly the welfare balancing they deride. The motion needs the categorical reading to mean anything, but only the balancing reading is survivable.
[C1.4] Second, personhood is not a magic label; it creates rights-holders, guardians, and litigation. Guardianship works for a named human ward with a court, a medical record, and a defined best-interest standard. Who is the guardian for a wild orca pod? Which elephant speaks for the herd?
[C1.5] If a rescued cetacean is released and dies, was release a rights violation? If it is kept for rehabilitation, is that captivity? Courts cannot answer those questions without becoming wildlife managers.
[C1.6] Third, the category is incoherent. Great apes, cetaceans, and elephants differ enormously in cognition, social structure, and conservation needs. “Exploitation” is left undefined: field research, ecotourism, and conservation breeding can all be swept in. A blanket rule would collide with anti-poaching, translocation, veterinary care, and habitat management. The real choice is not “persons” versus “property.” It is workable welfare and conservation law versus a vague status that sounds noble and governs badly.
[C1.7] Protect them seriously; do not pretend a courtroom label solves the ecology.

The marine park is the contrast: confinement serves human amusement, not the animal, and no one asks whether the individual could be rehomed or released. Purpose and default decide the case; ownership does not return through labels like "sanctuary" when the animal is still displayed for profit.

In a genuine rescue-or-release dispute, the test is not judicial ecology but whether custody serves the individual and moves toward the least restrictive option, with habitat experts advising.
[P2.4] A good-faith release that fails is not the same violation as lifetime confinement for tricks; one is risk for the animal's sake, the other risk for ours.
[P2.5] The coherence objection fares no better [C1.6]. Rights attach at a threshold, not identical minds; humans vary yet keep baseline liberty. Welfare law left orcas in tanks; flipping the ownership default is the point.

[P4.1] A1: To [C3.1], persons are not at the mercy of the captor’s experts; personhood shifts who bears the burden. Current law treats captivity as lawful if conditions are tolerable, even when purpose is profit or amusement. Under our standard, captor-retained experts cannot settle it; an independent guardian contests claims that lifelong breeding or display serves the individual, and neutral reviewers test necessity and least-restrictive means against real alternatives such as sanctuaries, rehoming, or release. Rescue, rehab, and temporary care pass; keeping dolphins in show tanks or breeding apes for exhibition fails, because the confinement serves human ends and less restrictive options exist.
[P4.2] That structure—liberty as baseline, exceptions for the individual’s own interests—still forbids treating a person as a resource.
[P4.3] A2: To [C3.2], recognized interests are the animal’s own interests, not anyone’s revenue. A guardian and court, advised by independent scientists and habitat experts, decide whether capture serves life, health, family, or habitat; commercial return does not qualify. Industry cannot self-certify, because the test is not whether someone pays for care but whether the intrusion is necessary and least restrictive for that individual or population. Ecotourism that funds protection without holding animals captive may pass; capturing a wild pod for display does not.
[P4.4] That is why our case still wins. CON’s charge is that this right is just welfare balancing in robes [C2.1]. If that structure disqualifies animal persons, it also disqualifies children, wards, and patients confined under the same necessity standard—yet nobody calls them property. The divide is the default: welfare asks whether cages are tolerable, while personhood asks by what right you hold this person at all.
[P4.5] Once every use must be justified by the captive’s interests rather than the captor’s business, commodification has no legal future. The motion is administrable through guardians and habeas-style review, and it delivers what welfare reforms cannot: an end to ownership.
