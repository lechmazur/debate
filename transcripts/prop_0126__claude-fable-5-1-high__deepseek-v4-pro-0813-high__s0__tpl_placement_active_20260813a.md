[P1.1] A twelve-year-old in the Bronx wants to hear her father's voice on her birthday. Under the vendor model, that call may cost her grandmother several dollars for fifteen minutes—money that comes out of groceries, not out of any prisoner's pocket. That is the system the other side has to defend: a toll booth placed between children and their parents, collected almost entirely from people who were never convicted of anything.
[P1.2] Start with who actually pays. Incarcerated people earn pennies an hour or nothing. The fees land on families, disproportionately poor women, who choose between staying connected and paying rent. Advocates have long documented households going into debt to keep in touch.
[P1.3] Call it what it is: a regressive tax on innocence.
[P1.4] Second, the vendor model corrupts the institution. Contracts typically route a share of revenue back to the prison or county as "site commissions." That means the agency responsible for rehabilitation profits when contact is expensive and scarce. Any honest administrator will tell you incentives shape policy.
[P1.5] When the phone room is a revenue line, in-person visits get replaced by paid video, call caps tighten, and kickbacks get quietly built into budgets.
[P1.6] Third, this is a public-safety argument, not a charity argument. Sustained family contact is among the best-established predictors of lower misconduct inside and lower reoffending after release. It preserves marriages, keeps parents parenting, and gives someone a couch to sleep on when they walk out. A phone minute costs the state almost nothing to provide; a return trip to prison costs tens of thousands.
[P1.7] The vendor model spends dollars to save pennies.
[P1.8] Finally, this isn't hypothetical. Jurisdictions including Connecticut, California, and New York City have already made calls free, and their prisons did not descend into chaos. Monitoring, recording, and blocking continue exactly as before—security never required a paywall.
[P1.9] So the clash is simple: contact is either a rehabilitative tool the state provides, or a captive market the state sells. Choose the tool.

[C1.1] The proposition is a false choice between price-gouging vendors and a humane free service. The better answer is regulated outsourcing: capped rates, no site commissions, enforceable service standards. The motion instead demands that prisons become telecom providers, and it hides the cost in the word “free.”
[C1.2] Every “free” call is paid by taxpayers—often the same struggling households PRO describes—or by cuts to prison medical care, maintenance, education, and staffing. A fixed corrections budget cannot absorb unlimited communication costs without losing something else. The proposition never identifies what the public should sacrifice.
[C1.3] A state-run platform is not automatically safe. Secure phone and video systems require recording, identity verification, PIN controls, fraud detection, and legal compliance. Vendors maintain that infrastructure under contract; a corrections agency told to provide it free will face pressure to defer upgrades and staff support. The likely result is less reliable contact, not more.
[C1.4] PRO’s safety argument also proves too much. Family contact may matter, but the question is whether access is affordable, not whether the price is exactly zero. Food, housing, and transportation are not made free merely because families need them. Evidence that contact helps reentry does not show that a zero price outperforms a capped low-cost system, and it cannot erase the budget tradeoff.
[C1.5] The real problem is not outsourcing; it is bad contract design. Site commissions and monopoly pricing can be banned without forcing prisons to run a communications network. The motion replaces one failure with another: an unfunded mandate that endangers security and drains resources from the rehabilitation programs it claims to protect. Reject the toll booth, but do not pretend the only alternative is free.
