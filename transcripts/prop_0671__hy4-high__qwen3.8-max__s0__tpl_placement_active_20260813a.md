[P1.1] The decisive comparison is timing, not technology: K–12 systems should sequence tools by developmental purpose. Early grades should build attention, language, handwriting, number sense, and persistence before children are handed an interface that rewards speed. Paper is slow, physical, and single-purpose: print books, notebooks, pencils, paper math, and teacher-led demonstration should be the default, with devices used briefly only when they clearly serve the lesson. Device-light is not anti-tablet; it is foundations first, tools second.
[P1.2] A page does not flash a badge, autocorrect an error before it is noticed, or open ten tabs. Forming letters, drawing a number line, crossing out a wrong sum, and revising a sentence by hand require fine-motor control, memory, sequencing, and sustained gaze. A first grader tracing “b” and “d,” saying the sound, and writing it in a notebook gets richer feedback than tapping a multiple-choice answer. When school time is scarce, screen minutes displace that slow practice.
[P1.3] The usual defenses of early one-to-one devices misfire. Basic operation is easy to learn later; judging sources, composing sustained arguments, and resisting distraction depend on maturity and strong offline literacy. Adaptive apps can keep a child busy and produce quick scores, but they can turn confusion into hints and guessing into apparent progress. The child who can listen, copy, explain a mistake aloud, and finish a page will use a tablet as an instrument.
[P1.4] The tablet-first child is trained to need it.
[P1.5] Equity also favors paper-first. Affluent families can buy books, tutoring, and supervised off-screen time; schools should guarantee print-rich, adult-guided practice, not another interface. A device-light table keeps faces, voices, and hands visible: a teacher can see pencil grip, hesitation, erasure, and a wandering eye, while an app mostly reports answers. Later one-to-one tablets remain fully available for research, drafts, simulations, and feedback.
[P1.6] Why spend scarce developmental years optimizing children for interfaces before they master the slower human capacities those interfaces amplify?

[C1.1] The proposition treats paper as a moral good and tablets as a contaminant, but early learning is not won by the least flashing object. It is won by instruction that matches the child’s need. A blanket device-light policy and paper-first default makes that harder.
[C1.2] First, paper cannot individualize at scale. In an early-grade classroom, children arrive with different vocabularies, language backgrounds, motor skills, and reading readiness. A tablet with audio support, adjustable text, speech-to-text, translation, and immediate corrective feedback gives a struggling reader repeated practice without singling her out. For a child with dyslexia, audio-backed text is not a shortcut; it is access.
[C1.3] A child who cannot yet grip a pencil can still compose ideas by speaking and seeing words appear. PRO’s “rich feedback” from tracing letters is real, but it is only one kind of feedback, and for some children it is the slowest route to the goal.
[C1.4] Second, early guided technology use is how schools teach responsible use. If the concern is distraction, the answer is not to postpone devices until later grades, when habits are harder to form and later availability only postpones the novelty. It is to use short, teacher-directed tasks: record a sentence, photograph a block tower, listen to a read-aloud, or practice phonemes with an app requiring spoken responses. Children learn boundaries when use is brief and purposeful, not when the device appears suddenly in fourth grade as a testing portal.
[C1.5] Third, the equity argument cuts the other way. Affluent families often provide supervised, enriching technology at home. Low-income students may not. If school delays devices, those children get less guided access, not more.
[C1.6] A classroom with tablets can close that gap, offer digital books and language exposure, and let teachers see progress quickly.
[C1.7] Paper belongs in early grades, but so do well-chosen digital tools. The motion privileges one medium over children’s varied needs.
