[P1.1] Land-value taxation wins on both axes named in this motion—housing supply and local public finance—because it targets the one input economists agree is inelastic: land itself. You cannot make more land by taxing it lightly, but you can absolutely make more housing.
[P1.2] Start with supply. An LVT falls on the unimproved value of a parcel, not on what you build on it. A vacant downtown lot and a fully built twelve-story apartment building sitting on equally valuable land pay the same tax. That is the entire mechanism: it punishes idle land and rewards development, because the owner who does nothing still owes the bill while the owner who builds captures rental income to cover it.
[P1.3] Transaction taxes do the opposite—stamp duties and transfer taxes are only triggered by a sale, so the safest strategy is to sit on land and do nothing, since holding is free and building raises the eventual tax bill on transfer. That is why land-banking around growing cities is such a persistent problem: current tax structures make patience profitable.
[P1.4] Rent caps attack the wrong variable entirely. They suppress the price landlords receive without touching the cost or incentive to build, so new construction and maintenance both dry up—owners convert units, defer repairs, or exit the rental market, shrinking supply exactly where demand is highest.
[P1.5] Now public finance. LVT revenue tracks land value, which rises when a city adds transit, schools, and parks—so the tax captures value the public itself created, funding further investment in a virtuous loop. Transaction taxes are volatile, cyclical, and shrink precisely when governments need revenue most, during downturns when sales dry up. Rent caps do worse: by suppressing market rents they suppress assessed property values too, quietly eroding the property tax base that funds the very municipal services tenants rely on.
[P1.6] One mechanism grows the pie; the other two just fight over slices while shrinking it.
