[P1.1] Violent crime is not spread evenly across a city—it clusters. Criminologists have found repeatedly that a small fraction of street segments, often a few percent of all addresses, generates the majority of shootings and robberies. That single fact decides this debate, because it tells us where the leverage is: at places, before the trigger is pulled, not in a courtroom years afterward.
[P1.2] Hot spot policing works through the mechanism deterrence research actually supports: certainty of apprehension. Decades of evidence—including randomized experiments in cities like Minneapolis and Philadelphia—show that concentrating visible, lawful police presence on high-violence blocks reduces crime there, and the feared "displacement" mostly doesn't happen; benefits often diffuse to surrounding areas instead. Offenders respond to the immediate, visible probability of getting caught tonight.
[P1.3] Longer sentences for repeat offenders, by contrast, fail on their own logic. First, deterrence: would-be shooters rarely calculate marginal years; severity increases have consistently shown weak or no deterrent effect compared to certainty. Second, incapacitation: criminal careers peak in the late teens and twenties and decline sharply with age. Adding years to a repeat offender's sentence mostly buys incarceration during the years he was already aging out of violence—paying maximum cost for minimum prevention.
[P1.4] Third, the policy only operates after victims have already been created, often multiple times. It is structurally reactive.
[P1.5] The motion specifies tight accountability, and that matters. The legitimate objection to place-based policing—that it can curdle into indiscriminate stop-and-frisk—is precisely what accountability constraints address: data-tracked stops, supervision, community oversight. With those guardrails, hot spot policing preserves community trust, which itself increases witness cooperation and clearance rates, compounding the violence reduction.
[P1.6] So the comparison is stark: a strategy that prevents shootings where they actually occur, validated by experimental evidence, versus one that arrives after the harm, deters almost no one, and warehouses men past their dangerous years at enormous expense. Prevention beats punishment on every metric the motion asks about.
