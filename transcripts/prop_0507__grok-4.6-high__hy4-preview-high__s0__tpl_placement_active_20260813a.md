[P1.1] The case is simple. Countries should favor demand-side industrial policy because batteries and clean tech succeed when they are bought, used, and improved at scale—not when a treasury underwrites a particular plant. Producer subsidies pick locations and firms. Demand-side policy picks the outcome that compounds: deployment.
[P1.2] Cost declines follow learning curves. Volume of packs in cars, storage on grids, and inverters on roofs teaches the supply chain to cut materials, yield, and labor. That volume is created by buyers. Tax credits, fleet procurement, charging build-out, and performance standards give firms a contestable market.
[P1.3] A factory grant gives one sponsor a balance-sheet gift. Weak offtake leaves stranded capacity. Strong demand still draws private investment—without the state choosing the firm or process that will be obsolete next cycle.
[P1.4] Batteries especially punish picking winners. Chemistries, cell formats, and manufacturing processes turn over quickly. A large CAPEX subsidy locks public money into last year’s process at last year’s site. Demand policy lets the next cheaper pack win the same buyer pool.
[P1.5] That is how costs actually fall.
[P1.6] Large producer subsidies concentrate rents. A handful of sites capture the money, then lobby for local-content shields and larger top-ups. The result is a subsidy race, duplicated lines, and periodic gluts—solar already showed the glut when every government paid to build. Demand-side tools spread benefits across households, utilities, and fleets, which is harder to capture and better aligned with climate goals.
[P1.7] Industry still grows by selling products that work.
[P1.8] Production grants invite retaliation and copycat gifts. A credit for a working battery or vehicle is harder to attack as pure protection. Supply security still matters, but offtake contracts, standards, and diversified procurement beat blank checks that make politics hostage to a few plants.
[P1.9] Public money is scarce. Spend it moving technology into service. Use is what drives cost, reliability, and real comparative advantage.

[C1.1] The motion asks governments to favor demand-side policy over large producer subsidies. That inverts reality. Demand does not pull capital-intensive supply into existence on command; it often just pays for imports.
[C1.2] PRO says clean tech succeeds when it is 'bought, used, and improved at scale' [P1.1]. For batteries, the improving happens inside the factory, not the driveway. A gigafactory is billions in upfront CAPEX, multi-year siting, permitting, and supplier co-investment. A buyer tax credit is a flow subsidy that says 'go shop.'
[C1.3] If no domestic line exists, the order is filled offshore. The factory—and the learning-by-doing on its line—lands in another country. PRO's learning-curve claim [P1.2] therefore cuts against them: cost falls where the packs are built and iterated. Demand without domestic capacity imports the product and exports the industrial base.
[C1.4] Timing matters too. First-of-a-kind clean-tech plants run at low yield, burn cash, and face financing gaps that private markets will not close when foreign rivals are state-backed. A consumer voucher cannot fund that valley of death. Producer subsidies can, if designed with milestones, clawbacks, and matching private capital, spread risk across the early scale-up that later makes the learning curve possible.
[C1.5] Dismissing producer subsidies as 'blank checks' [P1.8] is a straw man.
[C1.6] Demand tools are hardly capture-free. EV credits and charging subsidies skew to buyers who already have income and housing access, and they can be met by foreign incumbents. China's battery lead did not come from consumer rebates alone; it came from patient credit, land, offtake, and factory support. If we only write demand checks, we may hit deployment targets while renting our supply chain.
[C1.7] So the real choice is not demand versus producers. It is whether public money builds capacity or finances consumption that others supply. Demand-side policy is a useful hammer, but for batteries and clean tech it must follow producer investment, not replace it. Because the motion favors the wrong tool, it should be rejected.
