[P1.1] A restaurant menu is the only price list in ordinary life that lies to you by design. [P1.2] Three reasons the proposition is right.
[P1.3] First, wages are the employer's job. In much of the United States, tipped workers can legally be paid a base wage far below the ordinary minimum, with customers expected to cover the gap. [C1.2] The decisive issue is control. With tipping, the service dollar passes through the customer’s judgment to the worker, subject to wage law and pooling. Under the proposition, it passes first to the business, which sets wages, hours, and allocation. That is not employer responsibility; it is employer capture: a discretionary income stream relabeled as security.
[C1.3] Higher base pay can mean fewer shifts, slower hiring, higher prices, or high performers flattened into a house wage. “The same dollars flow” [P1.8] is an assertion, not a mechanism.
[C1.4] Tipping also does work PRO does not replace. Customers have table-by-table information managers lack. A compulsory charge severs pay from that feedback and asks strong servers to carry weak ones.
[C1.5] Nor does inclusion guarantee fairness for cooks or dishwashers. If the owner controls the allocation, the back of house depends on the very employer discretion PRO distrusts. Pay kitchen staff a real wage; do not make their raise depend on a vague customer-facing charge. The proposal does not eliminate guilt or hidden costs.
[C1.6] It transfers discretion from customers and workers to management, making the service payment less visible when judged. Enforce wage floors, disclose norms, and punish harassment, discrimination, and wage theft. Do not abolish a worker-linked practice for a compulsory house fee with a cleaner label.
