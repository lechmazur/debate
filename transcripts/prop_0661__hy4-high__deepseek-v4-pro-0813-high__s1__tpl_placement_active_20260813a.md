[P1.1] An unseen score is an accusation the worker cannot answer. If an employer says the system found someone slow, rude, unavailable, or low-performing, fairness requires the rule, evidence, and consequence. Algorithmic monitoring breaks that chain: keystrokes, location pings, call transcripts, task timers, customer ratings, and scan rates can alter shifts, pay, promotion, or dismissal while the person judged does not know which facts counted.
[P1.2] Disclosure need not publish source code. Workers need notice of what is monitored, when, which inputs feed a score, the main factors that raise or lower it, the decisions it affects, retention, and a way to contest error. Regulators need documentation: purpose, variables, weighting, validation, error patterns, logs, and audit access. Confidential review can protect trade secrets without hiding the rules of discipline.
[P1.3] The mandate matters because secrecy allocates error downward. Employers get cheap, continuous measurement; workers absorb bad data, miscategorized breaks, accent or disability effects, slow customers, software glitches, and impossible quotas. Voluntary disclosure will not fix that incentive because the same system that gives managers leverage gives them reasons to conceal weak proxies and mistakes. A fallible model becomes an unquestionable fact only while its assumptions remain invisible.
[P1.4] Disclosure is also the precondition for law. Anti-discrimination rules, wage protections, safety limits, privacy rights, and collective bargaining are hollow if the decision layer is invisible. A variable like "availability," "tone," or "efficiency" can encode unequal treatment, but no worker can identify the pattern and no regulator can test it without seeing criteria and outcomes.
[P1.5] The usual defenses fail. If workers "game" the metric, that often reveals a bad proxy; if employers cannot state the standard, they should not punish under it. Cost is not a license for unaccountable power. A call-center agent scored for "sentiment," a driver timed by route software, or a picker disciplined by scan rates should not guess the rule.
[P1.6] Disclosure adds a modest duty of explanation where employers demand sweeping obedience.

[C1.1] Mandatory disclosure sounds like accountability, but it is a blunt instrument that would burden every employer, invite metric gaming, and push firms away from objective data toward less transparent subjective judgment. The real problem is not secrecy; it is bad decisions and weak enforcement. A disclosure rule attacks the wrong link.
[C1.2] Consider the worker who learns the exact weights. A call-center agent told that “sentiment” drives the score will perform scripted empathy rather than solve the problem; a driver told that on-time arrival dominates will speed; a warehouse picker told scan rate is key will skip safety checks. The score improves while the actual job gets worse. The assumption that workers only need to contest errors is wrong; the predictable response to disclosed weights is optimization, not fairness.
[C1.3] The compliance burden is not modest. “Disclose what is monitored, which inputs feed a score, main factors, weighting, validation, error patterns, retention, and audit access” is a continuous documentation regime. Large firms can absorb it; small employers will abandon useful scheduling, routing, or quality tools and fall back on manager gut. That is not more accountable—it is less transparent and more vulnerable to bias.
[C1.4] The trade-secret and legal-uncertainty problems are real. “Algorithmic monitoring” could sweep in GPS, time clocks, customer ratings, and ordinary performance reviews. Requiring disclosure to every worker means a competitor can learn the system by applying for a job. Regulators already have subpoena, audit, and anti-discrimination authority; a blanket pre-disclosure duty adds paperwork without adding protection.
[C1.5] The claim that disclosure is a precondition for law is backwards. Law can require outcome audits, impact assessments, and appeal rights without forcing employers to publish weights. A worker who is wrongly disciplined does not need the full model; she needs a meaningful chance to show the specific input was wrong. Mandatory disclosure would create an illusion of fairness while leaving power imbalances untouched.
[C1.6] Reject the mandate.

[C2.6] A post-decision reason tied to an actual contest is stronger than a standing manual.
[C2.7] PRO’s contrast between checkable rules and post-hoc complaints imagines employment as adjudication. It is not. Managers weigh context, quality, teamwork, and changing demands; a published scoring rulebook makes the metric the job rather than improving it. The better target is appeal rights and outcome enforcement—not mandatory algorithmic transparency.

[P4.1] A1: The detail is evidentiary, not an optimization recipe. Workers should know record categories: calls, logins, scans, ratings; date range, main factors, affected decisions, and contest routes. That suffices to challenge a mis-transcribed call or a break coded as idle; it withholds thresholds, weights, and source code. The gaming objection [C2.1] confuses knowing a sensor exists with mastering the formula.
[P4.2] A driver already knows speed matters; secrecy only blocks her proving the route tool blamed her for a closed road.
[P4.3] A2: The line is functional: if digital records feed automated ranking, flagging, scoring, scheduling, pay, discipline, or similar material decisions, disclose; a payroll clock recording hours with no such downstream use does not. Routing dashboards and call-quality tools are covered only when they feed those judgments. The employer should carry notice costs and any boundary-litigation cost because it designs the system and holds the evidence; safe-harbor forms and confidential regulator review reduce small firms' need for counsel. Litigation over "the boundary" [C3.2] cannot justify no boundary; the test turns on the employer's own use of data.
[P4.4] Those answers are why the mandate still wins. Appeals, outcome audits, impact assessments, and adverse-action reasons [C2.5, C2.6] are disclosure-dependent. Regulators cannot audit an unknown model; workers cannot preserve logs they never knew existed; a reason after discipline arrives after shifts are cut and evidence may be stale. That leaves the employer controlling both score and records to decide when explanation begins.
[P4.5] Gaming has the wrong direction. If disclosed on-time pressure encourages speeding, that exposes a dangerous proxy to fix; hidden pressure is guessed at from punishment and arrives as neutral fact. Disclosure makes the rule checkable before pay or status changes, contestable with the actual input, and auditable with confidential documentation. Every later remedy needs this trigger.
[P4.6] Without prior disclosure, "meaningful appeal" is a door opened after the worker has already been judged.

[C4.5] If the real goal is appealing a wrong score, require the reason and the records. Reject the mandate.
