[P1.1] The bottleneck in American energy policy is no longer wind turbines or solar panels—it is the wire that carries clean power to homes and businesses. Transmission projects routinely stall for years, not because engineers cannot design them, but because local veto power lets a single county or municipality kill lines that benefit an entire region. When one town can block a multi-state corridor, the grid becomes a patchwork of local blackmail, and the public pays for it in blackouts, congestion charges, and extra fossil fuel generation.
[P1.2] The problem is structural. Electricity is a network good; its value comes precisely from crossing jurisdictional boundaries. A veto calibrated for a land-use decision about a single parcel is the wrong instrument for an asset whose benefits are regional and whose costs are often imposed on communities with the least political leverage. The rancher whose fields host the line and the urban ratepayer three states away have radically different stakes, yet current law gives the former a kill-switch over the latter’s access to cleaner, cheaper power.
[P1.3] Delay is not neutral. Every year a high-voltage line sits in permitting, grid operators fall back on dirtier peaker plants, renewables get curtailed, and households absorb higher costs. Local opposition, aggregated across thousands of miles of needed upgrades, functions as a hidden tax on everyone else—often the very low-income communities that can least afford volatile energy bills. It also leaves the grid brittle when heat waves and deep freezes spike demand.
[P1.4] Reform should therefore prioritize speed and treat siting as an exercise in regional balancing, not unilateral refusal. That does not mean ignoring local concerns. Communities can still demand meaningful mitigation, routing alternatives, and guaranteed compensation. What they cannot retain is the categorical power to hold the whole system hostage.
[P1.5] A line that serves millions should not die because one jurisdiction says no; speed is the difference between clean power reaching a city and another decade of gas backup.

[P2.3] CON warns that reform means “years of eminent domain lawsuits, court challenges, and grassroots resistance.” That gets causality backward. A regional process with clear timelines, enforceable routing criteria, and binding compensation packages reduces legal risk by making the path predictable. Every corridor killed by veto returns as court battles, federal overrides, or legislative workarounds; the status quo is already adversarial.
[P2.4] Eminent domain can require fair negotiation and just compensation; it does not require local unanimity.
[P2.5] CON says mitigation is “hollow without leverage.” But a veto is not leverage to shape a project—it is leverage to destroy one. The choice is not developers versus locals; it is parochial obstruction versus accountable regional planning. A line that serves millions should not die because one jurisdiction says no; speed is the difference between clean power reaching a city and another decade of gas backup.
