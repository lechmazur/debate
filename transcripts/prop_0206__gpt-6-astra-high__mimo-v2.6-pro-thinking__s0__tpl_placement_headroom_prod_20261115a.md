[P1.1] Public pensions should protect people from poverty in old age without making each generation of workers pay more for an increasingly long retirement at an unchanged eligibility age. The fair adjustment is to share longer lives between additional working years and additional retirement years, not put the bill mainly on payrolls.
[P1.2] When people collect pensions for longer, a fixed retirement age increases the years of benefits the system must finance. Gradually linking eligibility ages to remaining life expectancy addresses that pressure directly: people who can work longer contribute longer and draw benefits later. A predictable rule, announced well ahead of time, also gives households and employers time to prepare. It need not turn every extra year of life into an extra year of work.
[P1.3] Relying mainly on payroll taxes instead protects the existing retirement timetable by squeezing current earnings. Those taxes can reduce take-home pay, raise hiring costs, or both. Younger workers already supporting children and paying for housing should not be the automatic shock absorbers for every increase in pension costs. Raising taxes can move money around; longer employment can also expand the productive base supporting retirement.
[P1.4] The strongest objection is that longevity gains and healthy years are unequal. That argues for a protected route to earlier benefits for disabled workers and people with very long or physically demanding careers, not freezing the general age for everyone. [P1.5] Payroll taxes can supplement that framework. They should not substitute for adjusting the system to longer lives. Preserve adequate pensions, protect genuine hardship, and distribute the adjustment across generations rather than concentrating it on workers’ paychecks.
