[P1.1] The cost of waiting is not a matter of speculation; it is the price we pay for inaction once the shock hits. AI is already automating tasks that defined white‑collar work—legal research, bookkeeping, routine coding, even preliminary medical diagnostics. These tools spread faster than any past technology because they sit on a single, ever‑improving platform rather than requiring bespoke hardware for each industry. The trajectory is clear: productivity gains will be captured by firms, not workers, unless we intervene early.
[P1.2] History teaches the same lesson. When manufacturing automation surged in the 1980s, countries that delayed retraining programs saw long‑term spikes in unemployment, reduced tax revenue, and entrenched regional poverty. The United States’ Trade Adjustment Assistance, introduced after the damage was evident, cost billions in retroactive aid that could have been avoided with forward‑looking investment. The same logic applies now, only the stakes are higher because AI threatens a far larger share of the labor market—potentially a third of current jobs within two decades.
[P1.3] Waiting for “definitive proof” is a logical trap. Proof, by definition, requires observable mass displacement. By then, the economy will have already adjusted in painful ways: workers will have exhausted savings, firms will have locked in automation, and political backlash will erode policy capacity. Early retraining, wage insurance, and income support are cheap insurance policies.
[P1.4] They buy time for workers to acquire complementary skills—creativity, complex problem‑solving, interpersonal care—that AI cannot replicate, while cushioning the transition for families.
[P1.5] The proposition does not demand a premature, blanket overhaul. It asks for targeted, scalable programs now: modular upskilling curricula, temporary wage subsidies that keep workers attached to the labor market, and a safety net that prevents poverty spirals. Acting now preserves social cohesion, maintains consumer demand, and keeps the fiscal burden manageable. Delaying until the crisis is undeniable guarantees a far larger, more disruptive, and far costlier response.
