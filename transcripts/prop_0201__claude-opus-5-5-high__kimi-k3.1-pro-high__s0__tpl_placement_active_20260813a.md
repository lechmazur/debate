[P1.1] When a state invades its neighbour, flattens its cities and destroys its power grid, someone will pay to rebuild. The only question is who. Today the answer is the victims and foreign taxpayers, while the aggressor's central bank reserves sit untouched in Western vaults. This motion asks courts to correct that inversion.
[P1.2] I'll defend it on three grounds.
[P1.3] First, the law already says the wrongdoer pays. A state that commits an internationally wrongful act owes full reparation; that principle is about as settled as international law gets. Aggression is the gravest such act. Sovereign immunity was designed to stop courts from second-guessing a foreign state's ordinary governmental conduct.
[P1.4] It was never meant to shield a country that has broken the UN Charter's most basic rule. Countermeasures doctrine already lets injured states suspend obligations they would otherwise owe a lawbreaker. Redirecting frozen reserves toward reparation is that logic carried to its conclusion.
[P1.5] Second, courts are the right gatekeeper. The real alternative to judicial process is not inaction; it is executive confiscation by political decree. Courts require evidence that aggression occurred, proof of damage, and reasoned judgments open to appeal. That is the safeguard against abuse.
[P1.6] Consider Russia: roughly three hundred billion dollars in reserves frozen, mostly in Europe, while Ukraine's reconstruction needs run to hundreds of billions. A court process assessing documented destruction is far more legitimate than either leaving that money idle or seizing it by ministerial fiat.
[P1.7] Third, deterrence and precedent cut our way. There is precedent: after Iraq invaded Kuwait, a UN compensation body paid victims out of Iraqi oil revenues for years, and the global financial order survived. Expect my opponent to warn that reserve-holding states will flee Western currencies.
[P1.8] But the rule is narrow. It reaches only states judged to be aggressors. Any government that does not plan to invade its neighbours loses nothing. A government that does plan to learns that its war chest is also its reparations fund.
[P1.9] Leaving the money frozen forever tells aggressors that destruction is free. This motion ends that.

But a domestic statute cannot by itself extinguish another state’s international rights or make a unilateral seizure internationally lawful. Nor do commercial-activity and terrorism exceptions prove an aggression exception. Those exceptions rest on particular legal bases and categories of conduct or property; they do not establish that reserves held for sovereign monetary purposes become executable whenever a court finds aggression.
[C2.3] The proposed international trigger [P2.4] does not solve the problem. General Assembly condemnation is political, not a judicial determination of asset ownership or reparations. An ICJ ruling would depend on the dispute and parties before it; it would not automatically decide which domestic creditors, victims, or reconstruction agencies may take particular reserves. Externalizing the aggression finding still leaves the most dangerous decisions—execution, valuation, and distribution—inside the forum.
[C2.4] Insolvency [P2.5] is a false reassurance. Insolvency courts operate under established priority rules within a defined estate. [C2.5] Reserve flight also cannot be answered by pointing to existing freezes. Immobilization preserves the possibility of return; confiscation permanently reallocates the assets. [C2.6] Finally, leverage does not mean bargaining victims’ claims away. It can secure an enforceable reparations settlement rather than an early, fragmented distribution followed by years of litigation. PRO offers urgency as a substitute for authority and judicial procedure as a substitute for allocation rules. Neither bridge holds: the obligation to pay does not authorize every court to take.

[C5.3] Nor do grants and multilateral lending let the aggressor escape. They finance hospitals now while preserved assets remain available for reparations later.
