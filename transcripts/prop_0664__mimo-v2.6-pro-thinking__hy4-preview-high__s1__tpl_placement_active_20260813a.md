[P1.1] Economic punishment asks Beijing to believe that Washington will impose and sustain costs on itself for months after a war begins. Denial asks it to believe something far more basic: that the crossing will fail. The second proposition is easier to make credible, because it rests on physics and geography rather than on political will.
[P1.2] An amphibious assault across roughly 100 miles of open water is the most exposed moment in any Taiwan scenario. Landing ships and transport vessels must cross in a compressed window, in predictable lanes, loaded with troops and armor. That is where mobile anti-ship batteries on Taiwan's southern and eastern coasts, submarines lurking in the Bashi Channel and Luzon Strait, and layered minefields in the approaches do their damage. None of these systems needs to defeat the Chinese military.
[P1.3] They need to sink enough transports, and kill enough embarked forces, that the operation fails before a beachhead is consolidated. Dispersed air defense protects those shooters and keeps Taiwan's runways and logistics alive long enough for the maritime fight to matter.
[P1.4] Economic deterrence cannot do this job. Sanctions bite over quarters and years, not the days in which a fait accompli succeeds or fails. By the time export controls and financial restrictions are strangling Beijing's economy, Taiwan's ports may already be seized and the political question settled. Worse, economic measures are porous in ways military geography is not: energy and commodities find alternate buyers, middlemen erode enforcement, and allied unity frays as European and Asian economies absorb the blow.
[P1.5] Every sanction package also has to survive its own domestic constituency — which Beijing's planners can see as clearly as we can.
[P1.6] Forward-deployed missiles and air defenses are the one instrument that operates on the timeline of the crisis itself. Deterrence by denial also has a crucial escalation advantage: it threatens the invasion force in the strait, not the Chinese homeland, and it makes the window of opportunity close before it opens. Economic pressure is a useful complement. Alone, it deters a leader who is already crossing.
