[P1.1] Mandatory retirement treats a birthday as a verdict. At 60, 65, or 70, a worker who is competent, willing, and needed is forced out not because of what she can no longer do, but because of what an employer assumes she can no longer do. That assumption should not be a routine tool of workforce management; in most occupations it should be abolished.
[P1.2] Age is an exceptionally blunt proxy for capacity. People do not decline on a shared schedule: health, cognition, stamina, and expertise vary more widely among same-age workers as they get older than any cutoff admits. A 68-year-old teacher, engineer, accountant, or nurse may be at the peak of judgment and institutional knowledge, while another worker struggles at 55. Chronological rules fire the first and retain the second until the clock runs out.
[P1.3] As work becomes less purely physical and lives become longer and healthier, that mismatch only grows.
[P1.4] The costs are not private alone. Mandatory retirement drains mentors, scarce specialists, and experienced hands from hospitals, classrooms, firms, and skilled trades precisely when aging societies face labor shortages and pension strain. It pushes willing workers onto benefits sooner, shrinks the tax base that funds those benefits, and forces people who cannot afford to stop to stop anyway. It also wastes years of training that employers and the public paid for.
[P1.5] This does not deny that some workers decline. It denies that a birthday is the honest way to detect it. If the concern is performance, assess performance—a standard that also catches the impaired or burned-out 40-year-old. If it is succession, use mentoring roles, phased retirement, fixed-term appointments, and transparent planning.
[P1.6] If it is safety, use regular, job-specific fitness and cognitive testing, keeping only narrow exceptions where testing cannot manage a demonstrably high risk.
[P1.7] Judge workers by what they can do, not by the date on a calendar. Abolish the routine cutoff.

[C1.1] The proposition’s central image is false. [P1.1] calls mandatory retirement a birthday verdict on competence. It is not mainly that. It is a scheduling device for institutions, not a diagnosis of decline.
[C1.2] A law-firm partner, hospital consultant, or senior civil servant may still be excellent at 68; the organization still needs the post to turn over so 40-year-olds can be promoted and given a stake. Remove the default age and you do not liberate the 68-year-old; you petrify the promotion ladder and push younger staff to leave. That is why it should remain routine in most occupations, not abolished.
[C1.3] PRO’s alternative—“assess performance” [P1.7]—sounds precise but is worse. Firm-wide cognitive, physical, and productivity testing for everyone over 60 would be costly, invasive, and gameable. Those who fail would not gently retire; they would be labeled deficient and dismissed late in life, with stigma and lawsuits. A known retirement date lets people plan, draw pensions, and leave with honor.
[C1.4] The real choice is not calendar versus merit; it is a transparent tenure limit versus endless adversarial surveillance dressed up as science.
[C1.5] Nor is this only obvious safety work. Most professional roles carry third-party risk: surgeons, pilots, engineers approving designs, pharmacists. Even if testing catches average decline, it cannot guarantee performance on a bad night, during a crisis, or amid new technology. A routine age boundary gives employers a cheap margin of safety.
[C1.6] PRO also warns of drained mentors [P1.4]. Mandatory retirement does not ban mentoring; it ends permanent incumbency. The expert can return as a consultant, adjunct, or part-time mentor while the main post opens. Without a default exit, experts become immovable and the younger workers who fund pensions and drive renewal are blocked.
[C1.7] A society needs predictable succession more than unlimited tenure. Mandatory retirement is a humble, workable tool for that task; abolishing it would trade a clear rule for worse uncertainty.
