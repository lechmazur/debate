[P1.1] Noise disputes suffer from a basic evidentiary absurdity: the people who experience the noise must prove it, yet their testimony is often discounted as subjective, exaggerated, or retaliatory. A tenant kept awake by repeated nighttime machinery can describe the disruption; a neighbor can deny it; and the decision-maker is left comparing confidence, credibility, and sometimes who has the better lawyer. Certified monitoring offers something better than memory and mutual accusation: a reviewable record of when excessive sound occurred, how long it lasted, and whether it formed a pattern.
[P1.2] The proposition is about allowing that evidence to be used more, not making a machine the judge. Certified readings should be admissible and meaningful in rental proceedings, mediation, housing enforcement, and nuisance cases, while remaining rebuttable. A reading could support a claim, corroborate a diary, or just as easily clear a resident wrongly accused of hosting parties. That even-handedness matters: reliable evidence protects peaceful neighbors, responsible tenants, and landlords facing unfounded complaints alike.
[P1.3] Certification is precisely what separates useful monitoring from someone waving a phone application in a hearing. Appropriate requirements can cover calibrated equipment, documented placement, timestamps, tamper safeguards, and preservation of the underlying data. Opposing parties can challenge the installation, identify interfering sounds, or present contrary measurements. Decision-makers can then assign the evidence its proper weight alongside testimony and inspection records.
[P1.4] Excluding it categorically does not eliminate error; it merely forces reliance on weaker evidence.
[P1.5] Privacy objections justify limits, not evidentiary darkness. Certification can require sound-level logging rather than recording conversations, restrict collection to relevant periods, and penalize misuse. Nor does admitting a reading mean that every brief disturbance becomes actionable nuisance: duration, timing, intensity, local standards, and reasonable residential expectations still matter.
[P1.6] The central choice is therefore not between perfect technology and perfect human judgment. It is between contestable measurements open to challenge and disputes dominated by selective recollection and unequal power. Where certification supplies reliability, safeguards protect privacy, and cross-examination exposes weaknesses, neighbors should be allowed to put that evidence before the people resolving their disputes.
