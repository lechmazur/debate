[P1.1] Predicting an individual’s likelihood of quitting, their future output, or their interest in unionizing hands employers a preemptive weapon against workers. Once the model flags someone, the company can quietly reduce hours, deny promotions, or manufacture performance issues long before the worker acts. The prediction itself becomes the justification, and the worker never sees the evidence or gets to contest it.
[P1.2] Unionization forecasts are especially corrosive. They allow management to identify and isolate potential organizers without waiting for any protected activity. A single high-risk score can trigger reassignment to a dead-end shift or removal from client-facing roles, chilling organizing efforts before they start. Workers lose the practical ability to exercise rights that labor law formally guarantees.
[P1.3] Productivity and retention predictions compound the problem by turning ordinary career choices into liabilities. An algorithm that scores someone as likely to leave can justify withholding training or pushing them toward exit, creating self-fulfilling cycles that punish normal labor-market behavior. Because the inputs often include off-duty data such as social-media activity or commuting patterns, the system reaches outside the workplace to police personal decisions.
[P1.4] These tools invert the employment relationship. Instead of responding to actual conduct, employers act on statistical shadows that workers cannot inspect or correct. A ban removes the incentive to build such systems and forces companies to manage people on observable behavior rather than opaque forecasts. Without it, the asymmetry of information and power simply grows.

[C1.1] The proposed ban rests on a false premise: that AI forecasts of worker productivity, retention, and unionization likelihood are inherently exploitative, when in practice they deliver tangible pro-worker benefits a ban would erase. Far from only enabling preemptive retaliation, these tools let employers identify burnout risks early, reallocate overburdened staff, and offer targeted retention support—flexible schedules, mentorship, adjusted workloads—to employees who would otherwise quit due to unaddressed workplace stress. For low-wage workers with high turnover, that stability protects seniority, team cohesion, and access to benefits they would lose if forced to find new jobs.
[C1.2] PRO’s framing also ignores that the harms they cite are already illegal under existing labor and anti-discrimination law. Retaliatory demotions, scheduling cuts, or promotion denials based on protected union activity or protected personal characteristics are already prohibited, and predictive tools can be audited for disparate impact far more easily than the biased, ad-hoc managerial discretion they replace. A ban would not stop bad actors: it would just push them to rely on unrecorded, unreviewable gut calls that are far more likely to discriminate on the basis of race, gender, or caregiving status.
[C1.3] PRO’s concern about off-duty data is already addressed by most state privacy laws and corporate governance standards that bar the use of non-work-related inputs for employment decisions. A blanket ban ignores the possibility of responsible, regulated use that benefits workers as much as employers, in favor of a one-size-fits-all prohibition that does more harm than good.

[C2.1] [P2.1] rests on a false assumption that employers will always use quit or productivity forecasts to push workers out, rather than retain them, ignoring basic labor economics. Replacing a mid-level employee costs 50-150% of their annual salary once recruiting, onboarding, and lost ramp-up productivity are factored in, a burden that hits high-turnover sectors like retail, hospitality, and warehouse work hardest. For these employers, retaining a flagged at-risk worker is almost always cheaper than replacing them, so the financial incentive aligns with offering support, not preemptive exit.
[C2.2] Many firms already route AI-flagged retention risks to existing flexible scheduling, mentorship, or workload adjustment programs that workers would never have accessed otherwise; the worker may not see the score, but they still receive concrete benefits that reduce unplanned turnover and protect their seniority and benefit access.
[C2.3] [P2.2]’s claim that existing law can’t regulate algorithmic harm is also wrong. Disparate impact liability under federal anti-discrimination law explicitly covers facially neutral practices that produce disproportionate harm to protected groups, and courts have already applied this standard to AI-driven employment tools. Ad-hoc “gut” managerial decisions, by contrast, leave almost no paper trail, making them far harder for workers to challenge than documented, auditable algorithmic outputs. A ban does not stop bad actors: it just forces them to rely on unrecorded, unreviewable bias that flies under the radar of any oversight.
[C2.4] Finally, [P2.3] overstates the regulatory gap around internal workplace data. Even where on-site behavioral inputs are used, targeted audit requirements and narrow input restrictions can address privacy and fairness concerns without eliminating the pro-worker benefits of these tools. A blanket ban sacrifices those gains for a symbolic gesture that does nothing to stop employers from acting on the same biased hunches, now without any transparency or accountability.
