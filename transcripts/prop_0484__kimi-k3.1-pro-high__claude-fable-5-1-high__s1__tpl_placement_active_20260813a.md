[P1.1] Climate negotiations decide who must cut emissions, who must pay, and how quickly the world acts. Defenders of population- or GDP-based influence must explain why negotiating power should track size or wealth when climate danger tracks neither. A small island state can face disappearing territory, contaminated freshwater, cultural dislocation, and eventually the loss of a viable homeland while contributing comparatively little to the problem. Treating that exposure as secondary because its population is small makes those with the most to lose easiest to ignore.
[P1.2] The governing principle should be that standing reflects stakes. This is not a demand that island states dictate global policy, nor that population become irrelevant to human welfare. It is a demand that, when choices distribute existential danger, the parties confronting that danger receive voting weight commensurate with it. Democracy is not merely rule by numerical majorities; it also prevents majorities from casually sacrificing minorities whose interests they do not share.
[P1.3] A homeland does not become more expendable because fewer people happen to live there. GDP weighting is still worse: it can reward economic scale built alongside high emissions while discounting losses that markets poorly capture, including sovereignty, ancestry, and place.
[P1.4] Existential risk is also workable, not merely rhetorical. Negotiators can use transparent assessments of territorial exposure, habitability, water security, disaster recovery, and displacement, with periodic independent review. No index is perfect, but population and GDP are not perfect measures of climate entitlement either; they are simply precise measures of the wrong things. Measurement difficulty should produce safeguards against gaming, not preserve systematic exclusion.
[P1.5] Finally, this reform improves decisions, not just symbolism. Representatives who cannot quietly externalize catastrophe onto island populations have stronger reasons to demand timely mitigation, adaptation finance, and enforceable commitments before damage becomes irreversible. The opposition may call that disproportionate influence. But the true disproportion is a system in which states facing national erasure can be outvoted by states debating manageable costs.
[P1.6] When survival itself is on the agenda, survival must carry weight in the vote.
