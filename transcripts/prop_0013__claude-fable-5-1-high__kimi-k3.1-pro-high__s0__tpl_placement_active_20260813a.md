[P1.2] Grievances do not expire when a government stops mentioning them. They expire when the people who hold them feel heard. Korea and Japan signed a settlement in 1965 and another agreement on the comfort women in 2015, and both times the issue roared back, not because Korean teachers kept teaching it, but because Japanese textbooks softened, politicians questioned whether coercion occurred, and survivors concluded the apology was a transaction rather than an admission. Silence did not close the wound; it reopened it.
[P1.3] Compare Germany, which teaches the Holocaust relentlessly, built a forced-labor compensation fund decades after the war, and today has stronger relations with Poland, France, and Israel than almost any former aggressor has with its victims. Thorough acknowledgment is the long-run diplomatic solvent, not the irritant.
[P1.4] Second, the people whose grievance this is get a vote. Surviving comfort women are in their nineties and dying. A state that stays quiet to smooth a summit is deciding that a living woman's forced enslavement matters less than a trade ministers' communiqué. That is a moral debt, and debts are not cancelled because the debtor finds repayment awkward.
[P1.5] Third, teaching these crimes is how a society builds the vocabulary to recognize them now. The enslavement of Yazidi women, forced labor in modern supply chains, rape as a weapon in current wars: citizens who have studied how ordinary states organized sexual slavery and conscripted labor can name these things when they recur. Citizens taught a sanitized past cannot.
[P1.6] So the real choice is this: either the truth a nation teaches its children is set by the historical record, or it is set by whichever foreign ministry is most easily offended. CON must defend the second. That is a veto for perpetrators, dressed up as diplomacy.
