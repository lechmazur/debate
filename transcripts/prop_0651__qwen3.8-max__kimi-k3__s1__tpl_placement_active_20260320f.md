[P1.1] The proposition is not that English never helps people communicate. It is that its dominance—being the default gatekeeper of science, commerce, and digital life—harms non-Anglophone cultures and knowledge systems more than it helps them.
[P1.2] The central harm is epistemic narrowing. When scientific legitimacy runs mainly through English, knowledge is selected by what fits Anglophone institutions, categories, and audiences. A researcher in Brazil, Vietnam, or Senegal may have to reshape questions, methods, and conclusions to satisfy English-language journals. Local studies become “regional,” while Anglophone datasets become “universal.” Indigenous ecological knowledge gains attention only when extracted, translated, and repackaged as Western-style environmental science.
[P1.3] What does not translate cleanly is treated as folklore, not knowledge.
[P1.4] This is not merely linguistic inconvenience; it is status loss. Languages carry concepts, metaphors, humor, legal traditions, and ways of classifying the world. When prestige careers require English-first publication, business meetings, and online presence, ambitious young people shift effort away from native intellectual traditions. The result is a feedback loop: less high-status content is produced in non-English languages, so those languages seem less suited to science or business, so more speakers abandon them.
[P1.5] English dominance also imposes unequal costs. Non-native speakers spend years and money acquiring academic English, paying for editing, and navigating gatekept journals and conferences. Small publishers, local tech platforms, and cultural media struggle against search algorithms, interfaces, and venture capital oriented toward English. In business, fluent English often becomes a class credential, rewarding access more than competence.
[P1.6] The obvious reply is that English lowers transaction costs and spreads ideas. But the proposition asks about effects on non-Anglophone cultures and knowledge systems. A single common channel can increase reach while still shrinking the ecosystem. If the price of participation is that local languages become private and decorative, while serious authority belongs to English, the harm is structural and durable.
[P1.7] That is the clash: not communication, but hierarchy.
