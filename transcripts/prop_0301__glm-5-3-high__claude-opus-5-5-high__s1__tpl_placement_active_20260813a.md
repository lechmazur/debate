[P1.1] Public money is a public trust. When a government writes a check, it is not merely subsidizing an institution; it is underwriting what that institution teaches, whom it hires, and whom it serves. That is why the proposition is not an attack on religious schools—it is the condition that makes funding them defensible at all.
[P1.2] Start with the state's own obligations. Every democratic government is responsible for ensuring its future citizens receive an education that prepares them for civic life: literacy in science and history, exposure to pluralism, and the basic capacity to participate as equals. If the state funds schools, it cannot outsource this responsibility wholesale. A religious school that teaches creationism as settled science, or teaches that members of other faiths are second-class, produces graduates the state has paid to under-educate.
[P1.3] Conditioning funds on curricular and civic standards is simply the state keeping its side of the bargain.
[P1.4] Second, non-discrimination standards are not meddling—they are the baseline of public funding everywhere. We do not give contracts to vendors who refuse to serve customers by race; we do not grant tax exemptions to charities that exclude beneficiaries by religion. A school that takes public money while rejecting students or teachers on grounds public institutions cannot use is asking taxpayers to fund exclusion in their own name. Parents who choose religious education retain that choice; what they lose is the entitlement to have exclusion subsidized.

Charter and voucher systems that collapsed into scandal—fly-by-night academies, financial mismanagement, diploma mills—did damage that discredited the entire sector. [C1.1] The motion fails on one word: "comparable." Nobody disputes that funded schools should teach children to read, do mathematics, learn real science, and keep them safe. The question is whether religious schools must meet standards comparable to *public* schools. Public schools are defined by religious neutrality.
[C1.2] Hold a Catholic, Jewish, or Muslim school to that benchmark and you have not regulated it. You have abolished it and kept the building.
[C1.3] Take non-discrimination. A public school cannot prefer a Catholic applicant for a teaching post. Apply that standard and a Catholic school cannot insist that the person teaching confirmation class, or leading morning prayer, shares the faith. A Jewish day school cannot give admission priority to Jewish families.
[C1.4] PRO says "a leash only binds those who wanted to do something the public could not endorse." But democracies openly endorse exactly this. The Netherlands has funded faith schools on equal terms for about a century. England funds thousands of Church of England, Catholic, and Jewish schools with faith-based admissions and hiring.
[C1.5] Ontario funds a full Catholic system. None of these meet public-school neutrality, and none are scandals of exclusion. They show that the right standard is tailored, not comparable.
[C1.6] Second, curriculum. Public schools must teach religion, if at all, as neutral comparative study. A religious school's purpose is to teach its faith as true, alongside a rigorous academic core. The sensible rule is to demand outcomes: exam results, literacy, accurate science.
[C1.7] Demanding comparability demands sameness, and sameness destroys the pluralism that justifies funding different schools at all.
[C1.8] Third, whoever writes "strong civic standards" wins every future culture war. Today it means respecting democracy. Tomorrow a governing majority can define civic virtue as endorsing its contested views on sexuality, history, or religion, and defund dissenting communities overnight.
[C1.9] The alternative is plain. Fund schools that meet firm floors: academic outcomes, safety, financial accountability, and no discrimination by race or ethnicity. That standard protects children and taxpayers without demanding that faith schools stop being faithful.
