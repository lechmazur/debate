[P1.1] Three mechanisms explain why engagement-optimized feeds are uniquely harmful to adolescent mental health, and none of them apply to chronological feeds.
[P1.2] First, the selection mechanism. Engagement-optimization algorithms select for whatever keeps a viewer watching, and negative emotional content—body comparison, conflict, appearance anxiety, extreme fitness or diet content—reliably produces longer watch times than neutral content. A chronological feed shows you your friends' posts in order; an engagement feed learns, within minutes, that a 14-year-old girl lingers on thinness-related content and then floods her feed with more of it. That is not incidental exposure, it's algorithmic escalation.
[P1.3] This is the well-documented "rabbit hole" effect: a teen who watches one video about sadness or self-harm gets served progressively more of that content because the system is optimizing for time-on-app, not wellbeing.
[P1.4] Second, the comparison mechanism. Chronological feeds show a representative sample of your actual social graph—ordinary posts, boring days included. Engagement feeds specifically surface the most extreme, curated, aspirational content across the entire platform, because extraordinary content generates more engagement than ordinary content. Adolescents are shown a constant stream of the most attractive, most successful, most dramatic strangers on earth, not the mundane reality of their peers.
[P1.5] This supercharges social comparison at exactly the developmental stage—early-to-mid adolescence—when identity and self-worth are most sensitive to peer comparison.
[P1.6] Third, the compulsion mechanism. Engagement optimization is explicitly tuned to maximize session length and return frequency, which functions like a variable-reward slot machine. This produces compulsive use patterns—checking, scrolling past bedtime, anxiety when separated from the app—independent of what content is even shown. Chronological feeds run out; there's a natural stopping point.
[P1.7] Engagement feeds are infinite by design.
[P1.8] The proposition doesn't require proving short-form video itself is harmful—CON may want to fight that battle. It only requires showing the *engagement-optimized delivery mechanism*, compared to a non-personalized alternative, worsens outcomes. That comparison is the one I intend to hold throughout this debate.
