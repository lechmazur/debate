By auctioning off physical proximity to their matching engines, exchanges institutionalize a rigged, two-tiered market. Wealthy private trading firms pay exorbitant rent to place their servers inches from the exchange’s data feed. This grants them a manufactured structural advantage, allowing them to receive market data and execute trades fractions of a millisecond before everyone else.
[P1.2] This is not an edge built on superior fundamental analysis or smarter investment strategies; it is a systemic tax. These firms use their purchased speed to arbitrage and front-run the block orders of pension funds, mutual funds, and retail investors, extracting wealth without contributing real economic value.
[P1.3] The opponent will likely claim that co-location is necessary to incentivize high-frequency traders who provide vital market liquidity. This is a dangerous mirage. The liquidity generated by ultra-low-latency algorithms is largely "phantom liquidity." Because these firms rely entirely on physical speed rather than fundamental asset valuation, their algorithms are programmed to instantly cancel orders and flee the market the moment volatility spikes.
[P1.4] Instead of stabilizing the market, co-location-driven trading exacerbates fragility. It directly enables the sudden, devastating flash crashes where supposed liquidity providers evaporate, leaving traditional investors to absorb severe, artificial price dislocations.
[P1.5] Furthermore, selling co-location fuels an economically barren arms race. It forces a zero-sum competition where trading firms squander billions of dollars and elite engineering talent purely to shave nanoseconds off data transmission. This endless pursuit adds absolutely zero fundamental value to capital formation, corporate growth, or the broader economy.
[P1.6] Prohibiting the sale of server co-location restores the integrity of the market. It neutralizes an exploitative physical advantage and forces competition back to where it actually benefits the economy: deeper fundamental research, better predictive models, and genuine, stable price discovery, rather than a contest over who can afford the most expensive real estate inside an exchange's data center.

The arms race simply moves off the exchange’s premises onto less transparent, less accountable private networks.
[C1.2] The two-tiered market the opponent deplores as “a rigged, two-tiered market” [P1.1] will survive; we just lose the only connectivity the exchange itself controls, supervises, and can regulate.
[C1.3] What co-location actually enables is tighter, more continuous two-sided quotes. Electronic market makers use ultra-low latency to refresh bids and offers in microseconds, compressing spreads and absorbing order flow. When a pension fund or mutual fund executes a block, it receives a better price because competing quotes are updated almost instantly; this is lower transaction cost, not a systemic tax. Ban the service and quotes update more slowly, spreads widen, and ordinary investors pay more.
[C1.4] When volatility spikes, fast automated systems are also first to re-price and return; slower links mean the liquidity gap lasts longer for end users.
[C1.5] The abuse the opponent targets—using speed to trade ahead of customer flow or exploit a broker’s order—is not co-location. It is conduct. A prohibition removes that supervisory vantage point while leaving the incentive to abuse intact.
[C1.6] The real choice is not between a neutral market and a rigged one. It is between a symbolic ban that pushes speed into the dark, widens spreads, and leaves manipulation untouched, and a disclosed, exchange-controlled service that regulators can police. On both effectiveness and harm, the proposition fails.
