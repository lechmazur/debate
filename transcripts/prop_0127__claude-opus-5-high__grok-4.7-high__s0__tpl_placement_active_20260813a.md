[P1.1] A pallet of yogurt three days from its date, still cold, still safe, is crushed into a compactor behind a store while someone two streets away is choosing between heat and groceries. That is not a market failure hidden in a spreadsheet. It is a decision someone makes, on purpose, every night.
[P1.2] And it is a rational decision under current rules — which is exactly the problem. For a large chain, dumping surplus is cheap, simple, and protects full-price sales. Deep discounting trains shoppers to wait for the markdown; donation costs staff time and coordination. So the private calculus says: destroy it.
[P1.3] The social calculus says the opposite, because the real costs — methane from landfill, the water, land, fuel and labor already burned to grow and ship that food, and the hunger the surplus could have relieved — land on everyone else. A cost you push onto the public isn't a "normal cost of doing business." It's a subsidy for waste.
[P1.4] The fix is modest, and it is already a live policy, not a thought experiment. France required its large supermarkets to sign donation agreements with charities rather than destroy unsold edible stock, and the sky did not fall; stores kept operating, charities got better-quality and more reliable supply. The duty as written is deliberately flexible: donate *or* discount sharply. If a retailer thinks half-price at 7pm moves the bread better than a van from a food bank, fine — the obligation is only that edible food must be offered to someone before it's destroyed.
[P1.5] Note what this mandate does not do. It does not tell stores what to stock, what to charge at full price, or how to forecast. It removes exactly one option: the deliberate destruction of safe food as the path of least resistance. Liability is solvable by statutory protection for good-faith donors.
[P1.6] Handling costs are solvable by tax relief.
[P1.7] What isn't solvable is a system where compaction is cheaper than charity.
