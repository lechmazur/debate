[P1.1] GDP can rise while a country gets worse to live in. An oil spill boosts it through cleanup spending. Longer commutes, treatment for preventable illness, rebuilding after floods, and insecure jobs with longer hours can all lift output while shortening lives and stealing time from family. Chasing that number as headline success teaches government to value output even when citizens pay in health, security, and leisure.
[P1.2] A headline target is not neutral. It sets the test every budget and project must pass. Ask first, “Will this raise GDP?” and a motorway through a neighborhood can beat green space, a casino can beat preventive care, and exhausting shift work can beat stable schedules, even when the second option leaves people better off. The metric steers policy, not just describes it.
[P1.3] GDP also misses distribution and cost. A windfall for the wealthy counts like broad wage growth for low-paid workers, though only the latter transforms security. Unpaid care counts for nothing until paid for; pollution counts when produced and again when cleaned up. Once basic needs are met, more aggregate output is an unreliable proxy for being healthier, safer, less lonely, or able to plan ahead.
[P1.4] Well-being can be governed by concrete measures: life satisfaction, healthy life expectancy, mental distress, housing and financial security, educational opportunity, safety, and air quality. No single happiness question need run a treasury. That dashboard is no fuzzier than GDP, which itself depends on conventions about what counts and how to price it.
[P1.5] This is not anti-growth. Growth that lifts struggling incomes, funds good services, and creates fulfilling work improves well-being and deserves support. Growth built on burnout, polluted air, and unaffordable homes should not pass merely because it enlarges a total. Governments should target the end — lives going well — rather than mistake market activity for the purpose of economic policy.

[P2.1] The scoreboard metaphor in [C1.2] concedes too much. Scoreboards decide what players optimize. Ministers do not order oil spills, but when GDP is the headline test, defensive spending is recorded as success, and regulations protecting health or neighborhoods are framed as drags on the number that decides elections. Put the constraint second and it loses under pressure; that is precisely how the headline steers policy.
[P2.2] The 'menu' objection in [C1.5] applies more forcefully to GDP. GDP is also an aggregation, but its weights are hidden: a dollar of luxury spending, gambling, or pollution cleanup counts according to market price, while unpaid care counts zero. That is not objectivity; it is weighting by purchasing power. A well-being headline can instead publish its components and weights beforehand, have them set independently, and judge governments against that fixed standard.
[P2.3] A minister cherry-picking one improving line would then be visibly failing the stated target, just as cherry-picking exports cannot hide a GDP contraction.
[P2.4] Nor is GDP uniquely timely or immune to gaming. Quarterly GDP is revised, can be flattered by debt-funded consumption, asset churn, and longer hours, and still depends on contested conventions. Several well-being measures move faster than healthy life expectancy: air quality, housing insecurity, financial distress, mental distress, and job security can be tracked monthly or continuously. Slow final outcomes are a reason to use leading indicators, not to steer by a fast measure of the wrong thing.
[P2.5] Finally, 'growth is how well-being gets paid for' [C1.9] proves only that resources matter, not that maximizing aggregate output is the best way to secure them. Growth that damages health, skills, and household stability erodes the workforce and tax base that compounding depends on. Targeting well-being protects productive growth — healthier workers, secure families, clean air — while exposing growth bought by borrowing against people's lives. The choice is not growth versus compassion; it is disciplined attention to what growth is for.
