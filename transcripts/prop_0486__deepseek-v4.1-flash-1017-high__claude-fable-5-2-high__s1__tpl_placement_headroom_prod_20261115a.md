[P1.1] A city cannot afford a policy that turns scarce land into a permanent, non-reproducing use. Permanent burial plots are exactly that: each one withdraws a piece of public land from housing, transit, parks, and climate adaptation forever, often for a single family’s private memorial. In a land-scarce city, that is not tradition-neutral. It is a massive hidden subsidy from everyone who needs land to those who secured a plot.
[P1.2] Composting and alkaline hydrolysis break that lock. They reduce a body to soil or sterile remains that can be returned to a memorial garden, scattered, or kept by families, while cemeteries can offer plaques, groves, and ceremonies. The dead are still honored; the land is not permanently entombed. The choice is not dignity versus disposal.
[P1.3] It is permanent private occupancy versus adaptable public memorialization.
[P1.4] Nor does transition require erasing grief or trampling culture. Cities already use limited-tenure graves, columbaria, and shared memorial spaces; mourning rituals adapt when public infrastructure requires it. Existing burial rights can be honored while new perpetual allocations stop. The facilities for composting and alkaline hydrolysis can be compact and integrated into existing cemeteries, unlike acre after acre of plots and vaults.
[P1.5] The opponent will defend permanence as sacred. But sacredness does not require a deed to city land in perpetuity. Public cemeteries exist to serve the living public, including those who will need land long after any particular grave is forgotten. A city that keeps promising forever is choosing to make its future generations pay for a promise it cannot afford.
[P1.6] The proposition is the responsible line: honor the dead, but stop sacrificing the living city’s finite land.

[C1.2] He concedes the point himself: "Cities already use limited-tenure graves" [P1.4]. Exactly. Across much of continental Europe and in dense Asian cities, graves are leased for a term of years or decades, then renewed or reused, with remains respectfully consolidated. The same ground serves generation after generation.
[C1.3] That fixes the "permanent, non-reproducing use" of [P1.1] without telling a single family that the earth is closed to their dead. If renewable tenure solves the land problem, the motion's demand to replace burial with two specific technologies is pure excess.
[C1.4] And that excess has victims. For observant Muslims and Jews, and for many Christians, burial in the ground is an obligation, not a preference. Neither composting nor dissolving a body in heated alkaline solution satisfies it. Public cemeteries are the provider of last resort.
[C1.5] When they stop burying, the wealthy buy private plots or ship their dead elsewhere, and the poor and devout are left with a method their faith forbids. That is the city telling minorities that their dead are not welcome on public land, which is hardly "adaptable public memorialization" [P1.3].
[C1.6] Meanwhile, the chosen substitutes are oddly unfit for the job. Human composting produces a large volume of soil per person that must go somewhere, takes weeks per vessel, and remains legally unrecognised in many jurisdictions. Alkaline hydrolysis is similarly new, contested, and unproven at municipal scale. Staking a city's entire public deathcare on them is a gamble.
[C1.7] Finally, cemeteries are not "entombed" land. In dense cities they are tree canopy, quiet, habitat, and heritage, often the largest green space a neighbourhood has.
[C1.8] So the choice is between ending burial and reforming it. Reform frees the land and keeps faith with every community.
