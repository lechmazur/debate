[C1.1] The proposition mistakes possible exposure for the measure of real-name policy. Real-name regimes can verify legal identity, display names, and link enforcement records to a person. Public display creates outing and doxxing risks; platform-side verification creates subpoena and breach risks. Neither proves the aggregate balance is negative.
[C1.2] Verification attacks a central abuse mechanism: the disposable fake account. Even when names are visible, it denies abusers the cheap cover of mass fabricated personas. Verified identities make it harder to mount brigading attacks, evade a ban after a credible threat, impersonate a victim, launch false-report campaigns, or distribute nonconsensual intimate images. It also links repeat offenders to prior violations.
[C1.3] Anonymity does not cause abuse, but lowers the cost of coordinating it; verification raises that cost before the first report. For a dissident, it can prevent opponents from manufacturing a crowd that overwhelms genuine support; for a victim, it makes remedies survive an abuser’s next account. That abusive people can sometimes obtain real names does not make mass impersonation costless.
[C1.4] The case against real-name policies is mostly conditional: if a dissident posts under her legal name; if the state subpoenas the platform; if a harasser discovers the link. The conditional identifies a serious risk, but not the policy’s net effect. That risk must be weighed against adversaries’ capacity to mass-produce credible identities and turn enforcement into theater. Identity checks reduce that capacity, and the benefit is recurring and cumulative.
[C1.5] The proposition therefore needs evidence that harms across major platforms outweigh that reduction, not merely vivid victims. If public display is unnecessary, accountability can be achieved while legal identity remains shielded from peers; if some harms cannot be engineered away, graduated verification and risk-based disclosure can limit them. Real-name policies still create privacy dangers, but vivid victims alone cannot establish net harm: the relevant test is whether reductions in abuse outweigh privacy harms created.
