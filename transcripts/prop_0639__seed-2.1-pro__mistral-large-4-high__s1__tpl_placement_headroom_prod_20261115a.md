[P1.1] Elected legislatures systematically fail on policy questions where broad public good clashes with concentrated, well-funded lobby power: long-term climate action, democratic reform, public health guardrails, and corporate accountability all see elected officials punt or water down rules, because re-election depends on donor access and partisan branding, not careful weighing of public interest. Sortition-selected citizens’ assemblies, by contrast, are demographically matched to the full public, carry no career political ambitions, and rely on balanced, independent expert input rather than lobbyist-crafted talking points when deliberating, with dedicated time to weigh tradeoffs instead of performing for partisan media audiences.
[P1.2] Critics will say these bodies should only advise, but advisory status guarantees their most bold, public-interest recommendations get ignored the moment they threaten connected insiders. The UK’s 2020 Climate Assembly, for example, produced thoughtful, widely supported proposals for fair, rapid decarbonization, only to have its core recommendations sidelined by successive governments that remained responsive to fossil fuel lobbying and partisan point-scoring, with no mechanism to force a vote or implementation. Binding power, limited to clearly defined, narrow policy questions pre-defined by legislatures to avoid mission creep, rather than full legislative replacement, eliminates that dodge.
[P1.3] These bodies do not replace representative democracy; they fix its most predictable blind spots. Unlike legislators, assembly members have no reason to trade policy choices for campaign cash, no incentive to skew facts to please a partisan base, and no motive to kick hard choices to future elected terms. When ordinary citizens, free from those pressures, deliberate and reach a decision after hearing all sides, that outcome carries more democratic legitimacy than a legislative vote skewed by lobby access and short-term electoral math, and it deserves to be implemented, not watered down or shelved for political convenience.

[C1.1] Binding power for sortition assemblies mistakes a deliberative virtue for a governing authority. The core defect is accountability: when a body can make binding law, someone must answer to the public for that law. Elected legislators can be voted out when policy fails; sortition members simply vanish after their term, leaving no mechanism to correct course when their binding decision proves wrong. That breaks the chain of responsibility that makes democratic decisions legitimate in the first place, replacing electoral judgment with a one-shot gamble by temporary amateurs.
[C1.2] Consider the stakes. A citizens' assembly handed binding power over, say, pension reform or energy grid restructuring is not merely offering advice—it is locking in multi-decade policy commitments made by randomly selected citizens who heard weeks of testimony, not the sustained technical scrutiny of standing committees with institutional memory. Unlike advisory recommendations, which elected officials can refine, delay, or reject at the next ballot box, a binding decision removes the safety valve. If the assembly misjudges tradeoffs—and complex systems invite misjudgment—the public cannot reverse it except through constitutional crisis or waiting for a future legislature to repeal it, which defeats the entire point of granting binding status.
[C1.3] Moreover, binding power threatens minority rights. Electoral accountability forces politicians to build coalitions and respect minority constituencies to survive the next election. A randomly selected assembly faces no such check; a majority within it can impose binding costs on unpopular minorities with no electoral consequence whatsoever. Advisory roles preserve the genuine deliberative benefits—informed public input, reduced polarization, broader perspectives—while leaving final legitimacy and rights-protection to institutions that remain answerable to the whole electorate.
[C1.4] Binding power severs deliberation from accountability, producing decisions that feel democratic in composition but act like unaccountable decree.

[P2.1] The accountability objection collapses under its own weight. Elected officials are not meaningfully accountable to broad publics for the exact failures these assemblies are designed to fix: they face no real penalty for punting on climate action, letting prescription drug prices spiral, or carving out corporate loopholes, because those harms are diffuse across millions of voters, while the rewards from lobbyists and partisan bases are immediate and concentrated. The “no one to vote out” critique treats every hard choice as a potential disaster to be reversed later, but the far more common, far more damaging democratic failure is that elected officials never make the hard choice at all.
[P2.2] Nor do binding assemblies lock in unchangeable, amateur policy. As noted from the start, assemblies apply only to narrow, pre-defined questions, draw on balanced, vetted expert input rather than lobbyist-crafted talking points, and produce decisions that future legislatures can refine just as they refine any other statute—there is no constitutional crisis, just an end to the loophole where legislators bury broadly popular, evidence-based policy to avoid donor backlash. The minority rights warning is equally backwards. Elected officials routinely target unpopular minorities to fire up their electoral base, from restrictive voting rules to punitive welfare cuts, because those moves deliver clear campaign rewards.
[P2.3] A random, demographically representative assembly, by contrast, includes members of those minority groups by design, has no incentive to scapegoat them for partisan or financial gain, and returns members to ordinary life to live under the exact rules they write, a more tangible accountability than facing elections funded by the very groups those rules regulate. Advisory bodies only deliver value if elected officials will act on public interest rather than private influence—we already have decades of evidence they will not.

[C2.1] The proposition's own rebuttal concedes the fatal flaw. [P2.2] claims binding decisions can be "refined" by future legislatures "just as they refine any other statute"—but that is the definition of advisory power. Either an assembly's decision binds, or it does not. If legislatures can freely rewrite or repeal it, the assembly never had binding authority; if they cannot, the public is stuck with a one-shot decision by temporary amateurs with no recall mechanism.
[C2.2] PRO cannot have it both ways.
[C2.3] [P2.3]'s claim that members "live under the exact rules they write" misunderstands the stakes. Pension reform or energy grid restructuring binds a society for decades; assembly members serve weeks and vanish, while catastrophic costs fall on millions who never consented to the gamble. That is consequence without responsibility. Elected officials remain answerable at the next ballot for long-term effects; sortition members leave no one to punish when their binding decision ages badly.
[C2.4] On minority rights, [P2.2] inverts the risk. Elected officials target minorities only when they calculate electoral reward and face backlash when they overreach; a randomly selected assembly majority faces zero electoral consequence for imposing binding costs on an unpopular minority. Group deliberation tends to push positions toward extremes, so the "no incentive to scapegoat" assumption is fragile. Without the ballot-box check, the assembly is a veto player without accountability.
[C2.5] Finally, [P2.1]'s charge that legislators "never make the hard choice" proves too much. If hard choices are so error-prone, why entrust them permanently to a body that cannot be corrected? Advisory assemblies shape debate, build consensus, and give officials cover to act—preserving deliberative quality and the safety valve of democratic correction. Binding power removes that valve, trading a flawed but correctable democracy for an unaccountable one.
[C2.6] We should refuse.

[P4.1] A1: The boundary is set in the public, democratically passed enabling statute that creates each assembly, enforced by ordinary judicial review of procedural ultra vires, just as courts already enforce the scope of ballot initiatives and delegated agency authority. Legislatures will only call these assemblies when sustained public pressure makes total inaction politically costly, so they cannot quietly sanitize the agenda to protect donors without inviting the exact public backlash that prompted the assembly in the first place. This is a far more transparent, enforceable boundary than the status quo, where lobbyists negotiate final bill language behind closed doors with no public guardrail at all.
[P4.2] A2: Sortition uses stratified random sampling, not unadjusted raw draw, to ensure small demographic groups hold seats proportional to their share of the public, a baseline of representation most elected legislatures fail to meet. The check on majority overreach is concrete: members hear balanced, mandatory testimony from affected communities, issue a public, written justification for their decision, and return to ordinary civilian life to live under the exact rule they pass, with no safe, gerrymandered seat or lobby-funded post to insulate them from its consequences.
[P4.3] These answers cut to the core clash. Every flaw CON warns of—agenda capture, majority overreach, uncorrectable error—is demonstrably worse in the current lobby-distorted elected system, where legislators face consistent rewards for serving narrow interests and almost no penalty for abandoning broad public priorities. Advisory assemblies give legislators a convenient PR prop to cite while ignoring recommendations that threaten donor interests; narrow binding power fixes that blind spot without replacing representative democracy, retaining the same ability of future legislatures to adjust policy as circumstances change that applies to every other statute, and delivering on the public interest promises deliberative reform was always meant to keep.

Replacing a flawed, contestable check with no check is not minority protection—especially when group deliberation can push a majority past the public's center.
