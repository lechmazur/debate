[P1.1] A seventy-year-old woman is walking on a sidewalk when a rented scooter hits her from behind. The rider apologizes, panics, and leaves. She has a broken hip. Who pays?
[P1.2] Today, usually nobody. The rider is a stranger with no insurance covering this. The company points to a user agreement in which the rider clicked "I agree" to stay off sidewalks and to shoulder all liability. The firm collected the fare for that ride; she collects the medical bills.
[P1.3] That must change.
[P1.4] First, control. These are not bicycles sold and forgotten. The company owns the vehicle, tracks it by GPS in real time, and can remotely limit its speed or cut its power. Geofencing and sidewalk-detection technology already exist and are deployed where cities demand them.
[P1.5] When the party able to prevent harm cheaply chooses not to, the law ordinarily makes that party answer for it.
[P1.6] Second, incentives. Liability is how we make businesses price the danger they create. Right now a ride through a pedestrian plaza earns exactly what a lawful ride earns. Put some of the cost of that collision on the operator's balance sheet, and slow-zones, sidewalk detection, repeat-offender bans, and real insurance stop being optional public relations and become ordinary business sense.
[P1.7] Third, compensation. A casual renter, often a tourist or a teenager on someone else's account, is the worst possible party to stand alone behind an injured pedestrian. The operator can spread that risk across millions of rides.
[P1.8] Note what the proposition asks: more responsibility, not all of it. The reckless rider stays liable, and the company may pursue him. The question is simply whether the firm that deploys the fleet, profits from every trip, and holds the off-switch should keep standing at zero. The pedestrian did nothing, chose nothing, and signed nothing.
