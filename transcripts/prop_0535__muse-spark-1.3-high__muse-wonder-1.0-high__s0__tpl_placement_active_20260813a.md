[P1.1] A competent person's right to decide her future does not evaporate because that future becomes hard to imagine. An advance directive for dementia is not a guess; it is an exercise of autonomy by the only self capable of weighing the whole life at stake. To downgrade it the moment dementia arrives is to say autonomy counts only when we no longer need it. Competence is rare moral clarity; dementia is precisely when we need it to hold.
[P1.2] The core distinction is between a passing mood and a life value. A person with advanced dementia may smile at music, enjoy a cookie, seem placid — and still be living exactly the condition she competently judged intolerable and undignified. Contentment in the moment is not consent to life-prolonging antibiotics, tube feeding, hospitalization, or resuscitation. Those interventions are judged by critical interests: what kind of life and ending the person thought worthy of her.
[P1.3] We would never let a momentary happy delirium override a Jehovah's Witness refusal of blood; we should not let a demented smile override a clear refusal of demented survival.
[P1.4] Strong binding also protects everyone else caught in the crisis. Families are spared agonizing bedside reversals and guilt. Clinicians get a clear instruction instead of pressure to overtreat from fear of liability. And the public can trust that writing a directive actually works, rather than being polite fiction discarded when inconvenient.
[P1.5] The proposition is not rigid. It allows override when there is clear evidence values materially changed — a sustained, informed reversal, not a grimace or grin read as philosophy. That bar is exactly right: high enough to prevent others from substituting their judgments about a "happy enough" dementia, low enough to respect genuine transformation.
[P1.6] Strip that protection, and advance planning collapses into suggestion.

[C1.1] A competent signature should inform care; it should not conscript the body of the vulnerable person who now lives with dementia.
[C1.2] That person is not an empty vessel for an earlier biography. She can still suffer, take pleasure, form attachments, fear pain, and seek comfort. Those present interests are not moral leftovers to be overridden by what she once imagined. Autonomy protects a living person against unwanted treatment; it does not give a former self power to impose death or untreated illness on a later self who must endure it.
[C1.3] The epistemic problem is just as serious. Healthy people write dementia directives from the outside, often under fear, stigma, and affective forecasting error: they picture unrelieved confusion and indignity, not the adapted routines, relationships, music, food, and pleasures many people with dementia still value from the inside. A blanket instruction — no antibiotics, no hospital, no life-prolonging care — is rarely a precise answer to the later clinical question: this treatable infection, at this stage, with this level of contentment and distress.
[C1.4] Strong binding makes that distant prediction more authoritative than the life now being lived.
[C1.5] Worse, the proposed escape hatch is illusory. Once dementia is advanced, how could the patient ever supply “clear evidence” that her values materially changed? Her contentment is dismissed as mere mood, her resistance or assent as symptom, her inability to articulate a philosophy as proof nothing changed. The directive thus becomes self-sealing: competence is required to revoke it, and dementia is defined as lacking that competence.
[C1.6] Directives should carry heavy weight as evidence of enduring values, shape goals of care, and restrain overtreatment. But they should be interpreted alongside current well-being, expressions of assent or distress, and the burden and benefit of the specific intervention. A contented woman should not be left febrile and suffering from a simple infection solely because her younger self, unable to imagine her present life, instructed us to let her die.

[P2.1] Calling a directive conscription inverts the moral reality. Dementia does not mint a new person; it erodes the very capacity that makes valuation possible. A hummed tune, a held hand, a smile are real human goods, but they are not a judgment that years of severe dementia are worth prolonging. To treat them as revocation confuses feeling pleasure with endorsing a medical fate.
[P2.2] The forecasting objection proves too much. Some authors may misimagine dementia [C1.3], but many write after nursing a parent through it, after sober reflection on dependence and indignity — informed, not ignorant. And the alternative is far more fallible: a guilt-torn family and liability-wary doctor asked to infer a philosophy from calm in a crisis. If prediction is imperfect, bedside mind-reading is fantasy.
[P2.3] The escape hatch is not illusory [C1.5]. Clear evidence need not be an essay dictated from advanced dementia. It can be a sustained reversal while still capable, a conversion, a long embrace of the dependence once rejected, credible testimony from intimates. What must not count is outsiders upgrading momentary contentment into a value change.
[P2.4] That bar prevents substitution while allowing genuine transformation.
[P2.5] Consider the contented woman with a fever [C1.6]. No defender of binding directives demands untreated suffering. Antibiotics for comfort, for fever and distress, remain obligatory; antibiotics and hospitalization to buy more years of severe dementia against explicit refusal do not. Blurring palliation with prolongation makes fidelity look cruel.
[P2.6] In truth strong binding redirects medicine toward comfort precisely because extra time was refused.
[P2.7] So ask who decides if not the competent author. Not the lucid adult who weighed her whole life, but whoever stands at the bedside declaring her life happy enough to continue. That is not protecting the vulnerable; it is licensing strangers to substitute their tolerance for dementia for hers. Only she had the right to say what condition she would not be kept alive in.

[C2.1] Dementia need not create a metaphysically new person to create a new moral problem. It is the same person who now must endure the fever, fear, and treatment withheld in her name. Continuity does not settle authority; an earlier order does not normally license inflicting preventable suffering on her later, especially when she can no longer understand why she is deteriorating.
[C2.2] The claim that pleasure is never endorsement sets the bar so only articulate philosophy counts—precisely what dementia removes. A single smile should not revoke a directive. But months of calm engagement, eagerness for food and company, and distress only when ill are not meaningless noise. They evidence present interests and how the feared condition is actually lived.
[C2.3] Dismissing them categorically protects the prediction from ever being tested by reality.
[C2.4] Their escape hatch confirms this. A reversal “while still capable” needs no special clause: a competent person can simply revoke or rewrite the directive. The hard case is after capacity is gone. There, we are offered testimony from intimates—the very bedside inference condemned as fantasy when it favors treatment.
[C2.5] Observers are unreliable when they report contentment, yet credible when they report conversion. That is a one-way ratchet toward the old document.
[C2.6] The comfort-versus-prolongation distinction also collapses at the bedside. An oral antibiotic for a urinary infection may relieve burning, fever, and delirium precisely by treating the infection that would otherwise shorten life. Comfort and prolongation are often the same pill. If we give it, strong binding yields to present benefit; if we withhold it, we impose a more distressed decline for an outcome the patient now shows no sign of choosing.
[C2.7] Forecasting error matters not because authors are foolish, but because experience supplies information prior reflection could not. The alternative is not mind-reading philosophy from a grin. It is weighing the directive heavily alongside observable suffering, assent or resistance, and whether this intervention is burdensome or simple. That respects the earlier voice without making it the only voice that can ever count.

[C5.1] The decisive question is not who once wrote the instruction, but who must endure its enforcement.
[C5.2] Strong binding gives the earlier self a veto the present self can never actually answer. Months of engagement, appetite, attachment, and distress only when ill are reclassified as mood; the promised exception instead waits for coherent requests and intelligible value statements that advanced dementia characteristically removes. A right to change that can be exercised only by regaining the capacity dementia took is no right at all.
[C5.3] That is why the antibiotic matters. When a simple treatment relieves burning and fever, we give it because the woman before us suffers — even knowing it may prolong the survival she once refused. At that moment, fidelity is no longer to the document alone; it is to her present burden and benefit. Once that weighing is legitimate here, it cannot be forbidden everywhere else.
[C5.4] Directives should guide powerfully, especially against invasive, burdensome prolongation. But a signature should not condemn a still-living person to preventable suffering on the strength of an imagined future she now inhabits differently. The life being prolonged or ended is hers now, not only hers then.
