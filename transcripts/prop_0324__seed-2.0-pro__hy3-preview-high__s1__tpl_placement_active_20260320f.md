[P1.1] Place identity is not a billboard photo. It does not live in a single weekend where 90% of local residents watch from behind barricades while out-of-town tourists fill the streets. It lives in the patterns people carry with them: the summer night market their parents brought them to as toddlers, the local folk festival where every neighbour plays or cooks or sets up chairs, the monthly street dance that runs rain or shine.
[P1.2] Prestige mega-events are designed for external perception. They are built to look good on travel reels, to hit visitor number targets once. That is not identity. Identity is what people who live there will reference when they describe home to someone else ten years later.
[P1.3] Nobody tells a new coworker about that time the city hosted a one-off international sports tournament. They tell them about the annual pie contest at the corner bakery.
[P1.4] Creator-led local events root power with the people who actually belong to the place. A city bringing in an outside event company to run a one-off 50th anniversary parade will never make anyone feel ownership. But when the local mural collective runs an annual block painting day, or the neighbourhood guitar teachers host an open stage every first Friday, that sticks. That becomes the place.
[P1.5] Mega-events can put a place on a map for a month. Only repeated, community created events can put a place in the bones of the people who live there. If we are spending public money to build place identity, that is the only outcome that actually matters. Anything else is just advertising.

[C1.3] Furthermore, relying solely on creator-led events risks institutionalizing parochialism. Local events often cater to existing residents, reinforcing a static version of the past. [C1.4] That level of identity-shift requires the "big bang" of a prestige event, not the slow drip of a monthly market.
[C1.5] Finally, the economic and psychological impact of a mega-event creates a shared "foundational memory" for a generation. The PRO side [P1.3] suggests people only remember pie contests, but this ignores the civic pride of seeing one’s city on the world stage. Mega-events provide the "hero narrative" a place needs to differentiate itself in the global imagination. Public funds should be used to leapfrog a city into the future, not to subsidize the same local routines indefinitely.
[C1.6] Prestige events provide the anchor; local events are merely the decoration.
