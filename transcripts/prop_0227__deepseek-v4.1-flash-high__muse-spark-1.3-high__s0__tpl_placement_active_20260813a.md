[P1.1] Ransomware is not a crime problem alone. It is a business model. Attackers do not need to hack every target; they need enough victims to pay. Every ransom is R&D, affiliate commissions, call-center staffing, and proof that extortion works.
[P1.2] Prohibiting ransom payments attacks that revenue stream directly. Funding victim support ensures the ban does not leave hospitals, schools, and small businesses to bleed.
[P1.3] The current voluntary approach fails because it asks each victim to solve a global problem alone. A hospital locked out of patient records faces an impossible choice: pay and restore care, or refuse and absorb chaos. Private rationality produces collective ruin. A legal ban changes the default.
[P1.4] Insurers cannot quietly reimburse; executives cannot authorize a payment and bury it; negotiators lose their market. That is not symbolism—it raises the cost and risk of the entire ransomware economy.
[P1.5] Critics will say victims need payment as a last resort. But payment is often a false rescue: attackers may demand more, leak stolen data anyway, or return because they know the victim pays. The real last resort is government-funded rapid response—forensic teams, backup reconstruction, continuity grants, legal safe harbor for reporting. That support makes refusal survivable.
[P1.6] A ban plus support is a bargain: we protect victims from the attackers and from their own desperate choices.
[P1.7] The proposition’s two halves are not in tension; they are the same policy: remove the attacker's revenue, remove the victim's reason to pay.
[P1.8] Yes, ransomware is global. A single country cannot eliminate it. But coordinated bans in major markets shrink the paying pool, force attackers to spend more on fewer targets, and make payments harder to launder. The alternative—unlimited ransom payments plus scattered advice—guarantees growth.
[P1.9] If we keep funding criminals, we will keep getting criminals. Stop the money, support the victims, and the ransomware market shrinks.

[C1.3] When lives and livelihoods are on the line, the state should expand options, not outlaw the last resort.
[C1.4] The promised victim support cannot fill that gap. Government rapid-response teams sound reassuring until thousands of schools, clinics, and small firms need help simultaneously. Support helps recovery, but it is not a substitute for decryption when time matters most.
[C1.5] Worse, a ban makes the problem harder to see and stop. If payment is illegal, victims will not report; they will hide breaches, use intermediaries, or route crypto quietly to avoid liability. [C1.6] A ban is also profoundly unequal. Large corporations can survive weeks offline with lawyers and redundant systems. A small manufacturer or rural clinic cannot; for them, a ban means close the doors to prove a point about deterrence.
[C1.7] And the global deterrence logic collapses under evasion. Ransomware groups are transnational. They will simply prioritize targets in countries without bans, demand more from those who can still pay, and exploit shell contractors and offshore affiliates to disguise payments. The paying pool does not disappear; it goes dark and moves.
[C1.8] The alternative is obvious: mandate prompt reporting, require real backup and security standards, fund disruption of infrastructure and exchanges, and let victims decide with expert advice. Do not make desperation illegal.
