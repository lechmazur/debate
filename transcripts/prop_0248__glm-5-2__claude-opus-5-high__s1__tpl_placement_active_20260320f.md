[P1.1] Family obligations don't expire at adulthood. The law already enforces this principle: parents who fail to support dependent children face neglect charges. That duty exists because the parent created the dependency—chose to bring a child into the world and accepted responsibility for that child's survival. The same principle runs in reverse.
[P1.2] A parent who raised, fed, and housed a child created a relationship of mutual dependence that doesn't vanish when the parent becomes the vulnerable one.
[P1.3] This proposal is deliberately narrow. It triggers only when parents cannot support themselves—not a blank check for luxury care. It asks adult children with means to contribute to subsistence-level care for parents who would otherwise go without. The scope is safety net, not open-ended liability.
[P1.4] Without such a duty, the public absorbs the full burden. Consider a parent in nursing care with two employed adult children who simply choose not to help. Taxpayers—many struggling themselves—cover what family could share. The absence of filial duty lets capable relatives free-ride on public assistance while preserving their wealth for inheritance.
[P1.5] That is privatized gain and socialized cost, dressed up as personal freedom.
[P1.6] The abuse objection is real but not decisive. We don't abolish child support because some parents are terrible. We build exceptions and safeguards. Estrangement, abuse, and genuine inability to pay are conditions for exemption, not reasons to abandon the rule entirely.
[P1.7] A legal duty of filial contribution is reciprocal justice. Those who benefited from years of parental care owe something when that parent faces destitution. The alternative shifts every case to the state, burdening taxpayers who had no role in the family relationship while letting the closest, most capable relatives walk away.
