[P1.1] A league that sells Saturday-afternoon fandom to ten-year-olds should not be permitted to sell those same ten-year-olds to bookmakers. That is the whole of this motion: when youth make up a large share of viewers, that audience closes to betting firms.
[P1.2] First, the mechanism. Sponsorship is not incidental advertising; it fuses a product to a child's heroes. A logo on the jersey a kid wears to bed, odds read out at halftime, a bookmaker branding the highlights clip — it teaches that gambling is simply part of what sport is. Children are the most persuadable audience there is: brand loyalties form early, skepticism late.
[P1.3] Betting firms know this. Nobody spends fortunes on shirt fronts and stadium boards to reach people who already bet; the money buys the next generation of customers.
[P1.4] Second, the stakes. Gambling is addictive in a way soft drinks and sneakers are not. Exposure to gambling marketing young is associated with earlier betting and heavier losses among young adults — exactly the group where gambling harm concentrates: debt, mental-health crises, broken families. And because industry profits lean on a minority of heavy losers, recruiting young is not a side effect of these deals.
[P1.5] It is the business plan.
[P1.6] Third, the precedent. Sport already accepts that some products cannot ride its reach into childhood. Tobacco sponsorship was stripped from football and Formula One, and the games survived. Italy and Spain have banned or sharply restricted gambling sponsorship, and England's top clubs have agreed to pull betting logos from shirt fronts — proof this money can be refused without collapse.
[P1.7] The other side will invoke revenue and adult choice. Revenue is fungible: another sponsor fills the slot, while a twelve-year-old's wiring is not replaceable. And choice is precisely what childhood marketing corrodes. Where children watch in large numbers, leagues owe them a clean game.
