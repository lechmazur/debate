[P1.1] A mandatory retirement age fires a worker for a date on a calendar, not for anything they have done. That is the whole problem: it treats age as a substitute for capability, and it is a bad substitute.
[P1.2] Modern workplaces already have the tools to assess actual fitness for a role. Annual physicals, recertification exams, competency testing, driver's licence requirements, peer review, performance metrics — these measure what matters: whether this person can do this job safely and well today. A 63-year-old surgeon who passes every skills check is dismissed while a mediocre 50-year-old stays. Chronology is the laziest possible proxy for competence, and we should stop using it as a routine management shortcut.
[P1.3] The strongest version of my case is not that every 80-year-old should fly an aircraft. It is that where genuine safety stakes exist, the right response is individualized fitness testing with clear, job-related standards — the same standard we would apply to a young worker with a medical condition. Blanket age cutoffs sweep out the capable majority to catch the impaired few who fail tests anyway. Aviation, policing, and firefighting forces in various countries have moved toward functional testing rather than pure age cutoffs precisely because strength, vision, and reaction time vary enormously between individuals at the same age.
[P1.4] If a standard is truly necessary, state the standard — don't smuggle it in as a birthday.
[P1.5] The costs of the status quo are real and compounding. Populations are aging and skilled workers are scarce; pushing experienced doctors, engineers, and tradespeople out the door wastes training we paid for and drains mentorship from younger staff. It also deepens the retirement savings crisis: people denied the chance to work longer are people pushed toward old-age poverty, disproportionately women with interrupted careers.
[P1.6] Abolition does not mean no management. Contracts, performance review, and negotiated pension arrangements all remain. It means the end of a rule that punishes a class of workers for a characteristic irrelevant to whether they can do the job.
