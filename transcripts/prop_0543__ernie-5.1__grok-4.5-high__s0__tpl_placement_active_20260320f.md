[P1.1] When a twelve‑year‑old signs up for a platform, the default should protect them, not expose them. Requiring privacy‑by‑design defaults for minors is a narrow, measurable intervention that cuts the most common sources of online harm—data harvesting, predatory contact, and algorithmic amplification of harmful content—without touching what anyone can say or see.
[P1.2] First, the harm reduction is concrete. Default private accounts, disabled location sharing, and opt‑in data collection slash the information that advertisers and bad actors use to profile children. Studies on adolescent online safety repeatedly show that when the safest settings are the default, minors receive fewer unsolicited messages, encounter less age‑inappropriate advertising, and report fewer grooming attempts. The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation already imposes stricter rules for children; early compliance reports indicate a sharp decline in data‑driven targeting of minors, a pattern a focused default‑privacy rule can replicate.
[P1.3] Second, this is not censorship. The regulation does not restrict speech, content, or viewpoints. It merely reshapes the background architecture—who can see a profile, what data can be harvested, and how algorithms may use that data. A teenager can still post, comment, and browse; they simply do so behind a stronger wall that prevents the platform from turning their activity into a commodity or a weapon.
[P1.4] Third, the policy is enforceable and proportionate. Large platforms already possess the technical capacity to toggle default settings at scale. A law that mandates private‑by‑default accounts, data minimisation, and limited ad profiling requires no new technology, only a legislative nudge. The cost to free expression is negligible; the benefit to children’s safety is demonstrable.
[P1.5] In sum, a default‑privacy mandate targets the mechanism of harm, leaves speech untouched, and can be measured through reduced data exposure and incident reports. That is precisely the kind of evidence‑based, low‑censorship regulation governments should adopt.

[C1.1] The proposition fails on its own terms. Mandating default “privacy-by-design” settings for minors will not produce durable, measurable harm reduction, and the machinery needed to make it stick will expand speech and association controls far beyond a harmless toggle.
[C1.2] Defaults are not destiny. Adolescents routinely change privacy settings, open secondary accounts, misstate ages, or shift activity to less regulated apps the moment restrictions bite. Private-by-default profiles mainly throttle stranger discovery of public posts; they leave peer bullying, group-chat pressure, acquaintance grooming, and the minor’s own consumption of content largely untouched. Once a teenager joins closed friend networks or simply opts out, the data surface and the social risks reappear.
[C1.3] Without continuous age enforcement and behavioral monitoring—which the proposition does not claim—the promised drop in unsolicited messages or predatory contact is short-lived theater, not a stable metric.
[C1.4] Worse, turning the default into a legal requirement forces platforms into age-gating at scale. Age estimation, document checks, or biometric signals create new surveillance trails, exclude or silo users who cannot clear the gate, and give platforms a compliance incentive to over-restrict messaging, follows, algorithmic reach, and even posting opportunities labeled “minor.” Constraining who can see, contact, or recommend a profile is not neutral architecture; it is a speech and association limit. Platforms facing liability will darken more content and more interactions than the narrow privacy rule ever required.
[C1.5] True limits on commercial data harvesting can be pursued through consent rules and competition without the state redesigning every minor’s default social graph. This mandate confuses architecture with safety while handing regulators a pretext for broader controls. The harm reduction is fragile; the censorship expansion is structural.
