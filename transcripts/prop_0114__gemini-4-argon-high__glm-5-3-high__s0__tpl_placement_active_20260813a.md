[P1.1] Imagine two people spending forty hours a week caring for an adult with severe dementia. One works in a care facility, earns a wage, and accumulates social-insurance credits toward retirement. The other cares for her own mother at home, saving the public healthcare system immense sums—yet the pension system records her contribution as zero. Treating unpaid family care as economic non-existence is not fiscal neutrality; it is state free-riding.
[P1.2] Contributory pension systems were built around a flawed premise: that only market wages represent valuable labor. Yet the formal economy cannot survive without unpaid care. Someone must raise the next generation of taxpayers and tend to aging or disabled relatives. When family members leave a job or cut hours to provide that care, they suffer a double penalty: they forfeit immediate income today, and they are punished with a gutted pension decades later.
[P1.3] That second penalty systematically manufactures old-age poverty. Because unpaid caregiving falls disproportionately on women, treating care work as invisible is a primary driver of the gender pension gap. A worker who spends years raising young children or nursing an ailing spouse should not reach retirement only to find the state treats that vital labor as an extended vacation.
[P1.4] Social-insurance credits are the fairest, most pragmatic remedy. First, they are fiscally smart: granting deferred pension credits costs a fraction of what governments would pay if families stopped providing informal care and shifted that burden onto public institutions. Second, credits reduce future spending on means-tested welfare by building earned retirement security up front.
[P1.5] Third, the policy is administratively straightforward: governments can tie credits directly to existing birth registries, child-benefit records, or medical assessments of a relative’s care needs, as many pension systems already do.
[P1.6] A society that relies on family caregivers to hold up its social safety net has no right to cut them out of it when they grow old.

[C1.2] Start with what a credit actually does: it pays a caregiver nothing for decades, then delivers money at retirement, roughly proportional to how much formal pension she already earned. That means the biggest beneficiaries are those with long earnings histories—people who dipped out of work briefly. The woman who spent twenty years out of the workforce gets a thin supplement stacked on a thin pension. If the injustice is lost income and lost career, the remedy must arrive during the caregiving years: cash carer allowances, respite care, subsidized formal care, or wage replacements.
[C1.3] Telling a fifty-year-old with no income, "we'll help you at seventy-five," is a deferral, not a solution.
[C1.4] Second, credits create incentives that no one wants. They pay families to withdraw labor from the formal care sector—precisely where we need workers—and to keep relatives out of professional care even when institutional support would serve them better. They reward people who would have provided care anyway, at full cost. That is a subsidy for behavior, not compensation for sacrifice.
[C1.5] Third, the administrative claim collapses on contact with reality. Paid care work is verifiable: an employer, a wage, a contract. Unpaid care is diffuse and self-reported. Who certifies that a daughter provides "forty hours" of care?
[C1.6] At what intensity threshold? Caring for an aging parent is near-universal; drawing the line anywhere either excludes the truly burdened or converts the pension system into a universal transfer dressed up as insurance.
[C1.7] Finally, the framing is confused. Caregivers are not invisible—most welfare states already pay child benefits, carer allowances, and tax relief. The question is whether pension credits are the best marginal tool, and on fiscal cost, targeting, timing, and incentive effects, they lose to nearly every alternative.
