[P1.1] When a democracy goes to war, it is supposed to feel it. Soldiers come home in flag-draped coffins. Legislatures vote on troop ceilings. Courts-martial punish those who kill unlawfully.
[P1.2] Those frictions are not bugs; they are the entire machinery by which citizens restrain their governments' use of force. Private military contractors in combat exist precisely to remove that friction. That is why they must be banned from combat roles, not merely regulated.
[P1.3] Consider three mechanisms. First, contractors erase the visible cost of war. Contractor dead are not counted in official casualty tallies, not honored at ceremonies, not reported on the evening news with the same weight. In Iraq and Afghanistan, contractors at times outnumbered uniformed troops, and thousands died largely unnoticed.
[P1.4] A public that cannot see the price of a war cannot judge whether it is worth paying.
[P1.5] Second, contractors let executives wage war around the legislature. When a parliament or Congress caps troop numbers, the executive simply hires the difference. The "boots on the ground" number stays politically acceptable while the actual armed footprint doubles. Democratic authorization becomes a formality that bounds nothing.
[P1.6] Third, contractors escape legal accountability for killing. Soldiers answer to military justice; contractors fell into jurisdictional gaps. Blackwater guards who killed Iraqi civilians at Nisour Square operated under an occupation order granting immunity from local law, and the domestic prosecutions that followed took over a decade, were partly overturned, and ended in presidential pardons. When killing on a state's behalf carries no reliable legal consequence, the state's monopoly on force has been quietly privatized.
[P1.7] Expect to hear that the answer is better oversight. But oversight has been promised for twenty years, and the gaps persist because they are structural: a commercial actor's incentive is to keep its principal's war cheap, quiet, and deniable. You cannot regulate away the reason the product is bought.
[P1.8] The ban is narrow: combat roles only. Logistics, training, maintenance remain. What citizens lose is nothing; what they regain is control over who kills in their name.

[C1.1] Democratic accountability does not require everyone firing a weapon in a state's name to wear the same uniform. It requires that an elected government authorize the mission, fund it publicly, set its rules, and answer for failure. Contractors can satisfy that test, and banning them would weaken, not restore, democratic control.
[C1.2] A contractor force exists only because a legislature appropriated money and an executive signed a contract for a defined task. Parliament can cap contractor numbers as it caps troops, require casualty reporting, subpoena company executives, cut funding, or cancel the contract — often faster than withdrawing a deployed brigade. If lawmakers choose not to ask, that is legislators evading their job, not contractors evading democracy. Removing one tool leaves the abdication untouched.
[C1.3] A ban would not end controversial wars. It would push them toward less accountable substitutes. A government facing an embassy defense, a hostage rescue, or a sudden surge will turn to covert units, intelligence paramilitaries, foreign proxies, and local militias with no written contract, no filed rules of engagement, and no firm to sue or debar. A contractor, by contrast, leaves a paper trail: a price, a purchaser, and a chain voters can trace.
[C1.4] Legal gaps likewise argue for enforcement, not abolition. Contractors can be subjected to home-state criminal law, host-state law, civil liability, and loss of contract, while uniformed soldiers have hardly been immune from impunity, delayed justice, or political pardons. Punish unlawful killing and mandate disclosure of every person deployed; do not pretend wrongdoing disappears when the shooter is salaried.
[C1.5] Without a voluntary, temporary supplement, democracies must keep far larger standing armies, extend exhausted troops, revive conscription, or abandon missions voters support. Contractors let an elected government match force to an authorized purpose, then end it by ending a contract. That reversibility is accountability in practice.
