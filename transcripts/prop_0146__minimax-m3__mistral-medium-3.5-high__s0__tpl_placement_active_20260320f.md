[P1.1] The proposition before us is straightforward in principle, urgent in practice, and modest in scope. We are not asking to outlaw family vlogging, not to imprison parents, not to erase the past. We are asking whether an adolescent should have a legally recognized way to say: please take down what you posted about me without my consent, content that is still circulating to strangers long after the cute moment passed.
[P1.2] The central clash is this. Family vlogging turns a child's name, face, routines, and embarrassments into a commercial product. The parent is the producer, the platform is the distributor, the stranger is the consumer, and the child is the raw material. In that arrangement, the one person with the least power and the most to lose is the child.
[P1.3] The parent collects the revenue and the clicks. The child collects a permanent digital record they did not choose, and that record is indexed, searchable, and often impossible to scrub by ordinary means.
[P1.4] Consider the cases that should anchor this debate. A twelve-year-old on a school trip has classmates pull up a video of her tantrum at age six, seen by huge numbers of strangers. A fifteen-year-old applies to college, and admissions officers see footage of a bedtime mishap the family once thought was charming. A sixteen-year-old comes out as transgender, and old videos of the wrong name and pronouns sit on a parent's channel, monetized.
[P1.5] These are not hypothetical edge cases. They are the predictable products of a content model that treats children as inventory.
[P1.6] The adolescent's request, when it comes, is not a rejection of the family. It is the first exercise of a basic right: the right to control one's own public image. Legal recognition of that right at adolescence is overdue, because the harms compound with time, and because parents, however loving, have an obvious financial conflict of interest that makes voluntary restraint unreliable.
[P1.7] That is the case I will defend.
