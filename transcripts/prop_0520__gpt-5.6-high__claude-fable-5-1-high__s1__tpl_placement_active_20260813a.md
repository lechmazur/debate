[P1.2] Credential recognition addresses the right question—whether a person can safely and competently perform the job. Labor-market testing asks employers to demonstrate that no suitable local worker is available, often through prescribed advertising or delay. That process is a poor proxy for genuine need. Vacancies change quickly, local availability varies by region and specialty, and an unfilled posting does not show whether applicants possess the required experience.
[P1.3] Employers bear extra delay and uncertainty; hospitals, construction projects, and businesses bear the vacancy; the public bears reduced services and output.
[P1.4] Faster recognition need not mean automatic recognition. [P1.5] The policy goal is not lower standards, but faster answers and proportionate remedies.
[P1.6] Tighter labor-market testing also protects workers less effectively than its advocates suggest. An employer willing to underpay can manipulate recruitment formalities; a responsible employer with an urgent, legitimate vacancy still suffers delay. Wage floors, equal labor protections, licensing rules, and enforcement against exploitation target abuse directly. Blocking qualified migrants does not train a local nurse, engineer, or electrician in time to meet an immediate need.
[P1.7] The decisive comparison is between allocation and obstruction. Faster recognition converts existing human capital into tax revenue, essential services, higher productivity, and fuller integration. Tighter testing spends administrative capacity trying to prove a negative about a constantly changing labor market.
