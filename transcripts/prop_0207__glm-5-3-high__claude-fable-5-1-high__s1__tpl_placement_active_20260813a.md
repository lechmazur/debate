[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies fail the very people they claim to protect. A government-set fuel price below market value benefits whoever burns the most fuel — and the poor burn the least. The wealthy household owns two cars, runs air conditioning, and drives long distances; the poor household walks, shares a bus, or uses a motorcycle sparingly. When a dollar of subsidy is spent, the majority of it flows uphill.
[P1.2] This is not a theoretical quibble: studies of energy subsidy reform across the developing world consistently find that the top income quintile captures several times more subsidy value than the bottom quintile. Universal price controls are, in practice, regressive welfare for the rich, paid for by the whole treasury.
[P1.3] Targeted cash transfers reverse that arithmetic. Give the poorest households the cash directly, and the same fiscal envelope reaches them with no leakage to SUV owners. Indonesia's 2005 and 2013 reform rounds are instructive: when fuel prices were raised, the government paired the move with unconditional cash transfers to low-income households, and independent assessments found poverty did not spike — the compensation worked. Iran attempted something similar in 2010.
[P1.4] These programs have real implementation problems, but a flawed transfer beats a perfectly targeted subsidy to billionaires.
[P1.5] Price controls also distort everything downstream. Cheap fuel means overconsumption, worse congestion, urban air pollution, and higher carbon emissions — while draining treasuries so badly that spending on health, schools, and infrastructure gets crowded out. That crowding-out is itself regressive: poor households depend on public services far more than the rich, so every budget dollar burned as fuel subsidy is a dollar taken from the services that actually reach them. Subsidy removal in Egypt, India, and elsewhere repeatedly freed fiscal space for exactly those programs.
[P1.6] The proposition is not "abolish help." It is: keep the help, change the delivery. Cash transfers are cheaper per dollar delivered to the poor, transparent, auditable, and preserve the price signal that encourages efficient energy use. Broad price controls do none of this.
