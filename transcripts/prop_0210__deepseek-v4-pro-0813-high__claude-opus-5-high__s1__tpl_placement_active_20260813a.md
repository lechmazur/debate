[P1.1] A medicine can be sold only because regulators decide its benefits outweigh harms. That decision is impossible if the company controls which evidence exists publicly. Negative results are not failed trivia; they are safety and efficacy data. Requiring their disclosure as a condition of market access makes the privilege of selling conditional on completing the scientific record.
[P1.2] Without public disclosure, negative trials become invisible. A favorable trial can be published twice while several null or harm-signal trials remain unreleased, making a weak drug look strong. Doctors, guideline panels, and purchasers then make decisions on a biased sample. Patients receive treatments that are no better than alternatives or that carry hidden risks.
[P1.3] The cost is measured in unnecessary side effects, delayed effective care, and wasted public money.
[P1.4] Voluntary registries and selective journal policies have not fixed this. Companies still have strong incentives to bury unfavorable findings because market access and prescribing depend on perceived benefit. A condition attached to market access changes the incentive: if hiding null or harm results delays or revokes the right to market, disclosure becomes commercially rational, not optional corporate virtue.
[P1.5] The likely objection is that compulsory disclosure burdens innovation or exposes proprietary information. But trial-level outcome data need not reveal manufacturing trade secrets, and the burden of posting datasets is small compared with the cost of marketing a product. The larger competitive harm is the status quo: a company that hides a failed trial gains an unfair advantage over honest competitors.
[P1.6] Regulators and the public should not have to bargain with companies for evidence about medicines people are already taking. Public disclosure should be a precondition, not an afterthought. Market access is a license to profit from public health; the public has a right to see the evidence on which that license rests.

[C1.1] Nobody here defends burying data. The question is the lever. PRO wants to attach the hostage of market access — the patient's ability to get the drug — to a paperwork condition. That lever misfires in three predictable ways.
[C1.2] First, the penalty lands on the wrong people. If a company files late, files incompletely, or disputes what counts as "negative," the sanction PRO's mechanism threatens is delay or revocation of marketing. That means a cancer patient or a diabetic loses access to a medicine regulators have already judged safe and effective. You do not punish a corporation by rationing its product to sick people.
[C1.3] Fines, publication orders, and personal liability for executives hit the company directly and leave patients untouched. If a better instrument exists for the same goal, the market-access condition is not principled — it is just clumsy.
[C1.4] Second, "negative results" has no clean edge. Is an underpowered trial negative? A trial with a nonsignificant primary endpoint but positive secondaries? A discontinued Phase II?
[C1.5] Regulators already receive all of this — the sponsor must submit every trial, favourable or not, and agencies publish assessment reports describing them. So the real marginal gain is smaller than PRO implies, while the cost is a vague condition that invites litigation, gaming, and companies defining categories to their own advantage.
[C1.6] Third, the incentive runs the wrong way at the margin. Negative data is the most commercially valuable data in pharmacology, because it tells rivals which targets are dead ends — knowledge bought at enormous cost by one firm and handed free to every competitor. Tie forced publication to the right to sell, and firms respond rationally: run fewer exploratory trials, avoid head-to-head comparisons, skip post-marketing studies that might generate publishable failure. The trials that are never run produce no negative results to hide, and no knowledge either.
[C1.7] And "countries should require" means fragmented, unilateral regimes. Faced with a hostile small market, a firm simply doesn't file. That is not transparency. That is a country trading its patients' access for a database entry.

[P2.1] CON’s central trade-off is false. It treats continued sale on a selectively published record as a patient benefit. It is not. A patient prescribed a drug because a favorable trial is public while null or harm-signal trials are hidden is already bearing the penalty of secrecy.
[P2.2] The access CON defends is access under distorted information.
[P2.3] The “wrong people” objection [C1.2] also misdescribes the mechanism. Disclosure need not mean instant revocation for a late upload. It can be enforced like other regulatory conditions: fines, suspension of new indications, and withdrawal only for deliberate concealment. Companies pay first; patients are not the automatic hostage.
[P2.4] And if deliberately hidden results reveal real harm, keeping the drug on the market is what punishes patients most.
[P2.5] The definitional objection is a lawyerly dodge, not a practical failure. No one needs a metaphysical category of “negative.” The condition can require posting all completed interventional trials for the authorized product, with prespecified primary outcomes, key secondary outcomes, and safety results. CON itself says regulators already receive all this [C1.5]. That makes public disclosure a marginal administrative step, not an impossible new adjudication.
[P2.6] The incentive argument also cuts against CON. If negative data were mainly a free gift to rivals, firms would not need compulsion to hide it; they hide because publication lowers prescribing enthusiasm and damages the product’s image. Those are precisely the public-relevant effects the motion targets. The claim that firms will run fewer trials is speculative: a company does not abandon a profitable market to avoid posting final results.
[P2.7] And if disclosure deters low-value trials run only to generate favorable noise, that is a feature, not a cost.
[P2.8] Finally, fragmentation is an argument for coordinated major-market action, not against any requirement. Large regulators already set de facto global standards. A firm that refuses to sell where transparency is required makes a commercial choice; it does not prove the policy harms patients.
