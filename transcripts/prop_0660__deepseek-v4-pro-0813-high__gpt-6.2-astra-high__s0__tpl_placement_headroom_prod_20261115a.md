[P1.1] The real question is whether off-hours responsiveness is a neutral productivity tool or a cost imposed on workers. In knowledge industries, it is the latter. Constant availability fragments attention, delays recovery, and converts salaried expertise into 24/7 monitoring. Right-to-disconnect laws change the default from 'always on' to 'protected off,' and that shift improves welfare more than it damages output.
[P1.2] The welfare gain is direct. Workers gain predictable evenings, sleep, and family time without fear that ignoring a message will hurt evaluations or advancement. That reduces chronic stress, anxiety, and burnout—major drivers of illness and turnover. These are not peripheral benefits; they are core elements of worker welfare, especially where cognitive work follows employees home through phones and laptops.
[P1.3] The claimed productivity loss is overstated. Knowledge output depends on judgment, coding, writing, and analysis, not rapid replies. A 10 p.m. interruption rarely produces better code or strategy; it often produces a tired person making worse decisions the next morning. Connectivity norms create shallow work, error, and presenteeism.
[P1.4] By forcing managers to batch requests and clarify genuine emergencies, the law reduces unnecessary digital noise. The visible cost—a delayed non-urgent message—is small. The hidden gain—recovered focus and fewer mistakes—can be larger.
[P1.5] Firms also avoid deeper costs. Burnout drives expensive attrition in knowledge roles, taking tacit project knowledge and client relationships with it. Healthier, rested workers stay longer and produce more consistently, so even productivity measured over months may improve, not fall. Exemptions for real emergencies, on-call roles, and time-zone arrangements preserve genuinely time-sensitive work.
[P1.6] Even if some coordination friction remains, it is limited and adjustable. The welfare benefit is broad and cumulative. A right to disconnect is not a right to shirk; it is a right to replenish, and replenished workers are the most productive asset a knowledge firm has.
