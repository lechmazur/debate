[P1.1] Large social platforms already pick a goal for your feed. It is just not yours. It is to keep you scrolling, reacting, and returning, because that is what sells attention. Calling that feed personalized confuses prediction with purpose: it predicts what will hold you, not what you came to do.
[P1.2] A student opening an app to revise, a parent checking on relatives, a worker following industry news, and the same person wanting light comedy at night do not need one compromise ranking. They need different goals at different moments: learn something durable, catch up with close ties, see timely updates, be lightly entertained, or encounter views they would not normally choose. Far more control means making those goals explicit and choosable — presets, sliders, and a real chronological or following-only option — instead of forcing every intention through an engagement filter.
[P1.3] That misalignment is where familiar harms come from. Content that outrages, shames, or teases the next revelation is often excellent at retention even when users regret the hour afterward. A goal they select themselves changes the test for success: a learning feed can reward completion and saved resources over arguments in comments; a close-friends feed can reward recent posts from chosen people over viral strangers; a calm feed can down-rank pile-ons. Users cannot correct a system whose objective they are not allowed to set.
[P1.4] This is practical, not utopian. Platforms already juggle internal objectives, run ranking experiments, and segment users invisibly. The proposal moves some of that existing power into the open: keep a sensible default for people who never touch settings, but make the goal visible, switchable in one tap, and sticky until changed. Choice also improves creators, because success would no longer mean gaming one mystery metric to reach everyone at once.
[P1.5] One feed for everyone is not neutrality — it is the seller planning your attention. Users should supply the purpose.
