[P1.1] Cities cannot police their way out of a housing failure. Homelessness is, by definition, the absence of housing. People sleep on streets because there is nowhere affordable to sleep, and stay there because services for addiction, mental illness, and job loss are inaccessible, fragmented, or conditional on sobriety they cannot yet maintain.
[P1.2] A strategy centered on policing treats the symptom as the disease. Encampment sweeps, move-along orders, citations for sitting or sleeping — these do not create one bed. They shuffle people block to block, destroy ID, medication, and phones needed to get housed, and create criminal records that landlords screen out. Every contact costs dispatch, officer time, jail beds and court dockets, and ends with the same person back on the same sidewalk days later, angrier and less trustful of any outreach worker who follows behind the police.
[P1.3] Treating it primarily as housing and service-access solves the actual deficit. A housing-first model provides a stable unit with no preconditions, then wraps voluntary case management, addiction treatment, and mental health care around it. When people have a door that locks, they keep appointments, take medication regularly, and reduce substance use. Cities that scaled this — Houston's systematic reduction of chronic homelessness, Finland's national conversion of shelters to apartments — saw sustained drops, not just relocations.
[P1.4] That also collapses public spending: one chronically homeless person can generate enormous costs in ER visits, ambulance rides and jail stays that fall dramatically once housing stabilizes them.
[P1.5] If the goal is genuine public order, housing is still the better tool for order. Shop owners and residents do not want a corner cleared for a morning; they want fewer tents over months. Policing can do the former. Only housing provides an alternative to the corner itself.
[P1.6] That is why officers themselves often say they are the wrong tool — social workers with handcuffs.
[P1.7] Secondary enforcement still matters when behavior threatens safety, but as the primary frame, policing fails even on its own terms. Housing and access succeed on both compassion and order.
