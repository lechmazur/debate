[C1.2] First, the resilience argument dominates any marginal price effect. A tariff raises costs by a calculable percentage today; supply chain coercion or geopolitical rupture halts deployment entirely tomorrow. When a foreign supplier restricts semiconductors or solar cells, there are no substitutes at any price. Tariffs build redundancy and strategic depth.
[C1.3] A domestic solar industry that meets even half of national demand prevents the catastrophic scenario where deployment stops dead because shipping lanes are closed or export controls are imposed. That optionality is worth far more than a temporary price differential, because a halted deployment cannot be restarted by writing a check.
[C1.4] Second, the alleged "slowdown" is actually a reallocation within the domestic economy. Tariff revenue and protected demand remain at home, funding the factories, workforce training, and R&D that make future deployment cheaper, faster, and more reliable. This view treats frictionless imports as a permanent entitlement, but import dependence is a structural vulnerability, not a baseline. Domestic manufacturing shortens supply chains, reduces shipping emissions and lead times, and insulates deployment from currency shocks and foreign industrial policy.
[C1.5] Every dollar of tariff is a dollar invested in the deployment infrastructure of the future.
[C1.6] Third, learning curves accelerate with protected volume. The same economies of scale that drove down costs abroad can operate domestically once capital is confident that demand will not be perpetually undercut by subsidized foreign overcapacity. The deployment that matters is not just this year's gigawatts, but the decade-long trajectory of cost decline and manufacturing depth. Without that depth, deployment remains hostage to foreign boardrooms.
[C1.7] The proposition weighs a temporary price wedge against a permanent industrial base. That arithmetic fails. We do not sacrifice deployment for manufacturing; we secure deployment through manufacturing.

The dollar comes out of a utility's project budget, an automaker's bill of materials, or a household's purchase decision. It goes to the treasury as revenue, minus the deadweight loss of projects that don't get built and vehicles that don't get sold.
[P2.4] The "investment" CON describes is imaginary; the cost to deployment is booked this quarter. A cancelled solar farm or a consumer who keeps their combustion car doesn't become capacity later.
[P2.5] Nor do "learning curves accelerate with protected volume" [C1.6]. Learning follows cumulative production volume, and broad tariffs suppress total volume precisely when cost declines are steepest. Protected domestic output at higher cost does not substitute for global learning-by-doing — and it doesn't exist yet anyway.
[P2.6] Meanwhile cheap imported panels were themselves the input that made domestic installation, development, and integration industries scale. Taxing those inputs slows learning in the deployable parts of the stack to protect a manufacturing segment that cannot yet deliver.
[P2.7] The weighing is straightforward: certain, front-loaded deployment losses versus delayed, partial, uncertain capacity gains — and CON's crisis scenario isn't even insured by the policy it defends.

[C2.1] PRO assumes that without tariffs, factories would exist anyway, so tariffs only raise prices. That reverses causality. Domestic clean-tech manufacturing requires a protected customer base to justify the capital risk. When foreign competitors benefit from state subsidies and overcapacity, no rational investor builds a semiconductor fab or solar ingot plant facing immediate undercutting.
[C2.2] The tariff is not a tax on deployment; it is the market signal that makes the factory — and thus future deployment capacity — possible. Yes, tariffs may raise some input costs temporarily, but that friction builds the domestic upstream capacity to replace those inputs. Without that signal, capital flees overseas. PRO's "price wedge" is actually the price of admission to a domestic industry.
[C2.3] The "deadweight loss" framing treats tariff costs as money burned. In reality, those dollars become revenue for domestic producers, wages for skilled workers, and the tax base that funds the deployment infrastructure PRO claims we need. More importantly, PRO ignores the catastrophic alternative: when deployment depends entirely on foreign supply, a single export restriction or shipping disruption doesn't raise prices by ten percent — it stops projects entirely. That is the deployment loss that actually matters, and it dwarfs any marginal cost increase.
[C2.4] On learning curves, PRO confuses global volume with secure volume. Learning-by-doing in a foreign factory whose government can restrict exports does not make American deployment cheaper or safer; it makes us dependent on their permission. Protected domestic volume, even if initially smaller, creates irreversible know-how, supplier networks, and process innovations that compound over time and stay onshore.
[C2.5] The weighing is simple: PRO offers marginal project delays this year in exchange for permanent strategic vulnerability. CON offers temporary price adjustments in exchange for the manufacturing depth that makes deployment sustainable. The proposition asks whether slowing deployment outweighs building capacity. It does not — because capacity is what guarantees deployment in a crisis, not cheap imports.
