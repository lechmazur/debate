[P1.1] If compensation depends on audience reach, viewing data is not marketing insight. It is the time clock. You cannot pay someone per view while telling them viewing is confidential. That is why residuals, bonuses, and performance tiers have become unenforceable promises.
[P1.2] A streamer is employer, distributor, and scorekeeper at once. It decides how a "view" is counted, whether it counts, and whether it triggered your payment, with no outside check. In theatrical film box office was public; in broadcast Nielsen was independent. Streaming replaced both with an opaque internal dashboard.
[P1.3] The WGA and SAG-AFTRA struck over exactly this - artists seeing their shows in Top 10 while being told they didn't earn a bonus. You cannot negotiate future value when you cannot prove past value.
[P1.4] Standardization is essential because without it disclosure means nothing. One platform counts two minutes as a view, another counts 70% completion, a third counts "accounts" not viewers. Those definitions determine money. A required, uniform metric - like hours viewed divided by runtime, reported in consistent windows - stops gaming the math and lets a performer compare Netflix to Hulu offers with real numbers.
[P1.5] This does not require dumping algorithms or public charts. It requires confidential, audited reports to the people whose pay depends on them, just as payroll records are shown to the employee. That narrow fix restores bargaining, reduces litigation, and makes streaming a functioning labor market instead of a black box where the house always says it lost.

[C1.1] The proposition contains its own escape hatch. It applies only "when compensation depends on audience reach." A streamer that dislikes the reporting burden has an obvious response: stop offering reach-based compensation. Pay a flat buyout, own the show outright, share nothing.
[C1.2] That is already the dominant streaming model, and this mandate accelerates it. The performer who today has a shot at a performance bonus becomes the performer with a fixed fee and no upside. A rule designed to protect the time clock ends by getting the clock removed from the wall.
[C1.3] Second, "standardized" is doing enormous, unexamined work. Hours viewed divided by runtime is proposed as the neutral metric. It is not neutral. It rewards the ninety-minute thriller people finish and punishes the children's series watched in eight-minute bursts, the documentary paused and returned to over a month, the comedy special replayed in clips.
[C1.4] Any single uniform metric becomes the thing everyone optimizes for. Studios already chase bingeability; a legally sanctioned formula makes it the only thing that counts. Filmmakers who think they are getting transparency are actually getting a state-approved definition of what a good film is.
[C1.5] Third, the problem described is a contract problem, and contracts are solving it. The 2023 union agreements produced viewership-based bonuses with data disclosed to the guilds precisely because the parties could tailor definitions to their own deals and adjust them at the next negotiation. A mandate freezes one definition across every format, every platform, every country, and hands its future revisions to a regulator who does not make television.
[C1.6] Finally, scale. Distributing detailed audience behavior to thousands of contractors is not "payroll records." Payroll is a number about you. Viewing data is a map of what subscribers do, and once it circulates that widely it leaks, gets sold, and gets weaponized against the very artists it was meant to protect when their next pitch comes with a public failure attached.
[C1.7] Transparency negotiated is leverage. Transparency mandated is a formula, a loophole, and a leak.
