[P1.1] A license to braid hair is not a shield against danger; it is a toll booth. When a state forces a braider to sit through hundreds of hours of cosmetology training focused on chemical treatments and hair cutting—skills she will never use—the rule is not calibrated to risk. It is calibrated to keep newcomers out. The same logic applies to interior design, where choosing fabrics and furniture layouts does not threaten public safety in any way that justifies a state-issued gatekeeping credential.
[P1.2] If the concern were structural integrity, the state would license engineers and contractors for that narrow task, not regulate aesthetic decisions across the board. The mismatch between the supposed harm and the mandated remedy is the smoking gun.
[P1.3] Licensing boards in these fields are typically staffed by industry insiders who control the number of training hours, the cost of examinations, and the scope of permitted work. That is not a neutral public-safety apparatus; it is an incumbent protection racket wearing the language of consumer welfare. When entry costs rise, wages and prices rise for those already inside, while aspiring workers—often from marginalized communities, as with traditional braiding—are pushed into unemployment or illegal markets. The discretion to define training hours and exam costs is exercised in the interest of those who already hold seats at the table.
[P1.4] Proponents will claim that these rules prevent shoddy work. But low-risk service failures are best handled by reputation, contract remedies, and liability, not by preemptive state prohibition. No one dies from a bad throw pillow. A license is a blunt instrument for a scalpel problem.
[P1.5] The burden of proof belongs to those who would restrict entry: they must show a specific, demonstrable hazard that only a license can address. They cannot, because the requirements themselves are too broad, too expensive, and too convenient for the established players. The evidence points to protectionism, not protection.

A braid set too tight produces traction alopecia and lasting follicle damage. Unclean tools and stations spread fungal infections, lice, and bacterial scalp disease. Heat, adhesives, and chemical products—routine even in many braiding shops—burn skin and trigger reactions.
[C1.2] The customer sees a finished style, not whether the practitioner knows when to stop, how to sanitize, or which products are unsafe on broken skin. A license requires that knowledge before the first appointment. Reviews and lawsuits arrive after the hair is already gone.
[C1.3] Interior design is not cushions and color boards. Practitioners specify finishes, lighting, layouts, and furnishings that interact with fire performance, egress, accessibility clearances, and ordinary load and installation limits. A plan that narrows an exit, uses highly flammable textiles in an occupied space, or ignores mobility clearances can injure people or lock them out of safe use. Those are technical failures, and a client cannot audit them from a rendering.
[C1.4] Code literacy is the point of the credential. Engineers own structure; designers still make choices that determine whether a room is safe to occupy.
[C1.5] The same logic covers other low-risk trades: delayed chemical exposure, sanitation failures, and installation errors that are hard to price because harm shows up late and causation is muddy. Liability and reputation punish some bad work after the fact. They do not restore a scarred scalp, reverse an injury in a fire, or equip the customer who lacked the expertise to choose correctly the first time. Minimum-competence screening is the ex ante safeguard.
[C1.6] Practitioner seats on boards reflect technical knowledge, not a racket. Excess hours are a calibration flaw, not proof that the license is mainly a toll. Narrower braiding rules have generally preserved health and sanitation training—the safety core—rather than dropping oversight. The requirements track preventable harm.
[C1.7] That is protection, not protectionism.
