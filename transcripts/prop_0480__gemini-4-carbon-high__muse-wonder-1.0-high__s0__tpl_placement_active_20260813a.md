[P1.1] Cash only buys freedom when competitive markets already supply affordable choices. For foundational necessities—healthcare, childcare, transit, and internet—markets are plagued by local monopolies, supply bottlenecks, and sudden cost spikes. Given the same public budget, expanding universal basic services builds permanent capacity and pools risk, whereas unconditional cash transfers get devoured by the very market failures citizens face.
[P1.2] First, universal services expand supply and slash unit costs, while cash poured into constrained markets fuels price extraction. Handing families modest monthly checks in a childcare desert or a single-provider broadband town does not build classrooms or lay fiber. Because demand for essentials is inelastic, private gatekeepers capture much of that cash through steeper prices. Direct public provision fixes the bottleneck itself.
[P1.3] Through economies of scale and collective bargaining—buying medicines in bulk, running high-capacity transit, or wiring whole neighborhoods at once—governments deliver far more real value per dollar than individuals buying piecemeal at retail markups. One frequent bus line connects thousands of workers to jobs for a fraction of what individuals spend trying to bridge transit gaps with small stipends.
[P1.4] Second, universal services pool uneven human needs that flat cash leaves dangerously exposed. Splitting a finite budget into identical checks spreads money thinly across everyone. That arithmetic fails the moment hardship strikes. Equal cash gives a healthy, childless adult a modest cushion, yet leaves a parent paying for infant care or a patient needing insulin hopelessly underwater.
[P1.5] Universal healthcare and childcare act as seamless social insurance: nobody faces demeaning means tests, and resources surge automatically to households hit by steep medical or caregiving costs, allowing parents to work and keeping families solvent.
[P1.6] Real autonomy requires guaranteed access to the infrastructure of modern life, not a small check surrendered to rent-seeking gatekeepers. Dollar for dollar, universal basic services permanently lower the cost of living instead of leaving citizens to outbid each other for scarce essentials.

[C1.1] Give people money, and they solve the problem in front of them. Give them a bus pass, a distant childcare slot, and an appointment months away, and government has decided their problem for them — often wrongly.
[C1.2] First, needs are radically unequal in kind, not just size. A rural parent needs a car repair to reach work, not a transit network that will never reach her road. A night-shift worker needs a neighbor paid for overnight care, not a center open nine to five. A young adult needs rent, training, or money to move for a job, not cheaper broadband.
[C1.3] Unconditional cash is the only instrument that converts the same public dollar into whatever each household lacks most. Universal services convert it into the same bundle for everyone, wasting resources on people who do not use them and leaving unlisted needs — food, rent, debt, heating, work tools — unfunded.
[C1.4] Second, public provision is slow, rigid, and politically captured. Building transit, staffing clinics, and licensing childcare takes years, locks spending into buildings and payrolls, and invites allocation by lobbying rather than need. Once created, services ration by queue, postcode, and bureaucracy, however universal in name. Cash is fast, portable, and disciplining: recipients can leave a bad provider, choose a private, nonprofit, or family arrangement, and force suppliers to compete.
[C1.5] That competition does more for quality and innovation than a state monopoly insulated from exit.
[C1.6] Finally, handing out services does not escape scarcity; it hides it. If childcare places or doctors are short, cash at least sends a clear signal and revenue that draws new providers in. A government service suppresses that signal, crowds out alternatives, and caps supply at whatever officials planned last year. The result is not guaranteed access, but guaranteed uniformity at a level politics can afford.
[C1.7] Real security is purchasing power people control, not permission to use what the state chose to build.

Cash to a healthy adult can be saved, spent on rent, or used when illness strikes; a free bus network given to someone who does not need it, or could already afford it, is value permanently sunk.
[C2.2] Their fungibility answer makes our case. If wiping out one bill frees wages for another [P2.2], giving the money directly is strictly better, because the household chooses which bill matters. A childless family gets zero relief from expanded childcare, however efficient its bulk purchasing [P1.3]; a parent preferring a grandmother or night-shift neighbor gets nothing usable from a centre with fixed hours. A service worth less to its recipient than it cost taxpayers is not stretched value; it is destroyed value.
[C2.3] On supply, there is no contradiction. Cash does not conjure a doctor overnight; it enables thousands of incremental responses public plans cannot make — a home provider adding places, a clinic extending hours, a driver, satellite connection, or car-share filling a rural gap — while public construction is still consulting and procuring [P2.3]. Public expansion faces the same bottleneck they attribute to cash: hiring scarce nurses and childcare workers bids up wages and poaches them from existing providers. Cash lets supply follow people; services require people to follow supply.
[C2.4] Finally, promising extended hours, telehealth and licensed home providers [P2.5] concedes the point. To work, 'universal' provision must mimic cash — portable, varied, chosen. In practice politics funds standard provision in standard places, then rations it by queue. Cash puts the scarce dollar with the person actually facing scarcity.
