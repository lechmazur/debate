[P1.1] An NDA that silences a harassment victim is not a neutral settlement tool; it is a repeat offender’s insurance policy. When courts enforce these clauses, they lend state power to conceal discrimination, harassment, and abuse—wrongs that harm more than the individual signer.
[P1.2] Workplace harassment, discrimination, and abuse are not ordinary commercial secrets. They are serious legal harms with systemic patterns. A contract that suppresses information about a serial harasser does not just settle a private dispute; it hides a continuing danger. Public policy has long refused to enforce contracts that conceal crimes or obstruct justice.
[P1.3] There is no principled reason to treat a sexual assault cover-up as more enforceable than a fraud cover-up.
[P1.4] Second, the apparent consent behind an NDA is often fictional. The employer controls the victim’s job, references, and legal resources; the victim often signs under financial pressure, without meaningful discovery, and with a threat of litigation. Enforceable secrecy converts that pressure into a permanent gag. It isolates victims, discourages reporting, and shields the institution that failed to protect them.
[P1.5] Third, enforceability is how predators move undetected. Repeated abuse scandals follow the same pattern: money is paid, silence is required, and the same person is sent to another office, another company, or another set of employees. Making NDAs unenforceable removes the legal guarantee that the next victim will not hear the truth. It forces institutions to choose between real accountability and ongoing legal exposure.
[P1.6] Opponents will say this reduces settlements. But the motion does not ban compensation; it only denies a court order silencing disclosure of harassment, discrimination, or abuse. A victim who wants privacy can still choose not to speak. What should not exist is a contractual right to force that silence and punish the victim for warning others.
[P1.7] Courts should not be the enforcement arm of a cover-up. These agreements should be unenforceable because the cost of silencing is not just one worker’s voice—it is the next worker’s safety.

[C1.1] Silence can be coerced. It can also be chosen. This motion erases the difference, and victims will pay for it.
[C1.2] Most harassment and discrimination claims never see a verdict. They settle because trial means years of cost, invasive discovery, brutal cross-examination, and professional ruin. In settlement, confidentiality is not just the company’s shield; it is the victim’s leverage. Employers pay more, offer better references, and agree to mutual silence precisely because the promise is enforceable.
[C1.3] Make that promise legally worthless and the premium disappears. Companies will offer less, or refuse to settle at all and force a scorched-earth fight to clear their name. The wealthy plaintiff who can endure trial gains a megaphone. The low-wage worker who needs rent money and a clean exit loses her best bargaining chip.
[C1.4] Worse, the ban strips privacy from those who desperately want it. A hotel housekeeper, a junior analyst on a visa, a gay man harassed in a conservative shop — many do not want their trauma to become public record or industry gossip. Under this rule a victim may choose not to speak, but she cannot buy the other side’s silence, because no court will enforce it. Her story becomes currency anyone else at the table can spend.
[C1.5] And the predator story proves too much. An allegation is not a finding. Many claims are disputed, ambiguous, or false. A blanket rule lets any unproven accusation be publicized even after money changes hands with no adjudication, no defense, no proportionality.
[C1.6] That does not produce accountability; it produces rumor as punishment and deters reporting systems that depend on promised confidentiality.
[C1.7] Protect the right to report to police and regulators. Require lawyers, time, and victim control. But keep consensual confidentiality enforceable. Justice should expand a victim’s options, not confiscate them.
