[P1.1] Remote and hybrid workers should be evaluated mainly on outputs because outputs are the product an organization actually buys. Presence signals—green status, keystrokes, mouse movement, immediate replies—are proxies at best and theatre at worst. [P1.2] Monitoring measures availability as though availability alone delivered value.
[P1.3] That metric also shapes behavior. Constant activity monitoring teaches employees to perform for the monitor: keep status green, respond quickly to low-value messages, generate visible motion, stretch tasks to fill the day. The rational employee optimizes what is measured, and surveillance measures showmanship more than substance. Output-based evaluation reverses the reward.
[P1.4] It gives credit for closed problems, met deadlines, delivered quality, and real improvements. It makes expectations objective and makes failure about the work, not about seeming engaged.
[P1.5] Output evaluation is also fairer across a distributed workforce. Remote workers differ by time zone, home conditions, disability, family duties, and work rhythm. Presence tracking punishes the parent working after bedtime, the engineer who blocks notifications to focus, and the employee who works in intense bursts. Outputs strip out that arbitrary noise and judge what matters: the result.
[P1.6] That reduces bias and gives quiet, high-producing contributors credit for their work rather than their chat responsiveness.
[P1.7] This does not mean ignoring collaboration or process. But constant monitoring should be an occasional diagnostic when outcomes falter, not the daily grading system. [P1.8] Focusing mainly on outputs aligns evaluation with value, incentives with real work, and fairness with the actual conditions of remote and hybrid employment.
