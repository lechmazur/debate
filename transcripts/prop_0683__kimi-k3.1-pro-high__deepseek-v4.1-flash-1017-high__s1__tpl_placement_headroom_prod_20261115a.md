[P1.1] A suspension can erase a creator’s income, sever a community organizer’s network, or lock a small business out of its customers. Notice, reasons, and a genuine appeal should be the legal minimum before users are suspended or banned.
[P1.2] First, due process prevents errors without preventing moderation. Platforms would still write and enforce their rules; they would simply have to identify the rule violated, explain the basis for the sanction, and allow the user to contest it. A short explanation often exposes these mistakes immediately.
[P1.3] Requiring reasons also disciplines the platform itself, discouraging arbitrary enforcement and revealing whether rules are applied consistently.
[P1.4] Second, this protects expression by constraining punishment, not by compelling platforms to endorse speech. [P1.5] Third, the burden is modest and scalable. This motion concerns account suspensions and bans, not a courtroom trial for every deleted post. Notice can be automated, reasons can be concise, and appeals can begin with rapid internal review, escalating difficult cases to humans. Serious cases can use expedited procedures without leaving dangerous content visible.
[P1.6] Large platforms already moderate at enormous scale; basic procedural fairness is a design obligation, not an impossible expense.
[P1.7] Finally, legislation is necessary because voluntary policies can be changed, ignored, or reduced to empty forms whenever fairness conflicts with engagement or public relations. The choice is not moderation versus chaos. It is accountable moderation versus unreviewable punishment. [clipped]

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a principle for a universal legal remedy. Due process is a constraint on the state because the state can imprison, fine, and prosecute; a social platform can suspend an account under terms the user accepted, and the user can leave for another service. That does not make every ban costless, but it makes a courtroom-style entitlement the wrong tool.
[C1.2] A legal notice-reasons-appeal mandate would not merely “discipline” platforms. It would hand every spammer, scam network, and harassing account a procedural lever. Notice tells bad actors what detection caught and when; reasons become a map for evasion; appeals become bulk queue-flooding. Platforms would have to keep accounts active or restore them while review proceeds, or build expensive human appellate machinery.
[C1.3] Large platforms can absorb that; small ones and startups cannot, entrenching incumbents.
[C1.4] PRO says errors are predictable. Yes—but the answer is faster correction and targeted transparency, not a pre-suspension legal hearing for every account. If a hacked account is locked, an automated recovery path fixes it; if satire is misread, an expedited review can restore it. A universal mandate also collides with urgent cases: scam accounts, coordinated harassment, and incitement require immediate action, and “emergency exceptions” would either swallow the rule or force platforms to wait while harm spreads.
[C1.5] Finally, the legal standard is unworkable: what counts as sufficient reasons for a context-dependent moderation call? Courts would be asked to supervise millions of daily decisions, or regulators would impose one template that ignores community norms. The result is not accountability; it is slower, more defensive moderation, more procedural gaming, and less room for diverse platforms to set their own rules. Voluntary transparency, competition, and narrow remedies for demonstrable bad faith beat universal legal due process.
