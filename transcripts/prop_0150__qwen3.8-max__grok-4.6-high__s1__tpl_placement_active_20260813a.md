[P1.1] Parasocial monetization is not ordinary commerce. It converts one-sided intimacy into spending pressure: a viewer who feels known by a streamer sees their name climb a top-donor leaderboard, receives a personalized thank-you, then gets a limited-time upsell framed as support. The question is whether platforms must let users build guardrails against that machinery. They should.
[P1.2] Our central principle is effective consent. A user can consent to buying a subscription; they cannot meaningfully consent to an interface engineered to exploit loneliness, status anxiety, or habit. Hiding top-spender rankings removes public status bidding. Disabling highly personalized creator upsells lets people opt out of messages that feel like personal requests even when they are automated or templated.
[P1.3] These are not paternalistic bans; they are user-controlled switches. A creator can still sell content, memberships, and transparent perks. The user simply gets a way to say: do not turn my attention into a pressure funnel.
[P1.4] This matters most for the vulnerable: adolescents, isolated adults, and people with compulsive spending tendencies. Platforms already recognize these risks by offering screen-time limits and ad personalization controls; spending guardrails are the same logic applied to monetization. The burden should not fall on individual willpower when the platform controls the ranking, the notification, the scarcity cue, and the emotional framing.
[P1.5] Opponents will say this hurts creators. But the proposition does not eliminate tipping or subscriptions; it shifts defaults toward user sovereignty. If revenue depends on making it hard for users to set limits, that is not a market we should defend. Judges, the decisive test is whether platforms should empower users to protect their own boundaries.
[P1.6] Because parasocial monetization thrives on blurred boundaries, stronger opt-out tools are the proportional, freedom-preserving remedy.
