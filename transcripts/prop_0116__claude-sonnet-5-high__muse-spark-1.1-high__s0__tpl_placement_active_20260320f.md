[P1.1] Every year, thousands of students leave programs — some coding bootcamps rebranded as master's degrees, some for-profit certificate tracks, some niche graduate programs — with five- or six-figure debt and job prospects that never justify it. When those loans default, the university that sold the credential walks away untouched. It already collected tuition. The student's ruined credit, garnished wages, and diminished life options are not its problem.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the whole case for PRO.
[P1.3] Here is the mechanism that should drive this debate: whoever bears zero risk has zero reason to fix what's broken. Universities set admissions standards, curriculum, program size, and marketing claims. They also possess something students don't — years of aggregated data on where their own graduates end up. A university running a master's program that reliably produces graduates earning less than their loan payments knows this, or should.
[P1.4] Right now that knowledge carries no cost. Attach even partial financial liability to repeated failure, and suddenly the program gets redesigned, shrunk, or cut — not because a regulator ordered it, but because the institution's own balance sheet demands it.
[P1.5] Notice the proposition's careful scope: this isn't about any single default, any unlucky graduate, or any inherently risky field. It targets programs that repeatedly leave graduates underwater relative to debt — a pattern, not a fluke. That framing forecloses the strongest objections in advance. We are not asking a philosophy department to guarantee outcomes for one struggling grad; we are asking an institution to answer for a track record it has had years to correct and chose not to.
[P1.6] Consider a concrete case: a for-profit university's paralegal certificate program where graduates routinely earn barely above minimum wage against $30,000 in debt, for a decade running. The university profits every single year that program stays open. Under the current system, the only party who pays when that debt collapses is the graduate — and ultimately, taxpayers through federal default absorption. Cost-sharing simply moves the bill toward the party that actually controls the product.

[C1.1] Education is not a toaster. Labor demand for teachers, social workers, artists, or paralegals is set outside any campus. Holding a university financially liable for loan defaults mistakes correlation for control.
[C1.2] The premise in [P1.3] that universities possess the data and levers to fix earnings is false except at the margins. A rural regional public cannot redesign its way to higher wages when the local wage pyramid is flat.
[C1.3] Attach a price to low earnings and universities will rationally respond, but not how PRO hopes [P1.4]. They will not magically raise wages. They will do what any risk manager does: avoid risk. That means closing programs that serve low-income students who need to borrow more, raising admissions cutoffs to screen out first-generation students statistically likely to earn less early, and shuttering socially essential but modestly paid fields.
[C1.4] The philosophy major, the public school teacher pipeline, the community college nursing assistant certificate with high social value but low starting pay — all become liabilities.
[C1.5] The costs will not land on the wealthy brand-name for-profits in [P1.6]. They will land on open-access public institutions that enroll borrowers because their students lack family wealth, while elite campuses whose students graduate debt-free skate by. To cover that liability, tuition rises, or access falls. That is the opposite of accountability.
[C1.6] If the goal is informed choice, publish earnings data. If the goal is borrower protection, expand income-based repayment that ties payments to actual earnings. Do not turn colleges into co-signers for a labor market they do not control.
