[P1.1] Start with the number that frames everything: roughly one in three adults has prediabetes, and the large majority have no idea. That is not a fringe worried-well population; it is the modal person walking past a pharmacy. Our current system catches them, if at all, through an annual fasting glucose or A1c—a single snapshot, often years after metabolic damage began. Continuous glucose monitors replace that snapshot with a movie.
[P1.2] The case rests on one mechanism: feedback changes behavior when it is immediate, personal, and actionable. "Eat healthier" is abstract advice everyone already knows and mostly ignores. "That bowl of oatmeal sent you to 170 and the eggs didn't" is a specific lesson about your own body, delivered within an hour. A ten-minute walk after dinner visibly flattens the curve.
[P1.3] Poor sleep visibly worsens the next morning. No pamphlet does that. This is the same reason home blood-pressure cuffs and step counters moved from clinic tools to household objects: measurement you can see is measurement you act on.
[P1.4] Notice what the technology pushes toward. Nobody looks at a glucose trace and reaches for a prescription; the levers it reveals are food order, portion, movement, and sleep. If "over-medicalization" means more drugs and more procedures, CGMs cut the other way—they shift effort upstream, before the metformin, before the retinopathy screening.
[P1.5] On anxiety: yes, some people will fret over a post-meal spike that is physiologically ordinary. But watch what actually happens to that person. After a week, they see the spike resolve every time, and they learn that a healthy body is forgiving. Lived data is a cure for vague dread, not a cause of it.
[P1.6] Meanwhile the person it alarms appropriately—the one whose levels stay elevated all afternoon—gets to a doctor five years early, while reversal is still possible.
[P1.7] So the weighing is asymmetric. The downside is bounded: some unnecessary appointments, some transient worry. The upside is unbounded: averted diabetes, dialysis, amputation, cardiovascular death. A sensor that trades a little nervousness for that is a public-health bargain.

We are trading certain, widespread anxiety for a hypothetical population benefit.
