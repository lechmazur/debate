[P1.1] A nursing home resident cannot shop around. She is frail, often cognitively impaired, moved once at great cost, and dependent on strangers for food, medication, and turning in bed. Markets discipline sellers through exit; here, exit is nearly impossible. That single fact is why ownership structure matters more in elder care than in almost any other industry, and why the private-equity model is uniquely mismatched to it.
[P1.2] Private equity is not just "an investor." It is a specific playbook: buy with heavy debt loaded onto the acquired company, hold for roughly five to seven years, extract value through management fees, dividend recapitalizations, and sale-leasebacks of the real estate, then sell. Every one of those levers works against a bedridden resident. Debt service crowds out staffing.
[P1.3] Selling the building and renting it back converts a stable asset into a permanent rent obligation that the home must meet before it buys nurses. A five-year horizon means the owner captures the savings from thinner staffing while the consequences—falls, pressure ulcers, infections—land after the exit.
[P1.4] This is not speculation. The collapses of Southern Cross and Four Seasons in Britain, and HCR ManorCare in the United States, followed exactly this pattern: real estate stripped, rents fixed, operating company hollowed until it broke, and governments left to rescue thousands of residents. Economists studying American nursing homes after buyouts have found measurably higher mortality and lower staffing relative to comparable homes. Homes can wear out under any owner; this model wears them out on schedule.
[P1.5] The state has both standing and tools. It licenses these facilities and, through Medicaid and Medicare or the NHS and councils, pays most of the bills—so taxpayers currently fund the returns. "Restrict" need not mean ban: cap leverage at acquisition, prohibit sale-leasebacks that separate building from operator, require disclosure of every related-party payment, and give regulators authority to block ownership changes that fail those tests.

[C1.1] My opponent’s premise is right and the prescription is wrong. Frail residents cannot exit [P1.1], which is exactly why protection must bind every owner to enforced outcomes, not banish one type of shareholder. The scarce thing in elder care is not virtuous ownership; it is capital, management capacity, and payment that covers the true cost of care. Restricting private equity attacks a label while leaving the engine of failure untouched.
[C1.2] Consider the home nobody wants: rural, outdated, deferred maintenance, serving Medicaid residents. A family owner wants out; local non-profits lack balance-sheet capacity. The buyer able to renovate, install records, centralize billing, and recruit staff is often private-equity-backed. Bar that capital and the likely result is closure, delayed upgrades, or sale to a thinly capitalized owner who defers the same investments.
[C1.3] The beds do not become safer; they become scarcer or more neglected. Residents are protected not by excluding investors, but by regulators requiring minimum staffing, audited falls and infection metrics, liquidity reserves, and public reporting from whoever signs the license.
[C1.4] The collapses my opponent cites [P1.4] are real, but they indict leverage, inadequate public payment, and weak oversight—conditions that also sink non-PE homes. Buyers frequently acquire already-troubled chains, so worse outcomes afterward may reflect inherited distress, not investor identity. Since government already pays most bills and grants the license, the fix is to pay adequately and enforce standards. An ownership test is a blunt proxy: it deters capital that can upgrade distressed homes, while ignoring cost-cutting by family owners or chains.
[C1.5] Leverage caps and sale-leaseback bans [P1.5] can be replaced by targeted rules: fair-rent limits, reserve requirements, related-party disclosure. Ownership neutrality with hard accountability protects more residents than excluding one investor class. Residents are safer when the state polices care and pays for it, not when it restricts private equity and hopes the shortage disappears.
