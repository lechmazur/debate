[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are a blunt, regressive mistake. Gulf and North African governments should phase them out within five years and replace them with targeted cash transfers.
[P1.2] The subsidy does not mainly help the poor. Fuel use rises with income, cars, and air-conditioned space. Wealthier households capture most of the benefit; a family relying on shared transport or walking receives little. That is an upside-down transfer, not social protection.
[P1.3] Cash transfers reverse it. Existing identity systems, utility records, and proxy means tests can identify vulnerable households and pay them directly. Leakage remains, but it is smaller and easier to correct than a price that cannot tell a luxury vehicle from a livelihood.
[P1.4] The fiscal cost is unsustainable. Subsidies lock large shares of volatile oil revenue or scarce tax receipts into an open-ended price guarantee, crowding out schools, clinics, and infrastructure. Importers bleed foreign exchange; exporters mortgage future budgets to today’s pump price. A published five-year glide path—stepwise price increases, with savings recycled into transfers—lets households adjust and governments build payment rails already emerging through mobile money and national IDs.
[P1.5] Predictability beats the sudden crises that follow when treasuries simply run out.
[P1.6] Price distortions do further damage. Cheap fuel encourages waste, congestion, and smuggling across borders, while weakening incentives for efficiency and public transport. Removing the subsidy does not abandon citizens; it stops subsidizing overconsumption and redirects support to people rather than liters. Transfers can be indexed or topped up when prices spike, protecting real purchasing power without freezing the entire energy market.
[P1.7] Political risk is real, which is why the motion requires a phase-out and a replacement, not an overnight cut. Blanket subsidies have themselves produced shortages, black markets, and abrupt collapses. Gradual adjustment plus visible compensation is more stable than promising cheap fuel forever. Compassion here means reaching the households that actually need help, not preserving a costly illusion.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to mandate a fixed five-year deadline and a wholesale swap to targeted cash transfers across two very different regions. That rigidity is exactly what should be rejected.
[C1.2] Start with capacity. Targeting only works if you can find the poor. Large parts of North Africa run on informal labor, undocumented income, and patchy national ID coverage. Egypt, Morocco, and Tunisia have made progress on registries, but building a system accurate enough to replace a universal subsidy for tens of millions of households, on a fixed five-year clock, invites massive exclusion error.
[C1.3] The family PRO worries is being overlooked by fuel subsidies is exactly the family a proxy-means test misses when it lacks a bank account, a formal payslip, or a fixed address. Universal subsidies are blunt, but blunt instruments don't exclude anyone by administrative accident.
[C1.4] Second, fuel is not just a consumer good, it is an input. Its price runs through transport, irrigation, food distribution, and manufacturing costs. A cash transfer calibrated to today's fuel price becomes stale the moment global oil prices move, and they move often and sharply. Indexing transfers in real time, across volatile currencies and inflation, is a far harder administrative feat than adjusting a subsidized pump price.
[C1.5] PRO's own transfer mechanism is more fragile to the very volatility they cite as the case against subsidies.
[C1.6] Third, the political economy risk cuts against PRO, not for it. Fuel subsidy removal has repeatedly triggered unrest across this region precisely when timed rigidly rather than in response to real fiscal and social conditions. A mandated five-year glide path removes the flexibility governments need to pause, slow, or accelerate reform around actual oil price shocks and social tolerance. Locking a specific calendar deadline into policy, rather than a conditional and reversible one, raises the odds of the abrupt crisis PRO claims to be preventing.
[C1.7] The honest reform path is gradual, means-tested where systems allow it, and flexible on timing, not a fixed five-year universal replacement mandate.
