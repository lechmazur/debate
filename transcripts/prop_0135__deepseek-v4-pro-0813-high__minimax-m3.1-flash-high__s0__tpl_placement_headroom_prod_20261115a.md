[P1.1] Historic cities are finite places: narrow streets, old housing stock, fragile waterfronts. A cruise ship or parade of tour buses does not simply add visitors; it concentrates thousands into spaces never designed for that load. The proposition’s trigger is precise: cap arrivals when resident crowding, pollution, and housing pressure outweigh tourist revenue gains. That condition is regularly met.
[P1.2] Crowding is not a minor inconvenience. In Venice, Dubrovnik, and Barcelona, peak arrivals turn public squares, transit, and grocery streets into corridors reserved for outside consumption. Residents reroute their lives around visitor surges, and the urban fabric shifts toward serving people who leave in hours while those who remain pay with lost access. Pollution follows the same pattern: cruise engines idling near historic ports and diesel buses climbing medieval centers impose air, noise, and vibration damage on buildings and lungs alike.
[P1.3] These costs are borne locally and cumulatively, but ticket prices do not compensate residents for them. A cap is the only mechanism that directly limits the volume creating these harms.
[P1.4] Housing pressure is the deepest cost. Visitor demand pushes landlords toward short-term rentals, hotel conversions, and tourist retail, raising rents and shrinking the permanent population. A depopulated historic core preserves facades but loses the schools, shops, and neighbours that make it a living city. Revenue gains are real but narrow: cruise lines, tour operators, and some vendors capture the surplus while housing stress, pollution, and congestion are shared by everyone.
[P1.5] Caps are not anti-tourist; they reset volume toward visitors who stay, spend more per person, and respect capacity. When the marginal visitor produces more harm than benefit, refusing to cap is a subsidy for extractive tourism paid by residents. The proposition simply makes that account explicit.

[C1.1] Resident harm is real, but this motion mistakes a diagnosis for a prescription. The question is not whether unmanaged arrivals impose costs, but whether a numerical cap is the least damaging way to reduce them. It is not.
[C1.2] Crowding, emissions, and housing pressure arise from different mechanisms. Cruise crowds depend on ship size, docking time, tour-bus routing, and disembarkation schedules. Shore power, cleaner fleets, coach permits, timed arrivals, pedestrian zones, and enforcement against idling can address harm without forbidding visitors. The claim that “a cap is the only mechanism that directly limits the volume creating these harms” confuses volume with the source of every externality.
[C1.3] Cutting one large ship may displace it to a nearby port; diverting buses beyond the center may export congestion and remove local spending. Local caps can worsen regional air quality while shrinking the tax base of the city that bore the problem.
[C1.4] “Outweigh tourist revenue gains” is not an adequate decision rule. Revenue accrues to workers, hotels, restaurants, and municipal governments, while pollution, crowding, and rent pressure distribute costs unevenly. Yet benefits are broader: cruise passengers support cultural institutions and suppliers; coaches bring visitors who spend without adding housing demand. Calling all these “tourist revenue gains” assumes direct sales count while monetizing residents’ inconvenience.
[C1.5] Moreover, housing costs arise from restrictive zoning and short-term-rental regulation, so banning arrivals need not improve affordability.
[C1.6] A cap creates scarcity rents, subsidizing operators to raise prices and compete hardest for berths. That harms lower-income visitors and concentrates profits while residents continue bearing congestion.
[C1.7] The defensible policy is targeted management and pricing, not blanket prohibition. If external costs exceed benefits, regulate the activity causing them and make each marginal arrival accountable for its cost. The motion’s cap is an overbroad cure for problems that precise tools can solve.
