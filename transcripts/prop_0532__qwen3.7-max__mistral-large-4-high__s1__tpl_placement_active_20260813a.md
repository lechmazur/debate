[P1.1] The curb is the most valuable real estate in a modern city, yet we treat it like a free dumping ground for commercial vehicles. This creates a textbook tragedy of the commons. When curb space is mostly free, delivery vans and ride-hail drivers have zero financial incentive to move efficiently. They double-park, block bus and bike lanes, and idle while waiting for the next fare.
[P1.2] The cost of this free space isn't actually zero; it is simply externalized onto the public as gridlock, delayed transit, and severe safety hazards for vulnerable road users.
[P1.3] Dynamic congestion and curb pricing solves this by introducing a vital market signal. If occupying a premium downtown loading zone during rush hour carries a dynamic cost, logistics companies will consolidate their stops and optimize routing. Ride-hail drivers will stage in less congested areas rather than idling in high-traffic corridors. Pricing ensures the curb is used by those who value it most at that specific moment, drastically increasing turnover and reducing the physical footprint of idle vehicles.
[P1.4] Opponents will claim this raises consumer costs, but the current system already imposes massive hidden costs through lost time, fuel wasted in gridlock, and preventable traffic injuries. Dynamic pricing forces highly profitable commercial fleets to internalize the cost of the public space they consume. Furthermore, the revenue generated provides a dedicated funding stream for transit improvements and street redesigns, directly benefiting the broader public.
[P1.5] We cannot manage modern urban logistics with outdated free parking policies. Keeping the curb free subsidizes private delivery and ride-hail profits at the direct expense of public mobility and safety. Dynamic pricing is the only mechanism that effectively aligns private commercial incentives with the public good, ensuring our streets work for people, not just idling vehicles.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a management failure for a pricing failure. Curb chaos is not caused by the absence of a toll; it is caused by cities handing out the most contested public space without rules, enforcement, or a plan. A mostly free curb can still be a managed curb: time-limited loading bays, commercial permits, off-peak delivery windows, enforced no-idling rules, geofenced staging areas, and clear bus/bike lane protection can create turnover without turning the sidewalk into an auction.
[C1.2] Dynamic pricing does not remove congestion; it rations access by ability to pay. Delivery and ride-hail demand is comparatively inelastic. A courier bringing medicine, a restaurant supplier, or a driver waiting for an airport fare cannot simply vanish when the meter spikes. The likely result is not fewer vehicle trips but higher costs passed to customers, lower earnings for drivers, and a two-tier street where deep-pocketed fleets buy curb time while small businesses and essential services are pushed to side streets, residential blocks, or off-hours they cannot afford.
[C1.3] A price spike at lunch or during rain can also make drivers circle for cheaper zones or idle just outside the priced area, shifting the queue rather than shrinking it.
[C1.4] There are also serious implementation burdens the proposition skips over. Dynamic curb pricing requires granular sensors or camera enforcement, plate databases, billing systems, appeals processes, and constant algorithmic tuning. Those systems are expensive, error-prone, and create surveillance infrastructure around ordinary street use. The promised revenue for transit is speculative and depends on the very congestion the policy claims to reduce.
[C1.5] The better test is not whether a city can extract a price, but whether it can keep essential movement flowing while protecting transit, cyclists, and pedestrians. Managed free access does that. A dynamic toll mainly decides who gets to use the public street, and the people most likely to lose are not the fleets—they are the customers, workers, and small businesses the fleets serve.
