[P1.1] Early education is not about training software operators; it is about building the cognitive and physical architecture for lifelong learning. A device-light policy in early grades recognizes a fundamental developmental reality: the foundational tools of thought—fine motor control, sustained attention, and deep literacy—are forged through physical interaction with the material world, not through glass screens.
[P1.2] First, paper-based work is irreplaceable for cognitive encoding. When a young child physically writes a letter, they coordinate visual, motor, and tactile feedback. The friction of graphite on paper creates distinct spatial memories that anchor phonics, spelling, and language acquisition. Swiping, tapping, or selecting multiple-choice prompts on a tablet eliminates this neuromuscular integration, degrading a foundational developmental milestone into passive, frictionless input.
[P1.3] Second, paper protects the fragile development of attention. Digital platforms, even educational apps, are built on continuous feedback loops: animations, sound cues, and gamified rewards. This conditions developing nervous systems to expect constant external stimulation, eroding patience for slow, effortful contemplation. A physical page offers stillness.
[P1.4] It requires a child to generate meaning internally rather than passively reacting to a screen, cultivating the focus and self-regulation essential for sustained comprehension.
[P1.5] Third, developmental sequencing is decisive. Screen proponents often treat early tablet adoption as urgent preparation for the modern world. But touchscreen interfaces are designed to be intuitive; an older child can master digital navigation in a matter of days. In contrast, the biological windows for pencil grip, spatial reasoning, and deep-reading habits are narrow.
[P1.6] If neglected in early childhood, these deficits require years of remedial intervention.
[P1.7] Prioritizing paper in the early years does not reject technology; it prepares children for it. Building the brain's internal hardware first ensures that when one-to-one devices arrive later, students have the discipline and literacy to use them effectively.

[C1.2] A device-light policy does not protect children—it deprives them of precisely the tools that can personalize instruction, provide immediate feedback, and reach learners whom paper alone leaves behind.
[C1.3] Consider a first-grader struggling with phonics. A well-designed app can detect exactly which sound blends trip her up, adjust difficulty in real time, and give her twenty practice repetitions in five minutes. A worksheet cannot do this. [C1.4] The teacher with twenty-five students cannot provide that level of individualized response either. [C1.5] The accessibility argument is even more decisive. For students with dyslexia, fine-motor delays, or visual impairments, tablets are not a luxury—they are an equalizer. Text-to-speech, adjustable font sizes, voice dictation, and interactive manipulatives allow children who are failed by paper to participate fully. A device-light policy says to these students: wait.
[C1.6] Learn the hard way first. We will accommodate you later. That is not developmental sequencing. It is gatekeeping.
[C1.7] The claim that older children can "master digital navigation in days" misunderstands what early technology use builds. It is not about swiping. Comfort and fluency with digital creation must develop alongside literacy, not be deferred until some imagined moment of readiness.
[C1.8] Paper is not the foundation. It is one tool among many, and privileging it by policy narrows education rather than deepening it.
