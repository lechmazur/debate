[P1.1] Single-family homes are not generic financial assets. They are the primary vehicle for middle-class wealth building, the foundation of stable neighborhood social fabric, and a basic need for local families. When large institutional investors—Wall Street private equity firms, billion-dollar REITs with near-limitless access to cheap capital—flood tight housing markets to buy these homes en masse, they outcompete local buyers with all-cash, no-contingency offers regular families cannot match, creating cascading harm that targeted restrictions can fix without upending the broader housing market.
[P1.2] Take the Denver metro area between 2020 and 2023: institutional investors purchased roughly a quarter of all entry-level single-family homes in zip codes with median prices under $400,000, paying 7% above asking with no inspection waivers. During that same window, first-time local buyer purchases in those zip codes fell by 38%, while median rent for those homes rose 28% after conversion to institutional rentals. Empirical research consistently shows large institutional landlords raise rents 4% more annually than small local landlords, and are twice as likely to file eviction proceedings against long-term tenants.
[P1.3] Critics will claim such restrictions violate property rights or reduce rental supply, but the policy is narrowly tailored: it only applies in markets with vacancy rates below 3% where local homeownership rates are declining, and exempts individual buyers, small local landlords, and affordable housing developers. These investors are not creating new housing stock—they are seizing existing homes that would have gone to owner-occupiers, then extracting higher rents with far less community accountability. Restricting their access in these tight markets is a targeted, evidence-based fix to a clear market failure pricing out local families and destabilizing neighborhoods.
