[P1.1] Mass university expansion promised to democratize upward mobility, but in practice, it catalyzed a costly positional arms race. When the supply of degrees expands far faster than the structural demand for high-skill labor, the university credential ceases to be an engine of social elevation. Instead, it becomes a blunt screening device. Entry-level roles that historically provided middle-class security to secondary school graduates—administrative coordinators, claims adjusters, entry-level managers—now routinely demand a bachelor’s degree not because the day-to-day work transformed, but because employers use degrees as a cheap sorting filter.
[P1.2] This is the core of credential inflation: it shifts the baseline without expanding the pie of good jobs. On an educational treadmill, when everyone stands on tiptoe, nobody gets a better view. Working-class students who were told a college degree was an escape hatch from economic precarity now discover it merely protects against downward mobility. Worse, as first-tier degrees become ubiquitous, genuine competitive advantage shifts to costly master’s degrees, unpaid metropolitan internships, and pedigree networks—luxuries still disproportionately held by the affluent.
[P1.3] Rather than leveling the playing field, expansion merely moved the goalposts further out of reach.
[P1.4] Genuine opportunity requires increasing real economic mobility and matching human capability to productive work. Credential inflation, however, converts higher education into a compulsory defensive tax. Millions of graduates now enter the workforce burdened by substantial debt and underemployment, forced to purchase an expensive four-year credential simply to secure the same standard of living their parents attained without one. When university expansion primarily forces workers to pay higher tolls just to stand in place, it has inflated credentials far more than it has unlocked authentic opportunity.
