[P1.1] A competent man refuses dialysis. He will die in days. His family finds that death undignified: a body failing, dependence, a slow surrender. The alternative is strapping him to a chair three times a week and running his blood through a machine he has begged them not to use.
[P1.2] If your instinct recoils at the second scene more than the first, you already accept the proposition.
[P1.3] Bodily autonomy has determinate content: whose body, which intrusion, consent given or withheld. Dignity does not. It is a vessel that every tradition fills with incompatible substance — modesty, endurance, productivity, sanctity of life, the refusal to look weak. Ask who decides which filling governs a stranger's bloodstream, and dignity arguments always answer: someone other than the person in the bed.
[P1.4] History shows what that answer costs. Competent hunger strikers force-fed. Patients overridden on transfusions because their faith seemed undignified to the clinician. People with disabilities told their lives lacked dignity by institutions that then controlled their reproduction and their daily hours.
[P1.5] The concept meant to protect persons became the license to handle them.
[P1.6] Weigh the errors. Err toward autonomy and the worst case is a choice observers find degrading, made by the person who bears it, revisable while they live. Err toward dignity and you commit precisely what dignity forbids: using someone's body as a platform for another's moral statement. One mistake is regrettable; the other is a violation.
[P1.7] Autonomy is not absolute — incapacity, coercion, and harm to others all limit it. But when a competent person's own body is the only thing at stake, dignity has no standing to veto. Its defensible core, that people are not objects, is itself a claim about who decides. Autonomy is that claim made operational.
[P1.8] Dignity as trump is that claim hollowed out.

[C1.2] Consider a person in a severe depressive episode who refuses chemotherapy for a curable cancer. Autonomy says: their body, their call. Dignity says: this person's worth is being annihilated by a choice their healthy self would never make. Which framework actually respects them?
[C1.3] The one that treats their present utterance as sacrosanct, or the one that insists they matter enough to be protected from themselves?
[C1.4] The PRO claims dignity is an empty vessel filled by outsiders. That is empirically false. Patients themselves invoke dignity when they refuse procedures that reduce them to objects, when they demand palliative care over aggressive intervention, when they say "I don't want to die like that." These are not impositions from without — they are the person's own moral judgment about what their body deserves.
[C1.5] Autonomy, by contrast, is morally thin. It tells us who decides but not whether the decision honors the decider. It cannot distinguish between a person freely choosing to live and a person freely choosing to die — because "freely" does all the work. Dignity does the harder labor: it asks whether the choice reflects or betrays the person's fundamental worth.
[C1.6] When the stakes are life and death, treating autonomy as the trump card doesn't empower patients. It abandons them to the worst impulses of their worst moments and calls that respect.
