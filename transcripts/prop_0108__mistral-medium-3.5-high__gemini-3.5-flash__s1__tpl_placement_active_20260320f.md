[C1.1] The push for ranked-choice voting (RCV) and proportional representation (PR) is built on a dangerous illusion: that changing the mechanics of voting will magically cure political division. In reality, these systems replace a transparent, accountable majority model with complexity, instability, and backroom political dealing.
[C1.2] First, proportional representation destroys direct geographic accountability. Under the current winner-take-all system, every citizen knows exactly who represents their district. If that representative fails, they are voted out. Proportional systems replace this with party lists, where party bosses—not voters—determine who gets seated.
[C1.3] Furthermore, instead of reducing polarization, PR amplifies it by empowering fringe, extremist parties. In multi-party coalitions, small, radical factions frequently act as "kingmakers," holding entire governments hostage to extract extreme concessions. This is not consensus; it is minority rule.
[C1.4] Second, ranked-choice voting does not deliver the promised majorities; it manufactures them through "ballot exhaustion." If a voter chooses not to rank every candidate—perhaps because they genuinely oppose the alternatives—and their top choices are eliminated, their ballot is discarded. The eventual winner is often decided by a "majority" of only a fraction of the original electorate. In practice, RCV confuses voters, increases spoiled ballots, and delays election results for weeks, severely damaging public trust in democratic outcomes.
[C1.5] Our current system encourages broad, big-tent parties that must appeal to a wide coalition before the election. Under winner-take-all, the path to victory lies in the center. Under RCV and PR, parties are incentivized to splinter and cater to narrow, intense niches, knowing they can leverage post-election horse-trading to gain power. We must not sacrifice democratic accountability and stability for systems that obscure voter intent and empower party elites.
