[P1.1] A fourteen-year-old watching her favorite singer launch a skincare line does not enter a regulated marketplace; she hears a trusted friend. When that friend says a gummy "supports hormonal balance," a serum "repairs skin at a cellular level," or a tea "detoxes," those phrases occupy a deliberate gray zone: medical-sounding enough to promise transformation, vague enough to escape the standards applied to actual medicine. The motion asks for one modest thing — that brands built on fame and aimed at the young say plainly which kind of promise they are making: a medical claim that must carry medical evidence, a cosmetic claim about appearance, or a wellness claim about general feeling.
[P1.2] Stop laundering weak claims through scientific vocabulary.
[P1.3] Why the young? Because the harm concentrates there. Adolescents are still developing judgment about evidence, they buy through parasocial trust rather than ingredient lists, and their sharpest insecurities — acne, weight, skin tone — are exactly the pressure points these products press. A delayed dermatologist visit because a cream promised to "heal" cystic acne is not a marketing quirk; it is scarring, literal and otherwise.
[P1.4] Why a requirement rather than education or self-regulation? Because the blurring is the business model. "Clinically inspired," "dermatologist-loved," "clean," "boosts" — the ambiguity is engineered, and voluntary approaches have had years to fix it; the gray zone persists precisely because it sells. Media literacy cannot outrun a child whose idol holds the product up to the camera.
[P1.5] The burden we defend is light: categorize the claim, meet the matching evidence bar, and display the category where young buyers will actually see it. Brands with honest products lose nothing; brands trading on implication lose only the implication. The other side will ask you to protect commercial flexibility. Ask instead whose flexibility is really at stake — the corporation's convenience, or the teenager's skin, wallet, and trust.
