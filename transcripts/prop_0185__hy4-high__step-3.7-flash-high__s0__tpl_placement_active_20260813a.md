[P1.1] A constitutional balanced-budget requirement with automatic-stabilizer exceptions is not an austerity straitjacket; it is a commitment device against deficit bias. In good years, every tax cut, subsidy, and project has an organized constituency; future taxpayers and recession-response capacity do not. Discretion therefore creates a ratchet: borrow in downturns, promise consolidation in recovery, deliver only part of it, then meet the next shock with less room. The real choice is precommitted countercyclical discipline versus the political business cycle.
[P1.2] Legislative discretion sounds like flexibility, but fiscally it is asymmetric: restraint is suspended quickly and restored late. Governments can also mask imbalance with rosy forecasts, one-off sales, off-budget vehicles, or delayed bills. A constitutional rule using transparent, cyclically adjusted accounting narrows that menu and moves the hard question into boom years, when correction is cheapest, instead of crisis years, when creditors and interest costs set the agenda.
[P1.3] Constitutional status matters: statutory targets can be waived by the same majority that wants the spending. The provision should be simple: balance the structural budget over the cycle; let deficits arise automatically when unemployment rises and revenues fall with the downturn; publish a correction path as conditions normalize; allow exceptional emergency borrowing only by supermajority. The exception is not a loophole but a safety valve that stops the rule from amplifying recessions.
[P1.4] Defenders of discretion invoke crises and national diversity, but the design already answers both: automatic exceptions respond without a new vote, and each country can use its own cycle indicators and debt path. What discretion cannot supply is credibility in booms. A government that cannot restrain itself when revenues are strong and unemployment low has already spent the flexibility it promises for the next emergency. What ultimately matters is fiscal space entering downturns and debt containment leaving booms.
[P1.5] Constitutional balance with stabilizer exceptions creates that pattern; legislative discretion trusts the institution whose incentives produce the ratchet.

But the 2020 COVID pandemic required rapid, massive public health spending and business support that fell far outside standard stabilizer triggers. Under this rule, polarized legislatures would have delayed or blocked that borrowing for months, forcing governments to impose austerity in the middle of a recession, deepening the downturn.
[C1.3] The rule does not prevent deficit bias—it guarantees that the only deficits allowed in crises are the ones politicians can agree on fast enough, the exact flaw PRO attributes to legislative discretion.
[C1.4] Second, the supposedly “transparent” structural-balance framework is inherently manipulable, so it fails to solve the very gaming PRO decries. Defining the economic cycle, potential output, and what counts as structural versus cyclical spending is not a neutral technical exercise—it is deeply political. Governments will rosy-project growth or narrow the definition of cyclical spending to justify larger deficits under the rule, while opposition parties challenge independent agencies’ calculations to block spending. All PRO’s rule does is move fiscal political fights from the legislature to the courts, creating endless litigation over whether a given deficit is permissible, without actually constraining long-term debt.
[C1.5] Third, the rule imposes catastrophic costs on long-term public investment. Responsible borrowing for infrastructure, green transition, or pandemic preparedness pays for itself over decades, but a constitutional balanced-budget rule punishes exactly this spending: even if it generates long-run savings, it creates a short-term structural deficit that must be offset by immediate tax hikes or spending cuts that are often politically impossible. U.S. balanced-budget amendment proposals have drawn opposition from economists for exactly this reason: they would force cuts to vital long-term investments, harming future taxpayers far more than moderate, responsible deficit spending ever could.
