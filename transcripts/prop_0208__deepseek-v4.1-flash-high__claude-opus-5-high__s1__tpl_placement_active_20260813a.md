[P1.1] Public universities are not museums for majors. They are publicly funded engines of opportunity, and opportunity means students leave with a credential that changes their life. When a program repeatedly takes tuition and state subsidy but graduates only a fraction of its students, that is not academic freedom; it transfers risk to the student. The student gets debt and no degree.
[P1.2] The public gets a subsidy bill and a missing worker. That is the case for shifting resources.
[P1.3] Shorter vocational and technical pathways—certificates, applied associate degrees, apprenticeships, licensed health and skilled trades programs—are not lesser education. They are structured, sequenced, and connected to work. For low-income, first-generation, and older students, the cost of a long degree isn't just tuition; it's years of forgone wages. Shorter pathways respect that reality and still allow stacking into further credentials.
[P1.4] Opponents will say this narrows education and starves the humanities. No. We are shifting resources from programs that fail their students, not banning broad learning. A struggling program can be redesigned with better advising, transfer routes, or stackable credentials. But it should not enjoy the same protected subsidy while students drop out.
[P1.5] Public universities already run vocational education for engineers, nurses, accountants, and lawyers. The question is not whether to be vocational; it is whose vocational pathway gets funded.
[P1.6] The equity argument is decisive. Privileged students can absorb a false start. Poor students cannot. Leaving after two years with no credential often means debt, no degree, and no wage premium.
[P1.7] Finishing a one-year IT certificate or a two-year radiography program means a credential, a job, and a ladder.
[P1.8] Resources are finite. Shifting toward shorter technical pathways is not abandoning the university's mission; it is finally taking that mission seriously for the students who need it most.
