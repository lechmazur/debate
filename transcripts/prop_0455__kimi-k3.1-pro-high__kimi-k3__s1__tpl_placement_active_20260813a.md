[P1.1] Routine flaring and excessive methane leakage are not the price of energy. They are the destruction of energy, accompanied by climate damage and avoidable pollution. The motion bans those practices; it does not ban oil and gas production, emergency flaring needed for safety, or every unavoidable emission. That distinction should frame the debate.
[P1.2] Methane is especially damaging to the climate in the near term, precisely when rapid emissions reductions can limit compounding harm. Flaring also produces carbon dioxide and, depending on combustion conditions, soot and other pollutants, with burdens often concentrated around production sites. Governments already restrict industrial pollution even when compliance costs money. The opposing position must explain why an operator’s convenience should create a special license to waste a public resource and impose costs on everyone else.
[P1.3] The supply argument is particularly weak because leaked methane supplies nobody. It is gas that never reaches a home, hospital, or power plant. Routine flaring likewise burns gas without delivering its useful energy. Where capture is impractical, continued wasteful production is not automatically preferable to temporarily reducing output: the motion explicitly accepts that trade-off.
[P1.4] Short-term supply matters, but it cannot become a permanent veto over every environmental rule.
[P1.5] Nor is prohibition technologically fanciful. Leak detection and repair, better seals and controls, vapor recovery, gas capture, reinjection, and pipeline connections can address many sources. Strict thresholds, credible monitoring, and meaningful penalties force operators to choose these measures rather than treat pollution as the cheapest option. A predictable ban also rewards companies that have already invested in cleaner operations instead of subsidizing laggards through weak enforcement.
[P1.6] Even accepting that compliance could temporarily reduce domestic output, governments can manage that risk through phased enforcement, contingency planning, storage, efficiency, and targeted protection for vulnerable consumers. Those tools address hardship without normalizing indefinite waste. The decisive comparison is therefore not pollution versus abstract environmental purity. It is temporary, manageable supply pressure versus continuing preventable damage simply because stopping it inconveniences producers.
[P1.7] A government serious about both energy security and public welfare should stop the waste.

[C1.2] First: cutting domestic supply does not cut demand. Every barrel or cubic meter removed at home is replaced — by imports, often from jurisdictions with weaker methane standards, and by dirtier fuels. Europe's 2022 scramble after losing Russian gas meant revived coal plants and a bidding war for LNG, some of it sourced from fields with far worse flaring practices than the producers a domestic ban would punish. The motion offers climate theater: emissions leave the national ledger and reappear, larger, on someone else's.
[C1.3] Second: the ban destroys real supply, not waste. The other side says leaked methane supplies nobody — but a ban doesn't pipe that gas to anyone either. Most flared gas is associated gas: it comes up with oil, often at sites where pipelines or processing don't yet exist. Faced with a strict threshold, the operator's compliant choice is not capture; it is shutting in the well and losing the oil with it.
[C1.4] Prohibition converts "wasted byproduct" into "destroyed production" — which is why the claim that this motion doesn't restrict production collapses on contact with geology.
[C1.5] Third: beware perverse substitution. Flaring exists because venting raw methane is worse — combustion converts a potent greenhouse gas into a far weaker one. Cliff-edge thresholds plus imperfect monitoring invite under-reporting, gaming, and in the worst cases straight venting: a measurable regulatory "success" that is a physical failure.
[C1.6] Fourth: short-term supply shocks cast long shadows. Price spikes fall hardest on the poor, trigger coal backfill, and poison public consent for climate policy altogether — the fuel-price backlash that set French climate politics back years is the cautionary tale.
[C1.7] We agree flaring should fall. The fight is over the instrument: targeted fees, infrastructure mandates, and phased standards cut emissions without sacrificing supply.
