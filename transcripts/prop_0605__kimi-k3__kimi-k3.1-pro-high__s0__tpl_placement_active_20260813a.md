[P1.1] A visa that chains a worker to one employer is not a labor policy; it is a leash. Under kafala, a migrant's right to live in the country belongs to their sponsor: quit and you become illegal, complain and you risk deportation. That architecture should be dismantled on three grounds.
[P1.2] First, coercion. When exit is criminalized, negotiation is a fiction. Kafala explains why wage theft, passport confiscation, and contract substitution are chronic across the region rather than exceptional: employers hold the ultimate sanction of cancelling residency. Domestic workers, isolated in private homes and often outside labor codes, bear the worst.
[P1.3] A system whose defining feature is that one party cannot leave produces abuse by design, not by accident.
[P1.4] Second, exit is the only enforcement mechanism that works at scale. Gulf labor inspectorates are badly outnumbered; no bureaucracy can police millions of private employment relationships. A worker who can leave enforces her own contract every day. Portability converts paper rights into real leverage — cheaper and more reliable than any inspection regime CON will propose.
[P1.5] Third, the economics. Kafala locks workers into mismatched jobs and lets uncompetitive firms survive on captive cheap labor rather than efficiency, undercutting the diversification agendas — Vision 2030 and its peers — that Gulf governments have staked their futures on. Portable visas reallocate labor to its best use and force firms to compete for workers on wages and conditions.
[P1.6] This is not utopian. Qatar scrapped the exit permit and no-objection certificate for most workers before the World Cup; Saudi Arabia's 2021 reforms loosened job mobility. The sky did not fall — employers adjusted, proving portability is administratively feasible now.
[P1.7] The question CON must answer is simple: what legitimate interest does any employer have in holding a worker who wants to leave? A contract you cannot quit is ownership by another name. The Gulf can run a modern labor market or a system of tethered labor. It cannot honestly claim both.

[C1.1] The motion confuses sponsorship’s coercive features with every function sponsorship performs. Gulf states should abolish passport confiscation, employer-controlled exit, retaliatory residency cancellation, and any sponsor veto over escaping abuse. But replacing the entire model with portable, worker-held visas is neither necessary nor reliably protective.
[C1.2] Sponsorship also assigns responsibility: who recruited the worker, arranged housing and insurance, pays wages, and funds repatriation. Abolish that anchor without constructing a replacement, and responsibility can dissolve among subcontractors, households, and workers themselves. A visa in the worker’s hands does not recover stolen wages, provide shelter after a domestic worker flees a home, or repay recruitment debt. Exit is leverage, but it is not justice: leaving an abusive employer can still mean unemployment while the abuser simply recruits someone else.
[C1.3] Nor is unrestricted switching economically costless. Employers commonly bear upfront recruitment, travel, onboarding, and training costs. If a new recruit can depart immediately for a competitor, rational firms will invest less in training, rely more on informal labor brokers, favor workers already in-country, or recover costs through lower starting wages and new fees. Those consequences fall hardest on future migrants—the people portability supposedly helps.
[C1.4] Labor mobility is valuable; pretending contractual stability has no value is not serious labor policy.
[C1.5] The Gulf examples PRO invokes prove something narrower than claimed. Qatar’s and Saudi Arabia’s reforms demonstrate that coercive restrictions can be dismantled incrementally. They do not demonstrate that every Gulf state, sector, and household employer is ready for wholesale replacement by worker-held visas. Feasibility of reform is not feasibility of this exact model.
[C1.6] What legitimate interest does an employer have? Not ownership, and not preventing escape from abuse. The legitimate interests are notice, handover, and predictable fixed-term commitments—enforceable without control over residency. The better system is state-supervised mobility, immediate transfer after credible abuse complaints, due process before cancellation, and enforceable wage and repatriation guarantees.
[C1.7] Remove the leash, but keep clear accountability. The motion throws away both.
