[P1.1] Location-based pay is the fair and sustainable standard for remote work. When an employee relocates from New York to rural Kansas, their cost of living can drop by 30–40%—housing, taxes, and daily expenses all shrink dramatically. It is neither just nor practical for companies to ignore this economic reality. Paying a San Francisco salary to someone in Lisbon creates immediate inequities: local Portuguese engineers earn a fraction of that rate, while the remote worker gains outsized purchasing power simply by changing address.
[P1.2] This isn’t merely about internal fairness; it’s about economic coherence. Labor costs are a fundamental business input, and if firms systematically overpay for roles based on irrelevant geography, they distort hiring decisions, product pricing, and long-term viability. The alternative—flat global pay—fails on two counts. First, it imports high-cost inflation into low-cost regions, effectively pricing local talent out of their own markets and destabilizing local economies.
[P1.3] Second, it makes remote hiring unsustainable for most organizations, forcing them to either abandon the practice or absorb unsustainable cost burdens. Compensation has always been tied to place—regional salary bands exist for in-office roles for precisely these reasons. Remote work changes where the employee lives, not the economic logic of compensation. When workers choose lower-cost areas, they already benefit from reduced expenses.
[P1.4] Location-adjusted pay simply aligns compensation with market realities, preserving fairness and sustainability for all.

[P2.3] The ‘geography is irrelevant’ point [C1.4] is self-defeating. [P5.1] Internal pay equity is the cornerstone of fair compensation. In any company with multiple locations, employees in identical roles are paid according to their local market conditions because cost of living and local demand differ. When a remote worker moves to Lisbon, they enter the Lisbon labor market. Without location adjustment, they would retain a New York salary, earning far more than Lisbon-based peers for the same work.
[P5.2] This creates immediate inequity: the remote worker gains an unfair advantage, local colleagues are undervalued, and the company’s pay structure becomes incoherent. The only fair resolution is location-adjusted pay, which aligns compensation with the local market where the work is performed. Without adjustment, the company faces an impossible choice: either inflate all Lisbon salaries to New York levels, pricing itself out of the local market, or accept systemic internal pay disparities. The worker’s choice to relocate to a lower-cost area means they must accept local market rates.
[P5.3] Relocating for lower living costs should not entitle them to an inflationary salary premium at the expense of colleagues and company sustainability. Equity demands the adjustment.
