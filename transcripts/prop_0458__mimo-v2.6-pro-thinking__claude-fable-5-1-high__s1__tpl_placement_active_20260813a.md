[P1.1] Every ton of LNG the United States sends abroad ships with a climate bill that starts at the wellhead and never really ends. That is the core of the case.
[P1.2] Methane first. Natural gas is only a climate advantage over coal if it mostly stays in the pipe. It doesn't. Between wells, gathering lines, compressors, liquefaction, and shipping, enough gas leaks, is vented, or is flared that the fuel's real warming profile sits far worse than its CO2-only spreadsheet suggests.
[P1.3] Methane traps on the order of 80 times more heat than carbon dioxide over twenty years — and twenty years is exactly the window in which we either bend the curve or lose the fight. When we export LNG, we export the leakage too, and add boil-off and flare emissions on the ocean leg. Fewer export pull-through means fewer new wells drilled into marginal, leaky formations — the wells whose gas is worth the least and costs the most in methane.
[P1.4] Lock-in second. An LNG contract is a thirty-year handshake. Every cargo we commit to drives new liquefaction trains, new regasification terminals, and new gas-fired generation in importing countries — infrastructure that outlives the remaining carbon budget. Buyers in Asia and elsewhere don't treat American gas as a bridge over coal; they treat it as a reason to defer renewables and negotiate down their coal exit timelines.
[P1.5] A U.S. export restriction is one of the few levers a single government can pull to slow that lock-in globally.
[P1.6] Domestic third. Large-scale exports pull American gas prices toward world levels. Higher domestic gas prices make heat pumps and electric appliances look dear next to the gas furnace, and slow the cleanest thing we can do to our own emissions: electrifying buildings. Restricting exports eases home energy costs and removes the price signal that keeps marginal drilling profitable.
[P1.7] Three mechanisms, one motion: less exported methane, less global gas lock-in, faster domestic decarbonization.

[C1.2] Start with Asia, where PRO says gas defers renewables. The marginal fuel in Vietnam, India, Bangladesh, and much of China's industrial belt is not solar; it is coal. When LNG spot prices spiked in 2022, Pakistan and Bangladesh did not build wind farms — they rationed power and fired up coal. Restrict American supply and you tighten that market again, and the fuel that fills the gap emits roughly twice the CO2 per unit of electricity, plus the methane that leaks from coal mines themselves.
[C1.3] Or the gap is filled by other gas. Qatar, Russia, Algeria, and Nigeria are all expanding capacity. American producers operate under federal methane rules, mandatory leak detection, satellite monitoring, and shareholder pressure; Russian pipeline gas has historically been among the leakiest on earth, and Nigerian flaring is notorious. A U.S. restriction does not remove methane from the atmosphere — it relocates it to jurisdictions where nobody measures it.
[C1.4] Now the domestic mechanism, which is where PRO's case actually inverts. A large share of American gas is associated gas — a byproduct of Permian oil drilling that comes up whether or not anyone wants it. Close the export outlet and that gas doesn't stay underground; it gets flared or vented at the wellhead. Restricting exports is a methane-increasing policy at home.
[C1.5] And cheap gas is not an electrification engine. U.S. gas already costs a fraction of what Europe and Asia pay, yet furnaces still dominate. Driving prices lower makes the gas furnace even cheaper against the heat pump, and makes gas power plants harder to retire. PRO's own price logic runs backwards.
[C1.6] So the honest question is not "is LNG clean?" It is "compared to what actually replaces it?" On coal, on unregulated foreign gas, and on flared Permian gas, the answer favors letting American cargoes sail.
