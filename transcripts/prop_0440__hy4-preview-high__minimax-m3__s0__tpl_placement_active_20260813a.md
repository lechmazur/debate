[P1.1] Gun violence in US cities is concentrated, not diffuse. A small share of people, groups, and blocks drives a large share of shootings, and much of it is retaliatory—one dispute sets off the next. That concentration decides which tool is cost-effective. Hiring more officers spreads an expensive resource across an entire city, hoping extra patrol randomly intersects the next trigger.
[P1.2] Focused deterrence and violence interruption instead aim where the risk already is: they identify the few crews and blocks where the next shooting is likely, pair credible consequences with support, and send credible messengers into disputes before they become homicides.
[P1.3] That focus makes them cheaper per shooting prevented. Police expansion carries heavy, recurring fixed costs—recruiting, academy training, salaries, benefits, overtime, equipment, supervision, pensions. The marginal officer mostly adds patrol capacity, which has weak and fast-diminishing returns against concentrated gun violence; a bigger department still cannot cover every hot moment. A targeted program can run on a small team whose budget is a fraction of one academy class, let alone lifetime personnel costs.
[P1.4] Boston’s Operation Ceasefire concentrated on a few dozen gangs instead of patrolling a whole city; Cure Violence-style outreach puts workers on the specific corners where retaliation brews. A handful of credible mediators can cost less than one unit’s annual overtime while preventing shootings that would otherwise incur far larger police, medical, and incarceration costs.
[P1.5] The CON will say police do more than stop shootings—they answer calls, build cases, provide general security. They do, but the test is which instrument buys more gun-violence reduction per dollar. Broad staffing buys a little coverage everywhere; targeted strategies buy intense pressure and interruption where violence actually lives. Added officers can also fray trust in the very neighborhoods where cooperation determines whether shootings are solved or repeated.
[P1.6] The targeted approach is cheaper not as a feel-good add-on, but because it matches the shape of the problem. Buy the scalpel, not the blanket.
