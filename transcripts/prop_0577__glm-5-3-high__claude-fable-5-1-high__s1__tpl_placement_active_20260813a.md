[P1.1] Irregular migration is not primarily an enforcement problem; it is a market problem. Migrants come because employers want their labor and workers want the wages. Border enforcement attacks the symptom—the movement—while leaving the underlying demand untouched. Legal pathways attack the demand itself: they give workers a lawful route, drain the smuggling market of customers, and redirect flows into channels governments can actually see and regulate.
[P1.2] Start with the smuggler economy. Smuggling thrives on the absence of legal alternatives. Every worker who cannot get a visa but can get a job is a potential client. Border walls, patrols, and deterrence raise the smuggler's price—but rising prices have not collapsed the market; they have professionalized it, making smugglers richer and routes deadlier.
[P1.3] A seasonal visa, by contrast, removes the customer entirely. A worker with a legal ticket in hand has no reason to pay a criminal thousands of dollars and risk death in a desert or the sea. Enforcement suppresses; pathways substitute.
[P1.4] Second, enforcement has a diminishing-returns problem. The United States has spent decades multiplying Border Patrol budgets, fencing, and surveillance technology, yet each crackdown has largely redirected crossings to different points or channels rather than eliminating them. Deterrence works only when staying home seems better than trying—and for someone fleeing poverty with a job waiting abroad, it rarely does.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, deportations often boomerang: removed workers with jobs and roots frequently reattempt the crossing. Legal pathways break this cycle, because they convert a one-way, high-stakes bet into a repeatable, legal round trip.
[P1.6] Third, legality generates information and leverage. Seasonal and sectoral programs create records—identities, employers, wages, return dates—that governments can use to manage flows. Enforcement in the dark can only guess. The H-2 visa system in the U.S. and seasonal schemes in parts of Europe, whatever their flaws, show that workers will use lawful channels when they exist and are workable.
[P1.7] The question is not whether enforcement matters at all; it is which lever reduces irregular migration *more effectively*. Substituting a legal route beats chasing an illegal one, every time.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a single assumption: that the person crossing a desert at night is the same person who would take a six-month strawberry visa and go home in October. Mostly, he is not. The bulk of contemporary irregular flows—Central American families at the Rio Grande, Syrians and Afghans in the Aegean, West Africans heading for the Canaries—are seeking permanent settlement, protection, or family reunion. A seasonal permit tied to one employer, one sector, and a return date is not a substitute for what they want.
[C1.2] Offer it, and they will still cross. Substitution only works when the product substitutes.
[C1.3] Second, legal pathways are additive, not subtractive. The Bracero era is the clearest case: the United States admitted hundreds of thousands of Mexican contract workers a year, and irregular crossings and apprehensions rose alongside them, because employers happily hired both, and every legal worker built the networks, knowledge, and expectations that brought cousins without papers. Any real-world program is capped, so the visa becomes a lottery. Losers do not go home; they were already at the border.
[C1.4] And winners overstay—overstays have long rivaled clandestine entries as a source of new unauthorized residents in the United States. A "legal pathway" that ends in an expired visa is irregular migration with a delayed start.
[C1.5] Third, enforcement's record is far stronger than the caricature of a wall that merely reroutes. Australia's maritime turnback policy took boat arrivals from tens of thousands a year to nearly none. The EU–Turkey arrangement collapsed Aegean crossings within months. Israel's fence on the Egyptian border ended a large irregular inflow almost completely.
[C1.6] These are the sharpest documented drops in irregular migration anywhere—and none came from a visa scheme. Deterrence works when the probability of getting in and staying falls decisively.
[C1.7] Pathways serve employers and are defensible on other grounds. But the motion asks what reduces irregular migration more. Enforcement has produced collapses; pathways have produced parallel flows.
