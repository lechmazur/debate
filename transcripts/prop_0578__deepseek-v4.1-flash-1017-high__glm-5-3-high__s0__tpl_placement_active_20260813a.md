[P1.1] The immigration court system’s core failure is not that it moves too fast; it is that it has far too little capacity to move at all. A multi-year backlog turns notices stale, evidence cold, and relief into a lottery. It also creates the very conditions that produce wrongful removal: people miss hearings because they cannot take a day off, find childcare, or travel hours to court; records are incomplete; and judges under crushing calendar pressure have less time to develop testimony.
[P1.2] Expanding staffing and remote hearings attacks those causes directly.
[P1.3] Staffing matters beyond adding judges. Clerks, interpreters, and courtroom support reduce the wasted hearings and continuances that clog dockets. More judge time per case means better-developed records, clearer credibility findings, and fewer rushed decisions. Remote hearings, with basic safeguards, expand participation: a respondent can appear from a legal-aid office or community center instead of losing a day’s wages and a bus fare; counsel and qualified interpreters can cover regions they could never reach in person.
[P1.4] That improves both compliance and fairness—people appear, testify, and receive a decision they can understand and challenge.
[P1.5] The wrongful-removal risk is real, but it is not primarily a speed risk. It is a capacity and access risk. In absentia orders, uncorrected paperwork errors, and unrepresented respondents are already hallmarks of the backlog. A resourced system can screen for in-person needs, ensure confidential interpretation, and give judges room to continue a case when the record is thin.
[P1.6] The relevant comparison is not perfect accuracy versus speed; it is slow, attritional dysfunction versus resourced adjudication. The first already removes people wrongly by default. The second makes accurate outcomes more likely.

[C1.1] The proposition smuggles in a rosy assumption: that "faster" and "more remote" means "more careful." History and mechanism both say otherwise. When an adjudication system is reorganized around throughput, the fastest disposition wins—and in immigration court, the fastest disposition is almost always a removal order.
[C1.2] Start with incentives. Expanded staffing only helps if judges are allowed to slow down. But the entire political rationale for hiring more judges and expanding remote hearings is to clear the backlog. Agencies under that pressure measure judges by completions, not correctness.
[C1.3] We have seen this before: when completion targets were imposed, grant rates fell, judges complained of being evaluated on speed, and appeals documented cursory, boilerplate decisions. Doubling down on throughput institutionalizes exactly that. A judge who continues a case to develop testimony looks like a failure; a judge who denies looks productive.
[C1.4] Remote hearings compound this. Credibility determinations—the heart of most asylum cases—depend on demeanor, hesitation, trauma responses, and interpreter nuance. Compress that through a laggy video feed and much of it is lost. Respondents with limited digital access, poor English, or no private space to describe persecution appear at a structural disadvantage.
[C1.5] And the failure mode is brutal: a missed bus is bad, but a dropped connection, a wrong dial-in, or an unopened email can end in an in absentia removal order—the default judgment with the harshest consequences in the system, issued without hearing anything at all.
[C1.6] Finally, weigh the asymmetry. The claimed gains—better appearance rates, faster decisions—are real but recoverable. A wrongful removal is not. Deporting someone to the country they fled is irreversible, and it concentrates on the most vulnerable: the unrepresented, the detained, children, trauma survivors who cannot testify coherently through a screen.
[C1.7] The proposition asks you to bet due process on an agency's promise of "basic safeguards." When throughput is the goal, safeguards are the first thing cut.
