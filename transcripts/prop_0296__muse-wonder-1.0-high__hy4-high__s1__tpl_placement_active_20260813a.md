[P1.1] A name on a school, a street sign, or a calendar is not a footnote. It is an act of honor. History belongs in textbooks, museums, and archives, where it can be taught critically. Honor belongs only to those a community can still defend to its children without apology.
[P1.2] When a holiday, school, or street primarily honors a colonizer, slaveholder, or dictator, the state is not preserving the past — it is endorsing it in the present.
[P1.3] That endorsement does real harm. Imagine being required to learn and graduate under the name of a man who enslaved people like you, expelled people like you, or ruled your family by fear. The message is unmistakable: your dignity is negotiable, but his reputation is permanent. Public institutions are funded by everyone and must serve everyone.
[P1.4] They cannot ask descendants of the oppressed to celebrate their oppressors as the price of public education or a public holiday.
[P1.5] Historical continuity cannot outweigh that harm, because continuity is not threatened. Renaming does not delete a chapter; it changes who we put on the cover. Columbus can be studied without Columbus Day, just as a dictator can be remembered in a history lesson without keeping his avenue. Cities rename streets for new heroes, developments, and directions routinely without losing their past.
[P1.6] What would be lost is not memory, but immunity from judgment.
[P1.7] The line is clear. This is not about purging every flawed figure from history. The test is primary legacy: was this person honored for conquest, enslavement, or tyranny, and is that domination central to why the name stings today? When the answer is yes, a democratic government should choose names that unite rather than subordinate — local teachers, civil rights leaders, Indigenous peoples, ordinary citizens who built the community.
[P1.8] Keeping such names asks the victims' descendants to carry the burden forever. Renaming asks everyone to carry history honestly, without forcing anyone to live under its celebration.

[C1.1] The claim that a public name is simply "an act of honor" [P1.1] is too flat. It is a receipt: evidence of who held power, whom a community excluded, and what later citizens tolerated. A school named for a slaveholder can be taught as an indictment of the community that chose him. Keep the name and that judgment stays visible; replace it and the institution buys a clean conscience with new letterhead.
[C1.2] Continuity is not nostalgia; it is the evidence chain that lets a child ask why the name survived.
[C1.3] That is why "the state is not preserving the past — it is endorsing it in the present" [P1.2] collapses two acts. A government can refuse celebration without deleting inherited names. Holidays can be days of testimony, not parades; plaques, curricula, websites, and ceremonies can state the full record; new schools, streets, and memorials can honor victims and local builders. The real choice is contextualized continuity versus symbolic cleansing.
[C1.4] A renamed avenue may inspire, but it does not tell citizens that their city once exalted conquest.
[C1.5] The proposal also gives governments vague power. "Primarily honors," "colonizer," "slaveholder," and "dictator" do not apply themselves. Historical figures are often builders and oppressors, liberators and authoritarians; each majority can call its enemies tyrants and its favorites complex. A primary-legacy test turns the map into an audit of the ruling coalition's virtue, inviting factional score-settling and civic whiplash.
[C1.6] The appeal to routine renaming [P1.5] is no answer: inherited names sit in deeds, addresses, diplomas, local memory, and administrative continuity.
[C1.7] The harm claim proves less than it needs. No one should be forced to celebrate an oppressor; that demands changed ceremonies, not compulsory erasure. Material inequality is not repaired when a sign becomes morally comfortable. Democratic governments should teach, add, mark, and explain.
[C1.8] Preserve the names as defendants in the dock, not convert the public square into a gallery of approved consciences.

[P4.1] A1: The placement is the evidence. We prove naming honors by how governments use it everywhere else: they auction naming rights, award them to donors and heroes, and unveil them with pride. Putting a name alone in large letters atop a school, on a diploma, and on every address retains that conventional meaning, however many disclaimers are appended. A street sign cannot carry a compulsory essay, a jersey and diploma will not read as an arraignment, and a holiday still titled for a man still invites his celebration.
[P4.2] If the words had no honorific force, opponents would not fight so hard to keep them.
[P4.3] A2: No new duty is needed because renaming does not rest history on goodwill. Existing, enforceable curriculum standards can require teaching the former name, why it was chosen, and why it was removed—just as they already require teaching conquest, slavery, and dictatorship without naming schools for their perpetrators. That is less factional than the alternative: a one-time, transparent renaming decision, followed by ordinary history teaching, versus a permanent bureaucracy policing repudiatory language on every sign, document, and ceremony forever. A future majority could neglect either history; only renaming stops it from also inheriting the honor.
[P4.4] That exposes the decisive flaw. Their arraignment depends on the state speaking with two voices at once—condemning in fine print while enthroning in headline—and policing that contradiction indefinitely. When enforcement lapses, the plaque fades but the prestige remains. Renaming is clearer, cheaper to sustain, and honest: remove the honor, teach the crime.
[P4.5] It does not promise a clean origin story; the public vote, the record of the change, and the mandated lesson are the origin story—one in which the community finally judged its past instead of continuing to display it as its identity. Continuity worth keeping is knowledge, not subordination printed on a child's diploma.
