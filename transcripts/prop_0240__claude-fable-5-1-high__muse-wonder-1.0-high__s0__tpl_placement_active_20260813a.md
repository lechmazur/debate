[P1.1] Every public decision carries two questions. The first is technical: will this bridge hold, will this tax cut emissions, will this vaccine work? The second is political: who should pay, whose neighbourhood gets the highway, which risks are worth bearing? Competence can answer the first.
[P1.2] It has nothing to say about the second, because there is no engineering solution to "whose interests count." That is why legitimacy matters more: it governs the questions that actually divide us, while competence only governs execution.
[P1.3] Consider what competence without legitimacy produces. Robert Moses was perhaps the most capable builder of the twentieth century, and he drove expressways through living neighbourhoods because the people living there had no standing to stop him. The decisions were technically sound and morally indefensible. Nobody today says the problem was insufficient expertise.
[P1.4] Legitimacy is also the precondition for competence to function at all. A policy that is correct but unaccepted is simply a policy that fails. Lockdowns, vaccination drives, austerity programmes: each depended on whether people believed the decision was theirs. When the eurozone's institutions imposed programmes on Greece over the heads of its voters, the arithmetic may have been impeccable, but the result was a decade of resentment and political rupture.
[P1.5] Imposed competence breeds the populist backlash that then elects the incompetents technocrats fear most.
[P1.6] And experts are not oracles. The financial regulators of 2007 were credentialed, confident, and catastrophically wrong. Technocracy has no mechanism for discovering its own errors; democracy does. Elections, a free press, and the threat of removal are the only reliable error-correction system ever devised.
[P1.7] Functioning democracies do not have famines; expert-run autocracies have.
[P1.8] The honest clash is what happens when the two conflict. Legitimacy does not exclude expertise; a democratic public can delegate to a central bank and revoke that delegation. Competence cannot generate legitimacy in return. One is the master, the other the servant.
[P1.9] When the servant overrules the master, we don't call it good governance. We call it rule.

[C1.1] A vote can authorize a decision; it cannot make the decision true, safe, or effective. A majority can legitimize a bridge design, an interest rate, or a pandemic response, but gravity, inflation, and viruses do not count ballots. When legitimacy and competence conflict, reality enforces competence — and pays for its absence in lives, livelihoods, and state capacity.
[C1.2] That is why competence is the prior value. Legitimacy is not an end people experience; competence is. No family is consoled that a contaminated water system, a preventable hospital backlog, or a currency collapse was properly authorized. Governments in fact earn and lose legitimacy largely by delivering security, prosperity, and competent administration.
[C1.3] Treat legitimacy as supreme and you reverse cause and effect: you protect the procedure that produces failure while sacrificing the performance that makes self-government worth having.
[C1.4] Democratic authorization is also a weak guide to the hardest public choices. Voters are rationally uninformed on complex systems, discount future costs, and can impose harms on minorities and future generations who cannot answer back. That is precisely why mature democracies insulate monetary policy, drug approval, aviation safety, and public-health emergencies from direct majoritarian pressure — not as an embarrassment to democracy, but because on those questions being right matters more than being representative. Delegation is democracy admitting its own limit.
[C1.5] Nor does legitimacy reliably correct error. Elections punish unpopularity, not falsehood; they can repeat a popular mistake for decades and remove officials for telling an unwelcome truth. Competence has harder tests: predictions fail, inflation rises, infections spread, structures collapse. Those feedbacks are brutal, but they distinguish knowledge from applause.
[C1.6] Legitimacy without competence is permission to fail together. Competence without perfect legitimacy can still vaccinate, stabilize, build, and rescue — and in doing so create the trust legitimacy claims to supply. When we must choose, choose the decision that works. A state that cannot act competently will not remain legitimate for long.

[C2.1] Creating an agency does not make every later act a democratic judgment. A legislature may create a central bank, but it does not thereby set the interest rate, approve the drug, or certify the aircraft. If legitimacy mattered more at the moment of decision, an elected override of an inconvenient finding would improve it. Insulation exists for the opposite reason: where the two diverge, competence should win.
[C2.2] Nor is the priorities objection decisive. Public bodies seldom face empty goals: prevent crashes, contain disease, stabilize prices, supply clean water. The live conflict is over means, side effects, and the size of trade-offs, which only competence can estimate. Citizens may prefer less unemployment to less inflation, but they cannot vote on how much of either a policy will actually produce.
[C2.3] Flint reverses on inspection. Switching water sources without adequate corrosion control was not expertise triumphant; it was cost-cutting administration disregarding technical practice and warning signs. Prioritize competence, and treatment is added, samples are believed, and lead is detected earlier. Prioritize pedigree of office, elected or emergency, and officials can still ignore chemistry.
[C2.4] Residents needed decision-makers bound by competent practice, not merely removable ones.
[C2.5] Removal, likewise, is not correction. Elections can sack a person while retaining the false model or popular policy that caused failure, and can sack officials for imposing necessary pain. Competent correction is more specific: retest the water, revise the model, ground the unsafe fleet, change the rate. Democracy is potent at changing rulers; that does not make it superior at changing factual beliefs.
[C2.6] Finally, pain imposed by credentialed people is not thereby competent. Austerity that needlessly deepens collapse fails the competence test itself. When competence genuinely works — safe aviation, stable money, diseases controlled — it earns the trust on which delegation depends. Legitimacy may authorize power, but competence determines whether exercising it helps or harms.
[C2.7] In conflict, the outcome people must live with should outrank the procedure that blessed it.

Honouring the mandate implements the priority they enacted over the impulse of the moment.
