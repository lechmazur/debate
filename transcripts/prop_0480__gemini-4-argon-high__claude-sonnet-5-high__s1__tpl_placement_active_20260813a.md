[P1.1] If you hand every citizen a few hundred dollars a month, they still cannot buy a bus route that was never built, negotiate down the price of surgery, or conjure a daycare spot where none exists. The fatal flaw of unconditional cash transfers is the illusion that isolated consumers can buy basic security in private markets as efficiently as society can build it together.
[P1.2] With a fixed public budget, dollar-for-dollar efficiency determines how much hardship we actually prevent. Healthcare, transit, internet, and childcare are not ordinary consumer goods; they carry high fixed costs, inelastic demand, and severe market failures. Pouring cash into supply-constrained markets without building public capacity just fuels inflation. Give parents cash for private daycare, and providers raise prices to capture the subsidy.
[P1.3] Conversely, when governments build universal services directly, they leverage economies of scale, cut out profiteering middlemen, and expand real supply. A public dollar spent running trains or bargaining for medicine buys vastly more real-world value than a dollar spent alone against a private monopoly.
[P1.4] Furthermore, cash fails because human needs are profoundly uneven. Unconditional transfers slice the budget into identical, thin wedges. For a healthy, childless adult, a monthly check is a nice bonus. For a patient fighting cancer, or a working parent with two toddlers, that flat check is a drop in the bucket.
[P1.5] Cash privatizes risk; universal services pool it. Services deliver immense value precisely when life becomes unbearably expensive, ensuring nobody is ruined simply because their needs exceed the statistical average.
[P1.6] Finally, universal services generate collective returns that individual spending cannot match. Universal childcare unlocks the labor force for millions of parents; reliable transit cuts emissions while connecting workers to jobs; universal broadband educates the next generation. Cash treats structural scarcity as a mere liquidity problem. Universal basic services lower the cost of living at its root.

[C1.3] PRO claims cash "cannot buy a bus route that was never built." [C1.5] And dignity matters: conditional-in-kind benefits treat citizens as wards to be provisioned for. Cash treats them as adults capable of judging their own trade-offs — rent versus food versus medicine versus care for a sick parent. No bureaucrat drawing a bus route can replicate the information contained in a person's own choice about where a dollar does the most good for them.
