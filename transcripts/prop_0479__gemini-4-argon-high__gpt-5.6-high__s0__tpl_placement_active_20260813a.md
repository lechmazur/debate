[P1.1] When a government commits billions to an infrastructure project, a public health intervention, or an inflation target, the hardest problem is not finding smart people. It is getting honest probabilities out of institutions designed to punish dissent. Today, major policy relies mainly on expert panels and internal forecasts. That model fails systematically because its incentives reward conformity and political palatability over accuracy.
[P1.2] Consider how an internal agency forecast or blue-ribbon panel actually operates. Members are selected by leadership, deliberate in small groups prone to groupthink, and face asymmetric risks: being wrong together is safe, while challenging a minister’s pet project is career-limiting. Worse, vital ground-level knowledge—held by supply-chain contractors, field engineers, or regional clinicians—never reaches a twelve-person committee in the capital.
[P1.3] Subsidized prediction markets fix both the incentive problem and the information bottleneck. Instead of asking a committee for a negotiation-smoothed consensus, the government posts measurable questions—such as whether a transit project will finish on schedule, or whether a proposed subsidy will raise housing starts above a clear threshold—and seeds the market with public liquidity. That subsidy is the crucial mechanism: commercial betting markets only care about flashy elections, whereas public liquidity pays domain specialists, quantitative analysts, and frontline insiders to uncover hard truths on technical policy questions.
[P1.4] In a subsidized market, being a contrarian who is right is no longer a career risk; it is a financial reward. If an official forecast is distorted by political wishful thinking, independent traders view that bias as free money and trade the price back toward reality. Experts do not disappear under this system—they simply lose their monopoly as unaccountable gatekeepers and must test their models against decentralized evidence.
[P1.5] Making these markets a formal input rather than a side experiment ensures bureaucrats cannot quietly bury inconvenient numbers. When market odds diverge sharply from an internal brief, leadership is forced to explain why before taxpayers pay the price for institutional blind spots.

[C1.1] Prediction markets can be useful forecasting tools, but the proposition confuses a price with a policy judgment. Major decisions require causal analysis, ethical trade-offs, legal constraints, and contingency planning—not merely a probability that one observable event occurs. A market might estimate whether housing starts exceed a threshold; it cannot decide whether displacement, fiscal cost, regional inequality, or environmental damage make the policy worthwhile. Those judgments must remain accountable to evidence-assessing officials, not bettors.
[C1.2] The hardest policy questions are also poor market contracts. Governments need counterfactuals: what happens if policy A is adopted rather than policy B? Once one policy is chosen, the alternative outcome is never observed, so its contract cannot cleanly settle. If contracts instead track outcomes after adoption, they cannot distinguish policy effects from recessions, wars, weather, or other confounders.
[C1.3] Long horizons worsen the problem: capital is tied up, participation thins, and crucial decisions occur years before traders learn who was right.
[C1.4] Subsidies solve none of this. They manufacture liquidity, not knowledge. Participants remain self-selected, with unequal wealth, risk tolerance, and access to information. Worse, once market prices become a formal government input, interested parties gain a reason to trade strategically.
[C1.5] A contractor could spend money pushing up the apparent probability that its project succeeds; the eventual trading loss may be trivial compared with winning a billion-dollar contract. “Manipulators will lose eventually” is no answer when the policy is chosen today and settlement comes years later.
[C1.6] Markets can also reward conduct government should discourage: trading on confidential plans, delaying disclosure, or exploiting privileged operational knowledge. Preventing those abuses requires eligibility rules, surveillance, and adjudication—recreating bureaucracy around an opaque signal.
[C1.7] Expert institutions certainly suffer groupthink, but those defects are addressable through independent review, recorded dissent, forecast scoring, red teams, and transparent assumptions. These mechanisms preserve causal reasoning and democratic responsibility. Use markets as optional cross-checks where questions are short-term and resolvable; do not make them a formal substitute for the institutions that must explain not only what may happen, but why, for whom, and whether it is worth doing.
