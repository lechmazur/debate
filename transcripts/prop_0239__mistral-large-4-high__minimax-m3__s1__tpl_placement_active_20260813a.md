[P1.1] A transaction can be perfectly consensual and still monstrous. A starving worker accepts a wage that cannot sustain her family—both parties agree to the terms, signatures are exchanged, yet the arrangement remains deeply exploitative. Consent here is real, but it is consent manufactured by structural duress, hollowed out by desperation and the absence of any meaningful alternative. Ethics demands more than mutual agreement; it requires that the background conditions render that agreement genuinely free and the outcome minimally fair to both sides.
[P1.2] Consider markets that consent cannot legitimize, no matter how sincerely given. A vote sold to the highest bidder, a kidney harvested from a debt-bonded laborer, a child "consenting" to factory labor because poverty left no alternative—these transactions carry the formal trappings of agreement while violating the substance of autonomy. If consent were sufficient, we would be forced to call slavery ethical whenever the enslaved sign a contract under threat. We refuse this conclusion because ethics recognizes that some goods are inalienable, some bargaining positions are so radically unequal that "agreement" becomes a polite fiction, and some harms extend beyond the contracting parties to communities, ecosystems, or future generations who never signed anything and cannot veto the deal.
[P1.3] The claim is certainly not that consent is worthless, but that consent is a necessary threshold, not a sufficient ceiling. A payday loan at 400% APR, signed willingly by a desperate borrower, remains usury. A transaction passes through the door of consent, but it must still be inspected for substantive fairness, for freedom from manipulation and coercion, and for externalized harm that ripples outward. Without these additional safeguards, consent becomes a laundering mechanism—a way to dress exploitation, commodification, and degradation in the respectable costume of choice.
[P1.4] Because ethical transactions require consent plus justice, plus non-exploitation, plus respect for limits that no contract can override, consent alone is not enough.
