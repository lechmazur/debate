[P1.1] The proposition is not that institutions should never be used. It is that they should stop being the automatic destination. When an older adult begins needing help, the first question should be what it would take to support them at home. That is the ethical default.
[P1.2] The deepest reason is autonomy. Home is where older adults keep control over daily rhythms—when to eat, bathe, sleep, see friends, keep a pet. Institutions standardize care through shared rooms, fixed schedules, restricted visits, and staff turnover. Those routines may be efficient, but they teach dependence, turning people into patients.
[P1.3] Home care preserves identity and competence that institutional life often erodes.
[P1.4] This is not mere sentiment. Familiar surroundings support orientation, especially in cognitive decline. A known kitchen, porch, or neighbor's voice can anchor memory and reduce agitation. Relocation to an unfamiliar facility is itself a stressor: disrupted sleep, delirium risk, falls, isolation, infection exposure.
[P1.5] Even good facilities cannot reproduce continuity of home.
[P1.6] The phrase "when possible" matters. It concedes that advanced nursing needs, severe wandering, or unsafe family situations may require facility care. But "possible" should be tested seriously. Medication prompts, meals, bathing, mobility help, and overnight monitoring can often be delivered at home with proper support and equipment.
[P1.7] The current default often moves people because home supports are underfunded or discharge planners take the path of least resistance, not because home care is truly impossible.
[P1.8] Cost follows the same logic. For many older adults with low or moderate needs, home-based care costs less than residential placement while preserving social ties institutions cannot buy. The test of a humane system is not efficiency. It is whether it starts from the least restrictive setting and builds help around the person.
[P1.9] Home should be that starting point.

[C1.1] The motion says elder care should be organized around home care "when possible." That phrase sounds modest, but it smuggles in the whole problem: possible for whom, and at whose expense? In practice, "home care" means family care—overwhelmingly daughters and wives working unpaid, often around the clock, with no training, no backup, and no exit. A default built on home care is a default built on invisible female labor.
[C1.2] Institutions, whatever their flaws, distribute that burden across trained staff, shifts, and society.
[C1.3] Second, home is not automatically the safe harbor it's painted to be. Relocation has risks, yes—but so does staying. A person at home with advancing dementia faces unattended medication errors, unmarked fall hazards, and the quiet danger of unnoticed decline. A neighbor doesn't chart changes; a call button doesn't prevent a fall that happens at 3 a.m. with no one in the house.
[C1.4] And abuse is easier to hide behind a front door than in a facility with multiple staff, mandated reporting, and visitor flow. Isolation cuts both ways: many home-bound elders see fewer people in a week than a facility resident sees in a day. The "known porch" can become a prison.
[C1.5] Third, the economics only work at low needs. Once someone requires overnight supervision, help with bathing and toileting, and clinical monitoring, one-on-one home staffing costs multiply—single-client care is inherently the most expensive way to deliver labor. Facilities achieve that thing families cannot: twenty-four-hour coverage from rotating, rested, trained workers.
[C1.6] Finally, autonomy. PRO says institutions turn people into patients. But an elder dependent on one exhausted relative is not autonomous; she is a burden her children carry in silence, and everyone in that house knows it. Real autonomy includes a right to professional care that doesn't require your daughter to sacrifice her health, career, and family.
[C1.7] The right default is need-based assessment, not a romantic presumption for home. Institutions should remain a legitimate, well-funded first option—not the failure state this motion makes them out to be.
