[P1.1] The most dangerous assumption in cyber strategy is that the defender's skills can be kept in a barracks. They cannot. Cyber expertise has a half-life measured in months, not careers. The people who understand this year's exploits are the ones patching production systems at a bank on Tuesday, reverse-engineering malware at a security firm on Wednesday, and running a hospital's network on Thursday.
[P1.2] A standing military unit asks those people to leave that current, take a pay cut, and wait for a war. Most refuse; those who accept slowly fall behind the threat they are supposed to counter. A civilian cyber-reserve borrows them at the peak of their relevance instead of trying, and failing, to own them.
[P1.3] That points to the second problem: the terrain. The targets that matter are civilian: power grids, water utilities, ports, payment systems, hospitals. Militaries cannot legally or practically sit inside every utility's network, and they do not know those systems. Reservists already work there.
[P1.4] When Estonia was hit in 2007, the response that mattered came from a volunteer Cyber Defence League of bankers, sysadmins, and engineers who already knew the infrastructure. Ukraine's defense since 2022 has leaned heavily on civilian volunteers and private-sector defenders, not a uniformed cadre. That is not improvisation; it is the correct structure showing itself under fire.
[P1.5] Third, cyber crises are lumpy. A standing unit sized for peacetime is drowned by a NotPetya-scale event; one sized for that event sits idle and expensive for years. Reserves surge and recede at a fraction of the cost.
[P1.6] To be clear about the clash: the motion is about where a country places its reliance, its center of gravity. A small classified core for offensive and intelligence tasks can exist, but if the backbone of national cyber resilience is a standing military unit rather than a broad, trained, legally organized reserve, the country has bought a slow, expensive, and thin defense for an adversary that is fast, cheap, and everywhere.

It is command, clearance, continuity, and lawful force. Those belong to professional units already inside the chain of command, not to specialists who can be borrowed if an employer agrees.
[C1.2] The motion is substitution: build reserves rather than rely on standing forces. That is the error. Skills decay, but they decay faster without a daily mission, a shared playbook, and a classified picture of the adversary. Standing units hunt, exercise, and integrate intelligence every week.
[C1.3] A reserve drills, then returns to a network the military cannot even see. Peak civilian relevance is not peak national relevance. Knowing a hospital's tickets is not knowing a foreign service's campaign.
[C1.4] Availability fails at the worst moment. When a grid or payment system is hit, the people the other side wants to surge are the people those institutions cannot release. Dual employment is dual unavailability. A standing unit is paid to stay, already assembled, already authorized.
[C1.5] Legal authority is worse. Escalation, offensive response, and cross-border operations are not volunteer work. Civilians inside utilities cannot be the backbone of actions that risk armed conflict. Making them the center of gravity also widens the attack surface: more uncleared knowledge, more conflicts of interest, more leakage into firms that still sell software worldwide.
[C1.6] Estonia in 2007 and Ukrainian volunteers since 2022 show societies improvising under fire. They do not show that professional military cyber forces should be displaced. Those states also used intelligence services, military units, and allies. Stopgaps are not strategy.
[C1.7] Rely on standing units. Reserves may assist. They cannot be the country's reliance.
