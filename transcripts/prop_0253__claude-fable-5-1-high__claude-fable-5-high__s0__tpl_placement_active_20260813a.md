[P1.1] A driver with six years and several thousand five-star trips on one app is, on paper, a highly trusted professional. The moment she opens a competing app, she is a stranger with zero history, shunted to the back of the queue, given the worst jobs, and treated as a fraud risk. Her reputation didn't vanish. It is being held hostage.
[P1.2] That hostage-taking is the whole case. Ratings and verified work history are created by the worker's labor and the customer's judgment. The platform merely stores them. Yet platforms treat that record as proprietary because it is the cheapest switching cost ever invented.
[P1.3] A worker who cannot carry her track record cannot credibly leave, and a worker who cannot leave cannot bargain. When the app cuts the per-mile rate or changes the algorithm, her only option is to accept it, because starting over elsewhere means months of lower earnings. Lock-in is how platforms suppress pay without ever negotiating.
[P1.4] Portability restores the one form of power gig workers actually have: exit. It does not require unionization, price controls, or reclassifying anyone as an employee. It simply makes the labor market competitive again, so platforms must compete for experienced workers rather than warehouse them.
[P1.5] We already know this works. Mobile number portability turned carriers from captors into competitors within a few years. Open banking and general data-portability rights have shown that standardized, machine-readable transfer is technically ordinary once mandated. A signed, verified record of completed jobs, ratings, and account standing is far simpler than a bank ledger.
[P1.6] Anticipate the objection that a rating on one platform means something different on another. Fine. Portability means the worker can carry her record, labeled by source; the receiving platform decides how to weight it. Some context loss beats total erasure every time.
[P1.7] And "workers might game it" collapses when the data is cryptographically verified by the originating platform, not self-reported.
[P1.8] The question is simple: who owns the reputation you earned? The proposition says you do.
