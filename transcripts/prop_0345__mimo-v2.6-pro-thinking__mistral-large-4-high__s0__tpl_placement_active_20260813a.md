[P1.1] The question is narrow: when a large employer orders thousands of workers back to desks, is "culture" enough? It isn't, and the reason is simple — a mandate that imposes real costs on real people needs a reason that can be tested, and "culture" cannot be tested.
[P1.2] Consider what a justification requirement actually does. It doesn't ban return-to-office. An employer who can say "these roles require hands-on equipment access," "these teams handle regulated data on secure networks," or "this client-facing role requires in-person presence" complies easily. Those are role-specific, checkable reasons.
[P1.3] What fails is a blanket order applied to a security engineer, a payroll analyst, and a sales lead on identical terms justified by the word "collaboration."
[P1.4] That distinction matters because the costs of a mandate are not symbolic. Commutes are unpaid time and money moved onto workers. Caregivers and disabled employees bear disproportionate burdens, often forcing resignations that look voluntary but aren't. When employers know exactly which roles need bodies in rooms, vague mandates aren't just imprecise — they transfer cost to the people with the least power to refuse.
[P1.5] The asymmetry is decisive here. Employers hold the data: which teams produce, which roles touch physical assets, which functions depend on proximity. Workers cannot see inside those decisions. Requiring a role-specific reason puts the burden where the information already lives.
[P1.6] If a mandate is genuinely driven by business need, writing that need down is nearly free. The only ones burdened are those imposing costs they cannot defend.

[C1.6] Finally, markets already discipline employers. If a mandate imposes costs without benefit, talent exits, productivity falls, and the firm loses to competitors offering flexibility.
