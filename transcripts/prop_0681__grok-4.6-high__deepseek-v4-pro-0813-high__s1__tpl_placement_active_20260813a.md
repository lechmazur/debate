[P1.1] Mental health crises are medical and social emergencies, not crimes. Sending armed officers as the default first responders treats illness as a public-order problem. That mismatch produces force, criminalization, and trauma even when no one intended harm. Expanding civilian crisis teams is the correction: clinicians and peer specialists arrive to de-escalate, assess, and connect people to care rather than to handcuffs.
[P1.2] Speed is not the same as safety. A faster arrival that ends in a takedown, a jail booking, or a fatal encounter is not a successful response. Families calling about a relative in psychosis need someone who can talk a person down and get them to treatment. Minutes matter less than whether that person is alive, uninjured, and not entered into the criminal system.
[P1.3] Those same neighborhoods already absorb the aftermath: hospital boarding after a struggle, records that block housing, and people who stop calling after uniforms arrived. We already wait for ambulances when those are the right tools. We do not send the nearest squad car to a cardiac arrest merely because it can get there first.
[P1.4] The motion already faces the tradeoff: some neighborhoods may wait longer. That cost is real, but it does not justify the status quo. Uneven wait times are a staffing and dispatch problem. Cities can triage the highest-risk calls, keep police as backup when violence is imminent, and still pull officers off the large share of behavioral-health runs that do not require a gun and a badge.
[P1.5] A few extra minutes of waiting is a smaller harm than a combat-trained response arriving to treat a psychiatric emergency.
[P1.6] Keeping police as first contact because they arrive sooner locks cities into a harmful default. Expansion is justified because a better-fit response, even if slower in some places, reduces harms that speed cannot undo. The right question is not who arrives first. It is who should arrive at all.

[C1.6] Police-civilian co-response or better-trained officers can keep the rapid response and add clinical support.
