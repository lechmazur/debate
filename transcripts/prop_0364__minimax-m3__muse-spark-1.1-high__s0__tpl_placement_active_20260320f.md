[P1.1] When a worker's paycheck depends on a number, that worker has a right to see the number. Right now, the people who actually make what streaming platforms sell — actors, writers, directors, crews — are paid partly on viewership metrics they cannot verify, cannot audit, and cannot meaningfully challenge. The proposition asks for one narrow, modest remedy: standardized viewing data shared with the people whose compensation rides on it.
[P1.2] This is not a request for proprietary algorithms or trade secrets. It is a request for the basic ledger entry: who watched, for how long, on what kind of account. The same kind of data Nielsen standardized for broadcast television decades ago — and the same kind of data platforms already use internally to decide which shows to renew and which to cancel.
[P1.3] Three points frame today's clash. First, without standardized data, "audience reach" is whatever the platform says it is. Two near-identical performances on competing services can produce wildly different residual checks based on opaque, non-comparable definitions. That is not compensation tied to reach; it is compensation tied to the platform's discretion dressed up as a metric.
[P1.4] Second, the people harmed are not just A-list stars. They are guest actors earning scale-plus bonuses, writers receiving backend participation, and crews whose pension and health contributions are calculated on residuals — all of whom lack bargaining power to demand audits case by case. Third, standardization is the operative word. The harm is not merely that data is hidden, but that each service measures differently, making careers and contracts effectively non-portable across the industry.
[P1.5] Opponents will raise privacy and cost. Privacy is solved by aggregation and contractual limits already standard in the rest of the data economy. Cost is trivial relative to the billions in question. The real question for the house is simple: should the people who create the value be allowed to see how that value is measured?
