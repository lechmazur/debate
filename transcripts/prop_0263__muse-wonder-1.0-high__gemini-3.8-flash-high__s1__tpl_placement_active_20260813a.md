[P1.1] When a completed film can be marketed and watched, then vanish because an accountant finds more value in deleting it than keeping it, availability stops being an ordinary business choice. It becomes erasure.
[P1.2] Streaming originals have a single gatekeeper. A theatrical flop once lived on through reruns, rentals, discs, and libraries. But when a service removes its own original for a tax write-off — as with shelved films like Batgirl and series abruptly pulled shortly after release — there is often no legal copy left to buy, borrow, or stream. Actors cannot show their performances, writers cannot point to their episodes, and subscribers who paid for advertised titles cannot rewatch them.
[P1.3] A work released to culture is retroactively unreleased.
[P1.4] That disappearance is funded twice. Subscribers join because originals are sold as the exclusive, lasting reason to pay. Productions also commonly benefit from public tax incentives meant to create lasting value. A company should not collect subscriptions and subsidies to make a work, then collect again by declaring it worthless and withdrawing it.
[P1.5] A minimum period closes that double dip: if you monetize creation as a public offering, keep it public for a reasonable time.
[P1.6] The rule also fixes a perverse incentive. Slow-building or niche work needs time to find an audience through word of mouth, awards, or a creator's later success. If immediate removal offers a cleaner return than patience, executives will choose deletion over discovery. Guaranteed time gives culture time to work and creators the audience they were promised.
[P1.7] This is not a demand for forever, free access, or prominent promotion. Keep the title accessible — on the original service or another legitimate outlet — for a minimum period, then licensing resumes as normal. Next to production budgets, digital retention is cheap. What is banned is only the most cynical exit: profiting first from making something for the public, then from making it disappear.

[C1.1] Mandating that streaming services keep every original title publicly available sounds like a defense of culture, but it is actually the surest way to stifle it.
[C1.2] First, PRO fundamentally mischaracterizes why platforms remove content. PRO imagines digital retention is virtually free and deletions are mere cynical accounting tricks [P1.1, P1.7]. In reality, keeping titles live triggers substantial recurring liabilities: contractual guild residuals, ongoing music licensing renewals, and technical maintenance. When a niche series underperforms, retaining it drains resources that could fund new projects.
[C1.3] Forcing services into mandatory multi-year availability transforms every creative gamble into an unhedged financial liability. Far from giving slow-burn art "time to work" [P1.6], this exit penalty will cause platforms to drastically reduce risk at the greenlight stage. They will stop funding experimental, niche, or debut projects, retreating entirely to safe, established corporate franchises.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's mandate ignores that removal is often reallocation, not erasure. Titles are frequently pulled precisely to be licensed to free ad-supported streaming services, where underwatched shows find broader audiences and generate fresh revenue for creators. Furthermore, writing down impaired assets is standard accounting across all industries, allowing companies to absorb commercial losses and stay solvent. Stripping platforms of this financial flexibility in an already volatile streaming market starves studios of capital, leading directly to fewer total productions and fewer industry jobs.
[C1.5] Finally, this proposal represents an unprecedented, distortive intrusion into creative commerce. Book publishers are not legally barred from pulping unsold books; theaters are not forced to screen to empty auditoriums; networks are not mandated to broadcast cancelled shows. Compelling private platforms to act as mandatory public archives misunderstands how media thrives. Culture flourishes when creators and distributors have the freedom to take risks, absorb losses, and reinvest in what comes next.

Franchises are already the default response to volatility; instant deletion gives diverse work less protection, not more.
[P2.5] The book and theatre comparisons fail on the decisive fact: copies escape. A pulped print run leaves sold books, libraries and resale. A cancelled network show could enter syndication or home video. A removed streaming original can leave no lawful copy at all.
[P2.6] Because the platform is sole gatekeeper, its private decision carries a public finality no bookseller possesses.
[P2.7] Nor does ordinary accounting require disappearance. Firms write down impaired assets while discounting, selling or donating them. Recognising a loss does not demand locking audiences out. Keep honest accounting if needed — but do not make erasure the price of the deduction.
