[P1.1] If someone’s pay is explicitly tied to how many people watched their work, they are entitled to see the number that decides that pay. That is the entire core of this motion, and it should be uncontroversial.
[P1.2] Right now, this is not how streaming works. A television writer, actor, or director can sign a contract that says residuals will be calculated from total viewership, then never be shown that viewership number. They get a check, and are told to accept that the streaming service did the math correctly. There is no audit, no verification, not even an explanation of what counts as a view in the first place.
[P1.3] This is unheard of in every other line of work. If you work sales commission, you get the full sales log. If you earn a factory output bonus, you see the production counts. No reasonable person would accept a job where their boss says “I keep a secret number that determines your pay.
[P1.4] You can never see it. Just trust me.”
[P1.5] Opponents will raise worries about competitive trade secrecy. But this motion only applies when the service has already chosen to tie compensation to audience reach. They picked the metric. They just refuse to let the people being paid by that metric verify it.
[P1.6] Standardized reporting does not require releasing strategic programming plans, or unannounced audience research. It just requires that everyone uses the same yardstick, that the people paid by that yardstick can actually read it.
[P1.7] This is not an attack on streaming business models. It is the minimum standard for honest contracts.

[C1.1] Pay should be verifiable. It does not follow that every filmmaker and performer is entitled to a standardized feed of a streamer's viewing data, and making that leap a legal requirement would corrupt the very number everyone wants to trust.
[C1.2] There is no neutral thing called a “view” waiting to be disclosed. Streaming is someone pressing play for thirty seconds, a show autoplaying while a household cooks, one account shared by a family, a film finished over three nights. Any standardized definition — two minutes watched, most of an episode completed, unique households reached — is an editorial and commercial choice, not an accounting fact. Mandate one yardstick and you do not reveal truth; you anoint an arbitrary metric and force all contracts and genres to bend around it.
[C1.3] That bending is destructive. Once a single reach figure is official and comparable, creative decisions chase it: front-loaded hooks to clear a threshold, episodes padded or chopped to count as completions, marketing concentrated on opening weekends. Niche documentaries, children's programming rewatched by the same households, and prestige series valued for retaining subscribers rather than winning first clicks will look like failures under a standard never designed to capture their value.
[C1.4] Disclosure at this scale is not contained. Viewing patterns are core competitive intelligence: what retains subscribers, what fails despite promotion, where audiences drop off. Hand that, title by title, to thousands of performers, agents, and representatives, and it will leak and be used by rivals not to check a paycheck but to copy strategy. Granular data also risks exposing habits of small, identifiable audiences.
[C1.5] The honest problem has a narrower solution: confidential verification. Let unions or independent auditors inspect underlying records under nondisclosure, challenge the definition in that specific contract, and confirm the check. That gives creators proof without imposing a universal metric, broadcasting trade secrets, or pretending an invented definition is objective truth.

A confidential auditor can verify titles automatically, confirm the contractual definition was applied unchanged, and give each creator a certified statement of their title's inputs and payout. Internally, a service can weigh completion, rewatching, retention, and acquisition differently for a documentary and a drama. Standardize that judgment, and agents negotiate to it, executives greenlight to it, and opening minutes get engineered to cross it.
[C2.5] The warping is worse when the count becomes a cross-platform currency for money and reputation.
[C2.6] The slogan in [P2.5] also proves too much. [C2.7] Creators deserve the definition promised, their own certified calculation, and a real challenge process. The motion demands more: an artificial common currency. That extra step distorts creative choices and exposes strategy without making any individual paycheck more accurate.
