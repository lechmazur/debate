[P1.1] When a government moves its benefit applications, school enrolment, tax filing, and medical appointments online, it has already decided that internet access is essential. The only remaining question is whether it is honest enough to guarantee what it now requires. That is what this motion asks: not whether internet is nice, but whether the state may make it the gateway to everything else while leaving the gate itself to chance.
[P1.2] Picture the student during a school closure doing homework from a fast-food parking lot because that is the nearest reliable signal. Picture the farmer who cannot file a subsidy claim because the form only exists online and the connection drops. Nobody calls these inconveniences. They are exclusions from public life, produced by the same institutions that then shrug at the gap.
[P1.3] The economics are the economics of rural electrification, almost line for line. The last mile to low-density areas costs more than private providers can recover, so without an obligation they simply don't build. Every country that electrified its countryside did it by imposing duties and funding cross-subsidies, not by waiting for the market to discover charity. Universal service obligations for telephones have existed for decades on exactly this logic.
[P1.4] Several countries, including the UK and Finland, have already extended a legal right to a baseline broadband connection. This is not a leap into the unknown; it is finishing a job already begun.
[P1.5] "Legally enforceable" is the load-bearing phrase. Governments announce broadband targets constantly and miss them constantly, because a target has no claimant. A right has one. When a household can demand connection and a regulator must answer, the money and the rollout follow, as they did for water and power.
[P1.6] "High-quality" means functional: enough to work, learn, and see a doctor, with the floor rising as technology does, exactly as drinking water standards rise. Not fiber to every cabin tomorrow; a guaranteed on-ramp to the society the state has already moved online.

[C1.1] Essential is not the same as legally enforceable, and useful is not the same as water. Food matters more than broadband, yet we do not guarantee every household a state-enforceable supply of a defined quality of food, because the delivery system, the product, and the costs are different. Internet fails the utility analogy on exactly those grounds.
[C1.2] Water and electricity are stable physical necessities: clean water in 1990 keeps you alive today, and a power socket delivers a standardized commodity through a mature, monopoly network. "High-quality internet" is a moving target. Speed sufficient for email became inadequate for video classes, which will become inadequate for whatever follows. Write that into enforceable law and you get one of two failures: a floor frozen into obsolescence, or a floor that escalates, committing taxpayers to perpetual upgrades no legislature voted to price.
[C1.3] That escalating commitment falls hardest where resources are scarcest. Laying fiber to a remote settlement can cost many times more per household than serving a city block, competing directly with clinics, clean water, and electricity for the same public money. A legally enforceable right does not repeal that arithmetic; it lets courts and regulators override democratic budgeting, forcing governments to fund the most expensive connections first to satisfy claimants, even when mobile coverage or targeted subsidies would connect more people for less.
[C1.4] The instrument misdiagnoses exclusion. The student in a parking lot may lack a device, data allowance, digital skills, or a quiet place to study as much as a line. A universal service obligation channels money into network build-out — often to incumbents best at claiming subsidies — while freezing out solutions like competition, satellite service, community networks, and device and affordability support tailored to need.
[C1.5] Governments should expand access aggressively. But through funded, revisable policies they can adapt as technology changes, not through an enforceable utility right that promises an undefined standard forever and pays for it by distorting priorities.
