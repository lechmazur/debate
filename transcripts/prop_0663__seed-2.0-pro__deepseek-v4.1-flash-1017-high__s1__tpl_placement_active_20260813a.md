[C1.1] The proposition mistakes speed of announcement for speed of response. PRO’s exponential clock is real, but the bottleneck is rarely that WHO lacks a legal key to the ministry of health. It is weak surveillance, slow labs, uncertain signals, and—most importantly—whether governments and publics trust the system enough to share bad news early. Attach stronger verification access to a loss of national messaging control, and you create a predictable incentive to hide: states delay notification, restrict samples, keep investigations informal, and route information around WHO.
[C1.2] You cannot verify data that frightened institutions never generate. Formal access becomes a dead letter, or worse, a coercive ritual that looks like transparency while real information goes dark.
[C1.3] National control over early messaging is not merely vanity. Early outbreak signals are ambiguous. Local officials know whether a cluster is in a hospital, a market, or a border community; they know the language, the stigma, and the political fragility of the moment. An external verifier can easily misread that context and trigger disproportionate fear, border closures, or discrimination.
[C1.4] The proposition says governments lose only the ability to hide “verifiable” evidence, but what counts as verifiable in the first days is exactly what is contested.
[C1.5] And trust is the actual currency of outbreak response. People comply with isolation, testing, and vaccination when warnings feel legitimate and locally owned. A globally correct message that communities reject is not faster; it is slower, because it breeds denial and resistance.
[C1.6] WHO is also a political body, not a neutral inspector. Stronger access would invite geopolitical fights over what counts as verification and punish the organization when great powers disagree. The durable route is incentives for rapid sharing, protected channels, joint verification, and local capacity—not trading cooperation for control over the first message.
