[P1.1] Consumer tablets are engineered so a toddler can master swiping in minutes; sustained attention and deep literacy take years of effortful practice. Adopting a device-light, paper-first policy in early grades gets the developmental sequence right: build the child’s mind before introducing machines that fragment focus.
[P1.2] Start with how young children actually learn to read and think. Forming letters by hand on paper forces the brain to coordinate fine motor control, visual tracking, and memory, cementing phonics and spelling far more deeply than tapping glass. Likewise, physical books provide tactile and spatial anchors—knowing where an idea sits on a page and feeling progress through a story—that strengthen comprehension. Screens condition beginners to skim and click.
[P1.3] During the critical window when children learn to read, replacing pencils and print with tablets trades durable cognitive wiring for shallow habits.
[P1.4] Worse, early-grade software actively erodes attention. Because six-year-olds struggle to self-regulate on screens, classroom apps rely on gamification—flashing animations, sound effects, and instant reward loops—to hold their gaze. That trains developing brains to expect constant novelty, making quiet, patient problem-solving feel intolerable. Meanwhile, tablets pull eyes away from peers and teachers, replacing rich spoken interaction and physical manipulatives with isolated screen time, while turning teachers into IT troubleshooters managing logins, glitches, and stray taps.
[P1.5] Crucially, a device-light policy is smart sequencing, not prohibition. Assistive tools for students with disabilities and targeted, teacher-led uses remain intact, and one-to-one devices still arrive in upper grades. That answers any worry about digital readiness or equity. A fourth-grader armed with strong reading stamina, handwriting, and impulse control will master classroom technology rapidly; a child raised on gamified apps enters later grades with weaker comprehension and fractured focus.
[P1.6] True equity in early schooling means guaranteeing every child foundational literacy and attention—not saturating classrooms with the screens already crowding childhood.
