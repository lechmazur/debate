[P1.1] A performer's voice is not raw material like footage of a set. It is the thing the audience actually hires them for — timing, breath, catch in the throat. AI dubbing does not translate that performance; it manufactures a new one, in their voice, saying words they never spoke, with inflections they never chose. Whatever a general "new and future technologies" clause covered when it was drafted, it did not cover a machine that can make an actor say anything, forever.
[P1.2] That is the whole case for opt-in. Consent is only meaningful when the person consenting can picture what they are agreeing to. A supporting actor who signed a standard agreement in 2015 could picture dubbing by another human performer in Rome or Mexico City. She could not picture a model trained on her that later voices her character in a sequel she was never cast in, a video game she never auditioned for, or a scene rewritten after she left.
[P1.3] Broad boilerplate does not transmit information; it transmits risk, downhill, to the person with the least leverage.
[P1.4] And leverage is the point. Boilerplate is not negotiated by day players and background performers; it is presented. If the industry genuinely believes performers would happily license synthetic voice replication, then asking should be cheap and the answer usually yes. Opt-in only bites where the performer would have said no — which is exactly the case the objection is admitting it wants to override.
[P1.5] Nor is this exotic. Music already clears samples track by track. Likeness rights already require specific grants for merchandise. Stunt doubles are contracted, not conjured.
[P1.6] A checkbox at signature, with a defined scope and a fee, is an administrative line item — not a barrier to innovation.
[P1.7] The asymmetry decides it. If we require opt-in and get it wrong, a studio pays slightly more and waits a week. If we rely on boilerplate and get it wrong, a performer's voice belongs to someone else permanently. Only one of those mistakes is reversible.
