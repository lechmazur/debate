[P1.1] Gene-editing therapies represent the most consequential medical revolution in a generation. Treatments that can functionally cure sickle cell disease, certain hereditary blindness conditions, and blood cancers already exist. The central question is not whether they work—it is whether only the wealthy will survive long enough to benefit from them.
[P1.2] Current gene-editing therapies routinely carry price tags exceeding a million dollars per patient. That pricing is not inevitable; it reflects patent monopolies and market capture, not proportional research costs. When a single company holds exclusive rights to a cure, it sets the price the market will bear—and for patients in low- and middle-income countries, or even uninsured patients in wealthy ones, that price is extinction by invoice.
[P1.3] A public-interest licensing pathway solves this directly. It follows the same legal architecture as compulsory licensing under TRIPS flexibilities, which governments have invoked for decades to produce affordable HIV antiretrovirals. The Doha Declaration confirmed that intellectual property rules must bend to public health emergencies. The proposition simply extends that settled principle—already tested, already legitimate—to gene therapies designated as essential.
[P1.4] Critics will argue this kills innovation. That argument was made about AIDS drugs and was wrong. Most foundational gene-editing research—CRISPR mechanisms, delivery systems, safety data—was developed through publicly funded universities and government grants. Taxpayers underwrote the discovery; they should not be held hostage to extract profits from it.
[P1.5] A licensing pathway does not confiscate patents; it ensures that when a therapy is essential and unaffordable, a government can authorize a licensed producer at a regulated price. The innovator still profits; they simply cannot monopolize access to a cure.
[P1.6] The real stakes: without this mechanism, gene editing becomes the sharpest instrument of health inequality ever devised—a technology that could end hereditary disease, selectively available to those born wealthy. That outcome is not a market externality to manage later. It is a preventable injustice that only a licensing pathway can structurally correct.

[C1.1] The core analogy propping up this proposal collapses on contact: comparing gene-editing therapies to small-molecule HIV antiretrovirals ignores fundamental differences in how these medicines are made and distributed. Antiretrovirals are stable oral pills easily reverse-engineered by generic manufacturers, which is why compulsory licensing drove their prices down sharply in the 2000s. Gene-editing therapies are complex biologics, often personalized to individual patients, requiring ultra-cold supply chains, specialized viral vector production, and highly trained administration teams. Even if a government forces a license, there are only a handful of global facilities capable of producing these therapies at scale.
[C1.2] The pathway would not lower prices—it would only add regulatory uncertainty while delivering no meaningful access gains.
[C1.3] Proponents also misstate the innovation calculus. While foundational CRISPR research received public funding, the translational work to turn lab discoveries into safe, approved therapies, plus multi-billion-dollar clinical trial pipelines and specialized manufacturing scale-up, are almost entirely private-sector investments. A public-interest licensing pathway that compels "affordable" pricing strips the return on that risk capital. If companies cannot recoup the massive costs to bring a gene therapy to market, they will divert R&D to less volatile therapeutic areas.
[C1.4] The result is not cheaper cures, but fewer cures developed at all.
[C1.5] The claim that innovators "still profit" under this system is equally hollow. "Affordable" is never defined against the full cost of R&D, supply chain maintenance, and future pipeline funding. If regulators set prices at a fraction of development costs, the margin needed to sustain next-generation research vanishes. Worse, the undefined "essential" designation gives governments arbitrary power to target any therapy, creating unpredictable risk that deters all biotech investment, not just for the single drug in question.
