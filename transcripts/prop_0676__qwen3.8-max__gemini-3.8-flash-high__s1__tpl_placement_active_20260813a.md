[P1.1] Automated video and personality assessments turn a person’s face, voice, and self-reported thoughts into a hiring score. That score often functions as a gatekeeper before any human ever speaks to the candidate. Governments should ban or heavily restrict this practice because it combines three failures: unreliable science, discriminatory impact, and unaccountable power.
[P1.2] First, many of these tools measure what they claim only weakly. Video interviews infer diligence, enthusiasm, or trustworthiness from micro-expressions, tone, and word choice. But expressions vary across cultures, languages, and neurological differences. A candidate with autism may score poorly on eye contact; a non-native speaker may be penalized for pauses; someone with anxiety may be marked as low-energy.
[P1.3] Personality tests often reward confident self-presentation rather than job competence. When a tool is not a valid measure of performance, using it at scale is not efficiency—it is automated guessing dressed up as data.
[P1.4] Second, the harm is structural. Employers do not need to intend discrimination for the outcome to be discriminatory. Once an opaque model screens thousands of applicants, small biases become massive exclusion. Candidates cannot cross-examine a black box, cannot see why they lost, and cannot meaningfully appeal.
[P1.5] Vendors protect algorithms as trade secrets, while applicants bear the consequences. That is exactly the kind of asymmetry regulation exists to correct.
[P1.6] Third, consent is not real when the alternative is no job application. Workers cannot negotiate away privacy and equality rights just to be considered for work.
[P1.7] CON will say these tools reduce bias or speed hiring. But speed is no excuse for unjust sorting, and bias claims are too often vendor promises, not proven outcomes. The right baseline is simple: if an employer cannot demonstrate that an automated assessment is job-relevant, validated, auditable, and accommodates disability, government should keep it out of hiring.
