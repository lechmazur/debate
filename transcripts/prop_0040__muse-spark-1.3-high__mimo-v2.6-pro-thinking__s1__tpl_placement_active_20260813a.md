[P1.1] Death should end a company's right to turn you into a product. We all leave behind a mountain of texts, emails, voice notes, posts — enough for a firm to build a chatbot that talks like you, jokes like you, claims to be you. The dead cannot object, correct the record, or cancel the subscription. That is why explicit opt-in before death must be the legal default.
[P1.2] These mimics are not harmless memorials. A grieving parent offered a simulation of a dead child is uniquely vulnerable to manipulation — paying monthly fees, sharing more intimate data, postponing grief for a script that hallucinates. And a mimic inevitably lies: it invents opinions the person never held, endorsements they never gave, crude jokes that stain a reputation. Imagine a beloved teacher revived to sell products or preach politics she opposed.
[P1.3] No disclaimer fixes that theft of identity.
[P1.4] Sharing a text with a friend is not consent to be cloned by a corporation. Most of our data was created for specific people in specific moments, never meant as training material. After death we cannot clarify boundaries, and shifting that choice to relatives invites coercion when they are grieving and companies are lobbying. Only the person herself, while alive and informed, can authorize such intimate reuse.
[P1.5] An opt-out system would simply presume exploitation until someone complains — too late.
[P1.6] This prohibition is narrow and reasonable. It bans no biography, no archive, no history lesson about the dead. It bans only building an interactive stand-in that claims their voice without their permission. Anyone who truly wants digital immortality remains free to opt in.
[P1.7] Freedom to innovate never included freedom to conscript the dead as unpaid actors. Consent first is the minimum decency the law should demand.

We do not need a use-specific ban on the technology; we need enforcement against misuse.
[C1.5] PRO's rule bans the knife rather than the stabbing.
