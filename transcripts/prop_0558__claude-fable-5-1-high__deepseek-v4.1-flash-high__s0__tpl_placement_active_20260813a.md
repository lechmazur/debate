[P1.1] Every liberal state accepts one principle without argument: you cannot lock a person up without telling them when they will get out. We apply it to convicted criminals. We deny it to people whose only "offense" is that a bureaucracy has not yet decided their paperwork. That is the practice this motion ends.
[P1.2] Be precise about what is on the table. The motion does not abolish detention. A government may still detain someone briefly to verify identity, or for a fixed, judicially reviewed period where an individual assessment shows a genuine flight or safety risk. What it prohibits is detention with no end date, and what it installs is the default every other area of law already uses: release unless the state proves, case by case, why this person must be held.
[P1.3] Three reasons this is the right default.
[P1.4] First, indefinite detention is punishment without conviction. In systems without a time limit, people have been held for years, some ultimately released because no country would take them, meaning the state inflicted years of confinement for nothing. Australia's highest court recently rejected indefinite detention where removal is not realistically foreseeable, precisely because open-ended imprisonment without a purpose is arbitrary by definition.
[P1.5] Second, it does not work better; it works worse. Detention costs governments hundreds of dollars per person per day. Case management (regular check-ins, a caseworker, legal information, stable housing) costs a fraction and, in programs run in several countries, produces appearance and compliance rates in the high nineties. People abscond when they are confused and hopeless, not when someone is guiding them through the process.
[P1.6] Engagement, not concrete, delivers compliance.
[P1.7] Third, indefiniteness itself destroys people. Prisoners with sentences can count down. Detainees without end dates deteriorate, self-harm, and break, and clinicians consistently identify the uncertainty, not merely the confinement, as the driver.
