[P1.1] A price increase disguised as a package change is still a price increase. When a 500g box of cereal quietly becomes 450g at the same price, the shopper has paid roughly eleven percent more. The only question this motion asks is whether that shopper gets to know.
[P1.2] Markets discipline prices through visibility. Consumers can punish a headline price hike: they notice, grumble, switch brands, trade down. Shrinkflation exists precisely to defeat that discipline. It works because people anchor on the familiar box and the familiar price tag, and because a unit price printed in grey six-point type tells you today's cost per kilo but never tells you it was lower last month.
[P1.3] The information the market needs to function is being deliberately routed around the shopper's attention. Requiring a clear flag — "this pack shrank from 500g to 450g; unit price up about 11%" — simply restores the signal that a sticker on the shelf edge would have carried if the manufacturer had raised the price honestly.
[P1.4] Why mandate rather than trust voluntary effort? Because the incentives run the wrong way. A retailer who flags shrinkflation embarrasses suppliers it depends on and risks looking like the store where prices climb. A few chains have experimented with shrinkflation labels, and some governments have moved toward notices, so feasibility is not in doubt.
[P1.5] But a voluntary disclosure that only the most scrupulous seller makes is a competitive penalty on scruples. Rules level that field.
[P1.6] The cost is trivial. Supermarkets already track pack weights and unit prices in their inventory systems; apps update prices in real time and could highlight a size change with one line of code. Nothing new must be measured — only displayed.

[C1.2] Start with what a shopper needs at the moment of choice: which of these fourteen cereals gives me the most food per pound *today*. What PRO adds is a history lesson about one box's past — information that changes nothing about which option is cheapest now. If the 450g box still beats its rivals per kilo, "it used to be 500g" is a grievance, not a decision aid.
[C1.3] The genuine problem PRO identifies — grey six-point type — is a legibility problem in unit pricing, and it is solved by making unit prices bigger and consistently formatted, not by bolting on a change log.
[C1.4] Then the mechanism problem. A shrunk pack is usually a new SKU with a new barcode; the old item is delisted, not amended. So a mandate requires defining a baseline: how far back, which pack variant, which store, and what counts as "an obvious headline price cut." That is not one line of code — it is a compliance regime with definitional edges lawyers argue over, and the cost lands hardest on small independents and app operators without enterprise data teams.
[C1.5] Worst of all is the asymmetry. Flag size cuts and stigmatise them, and firms take the unflagged route instead: thinner ingredients, cheaper cocoa fraction, more water, less cheese on the pizza. Shrinkflation is at least measurable. Quality shrinkflation is not, and no label will catch it.
[C1.6] A rule that punishes the visible form of cost-passing while leaving the invisible form untouched pushes producers toward the deception the shopper can never audit.
[C1.7] And if the look-back is generous, a large share of tags carry a notice — and a warning on everything is a warning on nothing.
