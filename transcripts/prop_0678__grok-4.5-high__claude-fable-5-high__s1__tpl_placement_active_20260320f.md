[P1.1] Allowing companies free rein to deploy AI-driven video and keystroke monitoring on remote workers would turn private homes into corporate surveillance zones. The only defensible rule is that such monitoring may proceed solely with clear opt-in consent and strict limits.
[P1.2] Video feeds capture living rooms, family members, and off-hour life. Keystroke logging records every typed word, pause, and correction—revealing not just output metrics but personal messages, passwords, and private thoughts. AI then scores this stream for “engagement,” “idle time,” or anomalies, multiplying error and bias. Without consent, the practice is forced intrusion into intimate space.
[P1.3] Opt-in consent is required because remote work already magnifies power imbalances. Workers must not face quiet pressure to accept constant watching as the price of a paycheck. Real opt-in demands a viable non-monitored alternative or equivalent role, full disclosure of every AI system and its purposes, and the freedom to withdraw without retaliation.
[P1.4] Strict limits shut down mission creep: monitoring only during contracted hours, only for stated performance needs, data minimization, short retention, no secondary use or sale, human review before any adverse action, and independent audit rights. These bounds stop a race toward who can spy most ruthlessly.
[P1.5] Unrestricted monitoring breeds distrust, anxiety, and performative busyness rather than genuine results. Consent-plus-limits still permits useful tools for those who choose them, while protecting dignity, mental health, and the boundary between work and life. That is the only sustainable foundation for remote work.

[C1.1] The motion sounds moderate, but read the fine print: monitoring is permitted "only" if every worker individually opts in, with a guaranteed non-monitored equivalent role and the right to withdraw at any moment. That is not regulation. It is a de facto ban wearing a compromise's clothing—because the employees most likely to opt out are precisely the ones with something to hide, and any monitoring regime that the monitored can veto is theater.
[C1.2] Start with what is actually being watched. This is not a camera in your bedroom; it is activity on a company-issued laptop, on company time, touching company data. A bank must be able to log keystrokes on terminals handling client accounts—regulators effectively demand it. A hospital system must detect anomalous access to patient records.
[C1.3] An insider quietly exfiltrating source code will simply "withdraw consent without retaliation" the week before he leaks. Making security controls optional per-employee guts them for everyone.
[C1.4] The right framework already exists and is better: transparent notice, proportionality, and purpose limits enforced by law. Tell workers clearly what is monitored and why; ban covert capture of private life; punish misuse. That preserves accountability without handing each individual a veto over the employer's ability to protect its systems, its customers, and its honest employees—who currently subsidize the shirkers and the fraudsters.
[C1.5] And consider the predictable consequence of the motion's rule. If remote monitoring becomes practically impossible, employers won't shrug; they'll recall people to offices, where a supervisor's eyes need no opt-in, or shift work to contractors abroad. The biggest losers are caregivers, disabled workers, and rural employees for whom remote work is a lifeline. A rule sold as protecting remote workers will shrink remote work itself.
[C1.6] Monitoring can also protect workers: objective activity records beat a biased manager's gut, and time logs document unpaid overtime. The choice is not surveillance versus dignity. It is workable transparency versus an unworkable veto.
