[P1.1] AI-enabled romance scams aren't just faster or cheaper fraud—they're a structurally different threat that demands a structurally different response. Treating them as ordinary online fraud collapses three critical distinctions that determine whether victims actually get protected.
[P1.2] First, scale and persistence. A human romance scammer can maintain maybe a handful of simultaneous relationships. An AI agent can sustain hundreds at once, each individually tailored, emotionally responsive, and indefinitely patient. It never tires, never accidentally breaks character, and never has to choose between targets.
[P1.3] The marginal cost of the hundredth victim approaches zero. Ordinary fraud enforcement was built around the constraint that each scam requires human effort and attention. That constraint is gone.
[P1.4] Second, the emotional architecture. Traditional online fraud—phishing, fake auctions, investment schemes—targets greed or fear. Romance scams target trust, loneliness, and attachment. When AI personalizes its manipulation by learning from weeks or months of private conversation, the resulting bond isn't just stronger; it's resistant to intervention in a way ordinary fraud isn't.
[P1.5] Victims often refuse to believe they've been scammed even after being told. Standard fraud-response tools—transaction alerts, complaint hotlines, account freezes—assume the victim wants to stop the loss. Here, the victim often doesn't.
[P1.6] Third, the regulatory lever. Ordinary fraud gets handled through criminal investigation: reactive, slow, case-by-case. A consumer-protection framework enables proactive interventions—platform mandates for AI-use disclosure, identity verification requirements, cooling-off periods before large transfers to new contacts, educational campaigns targeted at the specific psychology of romantic attachment. These tools don't exist in the ordinary-fraud toolkit.
[P1.7] The proposition isn't that romance scams are worse than other fraud. It's that the mechanism, the psychology, and the intervention points are different enough that forcing them through a framework designed for impersonal, one-shot scams means systematically failing the victims who need protection most. Distinct problems require distinct institutions.
