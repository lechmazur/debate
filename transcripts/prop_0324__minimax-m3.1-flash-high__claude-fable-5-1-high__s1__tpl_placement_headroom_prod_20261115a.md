[P1.1] Place identity is not the image a city buys for a weekend; it is the reputation people reproduce together over time. That makes recurring, creator-led local festivals the better instrument for building it. A food festival organized by neighborhood cooks, a street-art market shaped by local artists, or an annual music gathering developed by community collectives does more than attract visitors. It rehearses a distinctive local story, gives residents roles in producing it, and turns participation into memory.
[P1.2] The festival becomes evidence that “this is the kind of place we are.”
[P1.3] Mega-events can create attention, but attention is not the same as attachment. A concert, expo, or sports showcase may generate headlines and photographs, yet often leaves the host city with a generic global brand: the venue hosted an event, rather than the event revealing who the city is. Because the event is one-off, residents have no next edition to anticipate or contribute to. Its meaning dissipates once the scaffolding comes down.
[P1.4] Recurring events also distribute benefits more broadly. Local creators can retain more of the value through vendor fees, commissions, collaborations, and audience spending, while repeated programming builds customer relationships and reputational capacity among artisans and organizers. A mega-event may inject more money at once, but it can concentrate contracts, rents, and publicity among a few intermediaries. Local festivals may be smaller, not weaker: they embed money in networks that already belong to the place.
[P1.5] The decisive advantage is cumulative ownership. Prestige projects invite a city to consume spectacle; creator-led festivals help residents produce culture. The former gives people a story to watch. The latter gives them a tradition to inhabit.
[P1.6] When public institutions seek durable place identity, they should invest in the latter not despite their modest scale, but because recurrence, participation, and local authorship make identity self-reinforcing.

[C1.1] Place identity has two halves, and the motion only funds one. A place is who residents think they are, yes, but it is equally who everyone else recognises them to be. Identity is relational. Nobody in Lyon or Lagos knows about your neighborhood cooks' food festival, and that is the problem: an identity nobody outside can name is a private memory, not a place brand.
[C1.2] Mega-events are the rare instrument that forces the world to learn a city's name and attach a story to it, and that external recognition is what feeds the local pride PRO wants.
[C1.3] Second, the "one-off" framing is a sleight of hand. The event lasts a fortnight; its residue lasts decades. Barcelona's 1992 Games opened a walled-off industrial waterfront into beaches the whole city now treats as its living room. Glasgow's 1990 cultural capital year is widely credited with flipping its self-image from post-industrial decline to cultural confidence.
[C1.4] Even Edinburgh's festivals, PRO's ideal recurring model, grew from a deliberate post-war showcase, not from grassroots accretion. The mega-event is frequently the parent of the recurring tradition, not its rival.
[C1.5] Third, consider what "prefer" actually does to a city of many neighborhoods. Dozens of creator-led events produce dozens of micro-identities, each owned by whichever collective captured the grant. They rehearse "this is the kind of street we are" [P1.2], not the kind of city. A shared identity needs a focal moment every resident, from every district, can point to and say "we did that together."
[C1.6] That is precisely what a single, citywide, deadline-driven project supplies and what fragmented programming structurally cannot.
[C1.7] Finally, public institutions are the only actor capable of the big bet. Local creators already run markets and gigs without a ministry. The marginal value of public money is highest exactly where no one else can act: coordination, infrastructure, and the moment that makes a place legible to itself and the world.

[P2.1] CON mistakes recognizability for identity, and assumes every successful recurring festival began with a state mega-project. A city can become known because residents repeatedly stage distinctive events: repetition creates an association between the place and what its people produce. A connected network of recurring markets, performances, and volunteer projects can give the wider city a coherent story. Outsiders then learn the city’s name through distinctive local culture, not one expensive spectacle.
[P2.2] Barcelona and Glasgow show that one-off projects can leave a legacy, but not that they are the best means of building place identity. Their durable results emerged through rebuilt public spaces, institutions, and later cultural activity. Those are legacies catalyzed by an event; the proposition asks what institutions should prefer when identity is the goal. Moreover, Edinburgh’s recurring culture does not establish that every durable festival required one prestige predecessor; many traditions were built through ordinary participation.
[P2.3] CON’s micro-identities dilemma is false. Recurring community events can be citywide and coordinated without being centrally produced. Public institutions can fund shared promotion, accessible venues, and grant systems while local creators retain authorship. This represents districts rather than erasing them behind one generic logo.
[P2.4] Indeed, a centrally controlled citywide festival can flatten local identity, whereas recurring events let diverse communities appear together over time.
[P2.5] Mega-events also risk substitutability. Any city can bid for a major expo; recognition may follow, but it need not attach to a distinctive local story. Prestige can produce temporary consumption and imported programming. Creator-led events make resident culture itself the offering, so outside attention reflects local authorship rather than bidding capacity.
[P2.6] The comparison is not local versus global, or temporary versus lasting. It is between investing in spectacles that disappear and building durable capacity for residents to repeatedly author the place.

[P4.1] A1: Edinburgh is the strongest answer, though not a laboratory-pure one. Its recurring arts ecosystem—not one prestige showcase—is what makes the city globally legible as a festival city. [P4.3] A2: No single authority writes the story. Neighborhood creators propose the food, music, art, and rituals; local organizations decide how to produce them; public institutions provide grants, accessible spaces, sanitation, promotion, and coordination. [P4.4] That distinction matters because CON makes the mechanism of recognition the test, when the proposition asks about building place identity. A festival can become famous through recurrence, just as a market can become a civic tradition. The relevant question is whether residents participate in producing a recognizable culture, not whether one spectacular occasion concentrates attention.
[P4.5] Public institutions also retain their comparative advantage under this model. They can create the infrastructure that recurrent programming needs while keeping creative authority decentralized. A one-off mega-event instead concentrates opportunity: contracts, revenue, and narrative control largely arrive at one deadline, then disappear. Recurring events provide a steadier return on institutional attention.
[P4.6] The best policy is therefore not to replace all spectacle. It is to prefer the mechanism that leaves behind greater local ownership. CON’s examples establish that prestige projects can catalyze change; they do not show that temporary centralized spectacle is generally superior to the cumulative authorship through which residents come to recognize one another.

Institutions can amplify locally selected work, provide access, and connect events without deciding what the city must mean. Imposing a single narrative is the mega-event’s central risk: the host becomes branded by the spectacle rather than recognized through its culture.
[P5.3] The decisive standard is durable, locally owned capacity. A mega-event may leave infrastructure or confidence, but recurrence builds those capacities directly. CON’s ceiling-and-floor argument reverses the policy question: choosing a tool for its rare upside neglects its ordinary effects, while recurring events make sustained participation their normal case.
[P5.4] Public institutions should prefer recurring creator-led festivals because they turn place identity from imported performance into ongoing practice residents reproduce. They make residents authors, not spectators of a story staged periodically for them.
