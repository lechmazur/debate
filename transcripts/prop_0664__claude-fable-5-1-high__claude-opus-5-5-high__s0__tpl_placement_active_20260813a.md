[P1.1] War over Taiwan begins, if it begins, in a briefing room in Beijing where a leader asks one question: can we take the island before anyone can stop us? Deterrence succeeds or fails on the answer to that question. Everything else is secondary.
[P1.2] Economic deterrence alone never touches that answer. Sanctions threats operate on the ledger after the operation, not on whether the operation works. And Beijing has already discounted them. It watched Russia absorb the harshest sanctions regime ever imposed and keep fighting; it has spent years building payment alternatives, stockpiling commodities, and reducing dollar exposure precisely to survive a Taiwan cutoff.
[P1.3] A leader who has decided Taiwan is existential will not be talked out of it by a spreadsheet. Worse, the threat lacks credibility, because the sanctioners bleed too: Japan, Korea, Europe, and the United States all depend on trade with China, and Beijing knows those coalitions fracture under pressure. A deterrent your adversary believes you will not fully execute is barely a deterrent.
[P1.4] Forward-deployed anti-ship missiles and air defenses change the briefing itself. An invasion requires hundreds of ships crossing roughly a hundred miles of open water and troop-laden aircraft flying into contested skies. Missiles based in Japan, the Philippines, and Taiwan, plus layered air defense, make that crossing a shooting gallery. The general no longer says "ten days, then sanctions."
[P1.5] He says "we cannot get the fleet across." That is the sentence that prevents war.
[P1.6] Timing matters too. An invasion decision is made in days; sanctions bite over months and years. A deterrent must act on the timeline of the decision, and only military denial does.
[P1.7] Finally, these are the least escalatory tools available. Anti-ship and air-defense systems cannot seize territory or strike Chinese cities. They raise the price of aggression without threatening China's homeland, which is exactly why they stabilize rather than provoke.
[P1.8] Economic pressure can supplement deterrence. It cannot substitute for the one thing that actually deters: convincing Beijing the invasion fails.
