[P1.1] Irregular migration is a market problem before it is a border problem. Farms, packing plants, hotels and care homes need workers every season. When no legal door exists, that demand does not disappear; it gets filled through smugglers and informal hiring. Enforcement fights the symptom.

[P1.3] History shows this. When the United States ended the Bracero program in the mid-1960s, Mexican labor demand stayed, so seasonal workers kept coming, now without papers. Irregular crossings climbed for decades afterward. More recently, Germany opened a work route for Western Balkan nationals, and weak asylum claims from that region fell sharply as people chose the job visa instead.
[P1.4] When the US created parole channels for several nationalities in 2023, border encounters from those countries dropped noticeably within months. In each case, the legal door did the deterrent work that fences had not.
[P1.5] Enforcement, by contrast, has a perverse effect. Tightening the US-Mexico border from the 1990s onward did not end irregular migration; it raised smuggling fees, pushed crossings into deadly deserts, and handed cartels a business. [P1.6] A tougher border turned seasonal migrants into permanent undocumented residents, enlarging the very population enforcement aimed to shrink.
[P1.7] The test for this motion is simple: which policy reduces irregular migration more durably per dollar? Enforcement must win every crossing, every year, against rising smuggler adaptation. A seasonal visa wins once, by offering a lawful option that dominates the irregular one. A worker holding a visa, a contract and a guaranteed return next spring has no reason to pay a smuggler.
[P1.8] That logic wins this debate.
