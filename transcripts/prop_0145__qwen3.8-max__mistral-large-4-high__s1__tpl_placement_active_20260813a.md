[P1.1] The default rule in a household should be trust, not surveillance. The proposition does not say parents may never track or check messages. It says always-on location tracking and routine reading of private messages should not be the starting point for every teenager when no specific safety concern exists.
[P1.2] First, the intrusion is disproportionate. Location tracking can reveal a teen’s movements minute by minute; private-message monitoring exposes conversations with friends, mentors, romantic partners, and perhaps a counselor about anxiety, identity, shame, or mistakes. Adolescence is precisely the stage where young people need room to develop judgment, separate from parents, and learn to manage risk. A default that treats every ordinary teen as a suspect converts normal independence into evidence to be collected.
[P1.3] Second, this default undermines the very safety it claims to serve. Teenagers disclose danger when they trust that adults will respond fairly. If a teen believes every message is read and every movement logged, they are less likely to ask for help after being bullied, pressured, or harmed, because they fear punishment, embarrassment, or losing what little autonomy they have. Surveillance can also push risky behavior underground: secret accounts, hidden apps, or disabling location service.
[P1.4] A parent who knows less but has open communication often learns more than a parent who knows everything on a dashboard but nothing the teen is willing to say.
[P1.5] Third, targeted concern is enough. If there are warning signs—self-harm, grooming, violence, running away, a dangerous route home—parents can use specific, time-limited monitoring tied to that risk. That is prudent parenting. Blanket surveillance is not; it substitutes control for conversation and makes teens less likely to cooperate.
[P1.6] Vote PRO because it protects development, preserves trust, and reserves intrusive tools for real emergencies instead of turning them into household wallpaper.
