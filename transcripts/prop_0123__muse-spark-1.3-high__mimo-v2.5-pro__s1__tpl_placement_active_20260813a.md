[P1.1] The supermarket aisle is not a classroom. A tired parent with two minutes to choose cereal is not weighing pamphlets about balanced diets; she faces bright boxes shouting whole grain and natural while packed with sugar, salt and additives engineered to keep kids wanting more. To rely mainly on choice campaigns there is to pretend information is equal when power is not.
[P1.2] Prominent warnings fix the failure where decisions actually happen. A clear front-of-pack signal — high in sugar, high in sodium, ultra-processed — cuts through marketing noise in seconds, with no class, no app, no spare attention required. Places that adopted bold front warnings have seen shoppers shift away from the worst drinks and snacks, and manufacturers quietly reformulate to avoid the mark. No poster campaign has ever forced a soda company to cut sugar.
[P1.3] This is about fairness. Choice campaigns consistently help the already advantaged — people with time, literacy and flexible schedules — while sliding past everyone else. Diet-related illness does the opposite, concentrating in low-income neighborhoods flooded with cheap ultra-processed foods and soda ads. A label travels with the product into every store and every kitchen.
[P1.4] Children especially cannot weigh long-term risk, but they can learn what a warning means.
[P1.5] Labels also protect freedom rather than remove it. Nothing is banned; adults can still buy what they want, only now the choice is honest. Industry spends fortunes to shape cravings and blur health judgments; a government warning tells the truth with equal prominence. Information asymmetry is a classic market failure, and correcting it is government's most basic economic job.
[P1.6] When diabetes, heart disease and obesity crowd hospitals and drain public budgets, urging willpower is abdication. Education asks people to swim harder; labels slow the current. Require the warning first, then keep educating.
