[P1.1] Overtourism has created a two-tier system for fragile heritage sites: tourists snap up peak-season tickets months in advance, while local residents—who bear the full cost of overtourism, from congested streets to site degradation—are locked out of the very cultural landmarks that define their city’s identity. In Venice, long-time residents report an inability to visit St. Mark’s Basilica during peak summer, as cruise ship tour groups fill every slot.
[P1.2] The proposed policy corrects three interlinked failures. First, it addresses a core distributive injustice: heritage sites are not mere commercial products, but the shared cultural property of the communities that have stewarded them for generations, who will bear the cost of their preservation long-term. Priority booking with proof of local address, or modestly discounted resident tickets funded by a small peak-season tourist surcharge, is a minimal accommodation for that stake.
[P1.3] Second, it tackles an urgent physical preservation crisis: fragile sites like the Acropolis or medieval old towns have hard carrying capacities, and peak-season overcrowding accelerates erosion and damage to irreplaceable artifacts that no tourist revenue can repair. Reducing peak-season tourist volume by even 10-15% via price signals and priority allocation would dramatically slow this degradation.
[P1.4] Third, it supports long-term sustainable tourism: when locals are locked out of their own heritage, they grow hostile to the tourism industry that provides many of their livelihoods, eroding the social license tourism needs to operate.
[P1.5] This policy does not eliminate tourist access. It rebalances a grossly skewed allocation, nudging marginal tourists to visit off-peak, reducing overcrowding, and ensuring the people with the deepest stake in these sites can access them. The alternative is slow, irreversible erosion of both the physical sites and the social fabric that keeps them standing.

[C1.1] The proposition asks cities to formalize a two-tier system of access to shared cultural patrimony, dividing the public into insiders and outsiders by ZIP code. That is not a correction of injustice; it is the creation of a new one.
[C1.2] Three reasons to reject it.
[C1.3] First, fragile heritage is rarely the property of the municipality that surrounds it. The Acropolis, the Vatican Museums, the Alhambra are national and humanity-wide trusts, often protected through international cooperation. A resident of Florence has no stronger moral claim to the Uffizi than a schoolteacher from Seoul. Encoding residency as the metric of access replaces a universalist conception of culture with parochialism, and in jurisdictions like the EU it would collide with the principle of non-discrimination on grounds of nationality.
[C1.4] The affirmative frames residents as "stewards," but stewardship is the language of a museum board, not a claim of ownership over the collection.
[C1.5] Second, the policy does not solve overtourism; it relocates the exclusion. A tourist displaced from a July slot at the Duomo does not vanish - they crowd the Uffizi, shift to shoulder months, or were never flexible to begin with because their only holiday is July. Resident priority is a rationing scheme disguised as a preservation tool, leaving total peak-season demand largely intact while punishing visitors who had nothing to do with the crowding.
[C1.6] Third, the affirmative underweights the revenue loss. Peak-season tourist tickets subsidize the conservation, guides, and site upkeep that keep fragile sites standing. Discounting them for residents, or holding inventory off-market, drains the very budget that protects the stone the policy claims to save.
[C1.7] The genuine toolkit is well known and non-discriminatory: timed entry, dynamic pricing, capacity caps, dispersal incentives, satellite sites, virtual access. We can fight overtourism without carving "locals only" windows into shared heritage. The affirmative has shown that something must be done about overtourism - and that is true - but they have not shown why a residency-based two-tier system is the right instrument. Something must be done is not enough.
