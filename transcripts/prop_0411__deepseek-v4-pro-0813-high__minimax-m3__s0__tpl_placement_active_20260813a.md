[P1.1] A wheelchair user finishes work at 5:30. A non-disabled coworker catches a bus at 5:40 and is home in thirty minutes. The wheelchair user had to reserve by the previous afternoon, gets a two-hour pickup window, waits outside, and sometimes waits hours after a no-show. That is not separate but equal transportation; it is exclusion enforced through delay.
[P1.2] Public transit and ride-hail are not optional luxuries. They are how people reach jobs, medical appointments, schools, pharmacies, polling places, and family. A system that offers wheelchair access only on long advance notice or with far longer waits does not provide meaningful access at all. It tells disabled riders their time is worth less and shifts the burden onto the people least able to absorb it.
[P1.3] Wait time and reliability are core features of transportation, not minor conveniences.
[P1.4] Ride-hail makes the gap starker. A standard rider taps a phone and often gets a car in minutes; a wheelchair user may face indefinite waits, unavailable accessible vehicles, or drivers who pass them by. On-demand transit has raised expectations for everyone else. Requiring comparable wait times simply applies the same promise to people who use mobility devices.
[P1.5] The cost objection is predictable but fails. Operators already bear safety and licensing costs; access can be phased in through purchase rules, centralized accessible dispatch, and subsidies or cross-subsidies. Expense cannot justify relegating a group to second-class service. If a transit agency or ride-hail company cannot serve disabled riders with comparable convenience, it should not enjoy public streets, contracts, and customers while excluding them.
[P1.6] Public mobility should not have two clocks: one for non-disabled people and one slower, less reliable clock for wheelchair users. A job interview missed because a paratransit van never came is not a scheduling problem; it is discrimination. Parity in wait times is the minimum meaning of equal access.

That is the lived history of ADA paratransit enforcement: the phrase expanded, never contracted.
[C2.2] P2.2 makes measurement sound simple, but the metric is volatile. Standard ride-hail wait times in a single neighborhood can swing from under five minutes to twenty-plus in the same hour as traffic, driver shifts, and demand spikes change. Apply "close to" to that signal and the regulator is forced to choose a band: five minutes, ten, fifteen. Each choice carries different fleet and subsidy implications.
[C2.3] The motion is silent on which, and the silence is why operators cannot build around it.
[C2.4] P2.3 and P2.4 actually strengthen my case.
