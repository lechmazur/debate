[C1.1] The Antarctic Treaty worked because Antarctica had almost nothing to give up: no permanent population, no settled sovereignty, and territorial claims that most of the world never recognized in the first place. Freezing those claims cost the claimant states almost nothing. The Arctic is not that. It is home to millions of people, including indigenous nations whose self-governance rights are already recognized in law, sitting atop territory that has been continuously and legitimately administered by sovereign states for generations.
[C1.2] Asking Norway, Canada, Russia, and the United States to "freeze" sovereignty over inhabited homelands is not a design tweak—it is asking them to surrender something Antarctica's claimants never really had to begin with. That is why no such treaty has ever gotten traction, and why none will.
[C1.3] More importantly, the Arctic is not a legal vacuum waiting to be filled. The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea already gives every Arctic coastal state a binding, functioning framework: territorial seas, exclusive economic zones, and a formal process through the Continental Shelf Commission for adjudicating extended claims. Russia, Canada, and Denmark are currently working through exactly that process over the Lomonosov Ridge—slowly, but through law, not war. In 2008 the five Arctic Ocean states explicitly affirmed, in the Ilulissat Declaration, that they saw no need for a new comprehensive legal regime because the existing law of the sea already covers the region.
[C1.4] That is the Arctic states themselves rejecting the Antarctic model.
[C1.5] Layering a new binding treaty on top of this risks the opposite of stability. Reopening sovereignty questions invites outside powers like China to push "common heritage of mankind" framing to dilute Arctic states' authority, while a multi-year unanimous ratification process hands Russia years of breathing room to keep building bases unilaterally. The existing structure isn't failing; it's the safer path.
