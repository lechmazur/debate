[P1.1] Parks, beaches, and waterfronts are the last places in a city where your money doesn't decide whether you belong. That's not sentimentality—it's the core social function these spaces perform, and it's precisely what enclosure erodes.
[P1.2] Start with what these places actually do. A free beach is where a family on a tight budget takes the same vacation as a wealthy one. A public park is where teenagers who can't afford a gym, elderly people who can't afford a café, and children who can't afford a class all share the same ground. The moment you gate any significant portion behind a ticket, a membership, or a reservation fee, you haven't just priced out some users—you've changed the character of the space from shared to sorted.
[P1.3] Even partial enclosure does this, because the best shade, the best sand, the best view are exactly what gets enclosed first.
[P1.4] The economic argument for enclosure is also weaker than it looks. Paid experiences cherry-pick: a private beach club generates revenue precisely because it captures the most desirable stretch of a resource that everyone's taxes, and often everyone's shoreline-access law, helped create and maintain. Public spaces, by contrast, generate diffuse but enormous value—higher surrounding property values, tourist spending, public health savings, and the trust that comes from strangers mixing. That value doesn't show up on a vendor's balance sheet, but it shows up in the city.
[P1.5] And there's the ratchet problem. Enclosure never stays contained. One paid zone justifies the next; maintenance budgets quietly shift toward the revenue-generating sections; the free remainder becomes the neglected remainder, and then neglect becomes the argument for more enclosure. Public agencies don't discipline this drift—they succumb to it.
[P1.6] The proposition says these spaces should remain "broadly" free—not that no concession stand may ever exist. It draws the line at the direction of travel: toward the shore, the lawn, and the waterfront becoming products rather than places. That line is worth holding, because once access is sold, it is almost never bought back.
