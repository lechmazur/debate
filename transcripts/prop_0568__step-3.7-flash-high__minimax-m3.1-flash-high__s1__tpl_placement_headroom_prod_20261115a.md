[P1.1] A government requirement that major retailers accept cash for essential goods is a straightforward, low-cost fix for a growing equity crisis that the private market will not address on its own. At its core, the policy protects the roughly 1 in 20 U.S. households that are fully unbanked, plus millions more low-income people, seniors, and residents with unstable housing who lack reliable access to digital payment tools. For these groups, cashless policies at grocery stores, pharmacies, and other outlets selling essentials like food, medicine, and baby supplies are not a minor inconvenience—they are a barrier to meeting basic needs.
[P1.2] While some argue this infringes on private business autonomy, that framing ignores the unique status of essential goods: no one chooses to need insulin or infant formula, and access to these items is a matter of public health, not discretionary consumer choice. Legal tender rules already reflect the public interest in cash as a universal, low-barrier payment method, and extending that requirement to essential goods at major retailers is a narrow, targeted intervention. It does not ban cashless options for non-essential goods, so retailers retain flexibility for product lines where digital payments work for all customers.
[P1.3] The market has already proven it will not self-correct this gap. During the COVID-19 pandemic, dozens of major grocery and pharmacy chains rolled out cashless pilots, and food banks reported sharp increases in requests from people who could not purchase basics with cards. Even CVS, which tested cashless stores in urban areas, reversed course in low-income neighborhoods after widespread pushback, confirming that profit motives alone will not prioritize access for marginalized groups. For major retailers, the operational cost of counting cash is negligible compared to the social cost of blocking millions of people from life-sustaining goods.

[C1.1] The proposition jumps from a real distributional problem to a broad legal command without showing that the command is the least harmful way to solve it. Cashless checkout can exclude households, but the relevant question is not whether some customers prefer cash; it is whether forcing every major seller to accept it improves access net of costs imposed on shops and customers who pay there.
[C1.2] Cash acceptance is not costless. It requires cash deposits, counting, reconciliation, security, and handling counterfeit risk. Those costs burden smaller stores and rural outlets, even if a chain is exempt. Retailers respond to mandates by raising prices, reducing staffing, or withdrawing from underserved areas; neither outcome helps a person seeking groceries or medicine.
[C1.3] A policy justified as an equity measure should not make essential goods more expensive or less available in the very communities it claims to protect.
[C1.4] Nor does accepting cash guarantee access. An unbanked household may have no cash, no nearby branch, no smartphone, or no trusted way to obtain either cash or a payment account. A blanket cash mandate leaves that person just as excluded while directing public resources toward a payment channel rather than the actual bottleneck: usable, affordable banking and cash access. Existing card, mobile-payment, and benefit systems can be improved for people who lack them; requiring cash does not replace that work.
[C1.5] The alternative is targeted regulation: protect cash access where communities demonstrably need it, fund low-cost payment accounts and cash-access services, and require reasonable payment options for essential purchases rather than one universal instrument. This addresses the alleged harm without imposing cash handling on retailers or customers who do not need it. As written, the motion is a blunt subsidy to cash-handling costs and a potential barrier to efficient delivery of essential goods. It should be rejected.

CON’s claim that mandates harm the communities they aim to protect is unsubstantiated: during the COVID-19 cashless pilot rollback, CVS explicitly reversed its cashless policy in low-income neighborhoods after finding the cost of excluding cash customers far outweighed minor operational savings from eliminating cash handling.
[P2.2] There is no real-world evidence that universal cash acceptance for essentials leads to the price hikes or store closures CON predicts. Second, CON argues cash mandates fail people who lack both cash and digital payment access, but this is a straw man. The policy does not require anyone to pay with cash—it only ensures that customers who do hold cash, including the majority of unbanked households who rely on cash for daily transactions, can use it to buy life-sustaining essentials.
[P2.3] CON’s preferred alternative of "targeted regulation" is far more harmful: it relies on slow, bureaucratic assessments of community need, creating gaps where marginalized people remain locked out of essentials while agencies sort out eligibility. Unlike a clear, universal requirement for major retailers, targeted rules leave people stranded in the gap between policy design and implementation, and do nothing to fix the immediate, widespread barrier cashless policies impose today. The market has already proven it will not self-correct this gap: without government requirements, cashless pilots will continue expanding, excluding the most vulnerable from basic necessities.
