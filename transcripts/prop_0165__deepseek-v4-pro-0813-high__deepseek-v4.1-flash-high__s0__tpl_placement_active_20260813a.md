[P1.1] Public EV charging is basic transportation infrastructure, not a private digital service. A driver should no more need a platform-specific membership to charge than to use a public road. Yet that is the lived result of proprietary dominance: the same street can host three chargers requiring three different apps, payment balances, and data permissions. Governments have treated fuel nozzles, rail gauges, telecom roaming, and ATM networks as compatibility problems precisely because access cannot depend on which private owner happened to build first.
[P1.2] Proprietary networks face a strong structural incentive to lock users in. Each network hopes its walled garden becomes the default, so it bundles exclusive authentication, billing, reservations, and service. No individual operator wants to hand competitors a seamless path to its chargers. That is a coordination failure, not a problem voluntary goodwill can solve.
[P1.3] A government mandate is the only actor that can set the table for all entrants.
[P1.4] Open standards do not nationalize charging or dictate business models. They split the physical pipe from the service layer. Operators can still compete fiercely on uptime, price, siting, maintenance, and convenience; they just cannot compete by trapping a driver inside their app. That lowers the biggest practical barrier to EV adoption: charger anxiety.
[P1.5] A driver who knows any public charger will accept common payment and roaming does not have to gamble on whether the next station works with their membership.
[P1.6] The public money point makes this unanswerable. Governments are underwriting charger build-out. Taxpayer subsidies should not create private walled gardens that later extract monopoly rent. Open standards also let municipal utilities, rural co-ops, and small operators join the network, which is exactly where proprietary incumbents underinvest.
[P1.7] Mandate interoperability now, while the installed base is still young. The cost of retrofitting a fragmented proprietary system later will be far higher, and the delay will fall on drivers and climate goals.

[C1.1] The real question isn't whether drivers should be able to charge anywhere. It's whether a blanket government mandate for open interoperability standards is the right instrument. It isn't.
[C1.2] Public charging is not a dumb pipe like a road. It is a live market in energy, payments, reservations, grid balancing, cybersecurity, and battery diagnostics. A legally mandated interoperability standard freezes one layer of that stack while the rest races ahead. Today's "open" protocol becomes tomorrow's compliance relic, and regulators, not drivers, decide which innovations can ship.
[C1.3] Proprietary systems are not automatically walled gardens. Networks already bridge through roaming agreements, payment aggregators, QR and card readers, and vehicle-based authentication. Where drivers truly demand one account, the market has an incentive to supply it. Where they don't, they value a network's reliability or price enough to accept its app.
[C1.4] That is competition working, not a coordination failure requiring a command.
[C1.5] The public-money argument actually cuts against a universal mandate. If taxpayers subsidize a charger, attach conditions: accept cards, publish prices, allow roaming. That targets the subsidy and leaves privately financed networks free to differentiate. It is a scalpel, not a sledgehammer.
[C1.6] A blanket standard instead forces every operator—including small rural sites and new entrants—to spend on certification, back-end integration, and redesign before they have customers. Compliance costs favor large incumbents and consultants.
[C1.7] There is also a fatal timing problem. Charging technology is young and changing: higher power, bidirectional charging, plug-and-charge, dynamic grid pricing. Government standard-setting is slow, political, and vulnerable to capture. A premature mandate locks in the wrong design; a delayed one creates stranded investment.
[C1.8] The better path is outcome regulation where needed—payment choice, price transparency, reliability—plus open standards where public money buys them.
[C1.9] Open interoperability may be a good option. A legal requirement that it dominate is not. Let standards compete, and let drivers, not procurement rules, pick the winners.
