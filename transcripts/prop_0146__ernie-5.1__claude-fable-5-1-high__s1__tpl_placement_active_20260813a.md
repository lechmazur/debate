[P1.1] Adolescence is the moment a person first fully grasps how the world perceives them—and for millions of children, that world already includes thousands of strangers who've watched them cry at the dentist, seen their morning meltdown, or followed their school routine, all because a parent hit record. The law must catch up to this reality.
[P1.2] My case turns on three pillars. First, autonomy. By early adolescence, children develop the cognitive capacity for abstract, future-oriented thinking. A fourteen-year-old can understand that an embarrassing clip will follow them into college applications and job searches.
[P1.3] Denying them legal recourse at precisely this developmental milestone is arbitrary and paternalistic.
[P1.4] Second, harm. Family-vlog content routinely exposes names, schools, daily schedules, and vulnerable moments. This isn't abstract—it creates real safety risks. Stalkers, bullies, and predators can piece together a child's routine from footage.
[P1.5] Even without malice, the reputational damage of a viral embarrassing moment can derail an adolescent's social life and mental health. The digital footprint is permanent; the harm compounds over years.
[P1.6] Third, power. Parents profit from family vlogs—advertising revenue, sponsorships, brand deals. The child is the product, not a participant. Current law treats this as a parental speech issue, but that framing ignores the child's stake in their own image and data.
[P1.7] COPPA protects against data collection from young children but says nothing about a teen's right to scrub content they never chose to create.
[P1.8] Strengthening the legal right to request removal isn't censorship. It's recognition that adolescents deserve a say in how their own lives are broadcast. The proposition asks for a stronger right, not an absolute one—courts can weigh parental expression against a young person's growing dignity. That balance is overdue, and adolescence is exactly when it should begin.
