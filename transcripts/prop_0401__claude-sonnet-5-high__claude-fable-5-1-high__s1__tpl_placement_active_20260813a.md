[P1.1] Golden-visa programs sell something that should never be for sale: legal belonging in a democracy. Strip away the marketing and the mechanism is simple — pay several hundred thousand euros into real estate or government bonds, wait out a residency clock, and receive a passport or a visa-free key to the Schengen area. That is a price tag on citizenship, and prices attract exactly the people states should be most cautious about.

Cyprus's scheme, before it was scrapped, was found to have handed passports to individuals later hit with fraud allegations and sanctions concerns. Malta and Portugal faced EU infringement pressure precisely because "citizenship for cash" undermines the mutual trust that free movement inside the EU depends on — one country's lax vetting becomes every other member state's border problem.
[P1.3] Due diligence on these applications is done by the same governments financially incentivized to approve them; that is not a control, it's a conflict of interest.
[P1.4] Second, the economic case for keeping them is weaker than it looks. [C1.1] Notice what the motion asks: end, not fix. Every problem the other side raised is a problem of design, and design is what states control. The choice actually on the table is between a regulated, transparent channel for wealthy migrants and an unregulated one, because the demand does not disappear when the sign comes down.
[C1.2] First, the conflation. Golden visas mostly sell residency, not citizenship. A Greek or Portuguese investor gets the right to live there and travel in Schengen; a passport still requires years of residence, language tests, and the ordinary naturalization process. Nearly every developed democracy already offers investor or entrepreneur routes, and skilled-worker visas already select by income.
[C1.3] Wealth-conditioned entry is not an aberration of golden visas; it is how modern immigration works. Singling out one label changes nothing about that reality.
[C1.4] Second, abolition exports the problem. Cyprus scrapped its scheme; the oligarch money did not evaporate, it moved to jurisdictions with weaker vetting, and those passport-holders still arrive at Europe's borders visa-free. A reformed European program with independent, EU-level screening, sanctions checks, and public registries does more for security than pushing the market to Caribbean islands and shell companies. The answer to a conflict of interest is to separate the vetting body from the treasury, not to abandon the vetting entirely.
[C1.5] Third, housing. Lisbon's rents are driven by short-term tourist rentals, chronic under-building, and a population influx of ordinary Europeans. A few thousand visas a year did not do that. Portugal already showed the sensible fix: steer qualifying investment away from housing and into funds, research, and job-creating businesses.
[C1.6] Reform captured the benefit while removing the distortion; abolition would have removed both.
[C1.7] Finally, the money is not trivial for small states. For Malta, Greece after its crisis, and Portugal under austerity, this capital funded hospitals and infrastructure without new taxes on residents. Ending the programs means telling those citizens to pay instead.
[C1.8] Regulate the channel. Do not pretend closing it makes the flow stop.
