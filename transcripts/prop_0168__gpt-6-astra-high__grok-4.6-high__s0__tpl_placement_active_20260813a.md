[P1.1] Exploration budgets should buy the greatest scientific understanding, not the greatest number of people transported. Agencies should shift most spending from crewed exploration to robotic probes and telescopes, while retaining focused human missions where their distinctive capabilities justify the cost. The central question is not whether astronauts can do science. It is how much science we forgo to send them.
[P1.2] Crewed missions must support an entire living environment: breathable air, water, food, temperature control, protection, and provisions for returning people safely. These are essential obligations, but they consume launch capacity, engineering effort, and money that could otherwise support scientific instruments and observations. Robots also need protection and reliable engineering, but they do not need a habitable spacecraft or a ride home unless returning samples serves the science.
[P1.3] That difference changes what exploration can attempt. Robotic spacecraft have investigated the outer planets; rovers conduct sustained fieldwork on Mars; telescopes study distant galaxies and planetary atmospheres. These are not consolation prizes for failing to send humans. They address major questions in places where crewed exploration is presently impractical.
[P1.4] A science-led budget should favor the instruments best suited to the question, rather than make human presence the organizing requirement.
[P1.5] The strongest defense of astronauts is their adaptability: they can improvise, select samples, and repair equipment. Those advantages justify selective missions, not budgetary dominance. Faster work at a destination does not establish better science per dollar once transportation, support, and return are included. Nor must every robotic mission be cheap or successful: the relevant comparison is expected scientific value across the full portfolio, with costs and risks counted consistently.
[P1.6] Human exploration can inspire, but so can images of distant worlds and discoveries about our origins. Inspiration does not establish that crewed missions deserve most exploration funding. Redirecting that majority would support more questions, more destinations, and more sustained observation. Keep astronauts where they offer exceptional value; make discovery, rather than occupancy, the default.
