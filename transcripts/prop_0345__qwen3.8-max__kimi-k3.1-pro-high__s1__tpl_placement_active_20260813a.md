[P1.1] The proposition is not anti-office; it is anti-blank-check. Large employers should be allowed to require in-person work where the job genuinely demands it, but when they override remote-capable work, they should have to explain what about the role requires presence—not merely that “culture” will improve.
[P1.2] Return-to-office mandates are not symbolic. They impose commuting time, relocation pressure, childcare disruption, and narrowed labor-market access. They also create legal and morale risks when the burden falls hardest on caregivers, disabled workers, and employees outside expensive metro areas. Because the costs are real and uneven, the decision should meet a basic test of necessity.
[P1.3] Role-specific justification is that test.
[P1.4] “Culture” is too thin a standard to discipline large employers. It is broad enough to cover anything, vague enough to evade scrutiny, and easy to use as cover for managerial preference, executive optics, or a desire to monitor rather than produce. If an employer cannot say which duties, interactions, or outputs require physical presence, then the mandate is probably about control, not business need. Forcing specificity changes the conversation: from “people work better together” to “this lab role needs equipment,” “this apprenticeship requires supervision,” or “this client-facing post needs on-site coverage.”
[P1.5] That standard remains highly deferential. It does not demand impossible proof or ban offices. It asks employers to connect the requirement to the job: confidentiality-sensitive tasks, specialized tools, hands-on training, safety-critical coordination, or recurring in-person collaboration that cannot be replicated. Where such reasons exist, the mandate survives.
[P1.6] Where they do not, the employer should not be able to impose costly one-size-fits-all rules simply because leadership misses the old office.
[P1.7] Large employers already manage job architecture, performance metrics, and real estate costs. Requiring a documented, role-specific reason is a modest administrative step that prevents arbitrary power, protects productivity, and keeps workplace rules tied to evidence rather than nostalgia. That is the decisive contrast: accountability over assertion.
