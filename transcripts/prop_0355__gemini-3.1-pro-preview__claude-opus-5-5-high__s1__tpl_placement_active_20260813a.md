[P1.1] Renters make up a massive portion of the population, yet they are systematically locked out of the electric vehicle transition by an outdated balance of power. We must strengthen renters' legal rights to install EV charging and restrict landlords' ability to arbitrarily block it.
[P1.2] First, the shift to electric vehicles is a societal imperative, but it faces a severe bottleneck: the "garage gap." When landlords have unchecked power to delay or deny EV charger installations, we create a two-tiered society where only homeowners can practically own EVs. Renters without reliable overnight charging are forced to rely on expensive, inconvenient public infrastructure or remain tethered to fossil fuels. The climate transition cannot succeed if millions of households are effectively barred from participating.
[P1.3] Second, home charging must be legally recognized as a modern utility, not a luxury amenity. Strengthening the renter's right to charge does not mean forcing landlords to foot the bill. Rather, it means that if a tenant is willing to cover the reasonable costs of installation, maintenance, and electricity, the landlord cannot unreasonably veto the project. We already restrict landlord interference regarding fundamental utilities like telecommunications or reasonable accommodations for disabilities.
[P1.4] As transportation electrifies, the ability to fuel one's vehicle at home reaches this same level of necessity. A landlord's administrative inertia or aesthetic preference should not outweigh a tenant’s basic mobility needs.
[P1.5] Finally, allowing landlords to block EV access is economically unjust. It denies renters the significant daily fuel savings associated with EVs and blocks them from fully utilizing government EV subsidies. Landlords who indefinitely delay charging infrastructure actively trap their tenants in higher-cost transportation.
[P1.6] To achieve both environmental progress and economic fairness, the legal default must flip. The burden of proof should rest on the landlord to demonstrate that an installation is structurally or electrically impossible, rather than forcing the renter to beg for access to the future of transportation.
