[P1.1] The proposition is narrow and precise: when public institutions *still benefit today* from land that was seized, some form of restitution is owed. This is not a demand that every descendant of every settler pay some abstract debt. It is a claim about ongoing unjust enrichment—a principle any legal system already recognizes.
[P1.2] When a government university sits on land taken without consent or compensation, it collects tuition, rents research contracts, and accrues endowment returns every single year. The historical wrong is not merely past; it is compounding in the present. The institution's balance sheet carries value that was extracted from a specific community without agreement and without payment. The question is not whether something bad happened in 1850.
[P1.3] The question is whether the state may continue profiting from that taking indefinitely, offering nothing in return.
[P1.4] That answer must be no, for three reasons.
[P1.5] First, the legal logic is straightforward. Unjust enrichment—receiving a benefit at another's expense without legitimate basis—demands remedy. States apply this principle between private parties constantly. The only reason it seems novel here is that the wronged party is Indigenous and the beneficiary wears a government seal.
[P1.6] Second, the motion's flexibility is a strength, not a weakness. It does not mandate a single remedy. Land return, revenue sharing, or equivalent restitution—the state has room to negotiate arrangements that work in context. New Zealand's Treaty settlements and some Canadian land-claim agreements demonstrate that structured, negotiated restitution is administratively achievable and does not collapse institutions.
[P1.7] Third, the alternative is structural permanence of dispossession. If no mechanism for remedy exists, the original seizure is effectively ratified in perpetuity. Every year the state collects revenue from that land, it re-chooses the outcome of the original theft. Silence is not neutrality—it is a continuous decision.
[P1.8] The debate turns on one question: should the state be permitted to profit forever from what it took by force? The answer that respects both law and basic fairness is no.
