[P1.1] If a billionaire team owner wants the absolute freedom to pack up and leave town whenever a richer market calls, there is a simple way to keep it: pay for your own stadium.
[P1.2] Binding community approval applies only to franchises that voluntarily took major public subsidies: taxpayer-funded venues, municipal bond debt, public land, and sweeping tax breaks. Owners pitch these deals as generational civic partnerships, promising decades of local identity and economic vitality in exchange for public capital. In any private venture, an investor putting up hundreds of millions demands veto power over moving the core asset. Taxpayers deserve no less.
[P1.3] Letting a subsidized franchise bolt for pricier luxury suites while residents keep paying off stadium debt, as taxpayers in St. Louis and Oakland were forced to do, is legalized asset stripping.
[P1.4] Without this check, ordinary contract negotiations systematically fail the public. Professional leagues manufacture artificial scarcity by capping franchise supply, then weaponize relocation threats to pit cities against one another. Local politicians negotiate on short election cycles against monopoly leagues; terrified of losing a beloved team on their watch, officials routinely accept weak leases financed by decades-long public bonds, passing the risk to future residents who never got a say. Requiring binding community approval breaks this hostage dynamic, ensuring public money always comes with real public strings.
[P1.5] Crucially, binding approval is not eternal captivity; it sets a fair exit price. An owner who genuinely needs to leave cannot simply run out a lease or cut a backroom deal with compliant officials. To win consent through a local referendum or accountable civic body, the franchise must make the community whole: retiring remaining public debt, reimbursing infrastructure costs, and offering real buyout value for local priorities.
[P1.6] When franchises socialize their capital costs, they forfeit the right to privatize their exit. Take the public’s money, answer to the public.
