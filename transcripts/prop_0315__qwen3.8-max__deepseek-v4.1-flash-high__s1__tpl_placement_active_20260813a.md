[P1.1] The core question is who owns the moment when a game is changed by an official’s review. Right now, leagues keep that moment opaque: fans, players, and coaches see the call flip, hear a terse announcement, and are left to guess about the standard applied. That vacuum is filled by suspicion, conspiracy, and abuse. Publishing fuller referee audio and decision explanations for major review calls would make the game more accountable, more consistent, and easier to trust.
[P1.2] First, legitimacy. Major reviews are not routine mistakes; they are deliberate, game-altering judgments. When a penalty nullifies a touchdown, a goal is disallowed, or a match is decided by a video review, the public deserves to know what the official saw and why the rule led to that outcome. Transparency converts a mysterious verdict into a reasoned decision.
[P1.3] If the explanation is good, confidence rises. If it is bad, leagues can correct it, train officials, and adjust rules. Opaqueness protects neither accuracy nor fairness; it only protects the league from scrutiny.
[P1.4] Second, consistency. Publishing audio creates a record that can be examined across games and seasons. Referees are more likely to apply standards evenly when their reasoning will be public, and leagues can identify officials who drift from the intended interpretation. This is not about shaming individuals; it is about building a learning system.
[P1.5] Courts publish opinions because explaining a decision disciplines it.
[P1.6] Finally, the practical objection is manageable. Leagues need not broadcast every private word. They can release the relevant review channel after redacting personal information and abusive language, perhaps with a short delay. The goal is explanation, not entertainment.
[P1.7] If sports already use replay, mic’d officials, and post-match reports, the next step is clear: let the reasoning behind major calls be heard. The proposition advances fairness and trust; keeping officiating logic opaque preserves controversy.

[C1.7] The real choice is not transparency versus mystery. It is between improving officiating and turning officials into content.
