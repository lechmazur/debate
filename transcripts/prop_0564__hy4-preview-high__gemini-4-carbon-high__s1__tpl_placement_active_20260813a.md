[P1.1] Major cities already subsidize transit heavily; fares usually cover only a fraction of operating costs, so charging riders is a toll on those least able to pay. We do not toll every block or invoice drivers per mile on local streets; we fund roads as public infrastructure. The motion asks whether we should do the same for buses and trains—through broad-based taxation—and drop the fare, even if the first ridership bump is small. That, not new trips, should be our test.
[P1.2] Fare-free transit wins beyond new trips. Without fareboxes, gates, inspectors, and evasion enforcement, agencies save money and move vehicles faster. Eliminating fares ends armed police encounters over a missed payment and criminal records for poverty. Quicker boarding, shorter dwell times, and more reliable service help existing riders immediately, even if ridership never soars.
[P1.3] It also removes fare bureaucracy: no discounted passes to verify, no fare-capping software, no eligibility checks that trap riders in paperwork.
[P1.4] Broad-based taxation also charges the beneficiaries who never buy a ticket. Employers gain a wider labor pool; property owners capture land-value gains from access; drivers face slightly less congestion; businesses gain foot traffic. This is not riders escaping payment; everyone already pays for congestion, pollution, and road crashes. A sales or payroll surcharge, congestion charge, or value-capture district can replace fare revenue at a fraction of the economic distortion fares create.
[P1.5] Even with modest ridership growth, the city removes a regressive fee and strengthens a network whose value is universal availability.
[P1.6] Kansas City’s zero-fare buses show the way: no overnight transformation, just smoother boardings, fewer conflicts, and resilient ridership funded by a broad local tax. This is bigger than riders. It declares mobility in a major city a public service. Judge the motion by access, efficiency, and fair funding—not by forcing poor riders to prove their worth at a turnstile before the city can move.

[C1.1] Free transit sounds generous until you ask what a major city sacrifices to buy it. Even where fares cover only part of total costs [P1.1], in metropolises running heavy subways and regional rail, that share still supplies immense dedicated operating funds. Unlike smaller, bus-centered systems like Kansas City [P1.6], major cities that permanently erase fares burn scarce tax capacity to buy zero new buses, zero extra train frequency, and zero expanded routes. Every new tax dollar merely replaces money the agency already had, leaving low-income riders waiting free of charge for late, overcrowded vehicles.
[C1.2] Universal abolition also gets equity backward. Dropping every fare hands a permanent windfall to affluent office commuters, business travelers, and tourists who can easily pay. To fund that giveaway, [P1.4] invites broad levies like sales taxes, hitting poor families on everyday essentials even when they live in transit deserts with scarce service. Targeted discounts and automatic fare-capping protect struggling riders at a fraction of the cost, keeping wealthier riders contributing so new tax revenue actually expands coverage and reliability.
[C1.3] Nor do fares require slow boarding or armed police [P1.2]. Contactless tap-to-pay, all-door boarding, and unarmed civil enforcement deliver fast dwell times and humane treatment without gutting budgets. Meanwhile, zero fares mainly shift short trips away from walking and biking rather than cars, crowding vehicles without cutting traffic. And in dense underground subway systems, permanently removing fare gates strips basic access control, straining cleanliness, maintenance, and perceived safety in ways that push riders back to driving.
[C1.4] Finally, locking agencies permanently into total tax dependence makes transit fiscally fragile. Sales and payroll taxes drop during recessions right when municipal budgets tighten. Stripped of independent farebox revenue, agencies face brutal service cuts precisely when workers need reliable mobility most. Keep modest fares for those who can pay, discount them for those who cannot, and spend broad taxes building transit worth riding.
