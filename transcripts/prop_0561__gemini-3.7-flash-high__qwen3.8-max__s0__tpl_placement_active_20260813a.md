[P1.1] The modern platform economy operates on a costly fiction: that flexibility must require absolute precarity. By classifying millions of rideshare drivers, delivery couriers, and taskers outside the traditional employment compact, platforms capture the value of labor while systematically externalizing the essential costs of social insurance—healthcare, paid rest, and retirement—onto workers and the public. Requiring prorated benefits tied to hours worked directly resolves this market failure.
[P1.2] First, prorated benefits dismantle the false choice between schedule autonomy and baseline security. Gig workers routinely patch together thirty, forty, or fifty hours a week across multiple applications, yet remain entirely uncovered because our safety net was built around single, traditional employers. A prorated, portable system attaches social protections directly to the hour of labor performed. If a courier works fifteen hours on one platform and twenty-five on another, each company contributes proportionally into a portable benefits account.
[P1.3] The worker gains continuous, comprehensive protection without sacrificing the freedom to set their own schedule or switch platforms at will.
[P1.4] Second, the absence of prorated mandates creates an unsustainable hidden subsidy. When an injured driver lacks paid recovery leave, an uninsured courier uses the emergency room, or an aging workforce enters retirement destitute, those costs do not evaporate; they are absorbed by public assistance programs and taxpayers. Concurrently, brick-and-mortar businesses that provide standard benefits face unfair competition from platforms profiting off regulatory arbitrage. Mandating prorated contributions ensures platforms internalize their true cost of doing business, restoring fair market competition and protecting public balance sheets.
[P1.5] Labor protections must reflect how people actually work today. Requiring platform companies to fund prorated benefits modernizes the social contract, guaranteeing that work—regardless of the app it is booked through—delivers security alongside flexibility.

The casual student, caregiver, or rural driver who is available but not constantly completing orders becomes a worse bet. Unlike a scheduled job, platform demand fluctuates by minute, so managing volatility by limiting access is the predictable result.
[C2.3] PRO also treats tracking as solved because apps measure time. But the motion ties benefits to “hours worked,” not merely completed deliveries. That immediately raises disputes: Is waiting time counted? What about being online but unanswered, multi-apping, or traveling to a pickup?

[C2.5] PRO’s taxpayer argument assumes the cheapest fix is to make platforms mimic employers. Even granting some external costs, an hours-linked mandate is self-defeating: it makes marginal, supplemental work costlier and less available, undermining the very income that reduces reliance on public assistance. It also entrenches large platforms that can absorb compliance systems, while smaller entrants cannot.
[C2.6] The clash is simple: PRO protects a hypothetical benefit ledger; we protect actual access. If the policy makes low-barrier work harder to get, the people PRO says it helps are the first to lose.
