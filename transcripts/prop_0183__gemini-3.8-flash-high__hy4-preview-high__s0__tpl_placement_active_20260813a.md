[P1.1] Every digital act of preservation requires duplication. To archive an endangered webpage, emulate an abandoned operating system, or digitize an out-of-print text before the paper degrades, a digital library must make copies. Under traditional copyright enforcement—anchored by strict liability and catastrophic statutory damages—every one of those essential archival acts is treated legally as an act of infringement.
[P1.2] This creates a devastating market failure. Traditional copyright was designed to incentivize commercial distribution through exclusive sales. But preservation is not a commercial enterprise; it is an archival safeguard against cultural oblivion. The vast majority of published works—orphaned books, ephemeral web media, forgotten software, and local journalism—lose commercial viability within years of release.
[P1.3] Rights holders stop selling them, cease maintaining them, and let servers go dark. When the commercial market moves on, the work faces extinction. If nonprofit libraries cannot step in without facing existential litigation, humanity’s digital heritage is surrendered to link rot, bit rot, and corporate neglect.
[P1.4] Exempting nonprofit digital libraries does not undermine authors or destroy publishing incentives. Bona fide archives exist for research, scholarship, and long-term stewardship, not commercial gain. They do not displace active consumer markets; they step in precisely where the commercial market has failed.
[P1.5] Historically, the law recognized this vital balance. Physical libraries bought books once and preserved them permanently under the first sale doctrine. In the digital era, however, rights holders have replaced ownership with revocable, expiring licenses, effectively privatizing and imperiling public memory. If preservation requires license renewals that publishers refuse to grant, history itself becomes contingent on corporate convenience.
[P1.6] To ensure that our collective knowledge survives beyond corporate balance sheets, nonprofit digital libraries must be exempt from traditional copyright enforcement.

[C1.1] The PRO case is a bait-and-switch. Preservation means keeping a work from vanishing, not giving the public a free copy on demand. [P1.1] is right that preservation requires duplication, but wrong to imply that every duplicate must be published. The real controversy is not dark archives; it is that nonprofit digital libraries such as the Internet Archive scan works—including titles still sold as ebooks—and lend digital copies to a worldwide public.
[C1.2] That is distribution, not preservation, and it is the activity the PRO wants immunized.
[C1.3] Heritage can be preserved without dismantling enforcement. Libraries already migrate formats, make backups, and maintain restricted-access or on-premises archives; existing legal doctrines and sensible reforms can protect those acts. The genuine gaps—orphaned works, obsolete software, rights holders who refuse to license—call for narrow tools: preservation copies confined to dark archives, format-shifting, diligent-search orphan-work clearance, and capped damages for good-faith archivists. The answer to refusals is a compulsory license for truly unavailable works, not blanket immunity that also covers titles still for sale.
[C1.4] Targeted privileges preserve heritage while keeping courts able to ask the decisive question: does this copy replace the market or stand in for a work no market serves?
[C1.5] A blanket exemption answers that question by abolishing it. Nonprofit status is a tax category, not a guarantee of stewardship. Any aggregator can call itself a digital library, copy everything, and distribute it while invoking “preservation.” Without traditional enforcement, there is no neutral test to separate saving a dead webpage from lending a living author’s novel. Picture a debut author whose book is still for sale as an ebook; a nonprofit scans a print copy and lends unlimited downloads.
[C1.6] That is not rescuing lost heritage, it is substituting a free copy for a licensed one. If we exempt such acts, we do not preserve culture—we strip creators of the incentive to produce the next generation of it.

[C2.4] PRO says preservation without access is ‘mere digital embalming’ [P2.1]. Preservation’s duty is survival, not instant free access. Heritage cannot depend on corporate maintenance, but the alternative to corporate veto is a compulsory license, not blanket immunity.
[C2.5] The clash is who decides. Under reform, a judge separates the dead website from the in-market ebook. Under the motion, the archiving nonprofit decides, and any aggregator can incorporate as a nonprofit and copy at will.
