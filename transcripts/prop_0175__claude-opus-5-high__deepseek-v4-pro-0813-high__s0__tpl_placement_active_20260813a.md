[C1.2] Take hair braiding. PRO says no chemicals, no heat, no blades. But the hazard is not chemical; it is mechanical and biological. Excessive tension causes traction alopecia that may not appear for years.
[C1.3] Shared combs and clips transmit fungal infections, lice, and staph. A sanitation and scalp-health curriculum is directly relevant, not unrelated filler. That is why a modest braiding-specific registration with hygiene and infection-control instruction is a safety tool, not a wall.
[C1.4] Interior design is even clearer. Commercial designers choose finishes, lighting, and layouts that affect fire egress, flammability, and accessibility. A consumer cannot eyeball whether a corridor width or finish meets code until there is a fire or a lawsuit. Licensing is ex ante screening for exactly the kind of hidden, catastrophic risk that bonding and after-the-fact liability handle poorly.
[C1.5] The PRO’s map argument proves too much. An absence of a reported wave of injuries in deregulated states is not evidence of safety; these harms are slow, diffuse, and underreported. You would not measure a license’s value by counting scalp infections in a data set that does not exist.
[C1.6] Nor does industry support prove the primary motive is protectionism. Practitioners see the consequences of untrained entry and often seek the same standards that consumer-safety agencies do. Grandfathering is a pragmatic transition; existing practitioners have demonstrated experience, while new entrants get training. Imperfect implementation does not convert a safety floor into a cartel.
[C1.7] The phrase “low-risk trades” is doing the work. Braiding and interior design are not low-risk in the ways buyers can easily judge. The real remedy for an overly broad curriculum is a narrower safety curriculum, not the conclusion that licensing is incumbent protection.
