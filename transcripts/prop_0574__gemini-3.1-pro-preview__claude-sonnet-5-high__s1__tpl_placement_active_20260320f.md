[P1.1] Artistic expression is fundamentally tied to human identity. A singer’s voice, an actor’s face, and a creator's unique style are not mere raw materials to be strip-mined by studios the moment the artist passes away. We must require explicit permission before using AI to generate new performances of deceased artists to protect both the dignity of the dead and the livelihood of the living.
[P1.2] First, AI generation without permission violates posthumous autonomy and artistic integrity. When a studio uses AI to synthesize a new performance, they are literally putting words into a dead artist's mouth. They are forcing the deceased to "perform" scripts they never read, sing lyrics they never approved, and endorse messages they might have actively opposed in life. An artist's legacy is their life's work.
[P1.3] Without an explicit permission requirement—whether established in the artist's will or granted by their estate—studios are free to puppet deceased icons for cheap nostalgia and endless profit. This turns deeply personal artistic identities into infinitely exploitable corporate IP.
[P1.4] Second, unrestricted corporate necromancy cannibalizes the market for living artists. If record labels and movie studios can legally and freely resurrect universally beloved superstars to front new projects, the financial incentive to discover, develop, and pay living talent is severely diminished. Why take a financial risk on an unknown contemporary artist when a studio can simply prompt an AI to generate a guaranteed hit by a legendary icon? Requiring explicit permission creates a vital check against studios flooding the market with artificial performances, ensuring that the entertainment industry remains a viable ecosystem for actual human creativity.
[P1.5] Demanding explicit permission is a minimal, common-sense boundary. It shifts control away from profit-driven corporations and back to the stewards of the artist's legacy. An artist’s voice and likeness belong to them and their chosen heirs, not to the boardroom. We must not allow the future of art to become an endless, non-consensual recycling of the past.
