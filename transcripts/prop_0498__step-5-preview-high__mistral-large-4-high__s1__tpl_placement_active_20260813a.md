[P1.1] When a company owns the toll road, sets the traffic rules, and sells products at the roadside, the conflict is not incidental—it is structural. Major app-store operators control distribution, payment rails, review standards, ranking algorithms, preinstallation, default status, and access to developer data. When they also compete as first-party developers, they are not just selling apps; they are judging the rivals of their own products.
[P1.2] The harm is not limited to obvious favoritism. It includes subtler advantages: seeing nonpublic competitor data before writing policy, changing APIs or guidelines in ways that happen to burden rivals more, delaying enforcement against first-party products, or making rivals’ features less discoverable. These conduct remedies are slow, case-by-case, and usually begin only after competitors have already been weakened.
[P1.3] Platform operators will say separation hurts innovation or user convenience. But the current structure already distorts innovation: developers build not only for consumers, but around the store’s opaque gatekeeping. A store remains useful without being owned by the first-party apps it carries.
[P1.4] The proposition does not require banning first-party apps. It requires separating the marketplace operator from the app maker. First-party products can compete like everyone else, under the same review, ranking, and data-access rules. But they should not control the gate they need to pass.
[P1.5] The decisive question is whether independent developers can compete when the rules are written and enforced by their rival. Today, in the most important mobile marketplaces, they cannot fairly do so. Structural separation is the only remedy that fixes the incentive itself, rather than repeatedly punishing its symptoms.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes an ownership problem for a conduct problem. Self-preferencing comes from specific levers—ranking, search, defaults, private APIs, developer data, enforcement timing—and those levers remain in the gatekeeper’s hands after a corporate split. Forcing a platform owner to separate its store from its own apps would not make the phone less controlled; the operator would still set review, payments, ranking, and API access, while the separated first-party app would still be a commercially favored customer, perhaps carrying privileged data and engineering habits from years inside the platform.
[C1.2] The gate remains; only the jersey changes.
[C1.3] What is lost is the integrated machinery users actually rely on: malware screening, privacy labels, parental controls, payment-fraud checks, coordinated security patches, and features that work smoothly because the OS, store, and first-party apps are aligned. That integration is not a loophole for self-preferencing; it is why many users prefer a curated store to a raw download site. A separated wallet, browser, or music app would still need permissions, data access, and distribution decisions from the same gatekeeper—now with more friction and less accountability.
[C1.4] Breakup would also fragment abuse response, raise compliance costs for smaller developers, and freeze today’s design so the incumbent cannot improve first-party features that compete with third parties. Real competition exists across ecosystems, web apps, and alternative storefronts where lawful. The proportionate remedy is enforceable neutrality: equal API and data access, independent review of ranking and enforcement, interoperability duties, and penalties that bite. Structural separation is at most a backstop if those rules fail, not the default the motion demands.
[C1.5] Vote CON: the proposal promises to prevent self-preferencing while mainly dismantling consumer safeguards and leaving the gatekeeper exactly where it is.

[P2.1] CON’s central image—[C1.2] “the gate remains; only the jersey changes”—is not a rebuttal; it is the mechanism. Today the jersey is the same, so favoring it is internal revenue generation. After separation, throttling a rival no longer pads the operator’s bottom line; it merely helps a customer that can now fail without dragging the gatekeeper’s earnings down. A gatekeeper that no longer sells the product being gated gains a concrete reason to keep the gate fair.
[P2.2] The integrated machinery praised in [C1.3] is also the machinery that creates the risk. Coordinated OS-store-app alignment is exactly what enables private APIs, pre-installation, and nonpublic data flows to be granted invisibly. A separated wallet or music app would still need permissions, yes—but on published terms, not insider terms. Separation does not disable malware screening or privacy labels; it stops the same team from writing safety rules and competitive roadmaps in the same room.
[P2.3] Separate entities are easier to audit, harder to shield internally, and clearer for developers to challenge.
[P2.4] CON’s fallback to cross-ecosystem competition is weak. A rival store on the same device is still governed by the same gatekeeper, and users do not abandon an ecosystem because a niche app was demoted. Nor does separation freeze innovation: the store remains free to improve curation and security; it simply cannot route those improvements into a sibling competitor.
[P2.5] Meanwhile, “enforceable neutrality” is the experiment we are already living—slow, case-by-case, and always one API change behind. You cannot police a conflict of interest by asking the conflicted party to document its good behavior. You remove the interest.
[P2.6] Structural separation is not a backstop for failed rules; it is the condition that makes neutrality enforceable. Keep the gate, but take the gatekeeper out of the business of walking through it.

[C2.1] PRO claims that after separation, throttling a rival "no longer pads the operator's bottom line" [P2.1]. That misses how platform economics work. The operator's bottom line is total ecosystem value, not one app's revenue. A thriving affiliated music or wallet app makes the device more attractive, deepens lock-in, and drives subscription income across the platform.
[C2.2] Even as a separate legal entity, the first-party app remains the platform's most important customer because its success is the platform's success. The incentive does not disappear; it moves from an internal budget line into revenue-sharing deals and "most favored" commercial terms that regulators must prove were collusive rather than merely preferential.
[C2.3] That is why separation does not make the gate easier to audit [P2.3]; it makes coordination harder to detect. Inside one company, favoritism leaves a paper trail. Across two affiliated entities, the same favoritism becomes a contract or data-sharing agreement—legally permitted and practically invisible unless a regulator proves illicit communication. We would trade visible internal bias for opaque bilateral dealing.
[C2.4] PRO also asserts that separated apps would face "published terms, not insider terms" [P2.2]. But the platform still writes those terms, and affiliated firms negotiate exceptions through commercial leverage. The insider advantage becomes a contractual one. Meanwhile, the security machinery users rely on [C1.3] depends on the OS, store, and first-party apps sharing threat intelligence in real time.
[C2.5] Splitting them inserts a legal boundary into the malware-response chain.
[C2.6] Finally, PRO calls separation "the condition that makes neutrality enforceable" [P2.6]. It is the opposite. Conduct rules—equal API access, independent ranking audits, data parity—attach directly to the levers the gatekeeper controls, whatever the corporate chart says. Separation leaves those levers untouched while fragmenting accountability between two entities that can each blame the other.
[C2.7] The gate remains, the incentive remains, and responsibility is now split in two. That is not a remedy for self-preferencing; it is a defense strategy for it.
