[P1.1] The core question is not whether high antibiotic prices are pleasant; it is whether societies can keep modern medicine functioning when resistant infections spread. The current market fails: antibiotics cure quickly, are used sparingly to preserve efficacy, and generate little recurring revenue. A developer can spend a decade and large sums, then be told to sell few doses at low prices. The rational response is to abandon antibiotics.
[P1.2] Advance market commitments reverse that logic. A country or coalition promises in advance that if a developer produces a needed antibiotic meeting defined criteria, there will be a guaranteed market at a substantial price. That pull reward makes investment possible without requiring overuse.
[P1.3] High unit prices are not the flaw here; they are the incentive. If we insist antibiotics be cheap before they exist, we often get no antibiotic at all. The alternative to a high-priced new drug is not a low-priced one; it is untreated resistant infection, longer hospital stays, invasive procedures becoming dangerous, and chemotherapy, surgery, and neonatal care placed at risk. A patient with a resistant bloodstream infection may need weeks of hospital care; an effective course of treatment can be enormously valuable even at a high price.
[P1.4] AMCs also allow governments to set conditions in advance: target pathogens, delivery volumes, access requirements, stewardship rules, and pricing ceilings or tiered prices across countries. Permitting AMCs does not mean surrendering to whatever a firm demands. It means choosing a controlled, transparent instrument instead of relying on ad hoc emergency purchases after resistance has already spread.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is between tolerating high unit prices under negotiated guarantees and accepting a pipeline so weak that future infections outrun available treatments. Countries should permit AMCs because the cost of inaction is measured in preventable deaths.

[C1.1] Advance market commitments that leave unit prices high do not rescue the antibiotic pipeline. They purchase a product that hospitals cannot stock routinely, patients cannot afford, and stewardship programs cannot use as intended. The proposition asks countries to accept that bargain. They should refuse it.
[C1.2] A high unit price is not a harmless accounting detail. It is the price a hospital pharmacist faces when a resistant infection appears overnight. If that price stays high, the drug sits behind formulary locks and prior authorization. Wealthy systems may buy a few courses.
[C1.3] Everyone else continues with older, failing agents. A last-line antibiotic that arrives as a luxury good does not keep surgery, chemotherapy, or neonatal care safe.
[C1.4] High prices also misfire on resistance. Those who can pay press for the newest agent. Those who cannot stretch inferior regimens or skip doses. An AMC that guarantees revenue while leaving the sticker price high therefore buys both overuse and underuse.
[C1.5] Contractual stewardship rules cannot survive a price that turns every indicated course into a budget crisis.
[C1.6] The instrument is not controlled if the unit price stays high. Governments lock in a large transfer to one firm. When budgets tighten, the commitment is delayed or redefined, and other infection-control spending is crowded out. Firms price that political risk into still higher demanded rewards.
[C1.7] Taxpayers fund a guarantee; patients still face the high charge.
[C1.8] The alternative is not untreated infection. It is delinkage: subscriptions that pay for availability, prizes for a defined product, and publicly governed manufacturing. Those tools can pull innovation without declaring high unit prices acceptable. Permitting AMCs even if prices remain high surrenders state leverage at the only moment it is real.
[C1.9] Countries should condition any pull on prices that make indicated use possible. A pipeline of unaffordable antibiotics is not a working pipeline.

The question is not the sticker price; it is whether the drug exists and can reach the patients who need it.
[P2.2] CON also claims high prices produce both overuse and underuse [C1.4]. That is a world without effective contracting. If the price is high but volumes are capped and funding is central, the budget impact is bounded.
[P2.3] If access is the concern, the answer is to write access obligations into the commitment, not to reject an immediately usable pull mechanism that can revive a candidate while developers are still deciding whether to invest.
[P2.4] The deepest flaw in CON’s case is its counterfactual. It says the alternative is delinkage, prizes, or public manufacturing [C1.8]. Those are not substitutes for permitting AMCs; they are additional tools that also require large public commitments and political durability. If countries can use subscriptions or prizes, fine—but forbidding AMCs narrows the toolkit at the moment the pipeline is weakest.
[P2.5] CON has not shown that high unit prices under an AMC are worse than no new drug, or that other pull mechanisms can reliably arrive faster, with less risk, and without their own access problems.
[P2.6] The real choice is between a controlled, high-price guarantee for scarce new antibiotics and continued dependence on older drugs that resistance is eroding.
