[C1.1] Premium large‑format screens are not a threat to ordinary moviegoers; they are the engine that keeps the whole system alive. The proposition assumes that a handful of standard‑price shows at odd hours constitutes a failure, but that framing misreads how modern multiplexes operate. When a blockbuster like *Top Gun: Maverick* opens, exhibitors typically allocate a majority of their auditoriums to IMAX or Dolby because the demand for those experiences is measurable, not imagined. Those premium screenings generate a disproportionate share of the weekend gross—often well over half—while the remaining standard shows still fill seats because the audience base has broadened, not shrunk.
[C1.2] The key point is revenue, not seat count. The extra margin from premium tickets funds the renovation of older houses, subsidises lower‑priced programming, and sustains the staff who keep the lights on. If we forced theaters to keep a fixed quota of cheap seats regardless of demand, they would cut back on the very investments that make a multiplex attractive. The result would be fewer total screens, not more, and a faster drift toward streaming.
[C1.3] Moreover, the condition “too few standard‑price screenings” is vague enough to be weaponised. What counts as “too few”? One show a day? Two?
[C1.4] The market already self‑regulates: when standard seats become scarce, secondary‑run titles and off‑peak times expand, and audiences adjust their habits. The claim that a 7 p.m. premium show is a “toll” ignores that the same audience could simply choose a later standard showing or a different venue; the choice is real, not manufactured.
[C1.5] Finally, blockbuster premieres are cultural events that benefit from the spectacle premium formats provide. Limiting that spectacle does not democratise cinema; it dilutes the experience for everyone, including the very families the proposition purports to protect. The real harm is not a shortage of cheap tickets but a shortage of revenue that keeps theaters open.
