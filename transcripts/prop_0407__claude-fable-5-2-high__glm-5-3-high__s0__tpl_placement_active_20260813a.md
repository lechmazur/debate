[C1.1] The proposition looks narrow only because its key phrase hides the fight. "Does not materially affect job performance" sounds like a clean filter, but the moment it becomes a legal test, judges and juries — not employers — decide what counts. That transfer of judgment, from the people who run the business to the people who litigate after the fact, is where this proposal goes wrong.
[C1.2] Consider what "material" means in a courtroom. A salesperson's viral video mocking the company's customers may not show up in this quarter's numbers, but the employer believes it will. A manager's off-duty conduct that coworkers find repugnant may not change his output, but it poisons the team. Under this proposition, the employer fires, gets sued, and waits years for a jury to decide whether the harm was "material" enough.
[C1.3] Employers will lose some of those cases they should win, win some they should lose, and pay legal fees in all of them. The predictable result is defensive management: hesitant hiring, more contractors, fewer permanent jobs — costs paid by the very workers this is meant to protect.
[C1.4] Second, the "power imbalance" argument proves too much. Employees may quit for any reason, including reasons employers find unfair, and no one proposes restricting that. A legal regime that lets either side exit freely is symmetrical; one that lets employees leave at will but forces employers to defend every departure in court is not balancing power, it is stacking it.
[C1.5] Third, the worst cases are already covered. Retaliation laws, anti-discrimination statutes, and state protections for union and political activity handle the scenarios that genuinely alarm us. What is left — the motorcycle, the cigarette, the yard sign — is the residue, and creating a broad new cause of action to sweep up that residue imposes real costs to fix a marginal problem.
[C1.6] The employer who wants to fire over trivia will find a pretext anyway; the honest employer is the one punished here. That is a bad trade.
