[P1.1] Heat is not weather when you are working in it. It is a hazard. For farmworkers harvesting crops, roofers laying tar, and delivery drivers walking pavement, extreme heat is a predictable, deadly workplace risk. Governments should treat it as a labor-rights issue with mandatory stop-work protections because the alternative leaves workers choosing between their health and their paycheck.
[P1.2] Labor rights exist to correct power imbalances in the workplace. Existing rules mandate harnesses for roofers and respirators for miners because employers, left to voluntary goodwill, often prioritize output over safety, and the science on heat stress is not in dispute. Extreme heat is no different. It is a physical stressor with a clear biological threshold—one that damages kidneys, causes heat stroke, and kills.
[P1.3] When temperatures cross a certain line, the risk is not discomfort; it is bodily harm.
[P1.4] Voluntary guidelines fail because workers cannot exercise them. A farmworker paid by the piece, or a warehouse worker facing attendance policies, will keep working through dizziness and nausea rather than risk termination or lost wages, even when the alternative is collapse. Only a mandatory, enforceable stop-work trigger breaks that coercive logic. It shifts the burden from the most vulnerable person—the employee—to the institution with the duty to provide safe conditions.
[P1.5] Some will argue this burdens business. But dead and injured workers are the greater burden: to families, to communities, and to the medical systems that absorb preventable emergency room visits. Protecting workers from extreme heat preserves productivity and human dignity.
[P1.6] The proposition is simple. If a government can mandate guardrails on scaffolds, it can mandate shade, water, and rest when the thermometer reaches a dangerous level. Extreme heat is a labor-rights issue because the right to survive a shift should never depend on a worker’s willingness to gamble with their life.

[C1.1] Extreme heat is dangerous, but the proposition leaps from “heat can kill” to “government must mandate stop-work protections.” That leap is not justified. Heat risk varies with humidity, radiant heat, work intensity, clothing, age, medical condition, and acclimatization. There is no universal temperature line that cleanly separates safe work from dangerous work. A fixed trigger will falsely stop relatively safe work while missing lethal combinations of heat and humidity.
[C1.2] Treating extreme heat as a labor-rights issue therefore does not require, and may not support, a mandatory stop-work rule.
[C1.3] The rule can also harm the workers it claims to protect. Many exposed workers are paid hourly, by the piece, or through commissions. A government-ordered stoppage with no wages means heat safety becomes another source of lost income, not meaningful protection. That is especially damaging for migrant workers, outdoor laborers, and employees with weak bargaining power.
[C1.4] Some will work informally or elsewhere to replace lost earnings; others may conceal symptoms rather than risk both lost pay and employer retaliation. PRO assumes legal protection ends coercion, but a right to stop work that carries financial penalties or exposes a worker to dismissal is no real remedy.
[C1.5] Mandatory stop-work also shifts responsibility away from controls that actually reduce risk: shade, ventilation, hydration, rest periods, acclimatization, lighter schedules, and access to cooling. Governments can impose enforceable safety duties without declaring that all work must cease whenever a statutory threshold is crossed. Such graduated regulation better matches the hazard than a binary prohibition.
[C1.6] Finally, calling every extreme-heat risk a “labor-rights issue” obscures the operative problem: what protections are owed to a worker facing dangerous heat? The answer may include paid heat leave, workplace standards, and a protected right to refuse imminent danger—not blanket stop-work. The proposition asks governments to adopt a blunt mechanism whose burdens often fall hardest on those least able to absorb them. Heat deserves strong regulation, but dangerous weather is not itself proof of a labor-rights entitlement to stop working.

[C2.5] The decisive comparison is therefore not advice versus law. It is graduated, enforceable safeguards versus compulsory termination of activity.
