[P1.1] Every argument against this motion collapses into one claim: that governments may ban a product their own safety regulators have already cleared. Read the motion as written. It does not ask you to trust cultivated meat; it asks what follows once safety standards are met — the same kind of review that clears any novel food ingredient. The only live question is whether the state may suppress a safe product because incumbents dislike competition or voters find it strange.
[P1.2] That ground is rotten. Governments should not pick winners among safe products. Pasteurization, canning, and frozen food were each denounced as unnatural; the right answer was standards and inspection, not prohibition. Regulatory neutrality — the same inspection, labeling, and fraud rules — is how food systems absorb innovation without capture by whoever already owns the market.
[P1.3] The stakes are not abstract. Livestock uses around a quarter of the world's ice-free land and a large share of medically important antibiotics, a known engine of resistant bacteria, while tens of billions of animals each year pass through industrial confinement and slaughter. Cultivated meat is real meat grown from cells: a fraction of the land, no slaughter, and sterile production that avoids the fecal-contamination pathway behind many E. coli and salmonella outbreaks. A government serious about climate goals, antibiotic stewardship, or animal welfare cannot rationally forbid a technology serving all three.
[P1.4] Nor is this hypothetical. Singapore and the United States cleared cultivated chicken after multi-year reviews; Italy and some US states moved to ban it, citing no failed safety test — only cattle-industry pressure and cultural politics. That is the fork: science-led regulation, or protectionism with a safety alibi.
[P1.5] CON must either deny that regulators can ever competently judge this product — an argument that indicts food regulation itself — or concede safety and defend banning a safe product anyway. Neither survives scrutiny. Once standards are met, the burden flips: prohibition needs a justification, and discomfort is not one.
