[P1.1] The proposition before us concerns what organizations actually purchase when they hire remote talent: results. Outputs—code committed, deals closed, documents delivered, problems solved—are the substance of the employment transaction. Activity monitoring—keystroke counts, webcam attendance, idle-time tracking—is at best a crude proxy, and at worst a fiction that rewards theater over substance.
[P1.2] Consider the perverse incentives surveillance creates. A worker measured by minutes online learns to look busy. They jiggle the mouse. They keep a chat window open.
[P1.3] They attend performative meetings. The metric becomes the goal, and actual work suffers. This is Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Output-based evaluation resists this corruption because it judges the deliverable, not the performance of working.
[P1.4] Remote and hybrid arrangements make presence signals especially misleading. A developer in Lisbon may solve in twenty minutes a problem that stumps an office-based team for days, yet register as "idle" by activity metrics. A writer produces a brilliant brief after hours of thinking, with no keystrokes to show for the cognition. Knowledge work is not factory labor; it cannot be measured by visible exertion.
[P1.5] The choice is between managing adults as professionals or as suspects. Output-focused evaluation grants the autonomy that drives excellence and retention. Surveillance-based management manufactures resentment, gaming, and attrition. We should evaluate remote and hybrid workers on what they produce, because that is what the organization needs, what the worker controls, and what distinguishes real contribution from mere presence.

[C1.2] First, outputs are lagging indicators. A remote account manager can coast for months on renewals while the pipeline quietly decays; a burned-out engineer can ship one last feature before disengaging. Regular presence signals—showing up to handoffs, responding within agreed windows, participating in planning—warn managers before the next output collapses. Waiting for deliverables turns management into an autopsy.
[C1.3] Second, output metrics are themselves gameable. Make tickets closed the target and workers will close easy tickets, inflate counts, or grab visible tasks. The same Goodhart problem PRO attributes to activity applies to outputs. And knowledge work is rarely solo: a strong design may come from one person, but review, debugging, and maintenance involve a team.
[C1.4] Judging mainly code committed or tickets closed rewards the visible author, not the person who unblocked them or mentored the new hire.
[C1.5] Third, output-only evaluation is unfair to new, sick, or reassigned workers. A recent hire may produce little while learning systems, yet be actively attending onboarding, asking questions, and building relationships. Judging mainly outputs punishes ramp-up and encourages fake early artifacts. Reasonable presence signals capture whether the person is engaged.
[C1.6] Finally, outputs say nothing about whether someone is reachable when needed, handles conflict acceptably, respects security rules, or is quietly burning out. Those are not theater; they are process, collaboration, and sustainability. Remote and hybrid work needs output plus activity and presence signals—not the final-deliverable reductionism the proposition demands.
