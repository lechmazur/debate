[P1.1] Security cooperation should be judged by whether it prevents crises and protects civilians, not by whether it makes diplomacy comfortable. On that standard, Japan and South Korea need institutions that work before missiles fly and before political relations deteriorate.
[P1.2] First, formal intelligence-sharing closes dangerous operational gaps. North Korea’s missile forces compress decision-making into minutes, while maritime and air contingencies can leave little time to reconstruct another country’s picture. South Korea offers proximity, local surveillance, and direct knowledge of North Korean military behavior; Japan contributes sensors, maritime awareness, bases, and regional perspective. A standing system with common classifications, secure channels, verification procedures, and defined access makes that information usable at speed.
[P1.3] Ad hoc sharing depends on personal trust and the political weather; deterrence cannot depend on either.
[P1.4] Second, joint command structures turn shared information into coordinated action. This need not mean subordinating either military to the other. It can mean permanent liaison teams, agreed procedures, and combined command arrangements for designated missions such as missile warning, maritime rescue, evacuation, and contingency defense. Without rehearsal and clear authority, even allies waste time determining who tracks, who communicates, who escorts, and who acts.
[P1.5] Coordination also reduces miscalculation: each side can see the other’s movements rather than interpreting them through suspicion.
[P1.6] Historical grievances are real, but they are not a security strategy. Leaving cooperation informal does not resolve forced labor, colonial memory, or territorial disputes; it merely ensures that every deterioration in relations also weakens defense. Properly designed institutions can instead insulate essential security from diplomatic cycles through legislative oversight, reciprocal obligations, transparency about mandates, and suspension mechanisms for abuse. Deepening cooperation should accompany historical accountability, not purchase amnesia.
[P1.7] The alternative is not principled independence. It is greater reliance on Washington to transmit information and improvise coordination between two capable neighbors. That leaves both publics exposed when urgency is highest. Formal cooperation makes deterrence more credible, crises more manageable, and each country less vulnerable—all without requiring either nation to abandon its history or sovereignty.
