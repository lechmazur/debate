[C1.1] The proposition makes sportswashing the premise and the answer. That is a non sequitur. Even if Saudi Arabia wants the World Cup to improve its reputation, that motive does not establish that awarding the hosting rights was unjustified. “Sportswashing” explains why a government wants the tournament; it does not show that the tournament lacks sporting value.
[C1.2] The human-rights concerns PRO identifies are serious, but they justify enforceable labor protections, monitoring, and sanctions for breach—not making the government’s image irrelevant. Disqualifying any bidder whose government has committed abuses makes government itself the disqualification standard. On that logic, few major sporting events would be possible.
[C1.3] Investment and infrastructure are not a convenient excuse when the motion insists they count for “nothing.” They are the substance of a bid: stadiums, transport, security, accommodation, broadcasting access, and event operations. These are testable contractual commitments. Awarding the rights gives FIFA deadlines, inspectors, and leverage to enforce them; refusing the bid would not itself improve one worker’s rights. The venues cannot redeem abuses, but neither can their absence.
[C1.4] FIFA also did not confer a general certificate of legitimacy. It awarded the right to stage matches under specific obligations. A government may seek prestige while also exposing local clubs, athletes, spectators, and the football economy to a global audience. [C1.5] PRO’s weakness is the unexplained alternative. If Saudi Arabia is excluded, which bidder should host, and on what objective ground? Condemning this bid while naming no candidate and providing no comparative process is symbolic exclusion, not ethics. Saudi Arabia may indeed be sportswashing; that does not prove FIFA’s reward was unwarranted, and the categorical “regardless” demands that this award solve problems no hosting decision could solve.
