Medical care produces mental-health gains for essentially every carefully screened adolescent who receives it; long-term regret falls on a small minority, and screening is specifically designed to shrink that minority. [P1.2] Start with the baseline these adolescents face. Persistent gender dysphoria — diagnosed over years, not weeks — is not teenage discomfort. Denial of care is not a neutral option.
[P1.3] It hands the adolescent the same dysphoria, with compounded distress and, in some cases, a body that is far harder to reconcile later.
[P1.4] Now the other side of the ledger. Care is staged precisely to control regret. [P1.5] Continuation rates in carefully assessed cohorts are high and regret is uncommon; where regret occurs, it is often about cosmetic outcome or social hostility, not a wish never to have pursued care at all. [P1.6] Weigh the magnitudes. Relief from a disabling, chronic, life-shaping condition for a clear majority, versus regret that is uncommon, partly mitigable, and detectable early through staged treatment. Care that is screened and monitored delivers more mental-health good than regret or harm.
