[P1.1] A three-bedroom starter house in a tight market is not just another asset. For a local teacher or nurse, it is the one chance to stop renting, build equity, and stay in the community they serve. For a large institutional investor, it is unit 4,000 in a portfolio, valued for rent extraction and resale. When those two buyers meet, the contest is not close — and that asymmetry is why cities should intervene.
[P1.2] Large funds buy with cash, waive inspections, close in days, and bid above asking because their return comes from future rents and portfolio scale, not from living in the home. A family with a mortgage approval, a contingency, and a monthly budget cannot match that without overpaying. The result is predictable: prices get set by investor math, not by local incomes, and otherwise qualified first-time buyers lose house after house until they either overextend or leave.
[P1.3] The harm does not end at the sale. Every home converted from ownership to corporate rental is a rung removed from the ladder. Renters pay more over time, build no equity, face less stability at renewal, and have less control over the street they live on. Concentrated buying in lower-priced neighborhoods makes it worse, because that is exactly where entry-level ownership was still possible.
[P1.4] A city can add housing over years, but it cannot quickly replace the starter stock being absorbed now.
[P1.5] This restriction is narrow by design. It targets only large institutional buyers, only single-family homes, and only tight markets where crowding out is happening. Small landlords, multifamily developers, and builders adding new supply remain free to invest. Cities already limit short-term rentals and condo conversions to protect residential stock; protecting owner-occupied stock is the same principle.
[P1.6] Without a limit, homeownership is allocated by balance sheet. With one, local buyers get a fair chance to live where they work.

[C1.1] The core flaw in this proposal is that it misidentifies the cause of tight housing markets and prescribes a solution that will worsen affordability for the households it claims to help. Institutional investors are not the root driver of price growth in constrained markets; decades of chronic underbuilding driven by restrictive zoning, lengthy approval processes, and disincentives for entry-level construction are. Banning these buyers from existing single-family stock distracts from substantive policy fixes—upzoning, expedited permits, density bonuses—that would actually expand supply and lower prices for all buyers.
[C1.2] Far from crowding out local owners, many institutional investors revitalize blighted, neglected single-family homes that local buyers are unwilling or unable to rehab. In post-industrial cities like Detroit and Cleveland, large funds have purchased and renovated tens of thousands of vacant, abandoned properties that would otherwise sit empty, dragging down neighborhood values and increasing crime. Restricting these buyers would leave that stock vacant, doing far more harm to local communities than allowing occupied, well-maintained units.
[C1.3] The claim that converting homes to rentals removes rungs from the equity ladder ignores the critical role stable, professionally managed rental options play for households that cannot yet afford to buy. Young workers with student debt, people who move frequently for employment, and those with imperfect credit rely on these units to avoid displacement, build savings, and establish credit—none of which is possible with a small, unpredictable landlord. Corporate rental options also tend to have more consistent maintenance and less arbitrary rent hikes than small-scale landlords.
[C1.4] The restriction’s targeted framing also fails to account for enforcement gaps and unintended consequences. Defining “large institutional investor” is notoriously tricky, and wealthy individual buyers or small speculator groups will simply use shell companies to avoid the rules, while the restriction does nothing to address the actual drivers of housing unaffordability. Worse, the policy would disincentivize new entry-level single-family construction, as firms shift capital away from the segment entirely, further shrinking the stock of affordable homes for local buyers.
