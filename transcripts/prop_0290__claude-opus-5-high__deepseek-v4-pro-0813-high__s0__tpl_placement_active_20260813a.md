[P1.1] A password can be changed. A card can be cancelled. A face cannot be reissued. That asymmetry is the whole case, and it's why biometric payment deserves a condition that no other payment method needs: if you deploy it, you must keep a convenient ordinary way to pay alongside it.
[P1.2] Notice how modest that ask is. Nothing here bans face-scan checkout or palm-reader turnstiles. It says only that the person who declines must not be punished for declining — no separate queue at the far end of the concourse, no surcharge, no "sorry, this lane is closed."
[P1.3] The reason is that consent without an exit is not consent. If the only gate into the metro reads your face, you have not agreed to biometric processing; you have been billed for it in the currency of getting to work. Transport, groceries, and stadium entry are not luxuries you can boycott in protest. A "choice" you must skip dinner or miss your shift to refuse is a formality, and a company that can only obtain agreement that way is telling you what its offer is really worth.
[P1.4] Then there is the ordinary failure case, which is not exotic at all. Fingerprint readers struggle with worn or scarred hands — manual labourers, older people, anyone who just did the washing up. Face matching performs unevenly across skin tones, and it stumbles on twins, injuries, veils, and the simple fact of ageing. When the fallback exists, a false rejection is a mild annoyance.
[P1.5] When it doesn't, the same glitch means a person is locked out of buying food, with no counter to appeal to and a queue behind them.
[P1.6] And keep the incentive honest. Operators drift toward biometric-only because it cuts staff, not because shoppers demanded it. Guaranteeing the alternative forces biometrics to win the way any product should — by being genuinely better — rather than by quietly removing the competition.
[P1.7] Restrict the deployment, not the technology. Keep the door people can walk out of.

[C1.1] The restriction looks small, but it is a requirement to run two payment systems wherever one might serve. “Always a convenient non-biometric way” is not free. It means card, QR, or cash infrastructure, staffed fallback lanes, maintenance, auditing, and liability. At a small shop, a rural bus route, or an unattended kiosk, that cost can eliminate the service altogether. The result is not freedom; it is fewer options.
[C1.2] Biometrics are not uniquely dangerous compared with the methods the restriction treats as safe defaults. Cards are lost, cloned, skimmed, and cancelled; PINs are forgotten; phones die; cash is lost or stolen. A face or palm requires no separate object to carry or remember. Payment systems normally store a mathematical template, not a photograph, and a template can be revoked, rotated, or re-enrolled.
[C1.3] If the worry is data misuse, the better remedy is direct security and data-protection regulation, not an obligation to preserve legacy payments everywhere.
[C1.4] The access argument cuts the other way. For a person with limited dexterity, chip-and-PIN can be impossible while face or palm payment is easier. For a commuter whose wallet is stolen, biometric transit can still get them home. For someone with memory loss, no PIN is needed.
[C1.5] A blanket condition treats those people as acceptable collateral for a hypothetical privacy holdout.
[C1.6] The claim that biometric-only amounts to coercion mistakes a payment rail for a public entitlement. Shops do not become tyrants by accepting cards but not cheques, or by requiring exact change. Payment systems inevitably set compatibility conditions. If a biometric system is genuinely unpopular, consumers can go elsewhere, and competing venues can advertise the alternative.
[C1.7] The law should target actual deception, misuse, or negligence, not the mere absence of a second rail.
[C1.8] Finally, every system fails. The answer to a failed scan is a sensible operational fallback, not a legal guarantee that every venue maintain parallel non-biometric capacity “always.” Reject the mandate and let payment methods compete on speed, security, and access.
