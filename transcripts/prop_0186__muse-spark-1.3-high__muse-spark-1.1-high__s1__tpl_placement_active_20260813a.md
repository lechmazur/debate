[P1.1] Diplomatic recognition is not a neutral observation that a gang holds a capital. It is a prize: embassies, treaties, loans, a flag at the United Nations, the right to sign away a country's resources. Handing that prize to whoever seizes territory is a moral and strategic surrender.
[P1.2] The rule of pure territorial control tells every general and militia leader the same thing: kill enough people, hold the palace long enough, and the world will call you legitimate. That is an open invitation to coups, ethnic cleansing, and wars of conquest. From Manchukuo to Crimea to the Taliban's Afghanistan, the lesson of automatic recognition is that brutality works if it is effective.
[P1.3] Making recognition conditional on minimum democratic and human-rights standards reverses that incentive. It says power alone is not title. When the world refused to recognize apartheid South Africa's Bantustan puppet states, or Rhodesia's white-minority regime, it denied them the legal cover they craved. When European states tied recognition of new states after the Cold War to democratic commitments and minority protections, several leaders moderated to gain entry.
[P1.4] Conditionality gives reformers leverage and tells victims they are not abandoned.
[P1.5] Critics call this idealistic, but unconditional recognition is what endangers stability. Regimes that crush dissent and persecute minorities at home do not become reliable partners abroad. They generate refugees, sponsor repression across borders, and break agreements because no domestic check constrains them. A club that admits torturers and coup-makers for the sake of tidiness destroys its own ability to enforce any rules.
[P1.6] The standard need not be perfection. No one demands Scandinavian democracy overnight. The bar is basic: no genocide or systematic persecution, no sham annexation by force, some genuine path for people to have a say and dissent without fear. Below that, states can still talk, trade food, negotiate ceasefires — but without conferring the seal of legitimate statehood.
[P1.7] Control may be a fact. Legitimacy must be earned.
