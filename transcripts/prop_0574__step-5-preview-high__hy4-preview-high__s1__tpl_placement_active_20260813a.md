[P1.1] The motion asks a simple question: when an artist dies, who owns their ghost? Not their soul, but their voice, their phrasing, their unmistakable style. PRO says the answer is their estate, their heirs, and their legacy—not whichever studio can scrape enough audio to clone a performance. Without explicit permission, AI turns dead artists into unconsenting instruments.
[P1.2] A label could mint a “new” Johnny Cash track, a “new” Amy Winehouse collaboration, or a “new” Freddie Mercury single without ever asking the family who actually knew the person behind the sound.
[P1.3] Some will call this art, progress, or just another tool. But the issue is not whether AI can imitate; it is whether a corporation can capture a person’s irreducible identity and sell it back to the public. A photograph is not the person; a voiceprint is closer. Style is not a mere technical feature—it is the accumulated biography of a human being, the product of specific choices, struggles, and contexts.
[P1.4] When a model generates a performance “in the likeness” of a dead artist, it does not merely reference them; it impersonates them, commercially.
[P1.5] Explicit permission solves this cleanly. It does not ban the technology. It does not freeze culture. It simply says: if you want to use Kurt Cobain’s cadence or Aretha Franklin’s melisma, ask.
[P1.6] If the estate says yes, fine. If the family says no, the artist’s death is not a waiver of their dignity. Permission is not a bureaucratic obstacle; it is the line between tribute and theft. We require consent from living artists for samples, endorsements, and biopics.

[C1.2] Style and likeness are not the person. They are patterns that already live in recordings, in the public ear, and in the tools of every musician who ever learned by imitation. Copyright has always protected fixed works, not style; that is why a trumpet player can evoke Miles Davis without asking his estate, and why a cover band can sound like Led Zeppelin. The motion would collapse that line and let an heir veto any AI performance that merely resembles the dead.
[C1.3] The "dignity" argument is really about heirs’ feelings, not the artist. The dead have no interests, no experiences, and no stake in how they are remembered. And permission does not stop commercialization—it privatizes it. Under the motion, an estate can license a "new Freddie Mercury single" [P1.2] and profit, while a label that already owns the masters and wants a documentary duet is blocked if a distant relative says no. That is not protection; it is a veto over culture.
[C1.4] Worse, the mandate is unnecessary. Studios and labels already need licenses for copyrighted compositions and sound recordings, and they already cannot deceive listeners into thinking an AI track is a real performance. What they do not need is a "style permission" slapped on top. The right response to synthetic media is disclosure: label it clearly as AI-generated, and let audiences decide.
[C1.5] Existing law and honest labeling protect legacy; a permission requirement only lets heirs tax and censor the future.
[C1.6] PRO’s [P1.7] says "death should protect the legacy." But a legacy is not a locked vault. It is a living conversation. This motion would hand the keys to the highest bidder among the deceased’s relatives.
