[P1.1] Minimum parking mandates do not accommodate reality, they manufacture it. When a city tells every apartment, store, and office it must build parking regardless of need, it guarantees vast lots that sit half-empty, push destinations farther apart, and make walking, biking, or transit impractical. That is not neutrality toward cars. It is a subsidy for driving written into zoning law, paid for by everyone.
[P1.2] The price is brutally concrete. Parking structures and underground garages cost tens of thousands of dollars per space to build, and surface lots devour land that could be homes or businesses. Those costs do not disappear; they surface as higher rents, higher home prices, and higher prices for groceries. Minimums force a single mother who rides the bus to pay for her neighbor's second parking stall.
[P1.3] Maximums flip that injustice: developers can still build parking customers actually want, but cannot be forced to bury needed housing under concrete.
[P1.4] This is most urgent in car-dependent regions, not least. Sprawl makes alternatives impossible, then minimums cite that impossibility as justification for more sprawl. A maximum breaks the loop. Set generously where transit is thin, it still prevents the familiar excess — the big-box store with hundreds of stalls for a Saturday peak that arrives twice a year, the apartment complex ringed by asphalt instead of homes.
[P1.5] Capped parking means closer buildings, shorter trips, land left over for infill, sidewalks, and eventually transit that can actually work.
[P1.6] And a maximum is not a ban. No one is prohibited from driving or from providing reasonable parking up to a generous ceiling. What disappears is only the legal requirement to overbuild, and the legal ban on building less where shared lots, smaller households, aging residents, or nearby jobs make less sufficient.
[P1.7] If car dependence is the disease, minimums are the prescription that keeps the patient sick. Maximums treat the cause while respecting present needs.
