[P1.1] Infertility meets every clinical criterion for a medical disorder: it disrupts normal reproductive function, produces measurable health effects including depression and elevated risks of certain cancers, and responds to targeted intervention. Treating IVF as basic healthcare simply applies the same standard already used for joint replacements, dialysis, or antidepressants.
[P1.2] A couple in their early thirties who discover blocked tubes or low sperm counts through standard diagnostics faces a binary the rest of medicine rejects. Either they absorb costs that can exceed annual household income, or they accept permanent sterility as the price of public policy. No parallel exists for other endocrine or structural failures covered without debate.
[P1.3] Excluding this domain also creates a two-tier reality. Private payment allows higher-income patients to obtain treatment while lower-income citizens cannot; the resulting health gradient mirrors the very disparities basic healthcare systems are designed to close. Extending coverage flattens that gradient at the point of service rather than through later social remediation.
[P1.4] Cost objections ignore downstream savings. Successful IVF reduces lifetime mental-health expenditures and supports workforce participation by parents who would otherwise exit employment. Aging populations gain from births that occur earlier and within stable households, easing pension and elder-care pressures that already dominate budgets. These mechanisms operate on the same fiscal logic that justifies covering contraception or prenatal care.
[P1.5] The decisive question is therefore not whether resources are finite, but why this single, definable medical condition alone should be carved out when its treatment restores normal physiological capacity.

[C1.1] The word doing all the work in this motion is "basic," and it cannot bear the weight. Basic healthcare is the floor every country guarantees before anything else: vaccination, emergency care, maternal health, dialysis, treatment for infection and injury. Calling something basic is not a compliment — it is a triage decision that puts it ahead of everything labeled non-basic in every budget fight that follows. IVF does not belong in that category, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, IVF does not treat the condition. A woman with blocked tubes who completes IVF still has blocked tubes; a man with low sperm count remains exactly as infertile. IVF circumvents pathology to produce a third person. That makes it categorically unlike dialysis or joint replacement, which restore the patient's own function.
[C1.3] It is closer to assisted reproduction as a service than to therapy — a legitimate service, but not the restoration of "normal physiological capacity" claimed in [P1.1].
[C1.4] Second, the motion binds all countries. A single IVF cycle costs thousands of dollars and frequently fails, requiring multiple attempts. In a health system where children still die of pneumonia and mothers of hemorrhage, declaring IVF "basic" is not generosity — it is a demand that scarce clinicians, embryology labs, and funds be diverted from interventions that save lives to one that, per cycle, more often than not produces no child at all.
[C1.5] Third, basic healthcare implies an entitlement that medicine cannot honor here. No one can be guaranteed a baby. A "basic" right to open-ended cycles invites limitless claims on public funds chasing diminishing success rates, especially at older ages.
[C1.6] None of this requires indifference. Countries can subsidize IVF, regulate prices, and offer means-tested support — as many already do — without the false promise of the "basic" label. The question is not whether infertility is real suffering. It is whether IVF should sit on the same shelf as insulin.
[C1.7] It should not.

[P2.1] The opponent's distinction between restoration and circumvention collapses under any consistent medical standard. Blocked fallopian tubes are a structural defect; IVF bypasses that defect to achieve the same functional end—pregnancy—that surgery or medication would pursue in other organs. Dialysis likewise does not repair kidney tissue yet counts as basic care because it restores the outcome the patient needs. The logic that labels one intervention therapy and the other a service is simply an ad-hoc carve-out.
[P2.2] The triage claim also overreaches. Declaring IVF basic does not require every system to fund unlimited cycles ahead of emergency care; it requires only that infertility not be singled out for exclusion once clinical criteria are met. Countries already make such priority decisions inside covered categories—limiting sessions of physical therapy, for example—without removing the category from the basic tier. The motion therefore does not force the false choice between pneumonia vaccines and embryo transfer.
[P2.3] Finally, the guarantee objection misstates what basic coverage provides. No insured treatment promises success; antidepressants, chemotherapy, and joint replacements all carry failure rates and repeated use. Coverage decisions turn on whether the condition is recognized as medical and whether the intervention addresses it, not on guaranteed results. By that measure infertility belongs inside the floor, not outside it.
