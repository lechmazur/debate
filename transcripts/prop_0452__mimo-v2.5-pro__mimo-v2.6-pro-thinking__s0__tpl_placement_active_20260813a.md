[P1.1] India stands at a decisive moment. It has the youngest major workforce on the planet, hundreds of millions entering labor markets in the coming two decades, and an urgent need to build the industrial base that can employ them. The proposition before us is not whether coal is desirable in the abstract—it is whether India should shut down functioning coal capacity on climate timelines if doing so undermines the manufacturing surge the country desperately needs. I argue it should not.
[P1.2] Manufacturing is the proven ladder out of mass poverty. Every nation that has moved large populations from subsistence to middle-class security—Britain, the United States, Japan, South Korea, China—did so through industrialization powered by reliable, affordable energy. India's electricity grid must support factories running around the clock: steel mills, chemical plants, cement kilns, electronics assembly. These loads demand baseload power that intermittent renewables cannot yet guarantee at scale.
[P1.3] India's existing coal fleet generates roughly three-quarters of its electricity. Prematurely retiring those assets before replacement capacity is firm and online risks blackouts, curtailed production, and the collapse of investor confidence in industrial corridors the nation is only now building.

[P1.5] Developed nations that industrialized on coal for over a century are in no moral position to demand India absorb that sacrifice.
[P1.6] This is not an argument for building new coal indefinitely. It is an argument for sequencing: use what you have to build the economic engine, then fund and manage the transition from a position of strength rather than poverty.
