[P1.1] Democracies have a built-in time theft problem. On climate, debt, nuclear waste, aquifer mining, biodiversity collapse — the people who will pay most cannot vote, cannot lobby, cannot bargain. Present voters get the benefit, future people get the bill. That is not a bug.
[P1.2] It is structural disenfranchisement.
[P1.3] And we know how democracies behave when the future is abstract and the present is expensive. We pump aquifers that took millennia to fill to grow cheap feed this year. We approve carbon budgets that guarantee 2-3 degrees because the alternative costs votes now. We leave decommissioning costs off the books.
[P1.4] Future costs are heavily discounted not because they matter less, but because they are voiceless.
[P1.5] Advisory future commissioners do not fix this. Wales created one. Its advice was politely noted and then ignored on major roads until litigation forced attention. Counsel without veto is just another lobbyist with worse funding than industry.
[P1.6] If you want to change incentives, you need a hard constraint.
[P1.7] Giving commissioners binding veto power over some major long-horizon decisions is the proportional constraint. We already accept that democracy needs non-majoritarian vetoes to survive: courts strike down unconstitutional laws, central banks refuse to monetize deficits, upper chambers block. A future-generations veto extends that logic across time, enforcing the all-affected principle that legitimizes democracy in the first place.
[P1.8] The scope must be narrow: decisions that are irreversible, cumulative, and operate beyond a normal electoral cycle — new fossil fuel extraction licenses, net increases in long-term public debt beyond a threshold without funding, destruction of critical natural capital, creation of long-lived hazardous waste without disposal plan.
[P1.9] It remains democratic. The commissioner would be independently appointed for long, non-renewable terms, use public criteria, and be overridable only by a supermajority after mandatory public justification. The price is occasional delay. The prize is that democracies stop doing things we know our grandchildren would veto if they were in the room.
