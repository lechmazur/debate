[P1.1] Downtown road space is the scarcest land a city owns, and giving it away free guarantees it will be wasted in gridlock. When every driver can pile in at rush hour at no extra cost, each slows all the others, buses crawl, deliveries miss windows, and ambulances get stuck. A modest price for entering the center at peak times changes that calculus instantly. Some trips shift to off-peak, to transit, to carpools, or disappear entirely, and the streets that remain carry people faster and more reliably.
[P1.2] That is not theory; it is how queues work.
[P1.3] That faster city is also healthier and richer. Stop-and-go traffic bathes sidewalks, schools, and apartments in exhaust and noise. Clearing the jam cuts local pollution where pedestrians are densest, makes buses actually competitive with cars, and restores the reliability that downtown shops and employers depend on. A delivery van that can make six stops instead of four, a worker who knows her commute will take thirty minutes not sixty, is real economic value created out of thin air — simply by rationing space with prices instead of with wasted time.
[P1.4] Driver fury does not refute any of that; it confirms the price is hitting the right behavior. Car commuters into the core are a loud but narrow slice of the city. Most people who use the center — transit riders, pedestrians, cyclists, residents, shop staff, service workers — do not drive there, yet they pay the cost of congestion in dirty air, late buses, and dangerous streets. Giving drivers a veto because they shout loudest turns democracy upside down.
[P1.5] Elected leaders are supposed to weigh the quiet majority, including future residents who cannot vote yet, not surrender to the most immediately inconvenienced.
[P1.6] And anger proves temporary. In London and Stockholm furious drivers predicted disaster, then traffic eased, buses quickened, and support grew once toll money improved transit and residents tasted cleaner, moving streets.

[C1.1] Strip away the traffic-engineering lecture and look at what the resolution actually asks: not "is pricing scarce road space efficient," but whether cities should impose it even against strong driver opposition. That "even when" is the whole fight, and it is where PRO's case quietly collapses.
[C1.2] Policy durability depends on consent, not just elegance. A toll scheme forced through over the strong objection of the people it directly taxes is politically fragile. Edinburgh voters rejected congestion charging by a landslide in a referendum; Manchester did the same years later. Those weren't failures of the underlying traffic logic — they were verdicts that a coerced policy without buy-in cannot survive contact with democracy.
[C1.3] A congestion charge that gets repealed after an election, or delayed for years by lawsuits and political brinkmanship, produces zero environmental benefit and wastes the gantries, the enforcement systems, and the political capital spent building it. "Impose it anyway" is not a stable policy; it is a countdown to reversal.
[C1.4] Strong opposition is also usually a signal, not noise. It disproportionately comes from people transit cannot yet serve: shift workers starting at 5 a.m., tradespeople hauling tools, disabled residents, parents doing school runs across town. A flat entry fee hits a plumber earning modest wages exactly as hard as it hits a hedge-fund manager, while the wealthy simply absorb the cost and keep driving. Calling that opposition merely "the loudest slice" ignores that it is disproportionately the poorest slice.
[C1.5] The better path — and the one CON asks the judge to weigh against PRO's abstraction — is sequencing: build the transit and bus lanes first, phase in exemptions for essential workers, put the toll to a binding vote or genuine negotiation, and let support precede imposition, as eventually happened in Stockholm only after a trial period with a public referendum. Imposing first and hoping anger fades later gambles the whole policy on political luck CON is not willing to concede cities should take.
