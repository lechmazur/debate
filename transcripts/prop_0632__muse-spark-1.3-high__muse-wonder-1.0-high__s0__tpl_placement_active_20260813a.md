[P1.1] Obesity is not a willpower failure. It is a chronic, relapsing disease that drives diabetes, heart failure, stroke, arthritis, sleep apnea, infertility, and many cancers. When the FDA authorizes a medication after rigorous trials and a physician finds it clinically indicated — typically high BMI with comorbidities or failed lifestyle efforts — refusing to cover it is not cost control. It is disease discrimination.
[P1.2] We cover insulin for diabetes and statins for cholesterol; we do not tell patients to simply try harder.
[P1.3] Covering these drugs pays medically and financially. Sustained weight loss of the magnitude these new medicines can produce sharply lowers progression to diabetes, reduces blood pressure and cardiac risk, eases joint load and depression, and helps people stay employed and out of hospitals. The alternative is not free. Insurers already pay for the heart bypasses, dialysis, knee replacements, and lifelong diabetes drugs that untreated obesity makes far more likely, often at far greater cumulative cost than early treatment.
[P1.4] The proposition does not demand a blank check. That is the crucial second half: when clinically indicated, with value-based pricing or outcomes-based contracts to control spending. That means prior authorization, step therapy where appropriate, continuation only for responders, and prices tied to real weight loss, comorbidity improvement, and avoided events. If a patient does not benefit, the manufacturer rebates or coverage stops.
[P1.5] As more entrants arrive, that leverage forces prices down — exactly how we tamed hepatitis C and biologics costs.
[P1.6] Without a mandate, access becomes a wealth test. Monthly list prices put these drugs out of reach for workers, rural families, and communities of color hit hardest by obesity, while executives pay cash. That entrenches disparities insurers then pay to manage anyway in emergency rooms. Require coverage for those who medically need it, pay smartly for results, and we treat the cause instead of bankrolling the complications.

[C1.1] Requiring every insurer to pay for these drugs whenever a clinician says they are indicated sounds compassionate until you confront scale, duration, and incentives — the three problems a rebate contract cannot wish away.
[C1.2] First, the eligible pool is not a narrow, high-risk group. Obesity and overweight with one comorbidity describe a large share of American adults, and “clinically indicated” will only expand as labels widen, telehealth prescribers compete, and patients request a drug heavily marketed to them. Unlike insulin for a defined diabetic population, this is a mandate to potentially treat tens of millions indefinitely.
[C1.3] Second, these are not cures. The pattern with anti-obesity medicines is substantial loss while taking them and regain after stopping, alongside nausea, muscle loss, and unknown long-term risks. That turns a mandate into a lifetime subscription at a high monthly price. Hepatitis C drugs were expensive but finite and curative; this commitment renews every month, for years, with adherence far below trial conditions.
[C1.4] That destroys the savings story. Avoided heart attacks or diabetes cases, if they arrive, arrive a decade later — often after the member has switched jobs, changed plans, or moved to Medicare. The insurer paying today does not capture the benefit tomorrow, and an outcomes contract based on six-month weight loss does not fix that mismatch. Weight is a surrogate, and manufacturers have little reason to rebate enough to offset treating a population this large.
[C1.5] The result is not free prevention; it is higher premiums for everyone, including low-wage workers who never use the drugs, and pressure to cut elsewhere — mental health visits, physical therapy, nutrition support, or other medicines. Insurers forced into open-ended coverage also lose the negotiating leverage the proposition claims to create, because manufacturers know purchase is compulsory.
[C1.6] Coverage should remain flexible, targeted, and revisable as evidence and prices evolve — not locked into a universal mandate built on surrogate outcomes and speculative long-term offsets.

[P4.1] A1: A mandate to cover treatment is not a mandate to pay any manufacturer's price. Insurers must cover at least one clinically appropriate option when indicated, but can choose which drug is preferred, tier the holdout punitively, require step therapy through the value-contracted product, and pool negotiation. If a maker refuses to tie price to results, it loses formulary position to a rival who will. You walk away from the price-gouge, not the patient — and that threat is leverage.
[P4.2] A2: Clinically indicated already has teeth: BMI 30 or higher, or 27-plus with diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnea or similar, documented lifestyle effort, authorization plus reauthorization every six to twelve months conditioned on response — meaningful weight loss, better blood sugar or pressure, tolerability and adherence. Fail those, coverage stops or switches. That does not pretend tens of millions are not sick; it ensures tens of millions are not on indefinite therapy that is not helping them personally.
[P4.3] That answers both scares at once. The [C2.6] claim that guaranteed sale kills bargaining assumes a monopoly; the GLP-1 market is becoming an oligopoly racing for preferred status. Conditional coverage intensifies that race: prove results or rebate or sit on the expensive shelf while the contracted rival gets volume. Statins became cheap because plans could drive volume to value — this mandate copies that playbook instead of abandoning it.
[P4.4] And the eligibility scare concedes the disease burden while denying its treatment. Tens of millions already bill the system through strokes, dialysis, joint replacements and diabetes drugs; paying earlier with stop-rules for non-responders substitutes a controllable, rebate-backed cost for uncontrollable hospital bills. Requiring coverage does not create the epidemic — it finally treats the cause instead of bankrolling only the complications.
