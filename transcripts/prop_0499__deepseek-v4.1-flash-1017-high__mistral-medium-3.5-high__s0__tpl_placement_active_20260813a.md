[P1.1] The ocean floor is the largest unexplored part of our own planet. We have coarse maps of Mars and the Moon, but large stretches of Earth’s seafloor remain unmeasured at the resolution needed to understand the systems that shape coasts, climates, earthquakes, and the deep biosphere. That is not a curiosity gap; it is a public-goods failure. Governments fund exploration where private markets won’t, because the returns—hazard prediction, basic science, sovereign knowledge, technology—are spread across society and arrive over decades.
[P1.2] The proposition is proportionate, not romantic. Space exploration gets serious, sustained public money because it expands humanity’s frontier and builds capability. The deep ocean is a frontier with more immediate stakes: it drives tsunamis and earthquakes, hosts ecosystems we barely know, and stores carbon and minerals whose future governance depends on knowing what is there. If we can justify comparable public investment in exploring distant worlds, we can justify it for the world whose changes hit us directly.
[P1.3] And crewed exploration belongs in that program. Robots are indispensable, but they are narrow: tethered, weather-limited, dependent on surface decisions, poor at improvisation and delicate sample handling. A human presence—even small, specialized, and supported by autonomous systems—can adapt in real time, make unexpected observations, and anchor a public constituency. The Apollo and submersible programs show that exploration infrastructure spills over into robotics, materials, communications, and inspiration.
[P1.4] Crewed deep ocean is not tourism; it is a capability multiplier.
[P1.5] The real comparison is not “ocean versus space” as if one must lose. It is whether governments will treat the deep ocean as a serious long-term frontier or as a leftover. Most of our seafloor is unmapped, the stakes are planetary, and the current investment is not commensurate. Comparable funding is how a society says this unknown matters.
