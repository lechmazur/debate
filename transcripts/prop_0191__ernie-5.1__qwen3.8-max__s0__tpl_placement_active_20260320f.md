The fundamental promise of a clinical trial is that what works in the lab will work in the clinic. When trials enroll overwhelmingly young, white, male volunteers, that promise is broken for everyone else. [P1.2] Consider what we already know. Women have historically been excluded from cardiovascular trials, yet heart disease kills more women than men. Dosages calibrated on male bodies leave women over- or under-dosed. [P1.3] These are not theoretical risks—they are documented patterns that current trial design perpetuates.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a statistical mirror for medical evidence. A trial’s job is not to reproduce the census; it is to answer whether a drug is safe and effective for the patients who will receive it, with enough power to detect real harms. Mandatory proportionate quotas across age, sex, and ethnicity would turn recruitment into an engineering problem and make delay an acceptable price.
[C1.2] But delayed approvals are not neutral. They are measured in patients who die waiting, in diseases that progress while sites struggle to hit demographic targets, and in therapies that could have helped the very groups the motion claims to protect.
[C1.3] This burden is heaviest where the need is most urgent. Oncology, rare diseases, and emerging infections often have small eligible populations, limited trial sites, and narrow treatment windows. A pediatric leukemia drug or a therapy for a disease concentrated in one community cannot always be forced into age, sex, and ethnic proportion without years of additional enrollment. If the drug shows clear benefit, withholding it from all patients because one subgroup is under-enrolled is not caution; it is a preventable loss.
[C1.4] The same is true for sex-specific cancers or diseases with skewed epidemiology. Proportionate representation as a universal mandate ignores the biology it purports to respect.
[C1.5] The better standard is targeted representation, not proportional quotas. Require enrollment plans, prespecified subgroup analyses, pharmacokinetic data, and post-market commitments where uncertainty remains. Use adaptive designs and real-world evidence to fill gaps without freezing access. Where women or specific ethnic groups face distinct risks, the remedy is stratified dosing and targeted safety monitoring, not withholding therapies that may benefit everyone.
[C1.6] Delay is not a harmless procedural tax. The question is whether a rigid quota, enforced through slower approval, saves more lives than timely access plus rigorous follow-up. It does not.
