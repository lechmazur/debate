[P1.1] A tourist tax should repair the pressures tourism creates before it becomes just another revenue stream. In overcrowded cities, those pressures are concrete: homes converted into visitor accommodation, buses and pavements packed beyond capacity, and extraordinary demands for waste collection and public toilets. Visitors need not bear the entire cost of urban life, but it is reasonable to ask them to pay a visible share of the burden their visit adds.
[P1.2] General-budget absorption breaks that link. Revenue disappears into a pool where it can just as easily finance unrelated priorities, while the affected neighborhood still waits for a repaired sewer, an affordable apartment, or a reliable bus. Residents then experience tourism as higher rents, congestion, and filth, with no credible guarantee that its proceeds return to them. That is not merely unfair; it erodes public consent for the visitor economy on which these cities depend.
[P1.3] Earmarking creates a disciplined feedback loop: the activity imposing the costs funds the systems absorbing them. Housing spending can preserve or create genuinely affordable homes and counter displacement near heavily visited districts. Transit spending can add capacity and maintain services used by residents and visitors alike. Sanitation spending can meet peak loads without forcing year-round taxpayers to subsidize every festival, weekend surge, or cruise arrival.
[P1.4] The result is both mitigation and protection of tourism’s long-term social license.
[P1.5] The likely objection is fiscal flexibility. But the motion says **primarily**, not exclusively: cities retain other revenue, general funds, and discretion over projects within these broad categories. Nor must essential services depend on volatile tourism receipts; cities can maintain baseline funding and use reserves or multi-year plans. Flexibility without a binding priority too often means that politically quiet, geographically concentrated harms remain unfunded.
[P1.6] The choice is therefore not between thoughtful budgeting and irrational rigidity. It is between a credible commitment that tourist revenue first repairs tourism’s footprint, and a promise easily diluted after collection. [C1.1] The motion mistakes a source of money for a good spending rule. Tourist taxes are citywide revenue; the relevant question is whether a dollar’s use improves residents’ welfare more than the alternatives. A general budget does not make revenue “lost.” It subjects every use to comparison with housing, transit, sanitation, safety, health, and other needs. Earmarking three broad categories guarantees their priority before anyone establishes that they are the best use of each marginal dollar.
[C1.2] Earmarking can weaken accountability rather than strengthen it. Money is easiest to defend because it has a label, not because it works. A transit line may be overbuilt for seasonal crowds; a sanitation contract may lock in permanent staff for temporary surges; housing subsidies may be captured by landlords or push rents upward. When projects fail, an earmark protects the spending, not residents.
[C1.3] General budgeting can cancel ineffective schemes and redirect funds.
[C1.4] Revenue volatility strengthens this case. Tourist receipts can fall abruptly, but earmarked payrolls, debt service, and contracts continue. PRO’s suggestion of reserves and multiyear plans concedes that the general budget must backstop the earmark. Once backstopping is necessary, the earmark offers little discipline while retaining a rigid claim on future funds.
[C1.5] A flexible budget can use stable revenues for recurring costs and tourist receipts for one-time improvements.
[C1.6] General budgeting is not indifference to tourism’s footprint. It can direct more money to burdened districts, affordable housing, transit capacity, and sanitation when evidence shows those uses outperform alternatives. It can also fund enforcement, public space, worker protections, or health services—tourism-related costs omitted by the motion. Indeed, guaranteed earmarked revenue can encourage cities to tolerate more visitors because every arrival appears to finance a benefit.
[C1.7] Visitors may rightly contribute to city life, but contribution does not imply a constitutional spending category. A general fund can match local needs with effective responses and lets an unsuccessful tax be reviewed or sunset. The destination should be accountable allocation, not a promise to spend first and learn later.

[P2.1] The opposition’s case depends on an ideal budget process: every dollar compared, every project tested, every failure corrected. But overcrowding is not an ideal budgeting problem. Its costs are concentrated in particular neighborhoods, while tourist revenue is politically attractive and easily redirected elsewhere. A general fund can repair the damage; an earmark makes repair the established priority rather than one competitor among louder claims.
[P2.2] Nothing in the motion requires spending “first and learning later.” Earmarking selects a destination, not a bad project. Cities can still compare housing programs, reject an overbuilt transit line, use seasonal sanitation contracts, audit outcomes, and cancel ineffective schemes. If a housing subsidy merely enriches landlords, that is an argument for better program design—not for sending the money to an unrelated general expense. General budgeting is equally capable of funding wasteful projects; it simply adds the risk that tourism’s victims never enter the winning coalition.
[P2.3] Volatility also proves much less than claimed. A city can preserve baseline service funding from stable revenues and devote tourist receipts to capital improvements, peak-season provision, and multi-year commitments with reserves. The need for a backstop does not make a priority meaningless: insurance does not make careful budgeting pointless. And using receipts for one-time improvements is fully compatible with earmarking them primarily for resident housing, transit, and sanitation.
[P2.4] The appeal to omitted needs—enforcement, public space, worker protections—ignores “primarily.” The motion leaves room for other spending; it prevents those exceptions from swallowing the rule. Meanwhile, transit and sanitation projects can themselves include enforcement and public-space benefits. Categories are broad, but the commitment is clear.
[P2.5] Finally, the suggestion that earmarks encourage cities to tolerate excessive visitors applies at least as strongly to unrestricted revenue: a city eager for general funds has the same incentive to monetize congestion, with fewer guarantees to residents. The decisive comparison is not flexibility versus paralysis. It is conditional goodwill versus an enforceable priority, with project-level flexibility retained. When tourism systematically burdens homes, mobility, and cleanliness, its revenues should first restore those foundations of urban life.

If receipts fall after a city borrows for transit upgrades or hires permanent sanitation staff, earmarking makes those commitments harder to unwind.
