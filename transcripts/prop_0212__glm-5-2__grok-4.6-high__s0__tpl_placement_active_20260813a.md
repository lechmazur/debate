[P1.1] The proposition already answers the strongest objection against it. "Once immediate security concerns have passed" means this debate is not about exposing active intelligence networks or endangering current operatives. It is about what happens to records of state violence—colonial administration, wartime occupation, secret-police surveillance—when the only thing left to protect is the state's reputation.
[P1.2] Families have a moral claim that does not expire. A daughter whose father was disappeared by a military junta, a grandson researching whether colonial authorities seized his family's land, a citizen whose name appears in a secret-police informant register—these people are not asking for favors. They are asking for information about their own lives, held by their own governments, paid for by their own taxes. Withholding such records indefinitely is not security policy; it is the perpetuation of harm by other means.
[P1.3] The institutional case is equally strong. Germany's opening of Stasi files gave citizens the ability to confront their own past and rebuild trust in public institutions. Latin American truth commissions, where they gained access to military and intelligence records, produced findings that helped prevent the re-normalization of state terror. Where archives stay sealed—in many post-colonial settings, in former Soviet states, in countries with unfinished reckonings—the result is fertile ground for denialism, conspiracy, and recurring abuse.
[P1.4] The pattern is clear: states that open their archives grudgingly do so only when the risk of accountability has already faded. That is exactly backward. The value of disclosure comes precisely when it costs the state something—when officials fear embarrassment, when institutions fear reform, when the convenient myth of a clean national history comes under pressure.
[P1.5] The motion sets the right threshold: not reckless disclosure, but broad access after genuine security concerns have passed. What remains is a choice between a citizen's right to truth and a state's interest in managing its image. That choice is not close.
