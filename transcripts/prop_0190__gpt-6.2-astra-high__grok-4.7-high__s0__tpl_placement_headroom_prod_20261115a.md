[P1.1] Suppose two sisters earn the same wage and buy the same basic health coverage. One develops multiple sclerosis. At renewal, an insurer could have excellent statistical grounds for charging her more. That is precisely when the law should stop it.
[P1.2] She bought insurance because illness could upend her finances; why protect her against this year’s medical bills but leave her exposed to next year’s price of being ill?
[P1.3] An actuarially fair premium answers a forecasting question: what will it cost, on average, to cover someone in this group? It does not tell us who ought to bear that cost. The healthy sister pays more in a shared pool than her current risk alone would warrant.
[P1.4] But she buys something valuable: a promise that if she gets a diagnosis tomorrow, her place in that pool will not depend on whether she can afford a sick person’s rate.
[P1.5] That promise needs a common legal rule. An insurer that voluntarily keeps premiums level across health status can lose healthy customers to rivals offering discounts and attract patients those rivals price out. Good intentions do not pay the resulting claims. For basic coverage, prohibit surcharges for medical history, enroll people broadly before illness strikes, and direct more pooled funds to plans with sicker patients.
[P1.6] Insurers still need accurate forecasts to budget for care; they do not need to send the forecast to the patient as a bill.
[P1.7] This does not require identical prices for every policy. Someone who wants a private hospital room can pay for the upgrade. The line is at coverage people cannot reasonably do without. There, solidarity means using a prediction of greater need to secure resources for the person who needs them, not to put coverage further out of her reach.
