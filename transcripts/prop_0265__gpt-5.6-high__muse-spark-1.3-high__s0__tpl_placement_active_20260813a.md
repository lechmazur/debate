[P1.1] A price should answer the customer’s simplest question: “What must I pay?” If a dish is displayed as $20 but a mandatory 20 percent service charge makes it $24, then $24 is the economically real price. Calling four dollars a “charge” rather than part of the price does not change the customer’s obligation. Restaurants should therefore advertise the amount they actually require, while remaining free to show the breakdown underneath.
[P1.2] All-in pricing protects meaningful choice. Diners compare menus before committing—often across restaurants with different fee structures. One venue may list a meal at $20 with a mandatory charge; another may list it honestly at $23 without one. Hiding the charge makes the first appear cheaper even when it is more expensive.
[P1.3] Consumers then choose using distorted information, and transparent businesses are punished for honesty. Including mandatory charges restores price competition: restaurants must compete on the real cost, not on who can move the largest fraction of that cost into fine print or a late checkout screen.
[P1.4] Late disclosure also exploits the moment when refusal is hardest. At a physical restaurant, the food has already been ordered and consumed before the bill arrives. Online, customers may have spent time assembling an order before checkout. They can technically object, but walking away now carries far more inconvenience than rejecting a clearly displayed price at the start.
[P1.5] Genuine consent requires disclosure before commitment, not after sunk costs and social pressure arise.
[P1.6] This rule does not prohibit service charges, dictate how revenue is distributed, or conceal worker compensation. A menu can say, “$24 total, including a $4 service charge.” Nor does it prevent different prices for clearly defined circumstances, such as large parties; the applicable all-in price can be displayed for that condition. Voluntary tips remain voluntary and need not be included.
[P1.7] The principle is modest: mandatory means unavoidable, and unavoidable means part of the price. Transparent prices improve consumer autonomy, reward honest restaurants, and make competition turn on value rather than surprise.
