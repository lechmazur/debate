[P1.1] **The Deterrence Paradox: How Ambiguity Invites the War We Fear**
[P1.2] My opponent will argue that committing to defend Taiwan risks a catastrophic war with China. But this framing gets the logic of great-power confrontation exactly backwards. It is strategic *ambiguity*—the refusal to clearly commit—that makes conflict more likely, not less. A clear, credible commitment to Taiwan's defense is the most reliable path to preserving peace across the Taiwan Strait.
[P1.3] The core mechanism is simple. China is currently weighing the costs and benefits of a military operation against Taiwan. If Beijing believes Washington might intervene, but also might not, it faces an incentive to gamble—particularly during moments of perceived American distraction. Ambiguity doesn't create caution; it creates opportunity.
[P1.4] By contrast, a firm commitment raises the expected cost of invasion high enough to make aggression irrational. This is the basic logic of deterrence that kept the Cold War from turning hot in Europe. NATO's explicit Article 5 commitment didn't cause World War III; it prevented it.
[P1.5] The stakes extend far beyond Taiwan's twenty-three million people. Taiwan produces the overwhelming majority of the world's most advanced semiconductors—the chips that power everything from smartphones to fighter jets. A Chinese seizure of those fabrication facilities would hand Beijing coercive leverage over the entire global economy and cripple American military modernization, which depends on those very supply chains. This is not a distant regional squabble; it is a direct threat to American economic and national security.
[P1.6] Furthermore, abandoning Taiwan would shatter the credibility of American commitments across the Indo-Pacific. Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, and Australia have structured their security around the assumption that the United States will stand firm. If that assumption collapses, allies will either accommodate China or pursue independent nuclear arsenals—both outcomes that make the world far more dangerous.
[P1.7] The risk of war is real. But the greater risk is communicating to Beijing that aggression will be met with hesitation. Deterrence works only when the commitment behind it is unmistakable.
