[P1.1] The question before us is not whether these animals are human—clearly they are not—but whether our legal categories must remain fixed to a single species, or whether they can finally reflect what science has already revealed. The proposition asks us to extend basic rights to non-human persons: great apes, cetaceans, and elephants. I will argue we must, and I will do so on the strongest possible ground.
[P1.2] The traditional boundary of legal personhood has never been biological continuity. We grant personhood to corporations, which have no brain, no feelings, no lifespan. We have consistently expanded the circle of moral consideration—historically, persons once meant white male property owners. The question is whether the expansion is justified, and for these three groups, the evidence is overwhelming.
[P1.3] Consider what we know. Elephants grieve their dead, returning to bones and gently touching them with their trunks—a behavior requiring memory, emotional processing, and something resembling mourning. Bottlenose dolphins and other cetaceans have distinct names—signature whistles—that function as identities, passed culturally across generations. Great apes use tools, teach skills to offspring, and demonstrate self-recognition in mirrors, a marker of self-awareness that even human infants lack until around eighteen months.
[P1.4] These are not party tricks or mechanical responses. They represent complex cognition, sustained social bonds, and the capacity to suffer. Captivity demonstrably harms them: stereotypic behaviors, shortened lifespans, psychological breakdown. The question is not whether they think like us—none of us thinks exactly like any other—but whether they are beings with interests that can be harmed and satisfied.
[P1.5] They are.
[P1.6] Legal recognition would not grant these animals the right to vote or sign contracts. It would mean what it means for any person: that their basic interests matter and cannot be dismissed simply because a more powerful entity finds it convenient. When we cage a cognitively sophisticated, emotionally complex being who mourns, plays, loves, and understands, we are not exercising legitimate authority. We are committing an injustice.
[P1.7] This debate asks whether we will name that injustice and end it.

[C1.2] It is not, and it would actively harm the animals it claims to protect.
[C1.3] First, personhood is the wrong instrument. Legal persons hold rights because they can hold correlative duties or be represented within a system of mutual obligation. A chimpanzee cannot be sued, cannot consent, cannot instruct counsel. So in practice, "ape rights" means activist organizations litigating in the animal's name—rights exercised entirely by humans who claim to speak for beings that cannot affirm or reject that representation.
[C1.4] That isn't liberation; it's a transfer of power to self-appointed guardians, with courts forced to adjudicate what a dolphin "wants."
[C1.5] Second, a categorical right against captivity is a conservation catastrophe. Captive breeding kept the California condor from extinction; sanctuaries hold elephants who cannot survive poaching-ridden ranges; rescued dolphins who can't be released would have to be—what? Released to die, to honor their rights? The proposition's absolutism forbids exactly the protective custody that endangered species increasingly require.
[C1.6] Third, the line is arbitrary. Pigs rival chimps on many cognitive measures; corvids use tools; octopuses solve puzzles. If sophisticated cognition grounds personhood, the proposition either proves far too much—unraveling agriculture, research, and pest control overnight—or it's an aesthetic preference for charismatic megafauna dressed up as principle.
[C1.7] The honest path is strengthening welfare law: banning cruel captivity conditions, ending circus exploitation, mandating habitat protection. That targets actual suffering without breaking the concept of personhood or sacrificing endangered animals to a slogan. Demand that the affirmative show why personhood—not protection—is necessary.
