[P1.1] Meat is not cheap; it is underpriced. The supermarket sticker excludes the methane warming the climate, the manure fouling rivers, the antibiotics breeding resistant bacteria, and the public health costs of diets high in red and processed meat. Those costs do not disappear. They are shifted onto everyone else, and they land hardest on people with the least money and the least healthcare.
[P1.2] The question is not whether honest pricing feels uncomfortable. It is about whether we keep sending the bill to those least able to pay.
[P1.3] Start with the externality. Livestock production drives greenhouse gases, land conversion, water pollution, and biodiversity loss, while industrial systems concentrate waste and antibiotics in ways that threaten nearby communities and global health. Lower-income households are not insulated. They are more likely to live near concentrated feeding operations, breathe polluted air, drink contaminated water, and suffer first from heat, floods, and food-system shocks.
[P1.4] Cheap meat looks like a subsidy for the poor; in reality it is a hidden tax they pay in asthma, diabetes, dirty water, and climate damage.
[P1.5] So yes—taxing production at its full externality will raise prices for lower-income shoppers. That is a real cost, and the motion accepts it. But the alternative is worse: an unpriced tax paid in worse health and a destabilized climate. The honest comparison is not cheap food versus expensive food.
[P1.6] It is visible prices now versus invisible, compounding costs later.
[P1.7] A production tax is the right instrument. It corrects incentives at the source—feedlots, processors, manure systems—where pollution is measurable and producers can innovate: better feed, manure capture, efficiency, alternative proteins. Revenue can be returned progressively through dividends, food assistance, or cheaper legumes, vegetables, and preventive care. Even if prices rise, the policy is justified: the hidden costs they bear are larger than the price rise they would face, and the poor gain most when the system stops offloading costs onto them.

[C1.8] Cross-border meat then undercuts the environmental gain while domestic food still gets costlier.
