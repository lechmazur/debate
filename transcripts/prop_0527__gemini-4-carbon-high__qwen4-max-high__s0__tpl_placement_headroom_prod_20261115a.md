[P1.1] New antibiotics face a lethal economic paradox: the better they defeat superbugs, the less doctors dare prescribe them. To slow bacterial evolution, clinicians must lock novel antibiotics away like fire extinguishers, using them only when older generics fail. In ordinary drug markets, near-zero sales volume guarantees bankruptcy, which is why biotechnology firms have repeatedly collapsed right after winning regulatory approval for life-saving antibiotics. Advance market commitments fix this broken market by guaranteeing innovators viable returns once an effective drug clears approval, preserving the pipeline that keeps routine surgeries, organ transplants, and chemotherapy safe.
[P1.2] Countries should not prohibit these commitments simply because per-dose prices remain high. Judging reserve antibiotics like everyday cholesterol pills misunderstands infection control. When novel antibiotics carry cheap unit prices, overburdened hospitals face little friction against prescribing them casually as a precaution, accelerating resistance and ruining a finite public resource. High unit prices reinforce clinical stewardship, prompting pharmacies to verify resistance before opening the vault.
[P1.3] Moreover, demanding rock-bottom unit prices kills deal viability. No single country’s commitment covers global research costs alone, and mandatory price cuts trigger international reference-pricing penalties elsewhere, whereas revenue-guarantee commitments allow unit sales to offset public payouts.
[P1.4] Finally, high unit prices do not create runaway budgets. Because stewardship keeps patient volumes tiny and treatment courses last days rather than lifetimes, total spending remains modest and predictable. Paying a premium per course for the small fraction of intensive-care patients battling pan-resistant infections costs far less than weeks of ventilator support, isolation wards, amputations, or preventable deaths. Refusing to permit advance market commitments over unit-price sticker shock trades away real survival for an illusory discount.
[P1.5] Health systems can easily reimburse hospitals for rare emergency doses, but no country can negotiate a bargain on a medicine that was never invented.
