[P1.1] Public money is not a gift; it is a trust. When a religious school takes taxpayer funding, it stops being a purely private community and becomes a public partner. The motion asks only that this partnership come with the same basic obligations every public school already accepts: teach children a real curriculum, prepare them for citizenship, and don't discriminate with the public's money. That is not hostility to religion.
[P1.2] It is the minimum price of admission to the public treasury.
[P1.3] Three pillars carry this case. First, children's rights come before institutional autonomy. Every child—whoever their parents are—is entitled to literacy, numeracy, science, and civic knowledge sufficient to function as an adult. We have seen what happens without standards: schools in several countries and some U.S. yeshivas have graduated students who can barely read or write in the national language, leaving them trapped, unemployable, and dependent.
[P1.4] The state may tolerate such choices privately; it must never subsidize them.
[P1.5] Second, non-discrimination is a taxpayer-protection principle. A Jewish family, a Muslim family, an atheist family all pay the taxes that fund these schools. Funding a school that would expel their child, or refuse to hire them as a teacher because of who they are, forces citizens to bankroll their own exclusion. That corrodes the legitimacy of the entire public system.
[P1.6] Third—and this is crucial—the motion preserves religious liberty completely. No school is forced to change anything. Schools that want full doctrinal freedom simply decline public funds, as many proudly do today. What religious liberty does not include is a right to other people's money on your own terms.
[P1.7] The Netherlands and much of Europe fund religious schools generously precisely because those schools accept national curricula and inspection; the model demonstrably works.
[P1.8] So the question for this debate is simple: should taxpayers fund schools that refuse public accountability? Every answer CON gives must ultimately defend subsidized discrimination or subsidized educational neglect. Neither is defensible.
