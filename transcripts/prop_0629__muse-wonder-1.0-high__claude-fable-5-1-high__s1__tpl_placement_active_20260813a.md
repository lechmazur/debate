[P1.1] Your phone is a computer, but unlike a laptop, you cannot decide where its software comes from or how you pay for it. Two companies control distribution to billions of users, and they tie that control to their own checkout. That is not a marketplace. It is a toll booth at the entrance to mobile life.
[P1.2] Requiring independent payments and sideloading for most consumer apps breaks that tie without abolishing app stores.
[P1.3] Start with payments. When every purchase, subscription, and tip must run through the platform, developers pay a commission that is often around a fifth to a third of revenue, then pass it on through higher prices, fewer features, or subscriptions pushed off awkward workarounds. A music app cannot simply say: pay us directly and save. That silence protects the platform's cut, not the user.
[P1.4] Allow rival processors and prices will face their first real test: compete on fees, fraud protection, refunds, and convenience, or lose the transaction.
[P1.5] Sideloading does the same for distribution. If a developer is rejected, price-gouged, or copied by the gatekeeper, there is today nowhere else to go on an iPhone and only a deliberately frictional path on many Android devices. Direct downloads, third-party stores, and web installation give creators an outside option. Even users who never sideload benefit, because the threat of exit disciplines review delays, opaque rules, and arbitrary removals.
[P1.6] Security does not require monopoly. Operating systems can still sandbox apps, require permissions, scan known malware, revoke certificates, and warn users before outside installs. Most consumer apps — games, media, productivity, shopping — do not need a single gatekeeper to be safe, any more than your laptop does. Keep strict limits for genuinely dangerous categories, but do not use worst-case risks to lock down ordinary commerce.
[P1.7] Choice here is not chaos; it is the minimum condition for a market to call itself open.

[C1.2] Call that curation what it is: a safety product, and the most successful one in consumer computing. The laptop analogy cuts the wrong way. Laptops are where ransomware, keyloggers, and fake "update your Flash" installers live. Phones became the safe device precisely by refusing that model.
[C1.3] Most real-world phone fraud is not a clever exploit; it is a persuasive person. The grandmother on the phone with a "bank security officer" is told to install a remote-access app. Today, on iOS, that sentence is a dead end. Under the mandate, it becomes a link, a warning dialog, and a tap.
[C1.4] Warnings do not protect the people scams target; they protect the lawyers.
[C1.5] The sideloading "outside option" will not stay optional. Once big developers can steer users off-store with a discount, they will, and users will follow the cheaper price into installers with no review, no sandbox guarantees, and no refund button. The pressure flows downhill onto consumers, not up onto gatekeepers.
[C1.6] Payments are the same story. Platform checkout gives one place to see every subscription, cancel it, dispute it, and set parental limits, without scattering card numbers across hundreds of merchants. Independent processors reintroduce the dark patterns the single checkout eliminated: cancel-by-phone, surprise renewals, chargebacks fought one merchant at a time. And the savings are illusory: commissions fall hardest on the largest developers, small ones already pay reduced rates, and prices track willingness to pay, not costs.
[C1.7] The beneficiaries are Epic, Spotify, and Match. The bill is paid by the users who chose the garden.
