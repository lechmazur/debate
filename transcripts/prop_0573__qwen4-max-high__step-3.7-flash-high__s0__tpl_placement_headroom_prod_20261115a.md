[P1.1] When a platform pays you a share of a pool, your check depends on everyone else's numbers. That is how most streaming royalties work: your streams divided by total streams, times a revenue pool the platform defines. So a creator cannot verify their own payment even in principle — they can see their numerator, never the denominator, never the pool. Any defense of secrecy has to defend that: the party writing the check is the sole witness to the arithmetic.
[P1.2] The word doing the work in this motion is standardized. Voluntary dashboards already exist, and they are useless for accountability precisely because each platform chooses its own definitions — what counts as a view, a stream, a completed listen, which territories count, what gets deducted before the split. When Netflix or Spotify releases a flattering slice of data, that is marketing, not disclosure. Comparable, fixed-format reporting is what turns numbers into something a creator can audit, a lawyer can challenge, or a union can bargain over.
[P1.3] Hollywood's writers and actors spent much of their recent strike on exactly this: residuals from streaming they could not measure. They won a foothold only by shutting down production — an option a solo musician or independent filmmaker does not have.
[P1.4] We already require this everywhere else money is pooled and split. Public companies file standardized audited accounts; box office is reported to third-party trackers; statutory royalty regimes mandate statements. Nobody calls that theft of trade secrets.
[P1.5] And the privacy objection answers itself: the motion asks for viewing and payout data shared with creators, artists, and rights holders — aggregate counts and accounting, not individual users' identities or watch histories.
[P1.6] Secrecy here isn't protecting competition. It's protecting the payer from being checked.
