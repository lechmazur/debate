[P1.1] A concert ticket is not an ordinary financial asset. It is a license to attend a community event, initially distributed through queues, lotteries, and purchase limits precisely because the seller chose not to auction every seat to the richest bidder. Unrestricted resale lets intermediaries overturn that choice and capture a scarcity premium without creating a single additional seat. The artist, venue, and fans bear the frustration; brokers and platforms can profit from the markup and transaction fees.
[P1.2] The requirement is modest and practical: major platforms must either limit resale markups or offer a verified face-value resale channel for most tickets. It does not dictate artists’ initial prices, ban dynamic primary pricing, or make tickets nontransferable. A fan whose plans change can still recover the money paid; a fan searching for a seat gains a route that does not require paying a broker’s toll. “Most tickets” also leaves room for genuinely distinct premium products and exceptional categories, rather than pretending every seat is identical.
[P1.3] The central efficiency objection confuses allocating scarcity with creating value. When demand exceeds supply, some fans miss out under either system. Resale at any price allocates heavily by wealth and by access to automated purchasing; capped or face-value resale preserves exchange while reducing the reward for buying tickets solely to extract that scarcity premium. High willingness to pay is not the same as high enthusiasm, and a market can clear efficiently while producing an unfair, extractive outcome.
[P1.4] This policy also attacks fraud where platforms have unique capability. Major ticketing companies can verify barcodes, cancel and reissue tickets, identify sellers, and return proceeds through a controlled transfer system. A safe official channel protects buyers from duplicate or invalid tickets and reduces the incentive to deploy bots when bulk inventory cannot be flipped for extraordinary gains.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is simple: CON defends higher broker revenue as though it were productive output.
