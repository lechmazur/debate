[P1.1] The climate clock does not wait for sequential permits, stacked lawsuits, and local vetoes dressed up as stewardship. Governments should significantly streamline permitting for renewable energy and transmission even when that trims some local review and litigation pathways, because the environmental cost of delay now dwarfs the environmental cost of a faster process.
[P1.2] Every year a wind farm, solar array, or high-voltage line sits in process is a year gas plants keep burning. The largest environmental harm this century is not a ridgeline turbine; it is unmitigated warming. Transmission is the hinge. Clean generation is often far from cities.
[P1.3] Without new lines, cheap power dies in interconnection queues while dirty power fills the gap. A local process that treats a line as optional convenience is, in practice, a fossil-fuel subsidy.
[P1.4] “Some” reduction is not abolition of protection. Habitat, species, and pollution rules can still bind at national and state levels. What should shrink is duplicative sequential review, unlimited venue-shopping lawsuits, and the power of a few nearby opponents to freeze projects whose climate benefits are regional and global. Local knowledge matters.
[P1.5] A local veto over climate infrastructure is not the same thing.
[P1.6] Litigation has become a strategy, not a safeguard. Delay is the product. Opponents need not win on the merits if they can raise financing risk and run out the clock. That is not environmental democracy.
[P1.7] It is a structural bias toward the energy system we already have.
[P1.8] Keep the thicket, and we get slower decarbonization, higher bills, and continued fossil generation. Streamline, and we trade narrower local process for actually building what the environment now requires. Local nature is not worthless. Climate-scale harm is larger, and the permitting system as written is failing that larger duty.
