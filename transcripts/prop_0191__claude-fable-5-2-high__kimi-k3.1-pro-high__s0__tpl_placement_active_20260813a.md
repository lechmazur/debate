[C1.1] Proportionate representation sounds like scientific rigor. As a mandatory condition of approval, it substitutes demographic accounting for scientific judgment. Trials should study relevant patients; the question is whether matching age, sex, and ethnic percentages must outweigh every other consideration, including material delay. That requirement is neither necessary nor sufficient for safer medicine.
[C1.2] It is not necessary. Zolpidem points to measuring sex-specific pharmacokinetics and dosing; carbamazepine and clopidogrel point to genetic mechanisms and targeted testing. An ethnically balanced trial that ignores the relevant variant learns less than a mechanistically designed study. Likewise, an age-proportionate trial can still exclude frail patients with multiple illnesses.
[C1.3] A headcount can look inclusive while preserving the actual evidence gap.
[C1.4] Nor is it sufficient. Unless subgroups are large enough, quotas produce small cells incapable of reliably establishing safety or effectiveness. They invite noisy subgroup claims or false reassurance. Powering every comparison demands still larger, slower trials.
[C1.5] And proportionate to what: the national census, the local population, or those with the disease? Those denominators can conflict. The motion promises valid answers but supplies only arithmetic.
[C1.6] The cost is not abstract, nor borne equally. Patients dying of cancer or suffering progressive disease cannot treat delay as a harmless pause; underrepresented patients bear that cost too. Recruitment networks and suitable participants are finite, so simultaneous quotas can turn scientific design into competition for demographic slots. PRO hopes infrastructure eventually shrinks delay, but the motion expressly accepts material slowdown now, without guaranteeing better subgroup evidence in return.
[C1.7] The rigorous alternative is enforceable, mechanism-based evidence: remove unjustified exclusions; include older and frailer patients where clinically relevant; measure pharmacokinetics, comorbidities, or genotypes when they plausibly alter outcomes; assess representation across a development program; and impose labelled limitations and enforceable post-approval studies where uncertainty remains. Decades of weak voluntary guidance justify enforceable duties, not this particular quota.
[C1.8] When proportional recruitment delays a beneficial drug while adding underpowered strata, it sacrifices patients for statistical symmetry. Reject a rule that makes the appearance of inclusion outrank evidence and access.

Zolpidem’s lesson was not simply that women needed to appear in a table; sex-specific pharmacokinetics and next-morning impairment had to be measured. A proportionate trial recording neither can miss the problem completely.
[C2.2] Trials can also collect broad clinical and biological data without knowing the culprit in advance. Representation widens inquiry; it does not make unknown mechanisms discoverable by arithmetic.
[C2.3] [P2.5] claims proportionality makes the headline result “honest.” Actually, a population-weighted average can conceal the very difference at issue: a drug may appear effective overall while doing little, or causing harm, within one subgroup. [P2.6] then retreats to “signals.” But a handful of participants produces noise and false alarms as readily as useful warnings. Detecting uncommon events requires adequate numbers, relevant outcomes, and follow-up, not symbolic presence. Pooling across trials helps only when comparable measurements and sufficient participants exist—scientific work that quotas themselves do not guarantee.
[C2.4] Nor is the denominator a mere drafting detail. “People with the disease” differs by geography, diagnostic access, disease stage, and treatment patterns. Requiring proportionality simultaneously across age, sex, and ethnicity lets the slowest cross-category hold up approval even when filling it adds little decision-relevant evidence. That is the motion’s specific defect: it converts a worthwhile recruitment objective into an inflexible approval veto.
[C2.5] Finally, delay is not bounded or equally shared merely because it occurs before approval. A patient with progressive disease may lose the drug’s benefit permanently, and underrepresented patients are among those waiting. They did not consent to that sacrifice either. Enforceable inclusive eligibility, relevant measurements, and justified post-approval commitments can address residual uncertainty without imposing one demographic formula on every drug.
[C2.6] PRO instead delays access for everyone to purchase evidence that may remain too weak to protect anyone. The real choice is targeted evidence with timely access, versus demographic compliance regardless of evidentiary value.
