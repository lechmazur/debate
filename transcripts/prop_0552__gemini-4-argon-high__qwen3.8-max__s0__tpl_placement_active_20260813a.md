[P1.1] Every year, wealthier nations generate mountains of discarded electronics and ship vast quantities to lower-income countries under the convenient labels of "recycling" or "second-hand goods." The reality on the ground is brutal. In informal scrapyards from West Africa to South Asia, workers burn plastic-coated wires in open pits for copper and use open acid baths to strip gold from circuit boards. This floods local communities with lead, mercury, and toxic fumes.
[P1.2] Exporting e-waste is not trade; it is outsourcing poison to countries lacking the regulatory capacity and infrastructure to handle it safely.
[P1.3] A total ban on these exports is essential because partial regulations systematically fail. As long as shipping e-waste to poorer nations remains legal under complex exceptions, unscrupulous brokers will exploit customs loopholes, mislabeling broken junk as working electronics. Furthermore, in lower-income destination countries, informal backyard processors who pay nothing for environmental protection will always outbid legitimate, safe facilities for imported scrap. Only a bright-line ban at the port of origin stops this toxic arbitrage.
[P1.4] Requiring domestic or certified regional processing also fixes a broken economic incentive. Cheap export dumping currently subsidizes wasteful design and domestic neglect. Neither governments nor manufacturers will invest in safe, automated recycling plants at home when they can put hazardous waste on a barge for pennies. Closing the export escape hatch forces countries to internalize their waste costs, driving investment into domestic green infrastructure and repairable product design.
[P1.5] It also retains critical minerals—like lithium, cobalt, and copper—within secure supply chains rather than losing them to crude, inefficient open-pit burning.
[P1.6] Finally, pairing domestic recycling with certified regional processing ensures practicality. Smaller countries need not each build expensive recovery facilities; they can share volume through audited, high-standard regional hubs. This captures economies of scale without exploiting global poverty. Countries must manage their own toxic digital footprint rather than burying it in the developing world.

Much cross-border electronics movement is reuse and repair: a discarded office computer in one country can become a school machine, a clinic terminal, or a source of affordable parts in another.
[C1.2] Ban it, and you do not make the device disappear. You strand useful equipment, raise digital costs for poorer users, and push legitimate refurbishers out of the market.
[C1.3] A ban also weakens, not strengthens, environmental control. When demand for metals and cheap devices persists, prohibition drives flows underground. Smugglers do not file customs declarations, accept inspections, or fund safe processing. The predictable result is more secrecy, more mislabeling, and less leverage for reform.
[C1.4] If the objective is safer processing, the better route is mandatory prior informed consent, exporter liability, and certification tied to auditable facilities—not a prohibition that removes visibility.
[C1.5] The domestic-recycling mandate is economically brittle. Many countries lack sufficient scale, technical capacity, or collection systems to process every waste stream safely today. Forcing all processing home can divert scarce public funds from collection, safe dismantling, and enforcement, while increasing transport and energy use inside countries already struggling with recycling economics.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, the proposition would still bar exports to lower-income certified plants, even when those facilities are safer and more efficient than fragmented national alternatives.
[C1.7] Most importantly, the proposition harms the people it claims to protect. Informal recovery exists because it provides income and access to materials. The task is to formalize and upgrade that work, not to eliminate the trade that supports it. Reject the ban because the right standard is regulated, accountable processing—not prohibition that increases smuggling, wastes usable goods, and ignores the comparative advantages of global recycling.
