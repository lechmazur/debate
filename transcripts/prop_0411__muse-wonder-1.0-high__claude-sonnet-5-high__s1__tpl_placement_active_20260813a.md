[P1.1] Mobility is not a luxury add-on. It is how people get to work, dialysis, school, and family. If a bus or car arrives in ten minutes for everyone else but a wheelchair user must book a separate van two days ahead, endure a three-hour pickup window, and accept a slow, detour-filled ride, that is not access. It is segregation by timetable.
[P1.2] Systems that serve the public should be required to actually serve the whole public, with wait times and convenience close to standard service.
[P1.3] That duty applies to both transit and ride-hail. Transit is publicly funded; ride-hail operates under public licenses, on public roads and curbs, and has displaced regulated taxis. A company cannot market itself as transportation for everyone, scale by replacing public conveyance, and then treat wheelchair users as an optional charity market. When access depends on goodwill, it disappears exactly where demand is sparse and need is greatest.
[P1.4] A mandate is also what makes parity possible. Accessible vehicles cost more to buy and operate, and securement takes extra minutes, so individual drivers rationally avoid trips they are penalized for taking slowly. Left voluntary, each platform waits for someone else to pay. A clear fleet and response-time requirement breaks that deadlock: it guarantees demand, justifies investment, pools dispatch across providers, and lets accessible cars serve any rider when idle so they are not sitting empty.
[P1.5] Supply creates reliability, and reliability creates riders.
[P1.6] “Close to” is demanding but reasonable. It does not mean identical to the second; it means no separate universe of advance reservations, punitive waits, and inferior booking. Same app, comparable pickup window, comparable price — measured and enforced like other service standards. Costs should be spread broadly through fares, platform fees, or public subsidy, because the expense per ordinary trip is small compared with the cost of exclusion: missed medical care, lost jobs, isolation, and dependence on far more expensive specialized transport later.

[C1.1] Mobility matters. Nobody disputes that. The question is whether the right policy tool is a blanket mandate that accessible service match standard service "close" in wait time and convenience — and that mandate is the wrong tool, because it is not actually achievable, and chasing it makes real-world accessibility worse, not better.
[C1.2] Start with the physical constraint PRO skips past: wheelchair-accessible vehicles are a small, expensive slice of any fleet. Retrofitting a car with a ramp and securement adds real cost and removes seats; building that supply to match standard-service density everywhere — rural routes, 2 a.m. pickups, thin suburban corridors — is not a dispatch problem solvable by "pooling," it is a hardware problem. You cannot conjure fleets of WAVs into sparse markets just because a regulation says wait times must be comparable.
[C1.3] When PRO says "demand will justify investment," they are assuming elastic supply that doesn't exist on any short or medium timeline.
[C1.4] That mismatch produces the real danger: when a mandate is unworkable as written, operators don't magically comply — they exit. We have already watched ride-hail companies pull out of cities or suspend service rather than absorb regulatory costs they judge unsustainable. If "close to standard" becomes the enforceable bar, the rational corporate response in marginal markets is not to build out accessible fleets; it's to withdraw service altogether, or throttle standard service down toward the accessible baseline to minimize the gap.
[C1.5] Either way, disabled riders in exactly the places where demand is "sparse and need is greatest" — PRO's own phrase — are left with nothing instead of a slower, scheduled ride.
[C1.6] The better path is not loose parity language but dedicated, funded accessible-transport standards — guaranteed maximum wait times, subsidized vans, pooled regional dispatch — judged on their own terms, not chained to a fluctuating standard-service benchmark that invites litigation over what counts as "close enough" while service on the ground degrades for everyone, disabled and able-bodied riders alike.
