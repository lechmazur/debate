[P1.1] A free society does not exist to eliminate all risk at the cost of the autonomy that makes freedom worth having. Security without enforceable privacy is not collective safety—it is unaccountable control, the core feature of every police state that ever promised safety in exchange for submission. Broad, privacy-eroding surveillance powers rolled out in moments of public panic are almost never scaled back once the immediate threat fades, and they are overwhelmingly deployed not to stop the high-profile attacks used to justify them, but to track political protestors, monitor labor organizers, target marginalized communities, and pursue petty, low-stakes offenses.
[P1.2] Ordinary people who insist they have “nothing to hide” forget that political orthodoxies shift, that today’s protected activist can be tomorrow’s targeted dissident, that the personal information vacuumed up by state agencies can be weaponized by whoever holds power next, no matter how trustworthy the current administration seems. Privacy is not a luxury for people with something to hide. It is the structural precondition for free speech, free association, and fair political contestation, the very traits that separate a free society from a closely monitored open-air prison.
[P1.3] When people know their calls, messages, location history, reading habits, and group affiliations are logged and accessible to authorities, they self-censor long before any official punishment is threatened, avoiding dissenting groups, anti-corruption advocacy, and unpopular ideas until public debate becomes a hollow, state-approved performance. Every state, no matter how brutal, promises security; many authoritarian regimes deliver lower rates of ordinary street crime and even some public disorder than messy, open free societies do. What makes a society free is refusing to surrender the private sphere that makes self-governance possible, trading permanent, universal vulnerability to arbitrary state overreach for temporary, often deeply illusory, marginal safety gains.
