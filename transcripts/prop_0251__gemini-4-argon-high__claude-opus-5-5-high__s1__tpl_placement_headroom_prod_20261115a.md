[P1.1] When a trench threatens to collapse or toxic fumes fill a factory floor, the law does not tell workers to drink water and tough it out; it stops the work. Extreme heat is no less lethal, yet the law still treats it largely as bad weather rather than a regulated workplace hazard. Beyond specific thresholds of heat and humidity, the human body loses its ability to cool itself, triggering cognitive impairment, acute kidney injury, and fatal heatstroke. This is fundamentally a labor-rights issue because exposure is dictated by workplace power.
[P1.2] A piece-rate agricultural laborer, a warehouse loader racing an algorithmic quota, or a construction worker on a tight deadline cannot simply choose to slow down when pausing means losing wages or being fired.
[P1.3] Without mandatory stop-work protections, worker survival is left to employer discretion, which routinely fails whenever safety conflicts with speed. Voluntary guidelines and basic mitigation like water and shade are useful in moderate heat, but in extreme conditions they hit a physiological wall. Moreover, relying on vague general-duty clauses forces individual workers to judge when conditions turn fatal and confront their bosses alone. Because fear of retaliation silences the most vulnerable workers, protection must be automatic and legally guaranteed.
[P1.4] When objective metrics like heat index and exertion levels cross a dangerous threshold, strenuous labor must legally pause or shift to cooler hours without penalty or retaliation.
[P1.5] Treating extreme heat this way is neither radical nor unworkable. Regulators already enforce hard operational halts for lightning, high winds on cranes, and toxic air quality. Heat standards can similarly calibrate triggers by industry, indoor or outdoor setting, and regional climate. The true economic disaster is allowing employers to externalize the costs of extreme heat onto workers' bodies, emergency rooms, and families.
[P1.6] No paycheck should require gambling with heatstroke.
