[P1.1] A performer's face and voice are not raw material a studio owns outright—they are the instrument the actor spent a career building, and the thing audiences form a relationship with. When a studio uses AI to de-age that face or synthesize that voice to materially alter a performance, it is manufacturing new expressive content out of a person's identity. That requires the same permission structure we already demand for any other use of someone's likeness: consent and payment.
[P1.2] Start with the property logic. Actors license their image for a specific performance under specific terms. De-aging or voice synthesis doesn't just touch up that performance—it creates new performance data, expressions, and line deliveries that the actor never actually gave. If a studio can generate "young Harrison Ford" saying new lines in a new context without his sign-off, it has effectively created a duplicate actor it can direct forever, off the original actor's back, without paying for the labor of creation.
[P1.3] That's not editing; it's manufacturing a synthetic performer wearing a real person's face.
[P1.4] Now the dignity logic. An actor's identity carries reputational stakes. If a studio can silently smooth a performance, alter line readings, or de-age a scene the actor find embarrassing or inconsistent with the character they built, the actor loses authorship over their own artistic legacy. Consent isn't a bureaucratic add-on here; it's the mechanism by which the person whose face and voice are on screen retains a say in how their own image speaks.
[P1.5] Compensation follows directly. If the studio profits from synthetic use of a likeness, the value flows from that person's identity—their recognizability is precisely why the audience cares. Uncompensated use is just extraction: the studio captures upside from an asset it didn't fully create and the actor did.
[P1.6] None of this bans the technology. It just insists on the ordinary market principle already governing likeness rights everywhere else in entertainment: if you use my face and voice to make money, ask me first, and pay me.

[C1.2] PRO’s rule sounds modest, but “materially alter” is dangerously broad. A de-aged flashback, a corrected lip movement, a restored early film, a dubbed version, or a voice patch for a line lost in sound mixing could all require fresh permission and payment. That converts ordinary editing into endless renegotiation. It gives actors final-cut authority: a performer who dislikes the finished film, the director, or the marketing campaign can refuse consent and block release.
[C1.3] The result is not dignity; it is hold-up power.
[C1.4] The compensation point is equally weak. Actors are already compensated for the performance they were hired to give. If AI de-aging preserves continuity inside that same role, the value comes from the production, script, direction, editing, and the actor’s contracted work—not from a new performance requiring separate billing. Where a studio wants genuinely new uses outside the original project—advertisements, new characters, unauthorized sequels—existing contract law, publicity rights, and union bargaining can address it.
[C1.5] The motion, however, imposes a blanket rule reaching routine post-production.
[C1.6] The real clash is control. PRO treats every downstream alteration as a new expressive act requiring permission; that turns filmmaking into a hostage negotiation. Protect actors from novel exploitation through targeted contracts, yes—but do not give them a consent-and-payment veto over completing work they were already hired to perform.
