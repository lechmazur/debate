[C1.2] First, the system already self-heals. Roughly 150 to 200 cable faults occur every year, overwhelmingly from trawling and anchors, and traffic reroutes across alternate paths within seconds while repair ships restore the physical link in days to weeks. This is why multi-cable incidents—Baltic cuts, Red Sea damage—produced slowdowns, not blackouts.
[C1.3] The scenario PRO needs, a genuine multi-week transatlantic blackout, requires a sustained, deliberate campaign that no amount of hardened landing stations or spare repair ships would stop anyway. [C1.4] Second, the cost asymmetry runs the wrong way. PRO compares cable spending to submarines, but the alternative to hardening isn't a fighter squadron—it's the undersea warfare, surveillance, and naval presence that raises the political price of attacking cables in the first place. [C1.5] Third, most of this is not a defense ministry's money to move. Private consortia own the overwhelming majority of capacity and already have commercial incentive to build duplicate routes where traffic justifies them. If a route doesn't merit private redundancy, it rarely merits scarce public defense dollars either.
[C1.6] "Much more" spending on a problem the market already prices and mitigates is not force protection—it's buying insurance against a risk that's already pooled, by canceling the capabilities that actually prevent the war.
