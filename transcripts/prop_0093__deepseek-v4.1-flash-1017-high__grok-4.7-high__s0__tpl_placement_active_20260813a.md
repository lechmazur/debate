[P1.1] Every city has a fixed number of curb feet, and for decades most of that public space has been handed to the storage of private cars—often free or nearly free—while buses crawl, delivery trucks double-park, and cyclists weave through traffic. The proposition does not ban cars or eliminate parking. It says the curb should serve the functions that keep a city moving and accessible, not merely protect most of it for car storage.
[P1.2] The decisive comparison is people served and public value created per linear foot. A single bus stop can move hundreds of riders an hour; one parking space holds one or two cars. A loading zone can keep an entire block of shops supplied without trucks blocking a travel lane; storefront parking mostly benefits the one driver who arrived first. A protected bike lane can carry a steady flow of commuters and make short trips safe enough that some people leave the car at home.
[P1.3] When cities fail to provide those spaces, the costs do not disappear—they show up as buses stuck behind parked cars, delivery drivers circling the block, and cyclists forced into dangerous traffic.
[P1.4] This is also a fairness argument. The current arrangement privatizes a valuable public asset for a narrow use and socializes the congestion it creates. A nurse on a bus, a small business waiting for a shipment, and a teenager biking to school all depend on curb space functioning well. A city that reserves more curb for buses, deliveries, and bikes is not anti-car; it is pro-access, pro-safety, and pro-throughput.
[P1.5] The question is not whether parking has any value. Of course it does, and accessible spaces must be protected. The question is whether most of a scarce public resource should be locked into low-value storage. It should not.
[P1.6] Cities should allocate the curb to the uses that move more people, support more businesses, and save more lives.
