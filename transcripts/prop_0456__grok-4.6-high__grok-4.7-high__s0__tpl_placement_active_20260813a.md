[P1.1] Copyright exists because creation is expensive and copying is cheap. Training a generative model on millions of books, songs, and films is not private study. It is industrial conversion of those works into a competing machine that can write in a novelist's cadence, steal a hit's groove, or remix a film's look. Publishers, labels, and studios should not receive that conversion for free, and individual authors should not hold a veto over every work in the corpus.
[P1.2] Permission-by-permission licensing collapses at this scale. A label that needs a representative training set cannot hunt down every songwriter, session player, and estate. Missing owners, orphan works, and strategic holdouts would turn lawful training into a lottery. Opt-in would privilege whoever already owns a fat catalog and lock out training on the rest of culture.
[P1.3] Copyright already answers markets that cannot form: mechanical licenses for cover recordings, statutory licenses for certain retransmissions. The use proceeds; the price is not zero.
[P1.4] The motion's condition is the point. They may train without permission only if they pay into a compulsory scheme. That rules out two failures. A total ban leaves the technology to whoever scrapes first and litigates later.
[P1.5] An unpaid fair-use rule hands the entire cultural corpus to the firms with the deepest pockets and the largest legal departments. A compulsory fund prices the input, distributes proceeds, and gives every studio the same lawful path.
[P1.6] These companies already sit at the center of creative revenue. They are the parties best able to pay, and the parties most tempted to train on everyone else's catalog as well as their own. If they want permissionless access, they buy it through the pool. Creators are compensated without a freeze.
[P1.7] Firms get certainty without a raid. That is the only version of permissionless training that still treats copyright as a right rather than a rumor.

[C1.1] Copyright is the right to refuse, not a right to be paid after the taking. This motion would let publishers, labels, and studios train generative models on other people's work without permission, provided money enters a compulsory pool. That is a forced sale. It should be rejected.
[C1.2] Training at industrial scale is not private study. [P1.1] is right about the conversion and wrong about the remedy. If a system is built to write in a novelist's cadence, lift a hit's groove, or remix a film's look, the author needs the power to refuse, to price the risk, or to keep the work out of the machine. Mechanical licenses and retransmission statutes are the wrong analogy.
[C1.3] Those uses are discrete and leave the original market standing. A model is built to compete with that labor. A fund does not restore the choice it erased.
[C1.4] The motion also licenses the wrong parties. It does not empower the novelist or the session player. It empowers the firms already sitting on catalogs and contracts to ingest everyone else at a regulated rate. [P1.6] admits those firms are the most tempted to train on other people's work.
[C1.5] A pool payment is a group discount for that temptation, not a check on it. Incumbents will dominate both the training and the distribution. Thin-leverage authors, unwilling estates, and moral objectors get the same offer: a check if found, and no veto.
[C1.6] Clearance friction is not a warrant for expropriation. Orphan works are narrow. They do not justify taking living work whose authors can bargain or opt in. A holdout is a seller refusing a price, not a defect to be repealed.
[C1.7] The rate will be political, shaped by the firms that pay it, and systematically low. Voluntary collective licenses can scale without abolishing consent. Certainty purchased by making refusal irrelevant is only a cleaner raid.
