[P1.1] Credit-card interest rates should be capped by law because the product is not just “credit”; it is a legally enforceable claim on a borrower’s future income, often created in an emergency. Ordinary price bargaining assumes comparison, exit, and understanding of long-run cost. Revolving debt fails that test: minimum-payment boxes, compounding interest, penalty rates, fees, and optimism about next month’s income make the real price opaque when the card is used. A family charging a car repair after a cut in hours is buying time under pressure, not calmly purchasing capital.
[P1.2] At a high APR, a $1,000 expense can linger for years and cost far more than the original charge.
[P1.3] So the cap is not hostility to risk pricing. It is a boundary against contracts whose profits depend disproportionately on distress and inertia. Lenders may underwrite, lower limits, require security, shorten repayment, or decline. What they may not do is enlist state collection power to turn a temporary shortfall into a semi-permanent income stream.
[P1.4] Usury limits rest on that principle: some bargains are too destructive to enforce merely because signatures exist.
[P1.5] Defenders of uncapped rates count lost credit offers as a harm. That is the wrong metric. An offer of a 25% or 30% revolving balance is not access to medicine or housing; it is access to a product with a predictable downside: compounding debt and pressure on rent, food, and utilities. A rejection letter can be painful, but it does not compound.
[P1.6] A high-rate balance does.
[P1.7] Even with rationing, the comparison is not “credit versus no credit,” but “no legal debt trap versus a market where higher-risk borrowers get the most punitive terms.” Cap first; safer channels can supplement emergency liquidity. They are not excuses for permitting uncapped APRs. If lenders cannot profit without rates that predictably deepen a borrower’s crisis, the law should let them decline—not let them harvest it.

[C1.1] "A rejection letter can be painful, but it does not compound" ([P1.5]) only if emergencies vanish when a bank says no. Take that exact family facing a $1,000 car repair after a cut in hours ([P1.1]). On a 30% APR card, roughly 2.5% per month, bridging that repair costs about $25 in the first month while the parent keeps commuting and rebuilds hours. Under a binding cap that cuts off higher-risk borrowers, the card is declined, the car stays broken, shifts are missed, and a manageable shortfall spirals into lost wages, late fees, utility shutoffs, or eviction.
[C1.2] Unfinanced crises compound far faster and more ruthlessly than revolving interest.
[C1.3] Rate caps force this rationing because unsecured credit carries no collateral. Higher-risk borrowers include hourly workers with volatile pay, young adults and immigrants with thin files, and households recovering from medical bills. Telling lenders to "lower limits" or "require security" ([P1.3]) misses the point: a family short $1,000 cannot post a $1,000 cash deposit for a secured card or fix a transmission with a $200 limit. When APRs are capped below expected default losses, issuers either cut those households off or shift revenue into steep upfront annual fees that penalize small balances even when repaid quickly.
[C1.4] Worse, denying regulated cards never ends distress borrowing; it drives families into overdraft cascades, pawnshops, rent-to-own contracts, payday loans, informal lenders, or auto-title loans that can seize the very car they needed to fix.
[C1.5] Using costly credit to save a paycheck is rational triage, not failed bargaining ([P1.1]). Meanwhile, starter cards are the main on-ramp for thin-file borrowers to build payment history and graduate to prime rates; a cap locks them out permanently. If minimum-payment boxes or penalty traps obscure costs ([P1.1]), the law should reform payment minimums and fee rules directly, not ban risk pricing. Saying "cap first" and hoping "safer channels" appear later ([P1.7]) strands vulnerable families the morning the car will not start.
