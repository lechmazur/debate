[P1.1] The dominance of English doesn't merely coexist with other languages and knowledge systems—it actively hollows them out. The proposition isn't that English is useless as a shared tool; it's that its dominance imposes asymmetric costs on non-anglophone cultures that outweigh the convenience of a common medium.
[P1.2] Consider science. When the vast majority of indexed journals, peer review processes, and international funding decisions operate in English, knowledge produced in other languages doesn't just travel slower—it becomes invisible. A botanist documenting medicinal plant knowledge in Quechua, a philosopher working in Arabic, or a public health researcher publishing in Japanese faces a system that treats their work as second-tier unless it clears an English-language gate. This isn't translation friction; it's epistemic filtering.
[P1.3] Entire knowledge systems—indigenous taxonomies, non-Western medical traditions, local ecological knowledge—get dismissed not because they lack rigor but because they don't fit English-language publication norms.
[P1.4] The same dynamic governs the internet. English isn't just one option among many online; it's the default infrastructure layer. Search algorithms, content moderation policies, and AI training data all privilege English content. Non-anglophone users must adapt to systems designed for English or accept marginal presence in digital life.
[P1.5] The standard rebuttal is that English provides a bridge. But bridges that only run in one direction aren't bridges—they're drainage systems. The benefit of shared communication flows mostly to those already inside the English-speaking world, while the cost—linguistic erosion, cultural homogenization, epistemic loss—is concentrated on everyone else. Non-anglophone scientists, entrepreneurs, and creators pay a continuous tax of translation, adaptation, and self-erasure that anglophones never experience.
[P1.6] When a language dominates science, commerce, and digital life, it doesn't just facilitate exchange—it sets the terms of what counts as knowledge, what gets heard, and whose frameworks matter. That structural power does more harm than good.
