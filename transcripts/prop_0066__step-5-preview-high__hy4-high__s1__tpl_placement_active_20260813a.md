[P1.1] A stranger across the subway car should not be able to learn your name, your employer, your home address, or your entire social history simply by looking at you. That is the quiet, foundational bargain of public life: when you step outside, you are recognizable to the people you already know, not to everyone. AR glasses with live face recognition shatter that bargain. They convert public space into a searchable database, and they do so without consent, without warning, and often without any reliable visible cue that it is happening.
[P1.2] The hardware looks like ordinary eyewear; the processing is instant and invisible.
[P1.3] The harm is not hypothetical. Put such a device in the hands of an abusive ex-partner, a hostile ideological opponent, or a political operative, and a crowded street becomes a stalking grid. The wearer gains instant access; the target loses the ability to move through the world unremarked. Even when no one acts on the information in the moment, the knowledge that any face can be instantly resolved into a full profile chills lawful behavior.
[P1.4] People skip protests, avoid support groups, and alter their routes. That is not a minor inconvenience. It is a structural narrowing of public freedom and association.
[P1.5] I am not arguing that face recognition has no legitimate uses. The proposition preserves a narrow professional exception for exactly those cases: documented, accountable deployments where the public interest is real and the scope is tightly constrained. What I reject is the idea that this power should be default, unregulated, and available to any consumer who buys the hardware. A tool that can strip anonymity from an entire crowd at once is categorically different from a smartphone camera, and it demands a categorically stricter rule.
[P1.6] Ban live face recognition in most public settings. Allow it only where a specific professional mandate justifies the intrusion and controls it.

[P2.4] When a technology creates infrastructure for mass abuse, the burden falls on proponents to prove containment, not on victims to prove harm after deployment.
[P2.5] The sympathy cases—prosopagnosia, dementia, parent pickup [C1.5]—and the book-club cue [C1.4] do not require general public face recognition. Conflating a benign contact list with the public biometric database that makes the technology dangerous is the core error. The motion preserves a narrow professional exception precisely because accountability scales with mandate; a mandated, documented deployment can be audited, while personal use cannot.
[P2.6] The principled line is not scalable identification in theory; it is the mass deployment of that capacity by default. Banning the general capability does not ban kindness. It prevents the normalization of a surveillance infrastructure that no after-the-fact penalty can undo.
