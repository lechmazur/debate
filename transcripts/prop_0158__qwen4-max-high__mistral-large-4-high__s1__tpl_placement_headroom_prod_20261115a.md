[P1.1] Every year, people die on dialysis waiting for a kidney that never comes. That shortage is not a fact of nature; it is a policy choice. We prohibit the one thing that reliably increases supply — meaningful compensation — and then call the resulting deaths tragic rather than chosen. The status quo has a body count, and it is not neutral.
[P1.2] Banning payment does not abolish the trade; it outsources it. Where desperate patients meet desperate sellers outside the law, brokers keep most of the money, screening is cursory, and donors vanish into poverty with no follow-up care. Iran has run a state-supervised compensated kidney program for decades, with payment routed through regulated institutions rather than brokers, and dramatically shorter waits. And dialysis, year after year, costs health systems far more than a one-time transplant.
[P1.3] The real comparison is a lawful system versus a black market, not a market versus a paradise of pure altruism.
[P1.4] Strict regulation is not decoration; it is the answer to every standard objection. A single public purchaser, fixed compensation, no brokering. Independent donor advocates paid nothing per transplant. Mandatory cooling-off periods and psychological screening.
[P1.5] Guaranteed lifetime medical follow-up and insurance. Allocation by medical need, never by wealth — the recipient's payment goes to the system, not to buy a place in line. Exploitation happens when a seller has no protections. Give her protections.
[P1.6] We already pay people to accept bodily risk: plasma donors, egg donors, trial volunteers, firefighters. Nobody calls that commodification when the risk is a vein instead of a kidney. The burden on the other side is not to describe an unsettling feeling. It is to show that a carefully regulated system kills or harms more people than the waiting list we already have.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to believe that a market in human organs can be made safe by government decree. But the organ trade does not become ethical because a bureaucrat sets the price. It selects its sellers by desperation, and no regulation can equalize the bargaining power between a patient who needs a kidney and a person selling one to pay rent. When the state licenses that exchange, it does not protect the vulnerable; it institutionalizes their vulnerability.
[C1.2] Look at what compensation does. Where payment is permitted, sellers are overwhelmingly the poor, the indebted, and migrants with no safety net. Iran's program, held up as the model, leaves donors in debt and without the long-term care promised, because follow-up is expensive and oversight thin. Strict regulation sounds like a shield, but it is a promise on paper.
[C1.3] Markets adapt faster than ministries: brokers reappear under new names, coercion moves from clinic to family, and a cooling-off period cannot undo the economic pressure that made the sale seem necessary. [C1.4] Payment also corrodes the gift. Donation rests on a norm: the body is not a commodity. Introduce cash and that norm shifts. Altruistic donors may stay away, viewing the system as a marketplace, and trust in fair allocation erodes when money changes hands.
[C1.5] But the shortage has lawful remedies: presumed-consent laws, better use of deceased donors, organ preservation, xenotransplantation, and kidney-disease prevention. These reduce the waiting list without turning the poor into spare-parts warehouses.
[C1.6] The choice is not between a regulated market and a black market; it is between protecting the desperate and auctioning their bodies.

The lawful remedies—presumed consent, better deceased-donor use, prevention—should be exhausted before we turn the poor into a reserve supply of kidneys.
[C2.5] The choice is not between a regulated market and a black market; it is between a society that refuses to buy organs from the poor and one that does.
