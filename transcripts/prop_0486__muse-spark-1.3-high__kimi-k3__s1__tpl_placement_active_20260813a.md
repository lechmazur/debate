[P1.1] Permanent burial in a land-scarce city is a promise the city cannot keep. Every year thousands more residents die, and every permanent plot subtracts forever from housing, schools, clinics, and parks. In places like Singapore and Hong Kong cemeteries already press against dense neighborhoods because the arithmetic never stops. A public cemetery cannot keep selling eternity by the square meter when the living have nowhere to go.
[P1.2] Composting and alkaline hydrolysis break that deadlock without abandoning care for the dead. Natural organic reduction places a body in a vessel with wood chips and straw and, through controlled microbial action over several weeks, converts it into soil that families can keep or scatter in a memorial garden. Alkaline hydrolysis uses heated water and alkali to accelerate natural decomposition, leaving only sterile liquid and soft bone that is dried to ash for families. Both take place in a facility the size of a small building, not acres of vaults and headstones, and both return something meaningful to loved ones.
[P1.3] This is also what public stewardship requires. Public cemeteries are taxpayer-supported land held in trust for all generations, not private estates for whoever died first. When permanent plots become scarce they become luxury goods, with prices that exclude ordinary families while maintenance costs drain municipal budgets. A garden where composted soil nourishes trees, or a columbarium wall for hydrolyzed remains, can honor thousands where burial honors dozens, at lower cost and with perpetual care.
[P1.4] Tradition is not a defense of permanence. Cities have moved graves, stacked burials, and limited tenure for centuries. Dignity lies in reverent treatment and remembrance, not in occupying scarce ground forever.

[C1.1] PRO's case runs on a false choice: eternity by the square meter, or the compost vessel. A city that stops selling permanent plots is not thereby forced to liquefy its dead or render them into garden soil. Limited-tenure graves, grave reuse, columbaria, and cremation — established, regulated, culturally absorbed — already answer the land arithmetic. Singapore itself manages scarcity through tenure limits and exhumation, not composting.
[C1.2] The motion leaps past every moderate option to mandate the two most radical disposition methods available. That leap is what PRO must defend, and it cannot be defended.
[C1.3] First, the mandate collides with religious freedom. Islam and Orthodox Judaism require intact bodily burial; Eastern Orthodox Christianity and many Indigenous traditions treat the corpse as inviolable. A public cemetery exists to serve all residents equally. Telling a Muslim family their mother must be composted or chemically dissolved is not neutral land management; it is the state dictating theology.
[C1.4] PRO waves this away as "tradition." Free exercise is a right, not a custom, and public institutions are bound by it.
[C1.5] Second, consent and legitimacy. Composting a person into soil, or draining the effluent of alkaline hydrolysis into ordinary wastewater, remains viscerally unacceptable to large shares of the public. People grieve as they believe, not as urban planners prefer. A public institution that overrides the grieving in the name of efficiency forfeits the trust that justifies its existence — and families will route around it, into private plots, worsening the very inequity PRO decries.
[C1.6] Third, feasibility. Both methods are legal in only a scattering of jurisdictions, with a handful of operating facilities worldwide. Mandating a citywide transition to infrastructure that barely exists, under regulations not yet written, for thousands of deaths a year without interruption, is not a plan. It is a wager on bodies.
[C1.7] Land scarcity is real. It does not license coercing every family into two novel, contested, largely unbuilt methods when proven alternatives free the same land without touching the body. Reject the motion.
