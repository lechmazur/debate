[P1.1] Banning open containers does not stop social drinking. It pushes people into alleys, parking lots, private parties that spill outward, and cars. These are spaces with less visibility, fewer rules, and weaker accountability. Tightly managed nightlife zones reverse that failure by concentrating drinking where cities can actually supervise it.
[P1.2] In a managed zone, hours are limited, entrances can be monitored, lighting and sanitation are planned, and police or licensed security stay present. Bars and vendors have licenses at stake, so they have reason to refuse overservice and call for help early. Disorder happens in public view, where it can be interrupted before it escalates or spreads. Cleanup and extra patrols can be funded by the activity itself rather than treated as an unpriced externality dumped on residential streets.
[P1.3] A bounded zone also lets cities post clear rules and enforce them consistently, so neighbors know where the activity ends.
[P1.4] The alternative is worse precisely because it is less visible. Unregulated gatherings lack capacity limits and trained staff. Drinkers move between hidden spots on foot or by car, raising impaired-driving and assault risks that no police force can cover citywide. Litter, noise, and violence become diffuse problems instead of bounded ones.
[P1.5] A paper ban creates the appearance of control while the real consumption continues under inferior conditions, and residents near unmanaged spillover absorb noise and damage they cannot easily escape.
[P1.6] Cities already permit alcohol in stadiums, festivals, and concert grounds and manage the crowds with design, staffing, and rules. Nightlife districts are the same problem at a smaller, more permanent scale. The honest comparison is not managed drinking versus no drinking. It is managed drinking versus the less regulated spaces the ban inevitably produces.
[P1.7] Partial oversight in a defined area outperforms the illusion of a citywide ban that no one can enforce uniformly. Cities should choose the option they can see, bound, and govern.

[C1.1] The choice PRO describes is not the real choice. Nightlife already happens inside licensed bars and clubs where staff, insurance, and liability law create accountability. The question is whether cities should additionally let people carry alcohol onto open streets in a "zone." [C1.2] Inside a bar, if someone is overserved, there is a bartender who can be identified, fined, or lose a license. On an open-container street, a person can buy from three different vendors, top off from a flask, or drink what a friend hands them. No one is accountable for how intoxicated they are, because no one served them all of it. That is not tighter management — it is the deliberate removal of the control mechanism PRO relies on.
[C1.3] PRO's stadium and festival comparison actually cuts against them. Those are single-entry, ticketed, time-boxed events with a hard perimeter and headcount. A nightlife district is a porous urban neighborhood with sidewalks, transit stops, and residential blocks a few doors down. There is no gate to close, no ticket to revoke, no clean edge where "managed" stops and "public" begins.
[C1.4] The zone doesn't contain the crowd; it just relabels the same open street as officially sanctioned for drinking.
[C1.5] And the spillover problem doesn't disappear — it gets worse. People who drink longer, with less friction, in more places, still eventually leave the zone to catch a train, walk home, or find a ride. Now they're doing it more intoxicated than a last-call bar system would have allowed, because open-container removes the natural checkpoints — tabs closing, bouncers cutting people off, doors locking — that currently force a pause before people re-enter the wider city.
[C1.6] The honest comparison PRO avoids is this: concentrated accountability inside licensed premises versus diffused accountability across an open public street. Cities should strengthen the first, not dismantle it in the name of visibility.
