[P1.1] Small live-music venues are the research and development departments of a city’s cultural economy. Just as a city would never zone a vital transit hub out of existence to build luxury condos, it must not allow grassroots music venues to be regulated into oblivion. We must treat these spaces as cultural infrastructure because they are foundational systems generating a city's identity, tourism, and broader entertainment economy.
[P1.2] The core problem is that municipal frameworks treat music venues as mere commercial nuisances rather than vital civic assets. When developers build high-end apartments next to a decades-old music club, standard zoning often weaponizes noise complaints against the venue. Licensing boards treat them like standard bars, imposing crippling compliance costs while ignoring their cultural output. By failing to classify these venues as infrastructure, cities inadvertently allow short-term real estate interests to cannibalize long-term cultural capital.
[P1.3] Treating them as infrastructure mandates proactive protection. In zoning, it means adopting "agent of change" principles: the developer of a new residential building next to an existing venue pays for soundproofing, rather than forcing the historic venue to close. In licensing, it means creating specialized permits recognizing a venue's primary purpose is cultural production, not just alcohol sales. In policing, it means shifting from punitive nuisance abatement to collaborative safety planning.
[P1.4] The opposition will likely argue the free market should decide which venues survive, or that protecting them infringes on residents' quiet enjoyment. But the market is already distorted by zoning laws favoring residential developers over cultural spaces. A city’s culture is not a naturally renewing resource; it requires physical spaces to be sustained. If we do not protect the small stages where artists develop, the larger cultural ecosystem starves.
[P1.5] We must protect the roots to save the tree.
