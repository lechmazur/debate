[P1.1] Somewhere tonight, a twenty-year-old is deciding whether to see a therapist. She has coverage through her parents' plan. But she knows how it works: within weeks, an explanation of benefits lands in the family mailbox naming the provider, the service, sometimes the diagnosis. So she doesn't go.
[P1.2] That envelope quietly gates mental-health care for millions of young adults, and it does enormous damage.
[P1.3] The proposition: let young adults obtain mental-health treatment confidentially, without parental notification and without billing paperwork that automatically exposes the visit. Three reasons, each sufficient alone.
[P1.4] First, confidentiality is not a comfort; it is a condition of access. Fear of disclosure is among the most commonly cited reasons young people delay or forgo care. And the stakes peak here — suicide is a leading cause of death among young adults, and most serious conditions first emerge in this window. A policy that deters treatment converts treatable illness into crisis.
[P1.5] Second, these are adults. At eighteen you can consent to surgery, be tried as an adult, and your medical records are legally your own. Automatic disclosure through billing is a loophole in that principle, not a principled exception — the paperwork never caught up with the law's view of adulthood.
[P1.6] Third, disclosure is not harmless. The policyholder may be the source of the trauma: abusive, hostile to a child's sexuality, or convinced depression is weakness. Notification assumes every parent is safe; that assumption fails exactly where treatment matters most.
[P1.7] Feasibility is settled. Insurers can suppress explanation-of-benefits forms for sensitive services while still processing claims, and some states already require exactly that. Parents' legitimate interest in costs can be met with aggregate information, not a diagnosis in the mail.
[P1.8] Expect the opposition to frame this as parents versus secrecy. The real comparison is a child who gets help quietly versus a child who gets none. Every parent should prefer the first.

[C1.2] Start with the money. An insurance policy is a contract, and the policyholder — usually a parent paying premiums and absorbing a deductible — is party to it. The proposition doesn't just protect a diagnosis; it engineers the account so charges appear that the payer is forbidden to see explained. That is not adulthood.
[C1.3] Adulthood is paying your own way. Suppressing the paperwork means a family funds care they cannot budget for, cannot catch billing errors in, and cannot appeal when a claim is wrongly denied. The young adult then eats the balance in collections — a very expensive kind of privacy.
[C1.4] Second, the secrecy itself is a clinical liability. Treatment for the conditions PRO invokes — emerging psychosis, eating disorders, serious depression with suicidality — depends heavily on the home environment. Someone has to notice the weight loss, the stopped medication, the week of not getting out of bed. A nineteen-year-old started on an antidepressant with a black-box warning about early suicidality, living at home, with nobody in the house told what to watch for, is not safer for the silence.
[C1.5] Good practice pushes toward family involvement precisely where the stakes are highest; this motion builds a system that structurally prevents it.
[C1.6] Third, PRO's closing framing — "quiet help versus no help" — is a false choice. Campus counseling centers, community clinics with sliding-scale fees, telehealth self-pay, and enrolling young adults in coverage of their own all deliver genuinely independent access without pretending money moves in a household unseen.
[C1.7] Fund their independence. Don't manufacture their invisibility.
