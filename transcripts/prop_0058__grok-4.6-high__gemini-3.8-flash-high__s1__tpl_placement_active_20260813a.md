[P1.1] DOGE-style drives treat government like a bloated startup that needs a chainsaw. That analogy is the first mistake. Governments run airports, pay veterans, inspect food, process disability claims, and collect the taxes that fund everything else. Those functions rest on expertise, legal process, and continuity.
[P1.2] Slash them for a headline savings number and you do not get a leaner state. You get a weaker one that costs more later.
[P1.3] The damage is not mysterious. Mass firings of probationary staff and specialists empty the rooms where institutional memory lives. The people who know which contractor actually delivers, which form stops fraud, which data feed keeps a benefits system from crashing leave. What remains is overtime, premium-priced contractors, and managers covering three jobs.
[P1.4] Queues lengthen. Errors multiply. Appeals clog courts. The “duplication” celebrated as waste was often a check: an inspector, an auditor, a second pair of eyes required by statute.
[P1.5] Apparent savings evaporate under that arithmetic. Cutting enforcement staff looks like waste reduction until uncollected taxes and fraud dwarf the payroll you eliminated. Freezing grants, then losing in court, means you paid lawyers, delayed work, and still owed the money. Rehiring the same skills after a panic, plus buyouts and litigation, is not efficiency.
[P1.6] It is churn.
[P1.7] State capacity is not headcount. It is the ability to execute law at scale without collapse. DOGE-style politics selects for speed and spectacle over that ability. It confuses politically disliked programs with waste and treats legal mandates as optional.
[P1.8] The result is not a surplus. It is missed payments, slower disaster response, thinner safety around food, skies, and money—and a government that spends more rebuilding what it just broke.
[P1.9] Paper cuts are easy to announce. Service delivery is what people actually live with.

[C1.1] The proposition makes a fatal error: equating state capacity with the sheer accumulation of administrative mass. True state capacity is not measured by layers of middle management, procedural red tape, or legacy contractor rents. It is measured by results—whether the state can swiftly build infrastructure, process benefits, and protect the public. Today, the administrative state fails at precisely these tasks, not because it lacks personnel, but because it is paralyzed by calcified bloat.
[C1.2] Bureaucracy does not organically prune itself. Left alone, agencies accumulate overlapping jurisdictions, procedural veto points, and perpetual funding streams. Watchdogs chronically flag hundreds of redundant programs—dozens of distinct federal initiatives for identical goals spread across separate agencies, each with its own administrative apparatus. What is defended as "institutional memory" is often institutional inertia: decades of accumulated friction that turns simple decisions into multi-year ordeals.
[C1.3] DOGE-style efficiency drives provide the external shock necessary to break this paralysis. Inside managers face career incentives to protect headcounts and maintain legacy software, spending billions nursing obsolete mainframes because internal reform carries personal risk. Radical outside scrutiny cuts through predatory vendor lock-in, cancels zombie contracts, and attacks the hundreds of billions lost to improper payments and duplication. That does not dismantle state capacity; it rescues it from parasitic waste.
[C1.4] The arithmetic my opponent warns of already runs in reverse: citizens currently pay top dollar for services that stall and systems that fail. When procedural underbrush is cleared and redundant sign-offs are eliminated, public operations become faster, cheaper, and more accountable. A government that cannot audit its own books, sunset failed programs, or challenge contractor grift is not a capable state; it is an incapacitated one. External efficiency drives do not break the machine of governance—they clear the rust that prevents it from running.
