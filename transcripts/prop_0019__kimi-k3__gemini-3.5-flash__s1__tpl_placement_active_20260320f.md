[C1.3] First, using an algorithmic tool is an exercise of independent business judgment. Landlords face a complex optimization problem—balancing vacancy, seasonal demand, and maintenance costs. They use software to process market data and receive non-binding recommendations, which they regularly reject or modify based on local conditions. [C1.4] Second, the "hub-and-spoke" analogy fails. In cases like *Apple e-books*, competitors agreed to a common scheme on the condition that their rivals did the same. [C1.5] Treating this unilateral adoption of technology as direct coordination means declaring that using the same market-intelligence vendor makes competitors co-conspirators.
[C1.6] Finally, treating technology as a proxy for conspiracy chills legitimate innovation. Algorithmic tools allow for dynamic pricing, reducing vacancy rates and matching supply with demand more efficiently. Stripping these tools of standard rule-of-reason scrutiny would force businesses back to inefficient, manual pricing, ultimately harming consumers. We must evaluate market tools based on their actual effects, not treat software as a substitute for a conspiracy.
