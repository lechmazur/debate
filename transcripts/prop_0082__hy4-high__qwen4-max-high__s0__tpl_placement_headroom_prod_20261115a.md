[P1.1] Dating apps are not slot machines with profiles attached; they are matchmaking intermediaries. Their promise is plausible compatibility and trustworthy access to other people, not maximum screen time. A swipe design optimized for sessions rewards speed, volume, and return visits; good matching requires attention, context, and the ability to stop looking. Infinite queues, rapid yes/no judgments, and variable rewards train users to treat people as inventory.
[P1.2] Even voluntary swipes are shaped by an architecture that produces more swipes, not more compatible conversations.
[P1.3] So the proposition is deliberately narrow. It does not ban discovery, ranking, subscriptions, or all promotion. It says apps should limit versions of swiping and paid visibility that corrode the service being sold. Caps on endless swiping, friction before low-information decisions, clear labels for promoted profiles, and rules keeping paid boosts from posing as organic fit are ordinary product constraints.
[P1.4] Success should be judged by mutually interested conversations, meetings users do not regret, and confidence that the platform is not manipulating them.
[P1.5] Paid boosts pose a distinct trust injury. Match-pool exposure is not ordinary advertising space; users read prominence as relevance, reciprocity, or interest. Once that signal can be quietly bought, a promoted profile may collect low-intent clicks, while everyone else wonders whether attention was purchased. The suspicion does not stay with the buyer.
[P1.6] Rejection feels manipulated, success feels paywalled, and ordinary browsing feels like a rigged queue. The platform profits from uncertainty about whether it is helping people meet or selling access to their attention.
[P1.7] Autonomy misses the asymmetry. Users can ignore boosts or leave, but cannot see the optimization target, separate paid prominence from fit, or compare durable-match design with repeat-frustration design. Attention can be monetized; trust cannot be degraded as the price. Romance markets fail when the intermediary profits from lonely customers.
