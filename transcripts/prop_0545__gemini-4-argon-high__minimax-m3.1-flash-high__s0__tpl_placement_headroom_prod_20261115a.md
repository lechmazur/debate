[P1.1] Every day, patients die on waiting lists not because society is selfish, but because human beings procrastinate. [C1.1] The proposition joins three claims: more organs are recovered, transplants rise, and neither ethical violations nor family distress increase. Evidence for the first would not prove the others. “For most patients” is also doing unjustified work: public approval of donation does not show that most dying patients would have chosen it, and only a fraction of deaths produce organs suitable for transplantation.
[C1.2] Presumed consent does not eliminate decision-making; it relocates the risk of a mistaken default onto patients and families. A right to refuse is meaningful only if people know the system, can act on it, and have that choice respected. Language barriers, digital exclusion, incomplete records, sudden death, and administrative failures all leave people who intended no donation formally “consented.” In such cases, clinicians may proceed on a legal fiction while the family never knew the deceased’s wishes. Calling silence consent does not establish informed consent or erase the ethical significance of bodily integrity and surrogate decision-making.
[C1.3] The family-distress claim is especially backwards. Opt-out removes the request to authorize recovery, but not the shock of discovering that recovery was presumed. Relatives may be denied the conversation they need to understand the death and may experience surprise, conflict, or retrospective doubt because the default displaced information about the patient’s values. A quieter burden can still be heavier when it denies closure.
[C1.4] Finally, additional organs are not automatically additional transplants. Donation depends on organ quality, medical suitability, matching, allocation, surgery capacity, and post-transplant care. Presumed consent may improve supply where infrastructure does not constrain it. Some opt-out systems have achieved more donation, but their experience cannot establish this motion’s broad claims.
[C1.5] The proposition therefore requires proof that gains reach transplant rates without ethical violations or distress; plausibility about supply alone is insufficient.

[C2.1] PRO’s central move is to equate majority approval with what individual patients want, then treat any default as burdenless because objection can be registered. Neither follows. General support for donation may predict choices by many, but it does not establish consent by a specific patient. “Most patients” cannot substitute for individual authorization, and benefiting the majority does not make every recovery ethically sound.
[C2.2] PRO also reverses the administrative-burden argument selectively. It says opt-in frustrates the many, while opt-out frustrates only the few, but which default misfires more depends on public knowledge, registration quality, and actual refusal rates—not on a bare assumption that approval equals informed choice. PRO’s proposed safeguards still rely on families to identify objections during a crisis. If relatives can block recovery, then a familiar relative’s uncertainty or guilt may become the effective veto; if they cannot, the “exit” is less real than claimed.
[C2.3] The family-distress claim is also unsupported. A conversation framed around a presumed yes can feel less demanding than soliciting a fresh “yes,” but it can also replace agency with confrontation: the family must either produce evidence that the deceased objected or acquiesce in recovery. That may be comfortable for some relatives and distressing for others. PRO offers no measurement showing that the change reduces acute distress, conflict, or later regret across most bereaved families; it only asserts that clinicians will still speak with them.
[C2.4] Finally, supply is not the same as transplantation. Organ quality, medical suitability, allocation, surgery slots, and post-transplant care determine whether recovered organs become transplants. The existence of waiting lists does not prove that all listed barriers are absent. Even if presumed consent raises recoveries, PRO has not established the motion’s causal leap to measurable transplant gains, much less that the gains occur “for most patients.”
