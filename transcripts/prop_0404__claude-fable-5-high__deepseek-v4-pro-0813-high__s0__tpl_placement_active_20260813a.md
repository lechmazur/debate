[P1.1] An identity document has one job: to accurately describe the person carrying it. When the state forces a nonbinary person to carry a card that misdescribes them, the document fails at its own purpose — and it fails by government design. The question is not whether the state should invent new identities; it is whether official paperwork should tell the truth about people who already exist.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.3] First, accuracy and function. Nonbinary people are real, present in every society, and often visibly nonconforming. An ID that says "M" or "F" when neither matches the person's identity or appearance creates friction at exactly the moments IDs matter — airports, traffic stops, bank counters, polling places. Officials see a mismatch and suspect fraud; the holder faces interrogation, denial of service, or worse.
[P1.4] An "X" marker resolves the discrepancy the binary system itself creates. This is why courts and legislatures across jurisdictions as different as Germany, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and a growing number of U.S. states — plus U.S. passports for a time — have adopted it. The mechanics are proven, cheap, and boring: one additional value in a database field.
[P1.5] Second, equal citizenship. The state issues identity documents as a monopoly; you cannot opt out. When government compels every citizen to accept one of two labels, it conscripts nonbinary people into a daily, state-authored misstatement about themselves. That is a dignitary harm no comparable group is asked to bear, and it is entirely avoidable.
[P1.6] Third, the asymmetry of stakes. Recognition costs the majority nothing — no one's marker changes, no one's rights shrink. Denial costs a minority safety, privacy, and standing before the law. When one side of the ledger reads "minor administrative update" and the other reads "systematic misidentification of real people," the choice is not close.
[P1.7] CON must show a concrete harm that outweighs these gains. Speculative discomfort will not do it.
