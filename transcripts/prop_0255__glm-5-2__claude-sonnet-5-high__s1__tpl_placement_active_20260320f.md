[P1.1] The proposition is narrow and precise: it targets digital traces that citizens have either deleted or never made public. This is not a sweeping ban on data brokerage. It is a line drawn around information that people have actively tried to remove from circulation or never intended to share.
[P1.2] When someone deletes a post, closes an account, or removes a listing, they are performing an act of consent withdrawal. Archiving that material and selling it is not merely a technical capability—it is the commercial negation of a person's autonomous decision. The broker's business model, in this slice, depends on overriding the very choice that makes deletion meaningful.
[P1.3] The harms are concrete. A person who removed an old address, an old photo, or an abandoned opinion should not face a hidden marketplace that has preserved it for sale to employers, insurers, investigators, or stalkers. The chilling effect compounds the damage: if deletion is theatrical rather than substantive, every digital choice carries permanent, irreversible stakes. People moderate their behavior accordingly—less candor, less experimentation, less willingness to engage online at all.
[P1.4] Why prohibition rather than disclosure or opt-out? Because the market failure is structural. Citizens cannot monitor what they cannot see, and they cannot opt out of systems they cannot identify. A disclosure regime asks people to police a shadow market they have no practical access to.
[P1.5] Prohibition is the only instrument that matches the asymmetry: the burden shifts from millions of unaware individuals to the handful of firms profiting from material people tried to discard.
[P1.6] The opposition will invoke legitimate uses—law enforcement, fraud prevention. But the proposition targets deleted and non-public traces specifically. Nothing here prevents access to genuinely public records or voluntarily shared data. The line is clear: if you tried to remove it, or never put it out there, it should not be for sale.
