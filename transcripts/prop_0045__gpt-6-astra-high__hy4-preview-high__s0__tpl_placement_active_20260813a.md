[P1.1] Employers should judge people by what they can do and how they actually work—not by personality profiles that promise more than they predict. The central question is not whether a profile feels insightful. It is whether it adds enough reliable, job-relevant information to justify influencing somebody’s opportunities or a team’s expectations.
[P1.2] Tools such as MBTI and DISC turn self-reported preferences into apparently authoritative descriptions. But describing how someone sees themselves is not the same as predicting performance. People behave differently across tasks, incentives, and relationships. In hiring, applicants also have an obvious reason to give desirable answers.
[P1.3] Type-based systems compound that problem by drawing categorical boundaries across differences that are often matters of degree. Two similar applicants can acquire different labels, and those labels can then acquire unwarranted significance.
[P1.4] Personality can matter without personality testing being a good employment practice. Even where a trait correlates with performance, employers need more: meaningful predictive value beyond the information available through job-relevant assessment. A candidate’s response to a realistic customer complaint tells you more directly about handling that situation than calling them a particular personality type. Structured interviews, work samples, and observed collaboration tie judgments to conduct applicants can demonstrate and employers can explain.
[P1.5] Team-building does not rescue weak tests by lowering the stakes. A label can shape who gets asked to lead, who is trusted with clients, or whose disagreement is dismissed as “just their personality.” That is consequential even without a formal hiring score. If the benefit is merely starting a conversation, ask employees directly how they prefer feedback, manage interruptions, and resolve disagreements. You get the conversation without presenting a speculative profile as knowledge.
[P1.6] The choice is not personality tests or managerial guesswork. It is weak proxies or evidence closer to the work. Employers should stop using these tests because their limited predictive value does not justify the authority they acquire—or the better assessment and communication practices they displace.
