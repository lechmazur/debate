[P1.1] Imagine a rider pays $38 for an airport trip believing most of it goes to the person driving her. The driver sees $19 and assumes the rider paid close to that. Only the platform knows they are in different transactions — and that ignorance is profitable.
[P1.2] That gap is no accident. With upfront and algorithmic pricing, the rider’s charge can reflect demand, route, and predicted willingness to pay, while driver pay follows a separate, shifting formula. The platform can raise one without raising the other, or cut driver pay without lowering the rider’s price, and neither side sees the widening margin.
[P1.3] The requirement is modest: show the full split by default, on the driver’s offer and earnings screen and the rider’s receipt. Total paid; amount to the driver; amount kept by the platform; taxes, tolls, and insurance broken out; tip shown separately. That is not publishing source code, banning surge pricing, or fixing wages. It is disclosing the outcome of a transaction to the two people who made it happen.
[P1.4] Disclosure changes real decisions. Drivers deciding whether to accept a trip, stay online, or switch services need to know whether low earnings reflect weak demand or a larger platform take. Riders deciding whether a fare is fair or whether surge is actually compensating the driver need the same fact. Without it, riders resent prices drivers never received, drivers chase bonuses masking falling base pay, and platforms compete on visible prices while quietly adjusting the invisible margin.
[P1.5] If a platform’s share pays for matching, safety, insurance, and reliable service, it should be able to show that share and defend it. Secrecy is only necessary when the split would change what riders would pay or drivers would accept. A market where the intermediary knows everything and both other parties know almost nothing is not efficient or free — it is managed ignorance, and default transparency is the minimum correction.

[C1.1] Start with the $38 airport trip and the $19 driver. The motion assumes the missing $19 is "the platform's take." It isn't. That trip was priced upfront to the rider before anyone knew which driver would accept it, whether traffic would double the time, whether the rider would cancel, or whether a promotion was running.
[C1.2] The platform absorbed all of that. Across a day, the same platform pays guaranteed minimums on dead trips, eats the loss when a fixed fare meets a wreck on the freeway, funds insurance per mile, and covers the rider who disputes the charge. A per-trip "split" shown on a screen is therefore not a disclosure of a fact. It is a manufactured number that looks like a fact, and the motion wants it mandated by default.
[C1.3] That matters because false precision changes behavior worse than opacity does. Give drivers a per-trip "platform kept $19" line and the rational move is to decline every trip where the visible margin looks fat relative to their cut, and hunt the ones where the platform appears to be losing. Short trips to low-income neighborhoods, airport queues, and pooled rides are exactly where the visible gap looks ugliest, because pooled pricing subsidizes them from elsewhere. The predictable result is longer waits where service is already thinnest.
[C1.4] Riders, meanwhile, learn the driver got half and start offering cash off-app, outside insurance and background checks. Transparency theater does not fix the margin; it dismantles the pool that lets the service exist.
[C1.5] There is a real problem underneath this debate: whether drivers are paid enough and whether platform margins are excessive. The tools that answer that are pay floors per mile and minute, audited aggregate disclosure to regulators, and giving drivers portable access to their own earnings data. Those attack the margin itself. A mandatory two-line receipt attacks nothing; it just hands both parties a number to resent, invites platforms to redefine "driver pay" to make the line look good, and tells competitors the pricing strategy in real time.
[C1.6] Disclose the aggregate. Regulate [clipped]

[P2.5] The off-app warning is equally backwards. Riders go around a platform when they believe its hidden cut buys them nothing.
