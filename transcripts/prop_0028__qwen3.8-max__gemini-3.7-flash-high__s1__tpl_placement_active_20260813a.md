[P1.1] Mass university expansion did not create more high-skilled work in proportion to degrees; it made the degree the price of admission to a fixed queue. When enrollment surges, employers respond to the surplus by raising credential requirements, not by inventing better jobs. A bachelor’s degree becomes what a high-school diploma once was, and jobs in administration, sales, public-facing service, and routine management begin to demand degrees they did not need before. That is credential inflation: the signaling threshold rises while the actual opportunity set barely moves.
[P1.2] The key test is not how many people enter university, but whether those who enter obtain better positions than they could have obtained without the credential race. Expansion encourages underemployment: graduates compete downward with non-graduates, pushing the latter further down the queue and locking everyone into longer, costlier preparation for unchanged positions. The result is a treadmill, not a ladder. If three applicants need a degree to be considered for the same clerkship, society has not gained three more opportunities; it has raised the entry fee.
[P1.3] This harm is deepest for the people expansion claims to help. Students from lower-income backgrounds are more likely to attend weaker institutions, borrow more, take longer to finish, and carry debt even when they do not land degree-level work. Meanwhile elite degrees keep their scarcity value, so the system reproduces hierarchy while pretending to democratize access. The policy response becomes self-defeating: because ordinary routes to stable employment have been starved of status, university is treated as the only respectable path, further inflating credentials.
[P1.4] The decisive comparison is between expanded enrollment and expanded genuine opportunity. More people can attend university and still face a labor market that rewards rank and certificates rather than creating broader mobility. Because mass expansion primarily revalued credentials upward instead of expanding real access to secure, well-paid work, the proposition stands.
