[P1.1] Dominant platforms buy the firms most likely to threaten them, then ask regulators to prove a future that the deal itself is designed to erase. That burden is backwards. When a firm already dominates a digital market, its acquisition of a nascent or adjacent competitor should be presumed anti-competitive unless the buyer proves substantial consumer benefit.
[P1.2] Digital markets tip. Network effects, data advantages, and default distribution make a second place fragile and a first place self-reinforcing. A challenger rarely needs to be a full substitute on day one; it needs a foothold, a user base, or a product path the incumbent has not yet closed. Buying that foothold is cheap next to the value of undisturbed rents.
[P1.3] Facebook’s purchases of Instagram and WhatsApp did not simply add features. They removed the two most plausible mobile threats to its social graph before either could discipline it. Serial acquisitions in advertising technology, mapping, and cloud tools have done the same work more quietly: potential constraints become internal assets, and the competitive horizon gets shorter.
[P1.4] The buyer knows which deals matter. It sees the overlap in users, the trajectory of the target, and whether promised efficiencies are real or ornamental. Agencies reviewing a stream of transactions cannot reconstruct that private information before the integration is done and the alternative product path is gone. A presumption does not outlaw acquisition.
[P1.5] It puts the cost of uncertainty on the party that created it and that profits if the uncertainty is resolved in favor of entrenchment.
[P1.6] Waiting for courtroom-ready proof of harm misses the harm that matters. Prices need not rise next quarter. The loss is the rival that is never scaled, the privacy or fee standard that is never competed upward, the innovation that dies inside the acquirer because it would have cannibalized the core. By the time that loss is obvious, the market has already tipped.
[P1.7] Substantial consumer benefit—real price, quality, or innovation gains that would not otherwise occur—can still clear the presumption. Shareholder convenience and speculative slide-deck synergies should not.

[C1.1] A blanket legal presumption of anti-competitiveness misunderstands how digital innovation actually functions. By treating every acquisition by a leading platform as guilty until proven innocent, PRO advocates a cure far more destructive than the disease: a de facto freeze on tech investment that starves startups of capital and denies consumers the benefits of rapid scale.
[C1.2] First, acquisitions are the lifeblood of startup creation. Entrepreneurs take enormous risks and venture capitalists fund speculative ideas largely because acquisition provides a viable exit. In technology, the overwhelming majority of successful exits occur through acquisition, not public offerings. If dominant platforms face a presumption of illegality, acquiring early-stage firms becomes a legal minefield.
[C1.3] When you eliminate the exit, you choke off seed funding at the root. The vibrant innovators PRO claims to champion will simply never be funded in the first place.
[C1.4] Second, PRO’s required defense—proving "substantial consumer benefit" in advance—is an evidentiary trap. Nascent startups often possess promising code or niche talent, not finished mass-market products. Their consumer benefit is unlocked only when combined with an incumbent’s compute, distribution, and security infrastructure. When Google acquired Android, it was an obscure startup with zero revenue; scaled through Google’s ecosystem, it became the free, open-source operating system powering billions of devices worldwide.
[C1.5] Under PRO’s presumption, that acquisition would have been blocked: an unproven startup cannot legally guarantee future consumer gains before integration even happens.
[C1.6] Regulators already possess the statutory authority to investigate, challenge, and block genuinely anti-competitive mergers under traditional antitrust standards. Inverting the burden of proof does not refine enforcement; it replaces targeted scrutiny with a blunt embargo. The result is a stagnant ecosystem where founders cannot sell, platforms cannot integrate complementary features, and consumers are deprived of the next generation of scalable technology.

[P4.1] A1: Not a finished product, and not a slide deck. [P1.7] rejected convenience dressed as synergy, not every forward-looking fact. Admissible proof is what the buyer already holds and can be held to: engineering records that the combination yields a gain the target cannot reach alone; a dated mechanism for price, quality, or coverage; and binding commitments—lower prices, open licensing, interoperability, hold-separate—enforceable if the gain fails. If a license or a non-dominant buyer can deliver the same gain, dominant ownership is not a consumer benefit.
[P4.2] Lack of revenue is not fatal, and the startup need not prophesy every feature. The acquirer must show an otherwise unavailable gain and accept conditions that make it real. That is a burden, not a ban. No mechanism means the deal’s value is the rival path it erases.
[P4.3] A2: Scale is not a prerequisite for classification. The standard is competitive constraint, using evidence available now: overlap in users, use cases, data, or distribution; a plausible path into the dominated market; the buyer’s documents on threat versus gap-filling; and whether another buyer or a contract could scale the tool without cementing the incumbent. Where there is no overlap and no foreclosure path, the benefits showing is straightforward and the complement clears. A foothold into the core does not clear merely because it would also be useful inside the platform.
[P4.4] [P2.3] and [P2.8] use ordinary potential-competition and foreclosure tests. The presumption changes who produces the record, not the tests.
[P4.5] Those answers end the Catch-22. The showing is mechanism, necessity, and commitment, not prophecy, and the complement-threat line is overlap, documents, and alternative paths. The buyer holds that file. Waiting for outsiders to prove a future the deal erases lets the market tip, then treats the missing rival as proof none existed.
[P4.6] A false positive can be conditioned, proved, or sold elsewhere. A swallowed challenger cannot. The burden belongs where the profit from entrenchment sits.
