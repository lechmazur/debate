[P1.1] Access to life‑saving medicines must not be held hostage by price gouging. When a drug’s cost exceeds what a country’s citizens can reasonably bear, compulsory licensing is the fastest, legally sanctioned tool to restore affordability. The proposition sets a clear, objective affordability threshold—say, a price that would require more than five percent of average per‑capita income for a full treatment course. Above that line, governments can issue a compulsory licence, allow local manufacturers to produce generics, and drive prices down without waiting for voluntary negotiations.
[P1.2] During the HIV crisis, Brazil and Thailand invoked compulsory licences for antiretrovirals, slashing prices by up to 90 % and saving millions of lives. More recently, several low‑ and middle‑income countries used the same tool for hepatitis C drugs, turning a $1,000‑a‑day therapy into a $50‑a‑day regimen. These cases show price‑triggered licences curb monopoly profiteering without killing innovation.
[P1.3] Consider insulin: in the United States a vial that costs $300 in Europe can exceed $1,000, pushing millions of diabetics into rationing. A threshold tied to per‑capita GDP would automatically trigger licences, enabling local production and cutting costs by 80 % within a year. India’s compulsory licence for a cancer drug reduced price from $10,000 to $200, proving the model works across disease areas.
[P1.4] Critics claim any compulsory licence deters research. But the threshold protects incentives: licences are only triggered when prices are demonstrably unaffordable, not when they reflect normal returns. Moreover, the bulk of drug discovery is publicly funded; the private sector already earns billions from patents. A price ceiling that activates only in extreme cases preserves the reward structure while guaranteeing that no one dies because a corporation set a price above what a nation can pay.
[P1.5] Thus a defined affordability threshold creates a predictable, rights‑based safety valve. It forces the market to align with the fundamental right to health, encourages competition, and does so without dismantling the patent system. Countries that adopt this rule will see lower prices, broader access, and stronger public‑health outcomes.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds humane, but it rests on a single confusion that I want to make the spine of this debate: **price is not the same as access, and a price trigger is not an access policy.**
[C1.2] Notice what the motion actually demands. Not case-by-case judgment—it already exists under TRIPS, and Brazil, Thailand, and India used it precisely that way. The motion demands an *automatic* rule: cross a defined affordability line, and a compulsory licence becomes warranted. That automaticity is the whole proposition, and it is its fatal flaw.
[C1.3] First, the trigger is miscalibrated by design. My opponent suggests "more than five percent of per-capita income." In a low-income country, that line is crossed by almost every patented medicine—cancer drugs, new diabetes therapies, novel antibiotics. So the "extreme cases" framing collapses.
[C1.4] This is not a narrow safety valve; it is a standing announcement that in poor and middle-income markets, patents are presumptively void. That is exactly the signal that pushes firms to delay launches, skip registration, and write off those markets entirely—leaving patients with no drug to license at all.
[C1.5] Second, a licence is a piece of paper, not a pill. Compulsory licensing only helps where domestic manufacturing capacity, quality regulation, active-ingredient supply, and distribution already exist. India could make generics because India is a pharmaceutical superpower. For most countries crossing the threshold, the binding constraint is capacity, not patents—and the motion does nothing about that while picking a costly fight with innovators.
[C1.6] Third, the motion crowds out better tools. Tiered pricing, voluntary licensing through patent pools, pooled procurement, and donor financing have delivered access *without* nuking investment incentives. The hepatitis C and HIV gains my opponent cites came substantially through negotiated voluntary licences, not just compulsory ones.
[C1.7] So the comparison is this: a rigid formula that overreaches, often can't be used, and discourages the very supply it needs—versus targeted judgment that already exists. Keep the judgment. Reject the formula.
