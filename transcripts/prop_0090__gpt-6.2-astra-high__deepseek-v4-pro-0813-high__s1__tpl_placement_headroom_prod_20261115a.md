[C1.1] This proposition confuses a control surface with a safety outcome. It assumes a touchscreen must be slow, deep, and visual, while physical controls are naturally glance-free. Neither is true. A row of similar black buttons or knobs can be hard to identify by feel, and many physical climate dials still require a glance at a small display to confirm the setting.
[C1.2] The better standard is not the material under the fingertip but how much attention the task demands and how often the driver must do it at all.
[C1.3] Touchscreens can meet that standard when they are designed for driving. Persistent, thumb-reachable edge controls, large targets, and haptic detents can reproduce the find-adjust-confirm sequence without a menu. Voice commands for defrost or temperature keep both hands on the wheel and eyes up—something buttons cannot do by themselves. Automatic climate can clear fog before the driver intervenes, so the scarce attention resource is used less often.
[C1.4] When hazard access is urgent, a hard-wired capacitive control with a fixed position and distinctive texture can be direct without being a physical button.
[C1.5] Flexibility is also a safety asset. A touchscreen layout can be updated after a model is already built: targets can be enlarged, poor layouts fixed, controls moved higher for visibility, and obsolete buttons removed instead of crowding the panel. Physical controls are still frozen at the factory. Mandating buttons protects yesterday's design, not tomorrow's driver.
[C1.6] This does not excuse poor hierarchy or slow response. Regulators and testers should focus on failure-to-access time, visual demand, and resilience, not on preserving a particular switch. The goal is direct, unambiguous control; whether it is a button, haptic surface, or voice command is largely incidental.
