[P1.1] An actor's performance is not raw footage. It is their face, their voice, their choices: the one thing they bring to market that nobody else can. When a studio uses AI to make a sixty-year-old look thirty, or synthesizes lines the actor never spoke, it is no longer editing that performance. It is manufacturing a new one under their name.
[P1.2] Should a studio do that without asking and without paying? No, for three reasons.
[P1.3] First, consent tracks authorship. Ordinary editing, like cutting a take or color grading, selects from what the actor actually did. Material AI alteration creates what the actor did not do. Put words in someone's synthetic mouth and audiences still hold the actor responsible for the line reading, the crude joke, the intimate scene.
[P1.4] Their reputation absorbs the risk of choices they never made. Nobody should carry public accountability for a performance they never authorized.
[P1.5] Second, compensation tracks substitution. These tools are not neutral polish; they replace labor. A voice model that can record pickups, foreign dubs, and sequel dialogue means the actor never needs to be hired back. If studios extract that value for free, the actor's own likeness becomes the instrument that undercuts their future work.
[P1.6] Paying for that use is not a windfall. It is paying for the thing being sold.
[P1.7] Third, bargaining power is wildly unequal, so "leave it to contracts" fails. A working actor, not a star, signs boilerplate granting rights in media "now known or hereafter devised." That clause was never meaningful consent to technology that didn't exist when the ink dried. Explicit, specific consent closes exactly that gap.
[P1.8] That is why AI consent and pay became a central fight in the 2023 actors' strike. The people closest to the problem saw it clearly.
[P1.9] Notice what we are not asking for: no ban, no veto over routine editing, living actors only. Just ask, explain, pay. If a studio's business model collapses under the duty to request permission before rebuilding someone's face, the problem is the model, not the rule.
