[P1.1] College degrees have become a default gatekeeper for jobs that do not need them. Employers should stop requiring those degrees when degree completion is not genuinely necessary for performance. The practice is inefficient, exclusionary, and often unrelated to the competence required in the actual work.
[P1.2] Consider common roles such as office administration, retail and restaurant management, many IT help-desk and support positions, logistics coordinators, sales representatives, and customer success jobs. Success in these positions turns on reliability, clear communication, judgment under pressure, trainable technical familiarity, and process knowledge. People develop those capacities through prior employment, military service, certificates, community programs, online courses, apprenticeships, or work portfolios. A four-year bachelor’s degree frequently adds little unique job-specific preparation for these tasks.
[P1.3] Instead it functions as a blunt sorting mechanism that advantages candidates who could afford the years of tuition and lost earnings.
[P1.4] The result is degree inflation that locks out talent and distorts labor markets. Employers artificially constrain their applicant pools and then struggle to fill openings. Capable workers are steered into student debt or delayed entry into careers for a credential that rarely predicts superior day-to-day performance in these roles. Skills-based approaches—targeted assessments, work samples, structured interviews, and verification of relevant experience—measure what matters more directly than a diploma.
[P1.5] Employers that have dropped unnecessary degree screens report accessing wider, more diverse, and often more motivated talent without quality loss.
[P1.6] Where specialized knowledge or licensed practice must be certified—physicians, structural engineers, attorneys—degree requirements make sense and should remain. Elsewhere they should not. Forcing unnecessary credentials harms economic mobility, inflates education costs, and muddies the efficient match between people and productive work. When a degree is not genuinely necessary for performance, the rational choice is clear: employers should drop the requirement and hire for what the job actually demands.
[P1.7] Performance, not pedigree, should decide.
