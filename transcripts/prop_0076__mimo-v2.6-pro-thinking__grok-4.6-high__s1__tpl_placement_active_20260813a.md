[P1.1] Long-term care is the archetypal risk that markets and families cannot pool on their own. The cost is lumpy, long-tailed, and wildly unpredictable: most people need little, a minority need a decade of round-the-clock dementia care that can consume an entire estate. No private savings plan tells an 80-year-old whether she should budget for two years or twelve. Insurance markets for this risk chronically fail — adverse selection, uncertainty about lifetime cost and duration, and moral hazard mean that private long-term care insurance has stayed niche and expensive everywhere it has been tried.
[P1.2] That is not an accident. It is the predictable result of trying to privatize a catastrophic, correlated, hard-to-price risk. Only a universal risk pool, backed by the state's ability to tax across the lifecycle and cohort, can spread that cost.
[P1.3] The family fallback is failing on the facts. Families are smaller, more geographically dispersed, and more often two-income than when this assumption was written into policy. The hidden price of "family care" is disproportionately paid by daughters who cut working hours, lose earnings and pensions, and burn out. Dementia prevalence is rising with longevity, so demand for the heaviest care keeps growing.
[P1.4] Shifting this to households does not eliminate cost — it hides it in unpaid labor and destroyed careers, and it falls on exactly those least able to bear it.
[P1.5] Means testing sounds targeted but delivers the opposite of security. It creates hard income and asset cliffs, punishes prudence and saving, forces elderly couples to impoverish one spouse to qualify the other, and generates costly, stigmatizing application bureaucracies that the frailest people are worst at navigating. Worst of all, it turns care into a stigmatized welfare good for "the poor," which drains political support and produces exactly the chronic under-funding we see in residual systems.
[P1.6] Universal systems — whether tax-financed like the Nordic model or social-insurance financed like Germany's and Japan's — do the things a private-and-family system cannot: pool catastrophic risk, fund professional care workers and quality standards, pay informal carers or give them respite, [clipped]

[C1.3] A near-universal promise turns that arithmetic into an entitlement. Workers then pay twice — in taxes, and when public money crowds out wages, housing, and children. That is not lifecycle insurance. It is a political claim on people who have not voted yet.
[C1.4] Family care is not a failure. Most older people prefer home and known faces. Relatives already supply most help, even where social insurance exists. [C1.5] Formal care does not lift the burden from daughters; it taxes their wages to hire other women the system then underpays. [C1.6] Means testing is blunt. Universalism spends on people who can pay, then rations anyway through wait lists, thin staffing, and institutions. [C1.7] Government cannot legislate extra nurses into existence, and high-tax models do not travel to aging societies with weaker public finances.
