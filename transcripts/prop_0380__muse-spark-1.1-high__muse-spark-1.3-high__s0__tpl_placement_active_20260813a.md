[P1.1] At sixteen you can work forty hours, pay income tax, drive a car, consent to medical care, and in many places be tried as an adult. Your school funding, curriculum, and whether your city invests in buses or bullets are set by elected officials. Yet you cannot vote for any of them. A country that taxes labor, regulates bodies, and mortgages the future in a teenager's name while denying them the ballot is not protecting democracy; it is violating the oldest democratic principle: no taxation without representation, no regulation without voice.
[P1.2] The objection is always maturity. But eighteen is not a neurological finish line. Cold cognition — the ability to understand policy, weigh tradeoffs, remember facts — is essentially mature by sixteen. What develops later is impulse control in hot contexts, which is why we restrict drinking and not voting.
[P1.3] If ignorance disqualified voters, half the adult electorate would be removed. On basic political knowledge tests, American sixteen-year-olds perform on par with eighteen-year-olds and often better than older adults outside school.
[P1.4] Lowering the age also makes democracy work better. Voting is a habit. Catch people at sixteen, when they still live at home, are in a civics classroom, and can be registered by their school, and you create lifelong voters. Austria, which lowered the age for all elections, and Scotland in its independence referendum, saw sixteen and seventeen-year-olds turn out at rates at or above older first-time voters, and they narrowed class gaps in participation because schools can reach everyone, unlike the chaos of turning eighteen and moving away.
[P1.5] Finally, sixteen-year-olds have the greatest stake in what elections decide now — climate, debt, pensions, wars fought by their generation. It is perverse to let those with the shortest future veto the longest future. Inclusion is the lower-risk mistake.

[C1.3] Sixteen-year-olds generally cannot. The claim that cold factual reasoning is mature by sixteen misses what voting actually is. Voting is not a classroom quiz. It is hot, identity-laden, socially pressured choice, made amid peer pressure, viral manipulation, and partisan appeals.
[C1.4] That is precisely where adolescents are most vulnerable. [C1.5] And the motion demands all elections — presidents, parliaments, wars, constitutions — not a mock school-board exercise. [C1.6] Eighteen is imperfect, but it marks a coherent line: legal adulthood, contractual capacity, criminal responsibility, military eligibility. Keep that line. Adding millions of dependent minors with the longest futures but the least independence does not protect democracy; it distorts it by empowering those least able to answer for their choices.

[C2.1] Calling voting 'the coldest political act we have' [P2.3] mistakes the last five minutes for the whole choice. The mark is secret; the mind is not. Opinions form over weeks in group chats, hallways, feeds, and at the dinner table — precisely the hot, status-driven settings where sixteen-year-olds conform most. A curtain around the booth cannot un-pressure a preference adopted to please parents, teachers, or friends.
[C2.2] Yes, adults have bosses, spouses, and algorithms [P2.4]. That is a reason to hold the line where suggestibility peaks, not erase it. Conformity, sensitivity to peer approval, and impulsive reward-seeking do not decline linearly; they spike in mid-adolescence. Social platforms already farm teenagers as the most persuadable consumers.
[C2.3] Enfranchise them for presidencies, parliaments, and constitutions, and you invite farming them as the most persuadable citizens.
[C2.4] Dependence is not just living at home [P2.5]. Most eighteen-year-olds may still sleep in their childhood bedroom, but they can legally leave it — sign a lease, sign a contract, refuse a parent, enlist, be sued in their own name. A sixteen-year-old generally cannot. That legal inability to exit is what makes independence of judgment fictional, even with a secret ballot.
[C2.5] Being tried as an adult proves nothing [P2.6]. That is an exception for grave offenses where society withdraws juvenile protection; it is condemnation, not certification of wisdom. And driving or part-time work are supervised, revocable licenses, not powers to tax, conscript, or imprison others.
[C2.6] Eighteen is not magic, but it bundles full legal agency as the rule. Sixteen bundles compulsory schooling and parental custody. Running registration through schools does not neutralize pressure; a teacher cannot see your vote but controls your grade, recommendation, and standing in a classroom where dissent is costly. Turnout bought that way measures compliance, not capacity — a dangerous currency to spend on all elections.
