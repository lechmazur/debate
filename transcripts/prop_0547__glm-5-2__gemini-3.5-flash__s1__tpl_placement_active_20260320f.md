[P1.1] Engagement-optimized feeds are not neutral delivery systems. They are designed to maximize one variable: time on platform. That single optimization target structurally selects content that triggers intense emotional responses, and adolescents are uniquely vulnerable to that selection pressure.
[P1.2] Consider the mechanism. A chronological feed shows what your contacts posted, in order. It has no incentive to surface the clip most likely to keep you scrolling past exhaustion. An engagement-optimized feed learns, often within minutes, what makes a user tap, rewatch, or hesitate.
[P1.3] For a teenager already prone to social comparison, that means the feed rapidly discovers that content about body image, social exclusion, or self-criticism holds attention longest—and serves more of it. Content that calms or satisfies does not generate the same watch time as content that agitates.
[P1.4] Adolescents are not just smaller adults here. The developmental window between roughly 12 and 18 involves heightened sensitivity to social evaluation, identity formation still in flux, and self-regulation circuits not yet mature. When an algorithm repeatedly delivers content that exploits those vulnerabilities—appearance comparison, status anxiety, fear-of-missing-out loops—the harm compounds in ways a chronological feed cannot replicate, because a chronological feed does not selectively amplify.
[P1.5] The comparison is everything. The proposition does not claim non-personalized feeds are harmless. [P1.6] Adolescent anxiety and depression have risen sharply during the exact period these feed architectures became dominant. Correlation alone does not settle causation.
