[P1.1] The operative word is "solely." No one is demanding that Visa process fraud, that Apple host unmoderated uploads, or that a bank ignore chargeback ratios. Those are measurable risks with measurable fixes. Reputational risk is different in kind: it is not a fact about the creator's conduct, it is a forecast of other people's disapproval.
[P1.2] And it is the one standard a lawful business can never satisfy, because compliance is not the test. Being disliked is.
[P1.3] That matters because these are chokepoints, not competitors. A handful of card networks and two mobile app stores sit between a creator and every customer she has. When a payment processor declines a lawful account, there is no rival rail to switch to; when an app store delists, the business does not lose a shelf, it loses the building. Private companies exercising taste at that scale are functionally writing law — without notice, evidence, appeal, or any legislature that can be voted out.
[P1.4] Notice too how the standard behaves in practice. OnlyFans announced a ban on explicit content, then reversed within days when creators and users revolted. The reputational catastrophe was speculative; the harm to livelihoods was immediate. That asymmetry is the whole problem: platforms are permitted to act on a vibe and creators pay in rent.
[P1.5] And reputational risk does not stay in its lane. The same logic reaches sex educators, queer artists, lingerie sellers, health information — anything an advertiser or a moral campaign might find distasteful. A rule that licenses exclusion for anticipated embarrassment is a heckler's veto with a compliance department.
[P1.6] The alternative is not chaos. It is rules: verify consent and age, police chargebacks, enforce content boundaries, publish the criteria, offer appeal. Deny on evidence, never on nerves.
