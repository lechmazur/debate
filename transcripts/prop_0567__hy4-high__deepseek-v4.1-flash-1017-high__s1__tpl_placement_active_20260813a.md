[P1.1] Courts should start from a simple principle: if a proceeding is public, the public should be able to see it. A courtroom door open only to those who can travel, take a day off work, and squeeze into a gallery seat is barely open at all. When a community cares about a homicide trial, a police-misconduct civil suit, or a public-corruption case, access becomes a lottery. A fixed camera changes no rules of evidence or procedure; it lets more citizens exercise the same right.
[P1.2] Most is not all: this is permission with judicial control, not compulsory broadcasting.
[P1.3] That matters because trials exercise coercive public power. Courts may imprison, fine, award damages, or judge the lawfulness of official force. Legitimacy cannot rest on a largely unseen ritual and a press summary. A cold transcript cannot fully capture demeanor, timing, objections, exhibits, judicial interventions, or witness treatment.
[P1.4] Cameras also discipline participants: not by making them performers, but by reminding everyone that public authority is being exercised in public.
[P1.5] The usual objections justify safeguards, not a blanket ban. A judge can place one fixed, silent camera at the back, keep jurors and sidebars off the feed, and exclude children, sexual-assault complainants, informants, or protective-order matters from the feed. If danger is concrete, transmission can be delayed or stopped; if a lawyer grandstands, the judge can stop it. Ordinary trials are not made theatrical by being recorded; most are slow, technical, and watched by almost no one.
[P1.6] In the rare notorious case, banning cameras does not prevent saturation coverage. It merely replaces the proceeding itself with sketches, hallway interviews, selective quotations, and rumor.
[P1.7] So the choice is not privacy versus spectacle. It is a visible, rule-bound proceeding with narrow exceptions versus a general blackout that protects neither privacy nor dignity when public attention is intense. Courts confident in their fairness should not ask the public to trust a process most people will never see.

[C1.1] The proposition treats a camera as a neutral window. It is not. A trial is not a show to be distributed; it is a high-stakes search for truth, and cameras change the incentives of everyone in the room. Lawyers learn which lines play well, witnesses know their words may be clipped and replayed forever, jurors know their faces may be examined by strangers, and judges must manage a second audience beyond the courtroom.
[C1.2] That is not discipline; it is distortion.
[C1.3] The access argument proves too little. Courtrooms are already open: galleries, press, transcripts, and appellate records allow public scrutiny. A camera does not merely let a distant citizen see what a visitor could see; it creates a permanent, infinitely reproducible record outside the judge’s control. Once a feed exists, a two-minute clip can turn a routine domestic-violence or gang trial into viral entertainment, exposing a witness’s address, employer, or fear long after the judge’s safeguards end at the courthouse door.
[C1.4] The harm is asymmetric: one clip can destroy a witness’s safety or poison a jury pool, while the marginal viewer gains little beyond what the press already reports.
[C1.5] The question is not whether cameras might be justified in an exceptional public-corruption case; it is whether they become the default in thousands of ordinary cases where privacy and candor matter most. PRO’s answer is judicial discretion: exclude sensitive cases, delay broadcasts, keep jurors off-screen.
[C1.6] But the motion says most proceedings, so the burden falls on the vulnerable to opt out, and judges cannot predict which ordinary moment becomes notorious. A fixed camera may be silent, but it is never passive. It invites performance, deters candid testimony, and substitutes a decontextualized clip for the proceeding itself. Public accountability is important, but it is better served by open doors and reviewable records than by turning every witness into content.
