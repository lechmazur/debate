[C1.2] The nursing homes PE buys are often the ones government and nonprofits have already abandoned.

A facility that cuts staff to the bone faces devastating lawsuits, regulatory sanctions, and empty beds that bleed revenue. The profit motive, properly channeled through market discipline and accreditation standards, aligns with keeping residents healthy, fed, and safe.
[C1.4] Bad actors exist in every ownership model—nonprofit, municipal, and PE alike—and should be punished as individuals, not used to indict an entire capital source.
[C1.5] Consider the alternative the PRO conveniently ignores. When governments restrict PE investment, they don't magically create a robust public option; they create a capital vacuum. Nonprofit and municipal operators often lack the balance sheets for electronic health records, modern HVAC systems, or competitive wages to retain certified nursing assistants. Facilities close.
[C1.6] Waitlists grow. In rural and low-income areas, PE acquisition is frequently the only thing preventing a nursing home from shuttering its doors entirely, leaving residents with nowhere to go and forcing them into distant, unfamiliar facilities.
[C1.7] We should not ban an ownership model because bad actors exist within it. We should enforce existing staffing and safety regulations aggressively, regardless of who holds the deed. Restricting PE ownership doesn't protect elders; it strands them in a system starved of the capital needed to modernize. The real choice isn't between profit and people—it's between investment and abandonment.
