[P1.1] The antibiotic market is broken by design. We need new drugs, yet we also need to use them sparingly. Ordinary sales cannot repay discovery when a last-line agent may sell only a few thousand courses a year. That is why the pipeline has withered and why resistant infections keep outrunning us.
[P1.2] Advance market commitments repair the incentive without pretending the economics have changed. A country pledges to buy a defined quantity of a qualifying new antibiotic at a known price. Developers then have a market they can actually finance. The science remains hard; the return becomes real.
[P1.3] The motion is right to accept that the unit price may stay high. A high per-dose price is not a defect. It is the honest reflection of tiny expected volume plus enormous social value. Cheap antibiotics get sprayed through livestock barns and outpatient clinics.
[P1.4] An expensive reserved agent stays inside the hospital formulary, used when cultures demand it. Stewardship and high price travel together.
[P1.5] Refusing the commitment because the sticker looks ugly is a false economy. A resistant bloodstream infection can erase a chemotherapy protocol, a joint replacement, or a neonatal ICU stay. The cost of that failure is measured in lives and in the collapse of ordinary medicine, not in the invoice for one vial. An AMC can still attach conservation conditions, volume limits, and access clauses for the poorest patients.
[P1.6] What it cannot do is invent a large, profitable market that does not exist.
[P1.7] Permit the commitment, high unit price and all. That is how countries keep a last line of defense from disappearing. The alternative is a future in which we have perfect principles about affordability and no working drugs left to afford.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a financing problem for a pricing problem. Antibiotic innovation needs reliable funding, not permission for health systems to pay extortionate unit prices for drugs that may then be inaccessible. Advance market commitments with high unit prices solve the first problem badly while creating worse second-order failures.
[C1.2] First, high unit prices corrupt stewardship. PRO says stewardship and high price travel together [P1.4], but price is a crude gatekeeper. It does not distinguish between a patient who needs a last-line antibiotic and a health system that simply cannot afford it. When prices are high, hospitals delay appropriate therapy, patients receive incomplete or inferior regimens, and access becomes rationing by budget rather than by microbiology.
[C1.3] Conservation should be achieved through diagnostics, formularies, infection control, and prescribing rules—not by making effective medicines unaffordable.
[C1.4] Second, AMCs lock in the very distortion they claim to fix. If governments guarantee purchases at high per-unit prices, they pay twice: once through publicly funded research and again through monopoly returns. They also create moral hazard. Developers are encouraged to chase narrow, high-price indications that qualify for the commitment, not the broad-spectrum, easy-to-deploy, or regionally relevant antibiotics that resistance patterns most urgently need.
[C1.5] A guaranteed buyer weakens the pressure to deliver real public-health value. That is an opportunity cost: money spent on expensive vials is money denied to diagnostics, vaccination, and infection prevention, which reduce resistance more durably.
[C1.6] Third, there are better tools. Subscription payments, prizes, public manufacturing, tiered pricing, and pooled procurement can delink developer revenue from high unit prices. They can finance innovation while keeping marginal prices low enough for rational use and wide access when cultures prove resistance.
[C1.7] The real choice is not between high prices and no drugs. It is between innovation purchased through affordable access and a promise of future drugs bought by accepting present scarcity. Countries should not permit AMCs on those terms.

[C2.7] If the goal is to finance late-stage risk [P2.4], pay for risk directly. Do not bake the reward into a price that keeps effective medicines out of reach.
