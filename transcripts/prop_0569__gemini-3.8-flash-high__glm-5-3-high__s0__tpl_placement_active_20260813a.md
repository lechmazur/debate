[P1.1] Mental-health interventions delivered by AI chatbots mimic clinical empathy without possessing clinical judgment. While an algorithm can process natural language and deliver structured cognitive-behavioral prompts, it cannot evaluate a patient’s safety, recognize somatic comorbidities, or form a genuine therapeutic alliance. When vulnerable individuals interact with a chatbot over weeks or months, they naturally develop parasocial trust. Leaving that relationship open-ended creates a dangerous illusion of treatment, effectively isolating struggling individuals inside an algorithmic echo chamber.
[P1.2] A legally mandated handoff after a fixed number of sessions solves this fundamental structural hazard. It preserves what AI does well while preventing its most catastrophic failure modes. In the earliest sessions, chatbots provide valuable, low-barrier support: rapid psychoeducation, symptom logging, and preliminary coping strategies.
[P1.3] However, as care extends into complex psychological terrain, the risk profile shifts dramatically. Machine learning models are inherently prone to sycophancy, subtle hallucinations, and missed nuance. When an AI misses the quiet signs of escalating suicidality or severe depressive decompensation, there is no professional duty of care to intervene.
[P1.4] A fixed session cap ensures that AI functions as an on-ramp rather than a permanent detour. It prevents tech companies and insurers from offloading complex patients onto cheap, unaccountable software under the guise of therapy. By requiring a handoff to a human professional, the law guarantees that every patient receives a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, real clinical accountability, and a pathway to evidence-based human care before crisis strikes.
[P1.5] Therapy is not merely conversational content; it is a licensed medical relationship bound by ethical and legal obligations that software cannot bear. Mandating a transition ensures AI remains a doorway to care, not a trap that replaces it.
