[P1.1] In almost every sector of the economy, if an institution sells a high-priced product that consistently fails to deliver, that institution bears financial risk. Higher education is the glaring exception. Today, universities collect full tuition upfront, funded by student loans, and walk away entirely insulated from the outcome. If a program leaves graduates buried in debt they cannot possibly repay, the student's financial future is wrecked and the taxpayer absorbs the loss, while the university keeps every dime.
[P1.2] Requiring universities to bear part of the cost of defaults fixes this profound moral hazard. Notice how surgically targeted this policy is. It does not penalize a college for a single unlucky graduating class, a temporary economic downturn, or an individual graduate's life choices. It triggers only when specific programs repeatedly leave graduates with poor earnings relative to debt.
[P1.3] That "relative to debt" standard is the linchpin. This policy does not wage war on the humanities, teaching, or social work, where salaries are naturally modest. Universities can proudly offer those degrees; they simply cannot saddle students with eighty thousand dollars of debt for a credential that reliably pays thirty thousand. Because universities control tuition prices, admission standards, and curriculum quality, they are in the best position to prevent systematic debt traps.
[P1.4] Putting their money on the line forces failing programs to either lower net tuition, invest in better job-placement support, or shut down.
[P1.5] Nor can universities hide behind the banner of student access. Low-income and first-generation students are the primary victims of overpriced, low-value programs because they lack the family wealth to absorb a ruinous educational bet. Enrolling vulnerable students into unpayable debt for a dead-end credential is not opportunity; it is predation. When a university repeatedly sells degrees that do not pay off, it should help pay the bill.

[C1.1] A penalty tied to defaults does not punish bad programs. It punishes programs for enrolling the students most likely to default, and universities will respond accordingly.
[C1.2] Start with who actually defaults. It is rarely the graduate with eighty thousand dollars of debt. It is typically the borrower with a small balance, no family safety net, and often no degree. Default tracks a student's background at least as much as it tracks program quality.
[C1.3] An identical nursing or business program will show very different repayment at a wealthy suburban campus and at an open-access college serving working adults in a depressed region.
[C1.4] Now consider the lever PRO praises. [P1.3] says universities control "admission standards." Exactly. The cheapest way to shrink default liability is not to improve teaching or cut tuition.
[C1.5] It is to stop admitting the single mother, the first-generation applicant, the student from a poor zip code. PRO calls enrolling these students "predation"; this policy's answer is to pay colleges to lock them out. That is redlining by spreadsheet.
[C1.6] Second, the bill lands on the wrong people. A university has no pocket except its students'. Penalties get paid out of tuition increases, cut advising, and shuttered programs, all borne by current students who did nothing wrong. Wealthy universities with large endowments barely notice.
[C1.7] Community colleges, historically Black institutions, and regional publics running on thin margins absorb the blow.
[C1.8] Third, the metric penalizes socially valuable choices. Earnings relative to debt falls when graduates teach in rural schools, do social work, or stay in low-wage hometowns. It also falls when a recession hits a region repeatedly. None of this is curriculum failure.
[C1.9] If a program truly keeps failing, the honest remedy is direct: stop lending for it, cap what can be borrowed, publish its outcomes. PRO instead keeps the loans flowing and sends an invoice afterward, which gives every college a financial reason to fear its poorest applicants.
