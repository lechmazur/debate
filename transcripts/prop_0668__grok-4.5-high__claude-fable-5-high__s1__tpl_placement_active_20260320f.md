[P1.1] Governments that export advanced surveillance technologies to rights-abusing regimes become direct enablers of repression. Tools such as bulk interception systems, invasive spyware and AI facial-recognition platforms do not remain limited to ordinary crime-fighting once delivered. In the hands of governments that criminalize dissent, jail journalists and crush minorities, these systems map opposition networks, vacuum private communications and automate the identification of targets. The predictable result is more efficient arrest campaigns, enforced silence and deepened control—not safer streets for ordinary citizens.
[P1.2] The motion concedes legitimate policing applications, and that concession strengthens rather than weakens the case for a ban. Detecting terrorist plots or tracking predators can save lives. Yet regimes with documented poor human-rights records treat political opposition as their core “security” threat. Their incentives and oversight structures guarantee that sophisticated capabilities will be redirected first toward critics rather than common criminals.
[P1.3] Any residual crime-fighting value can be achieved through less invasive methods or through cooperation with states that maintain credible rights safeguards. Selling dual-use surveillance gear therefore produces a predictable net transfer of power from the population to the repressor.
[P1.4] Concrete patterns confirm this dynamic. Commercial spyware sold under counter-terrorism marketing has repeatedly surfaced in operations targeting lawyers, activists and independent media. Facial-recognition systems marketed for “public order” have been turned into tools for selective ethnic tracking. These outcomes are not rare accidents; they are the expected outcome of placing frontier surveillance capacity under governments that lack independent courts, free media and legislative restraints.
[P1.5] A ban therefore draws an unambiguous red line. It refuses commercial profit and diplomatic convenience as justifications for arming repression. Domestic and allied markets remain open under strict end-use rules; only sales to verified abusers are cut off. Without that cutoff, exporting states become silent partners in modernizing the surveillance architecture of today’s most repressive governments.

[C1.1] The motion asks the wrong question. The choice is not "surveillance tools for dictators: yes or no?" It is "who supplies them, on what terms, and with what strings attached?" Once framed correctly, a blanket ban is the worst available policy.
[C1.2] First, bans do not reduce repression; they reassign the contract. Advanced surveillance is a globally contested market. When democracies refuse a sale, Chinese, Russian, and gray-market vendors step in eagerly—selling facial recognition and interception suites with zero end-use conditions, no audit rights, and often explicit training in political monitoring. Beijing has spent a decade exporting exactly this stack across Africa and Central Asia.
[C1.3] A Western ban does not disarm the repressor; it hands him a supplier who considers repression a feature, while democracies lose all visibility into how the systems are configured and used.
[C1.4] Second, exports are leverage; bans are abdication. Modern surveillance systems depend on updates, maintenance, licensing keys, and training. A conditioned sale gives the exporting government an ongoing throttle: violate agreed limits and the system degrades. That is real, continuing influence over how a regime polices.
[C1.5] A ban trades this durable leverage for a one-time gesture of moral hygiene.
[C1.6] Third, "regimes with poor human-rights records" is a category so broad it swallows most of the world where the worst crime occurs. Trafficking networks, jihadist cells, and child-exploitation rings concentrate precisely in weakly governed states with ugly rights records. Denying those police forces modern tools does not punish the regime—elites buy elsewhere. It punishes ordinary citizens whose kidnappers go untracked.
[C1.7] PRO's claim [P1.3] that "less invasive methods" suffice is asserted, never shown: you cannot dismantle a trafficking network in Lagos or Manila with cooperation agreements alone.
[C1.8] The genuine alternative to a ban is case-by-case licensing: end-use monitoring, contractual kill switches, targeted sanctions on abusive units. That regime addresses every abuse PRO cites while preserving the security benefits PRO concedes exist. The blanket ban sacrifices both.
