[P1.1] The social contract of the twentieth century tied basic security to a single employer. Today’s labor market is fundamentally fragmented. A delivery driver might work for three different apps, earning a substantial full-time income that anchors their livelihood while receiving zero protections. When these workers get sick or injured, the costs do not disappear; they are simply externalized onto public safety nets and emergency rooms.
[P1.2] A portable benefits floor solves this structural mismatch by attaching baseline protections to the worker, not the platform. Under this model, platforms contribute a prorated percentage to an individual worker’s benefit account based on earnings. If a freelancer earns sixty percent of their income on one platform and forty percent on another, both fund the floor proportionally. The worker retains their health, injury, and retirement coverage seamlessly across gigs.
[P1.3] This policy fixes critical market failures. First, it halts the regulatory race to the bottom. Currently, gig companies often compete by misclassifying workers and externalizing risk. A mandatory floor ensures platforms compete on service quality and innovation, rather than who can best dodge basic labor costs.
[P1.4] This protects responsible businesses from being undercut by regulatory arbitrage and ensures taxpayers aren't subsidizing corporate payrolls.
[P1.5] Second, it dramatically increases labor market dynamism. When benefits are portable, workers are freed from traditional "job lock"—the phenomenon where people stay in mismatched or toxic traditional jobs solely to keep their health insurance. With a portable floor, workers can fluidly transition between short-term contracts, allowing the economy to match skills to immediate needs much more efficiently.
[P1.6] The opposition will likely argue that this mandate destroys the flexibility of gig work or stifles innovation through added costs. But true flexibility does not require financial precarity. A portable benefits floor preserves the autonomy of independent work while ensuring that substantial labor yields basic security. We secure the worker without sacrificing the market.

[C1.4] The "substantial income" threshold compounds these failures. Any earnings cutoff creates a cliff where workers and platforms have powerful incentives to game the system—splitting income across family accounts, underreporting cash tips, or structuring contracts just below the trigger. We replace flexible labor with a compliance labyrinth that benefits lawyers and accountants more than laborers.
[C1.5] Finally, the premise ignores existing portability. Health insurance is already decoupled from employment through exchanges; retirement savings through IRAs; disability through private markets. Workers who value security can buy it. What they cannot buy back is the flexibility they chose.
[C1.6] This mandate trades worker autonomy for a safety net woven from red tape, punishing the very dynamism it claims to protect.
