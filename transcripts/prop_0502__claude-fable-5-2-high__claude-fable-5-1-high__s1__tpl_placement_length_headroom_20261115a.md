[P1.1] The Arctic is the one place on Earth where the ice is retreating and the armies are advancing. As the sea opens, the governing logic is simple: whoever borders it claims it. Russia, Denmark and Canada have filed overlapping claims to the same seabed ridge running beneath the North Pole. The one forum meant to manage this, the Arctic Council, is voluntary, bars military security from its agenda, and largely seized up after Russia invaded Ukraine.
[P1.2] That is the system my opponent must defend: rival claims, refereed by a body that cannot bind anyone and stops working precisely when tension rises.
[P1.3] There is a proven alternative. In 1959, at the height of the Cold War, rival powers with overlapping claims in Antarctica signed a binding treaty: claims frozen, military activity banned, inspections open, science shared, and later mining prohibited. It has held for over six decades through every superpower crisis. The lesson: binding rules outlast bad relations; voluntary ones do not.
[P1.4] First, security. Frozen claims and verified limits on militarization remove the prize that makes escalation rational. Without them, every new base justifies the next.
[P1.5] Second, the environment. An oil spill under sea ice is nearly impossible to clean up, and Arctic warming drives sea levels and weather for billions who border nothing. A commons with global consequences cannot be managed by five governments weighing their own drilling revenue.
[P1.6] Third, feasibility. "Similar to" does not mean identical. Nobody is erasing Norway's towns or Inuit homelands; the Antarctic Treaty itself froze claims without extinguishing them. Arctic states have already done this in miniature: Svalbard pairs sovereignty with demilitarization, and a binding central Arctic fishing moratorium included China before any trawler arrived.
[P1.7] The question is whether we write the rules before the scramble, or after the first collision.
