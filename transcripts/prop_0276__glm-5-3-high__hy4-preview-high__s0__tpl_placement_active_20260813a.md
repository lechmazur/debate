[C1.1] The motion sounds humane: if the airline is at fault, just pay. It rests on a false premise—that "controllable" delay is a clean, knowable category we can compensate automatically. It is not.
[C1.2] Start with causation. Delays are tangled: a flight leaves late because its inbound aircraft was held by weather, crew hit a duty limit, or a jet bridge failed at a congested hub. The airline may have caused part of it, or none, but "controllable" is decided after investigation, not at the departure board. A rule paying by default forces either blanket payouts for genuinely messy events or a new fight over exemptions.
[C1.3] [P1.4] cites Europe, yet the comparable regime did not end disputes; it moved them into arguments about "extraordinary circumstances" and created claims firms that take a cut of awards. Automaticity changes the battlefield, not the conflict.
[C1.4] Automatic compensation is also a fixed penalty, not tailored redress. It pays the same whether a passenger missed a meeting or napped in a lounge, and it still pays nothing for expenses above the flat amount unless the passenger files. That is not fairness; it is a blunt transfer funded by all travelers through higher fares, schedule padding, and thinner service to smaller cities. It also distorts behavior: pad schedules, cancel early, and stop selling efficient tight connections because a missed connection becomes an automatic bill.
[C1.5] The PRO says complaint systems are "a tax on the elderly, the tired, the non-native speaker" [P1.6]; Europe shows claims agents now harvest those awards, leaving the vulnerable to navigate a process anyway. The answer is not to abolish verification but to fix it: standardized proof, a public ombudsman, longer windows, and reimbursement of documented losses. Forcing automatic payouts raises costs and reduces convenience while moving money through intermediaries. Keep the claim, streamline the claim, reject the mandate.
