[P1.1] When water runs short, a city must answer one question: how do we get people to use less, fairly and effectively? The choice is between sharply rising prices on heavy users and blunt universal restrictions like "no outdoor watering on Tuesdays." I'll defend pricing on three grounds: it cuts the right water, it protects the vulnerable, and it actually works.
[P1.2] First, targeting. Blunt restrictions treat everyone identically regardless of how much they consume. A retiree taking short showers gets the same rule as a mansion irrigating two acres of lawn. That's both unfair and inefficient, because most discretionary water—lawns, pools, ornamental landscaping—is concentrated among the heaviest users.
[P1.3] Rising "tiers" let essential use stay cheap while making waste expensive. You discourage the swimming-pool refill, not the dishwasher.
[P1.4] Second, equity. People hear "prices" and imagine punishing the poor. The opposite is true if designed properly. A tiered structure gives every household a generous low-cost block covering drinking, cooking, and hygiene, then raises rates steeply only on consumption far above subsistence.
[P1.5] Low-income households, who use the least, are largely shielded; the lawn-and-pool crowd pays. Restrictions, by contrast, can't tell rich from poor at all—and wealthy users often simply ignore fines they can easily absorb.
[P1.6] Third, effectiveness and information. Restrictions are crude on/off switches enforced by patrols and complaints; they invite cheating and require armies of inspectors. Prices work continuously and automatically. Every heavy user faces a personal incentive to find their own cheapest cut—fix the leak, shorten irrigation, install a low-flow fixture—without a bureaucrat deciding which activities are "allowed."
[P1.7] People economize where it's easiest for them, which is exactly the conservation we can't centrally plan.
[P1.8] The motion says "rather than rely mainly on" restrictions—not that restrictions vanish. Pricing should be the primary lever; emergency rules can backstop genuine crises. But making waste expensive beats banning everything equally.
[P1.9] The core comparison is simple: restrictions punish use; pricing punishes waste. In a shortage, that distinction is everything. I urge you to vote PRO.
