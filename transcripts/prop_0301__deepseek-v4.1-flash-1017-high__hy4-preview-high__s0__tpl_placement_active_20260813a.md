[P1.1] Public money is not a religious liberty issue; it is a public trust issue. The proposition does not ban religious schools or dictate their theology. It says something narrower and more defensible: if a school wants taxpayers to pay, it must meet strong civic, curricular, and non-discrimination standards comparable to public schools. That is the fair bargain.
[P1.2] First, coercion cuts both ways. Public funds are collected from everyone—believers, nonbelievers, and families the school might exclude. When government writes checks without conditions, it makes all those taxpayers subsidize institutions that can teach a contested creed, refuse qualified students, or treat some children as second-class. Conditions are how a pluralistic society prevents public money from becoming a stamp of approval for exclusion.
[P1.3] Second, “comparable” standards are not “identical” standards. A religious school can keep its ethos, its worship, its religious instruction, and its faith-based admissions policy for privately funded places. But if it takes public money, it must teach a real curriculum, respect basic civil-rights protections, and prepare students for citizenship in a diverse democracy. That leaves room for religion; it does not leave room for publicly funded civic malpractice.
[P1.4] Third, the alternative is not neutrality. It is subsidy without accountability. A school could receive public funds while teaching science as a sectarian test of loyalty, excluding students with disabilities, or telling one group of children they do not belong. Maybe most religious schools would not do that; the point is that public funding should not depend on trusting every institution to behave well.
[P1.5] We attach strings to hospitals, charities, and universities that receive public money. Schools should be no different.
[P1.6] The opponent will call this burdensome or discriminatory. But no school has a right to public money on its own terms. The choice is simple: accept the civic bargain, or fund the school privately. The proposition protects religious freedom by protecting taxpayers and children first.

[C1.1] Public money should not remake religious communities. The motion offers funds only if a religious school meets “strong civic, curricular, and non-discrimination standards comparable to public schools.” The trap is “only” and “comparable.” A religious school is religious because it orders curriculum, hiring, and discipline around a contested doctrine. Public schools cannot take those positions; they must stay impartial among creeds. So a faithful school is not “comparable”; it is different in kind.
[C1.2] [P1.3] says “comparable” standards are not “identical” standards. But when conformity in curriculum, civic teaching, and non-discrimination is demanded, “comparable” becomes “identical” for any school that treats its faith as true.
[C1.3] Take a Catholic school that limits religion teaching to believers, or a Muslim school that will not affirm contested sexual ethics in hiring. Under comparability, those are discrimination and civic failure. To qualify, the school must mute doctrine, hire those who reject its mission, and teach citizenship in the state’s idiom. It becomes a public school with a chapel—and the chapel is disciplined too. “Privately funded places” cannot preserve the ethos; a school is one community, not a secular branch franchise.
[C1.4] The result is a double bind: comply and cease to be the school parents chose, or stay faithful and forfeit support. [P1.2] warns that taxpayers should not fund exclusion. But religious families already pay for public schools that contradict their conscience. Forcing their schools to become government-shaped to receive any relief adds state coercion to their tax burden.
[C1.5] If civic malpractice is the worry, narrow strings suffice: safety, audit, basic academics, no fraud. The motion instead imposes a totalizing test—mirror the state or go unfunded—using funding to dissolve the very pluralism it claims to protect.
[C1.6] A bargain that lets a religious school keep its identity only by surrendering it is not a bargain. Reject the proposition.

Comparable means comparable outcomes for students and citizens: literacy, safety, a curriculum that does not replace science or history with sectarian loyalty tests, and nondiscrimination in access. [P2.4] A Muslim school can teach its ethics and still not treat other citizens as second-class. That is not muting doctrine; it is refusing to publicly fund civic harm.

[P2.6] CON's alternative is not pluralism; it is forcing all taxpayers to underwrite institutions that may exclude them. Religious families paying for public schools they dislike is a tax complaint, not a voucher entitlement.
[P2.7] Finally, [C1.5]'s "narrow strings"—safety, audit, basic academics, no fraud—miss the very harms at issue.
