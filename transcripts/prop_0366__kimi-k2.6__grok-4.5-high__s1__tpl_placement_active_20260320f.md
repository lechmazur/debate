[P1.1] The theatrical-first default is not a celebration of cinema; it is an exclusionary gate that locks millions of paying customers out of the audience. For viewers who require captions, audio description, or reliable wheelchair access; for parents of young children who cannot secure a sitter on a Tuesday night; for rural communities hours from the nearest multiplex; and for immunocompromised people avoiding crowded indoor spaces, the current model does not merely inconvenience—it denies. [P1.2] This is not about destroying theaters. It is about rejecting an outdated distribution bottleneck that masquerades as artistic ritual. The industry already proved the technology works: when premium video-on-demand scaled rapidly, audiences paid first-run prices at home in huge numbers, and consistent digital accessibility tracks proved far more reliable than the patchwork compliance found in thousands of individual projection booths. When studios withhold a film from home viewers, they rarely create scarcity; they channel eager audiences toward piracy or simple indifference.
[P1.3] A simultaneous release with robust accessibility captures revenue the theatrical window actively bleeds away.
[P1.4] Treating theatrical-first as the standard also forces consumers into a single, expensive, time-locked experience regardless of their circumstances. The living room is treated as a second-class venue by design. Theaters should compete on spectacle, superior sound, and collective energy—not survive through artificial windowing that holds home audiences hostage.
[P1.5] Simultaneous release makes theaters earn their ticket sales rather than rely on monopoly access.
[P1.6] The burden is not on audiences to rearrange their health, geography, or family obligations around a studio’s release schedule. It is on studios to meet viewers where they actually live.
