[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are the most expensive way to help the poor that any government has ever devised. In Gulf and North African states, they drain tens of billions of dollars annually from public budgets, and the overwhelming majority of that money flows to households that are not poor.
[P1.2] The core problem is structural: wealthy people consume more energy than poor people. They drive more, cool larger homes, fly more, and own more vehicles. A universal subsidy prices fuel below market cost for everyone, which means the largest absolute benefit goes to those who buy the most. The poor household spending a larger share of income on energy still receives less in subsidy dollars than the wealthy family filling two SUVs.
[P1.3] This is redistribution running in the wrong direction.
[P1.4] Targeted cash transfers fix this. Instead of spending billions to make fuel artificially cheap for everyone, governments can identify vulnerable households and transfer cash directly. The poor receive more help than they do under the subsidy, at a fraction of the fiscal cost. The savings can fund schools, healthcare, or infrastructure—investments that compound rather than burn.
[P1.5] The five-year window is realistic. Egypt has already moved significantly on subsidy reform. Gulf states have the fiscal capacity and administrative infrastructure to design and deploy transfer programs. The technical challenge is identification, not invention—many of these states already operate welfare registries or national ID systems that can serve as delivery platforms.
[P1.6] Fuel subsidies also distort economies in ways cash transfers do not. Below-market prices encourage overconsumption, discourage energy efficiency, and in North Africa, fuel smuggling across borders erodes state revenue further. Removing the price signal removes the distortion.
[P1.7] The proposition is not that reform is painless. It is that universal subsidies are the wrong tool and targeted transfers are the right one. Five years is enough time to transition. The status quo is not free—it is paid for every day in wasted revenue, regressive transfers, and distorted markets.
