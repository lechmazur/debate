[P1.1] The issue is not whether managers prefer room to maneuver; of course they do. It is who pays for that room. Large firms know the salary architecture and advancement odds; workers see one offer, a vague promise of growth, and a guess about colleagues. That asymmetry decides whether someone can negotiate, change teams, invest in training, challenge a suspicious pattern, or judge a job's real value.
[P1.2] Pay bands and promotion rates by role make the rules visible.
[P1.3] Pay bands are modest: no individual salaries, no ban on merit pay, no identical wages—only the range attached to a role. That makes discretion accountable. If two people enter the same role and one starts near the bottom because she negotiated less aggressively, the firm may still call that judgment, but disclosure requires the judgment to fit a stated structure. The flexibility at issue is often the freedom to make exceptions without explaining why they track leverage, bias, or proximity to power.
[P1.4] Promotion rates matter because a job is not only a wage; it is a claim about a future. Employers advertise leadership paths and internal mobility, yet workers cannot test those claims when movement by role is hidden. One role may be a stepping stone; another a cul-de-sac where people are praised and parked. Disclosure exposes bottlenecks, glass ceilings, and managers who hoard talent.
[P1.5] It pushes firms to repair pipelines rather than blame employees for failing to navigate a maze they could not see.
[P1.6] Large employers are the proper target: they already keep classifications, compensation records, and promotion data, and their secrecy shapes labor markets. A mandate also stops the least transparent firms from making opacity the market norm. Yes, flexibility is reduced—but what disappears is secret rulemaking, not rewarding performance or meeting shortages. Flexibility purchased through worker ignorance is not productive freedom; it is a subsidy paid in weaker bargaining power, unequal pay, and misdirected careers.

[C1.1] A disclosed band looks like information, but a mandate by role forces firms to publish a simplified map of jobs that do not actually work that way, and then punishes them for deviating from it. The same title can cover different markets, skill scarcity, risk, revenue responsibility, and performance trajectories. Once that range is official, every exception becomes a grievance, a negotiation anchor, or a compliance risk — so managers stop making exceptions, even good ones.
[C1.2] That is how pay bands compress pay. A firm can still reward a scarce engineer, a turnaround manager, or a top salesperson, but only by pushing against a public ceiling, re-titling the job, or shifting compensation into bonuses and equity the band does not capture. The visible salary flattens while real compensation becomes more opaque. High performers lose a direct reward for exceptional output, weaker performance anchors negotiations upward regardless of productivity, and rivals gain a free guide for poaching and bidding wars that inflate fixed costs.
[C1.3] Promotion rates by role are even more misleading. A low rate may reflect a senior, stable team; a high rate may reflect churn, low standards, or a reorganization. Without tenure, eligibility, performance, vacancies, and business cycles — context a mandate cannot publish without exposing individuals — workers get a number that invites false conclusions. Managers respond rationally: create intermediate titles to manufacture promotions, avoid hiring promising juniors into hard-to-promote roles, delay backfills, or move work to contractors and subsidiaries outside the reported category.
[C1.4] Large size does not solve this; it multiplies job families, locations, and pay components, and in small teams a role band effectively identifies someone’s pay. The cost is therefore not just less managerial flexibility. It is fewer tailored offers, slower retention moves, more title gaming, and career data noisy enough to mislead the very workers it claims to empower. Bias should be audited and punished, but governing by published bands and rates replaces private unfairness with public distortion.

The motion requires visible bands and promotion rates, not a ban on exceptions, differentiated roles, or revised ranges. If a scarce engineer is worth more, the firm can pay more and widen the band or explain the exception.
[P2.2] An exception should be discussable. What disclosure removes is not the reward; it is the employer's ability to call one worker "strategic" and another "average" while hiding the rule.
[P2.3] That also answers the compression claim in [C1.2]. If salary flattens while pay migrates to bonuses and equity, the answer is to make the band cover regular pay, not preserve opacity. A published range does not cap compensation unless the firm chooses to make pay less legible. And rivals bidding from better information is a labor market working: employers lose the advantage of applicants not knowing their price.
[P2.4] Higher costs for an underpaying firm are not the injury.
[P2.5] The promotion-rate objection [C1.3] proves too much. A low rate in a senior, stable team is exactly what a junior needs before treating that role as a ladder; a high rate caused by churn is useful. Numbers need not supply every cause; they force employers to give causes. Secret systems also mislead, but only one side can check them.
[P2.6] Title games or outsourcing show visibility threatens practices that need scrutiny.
[P2.7] Finally, [C1.4] concedes bias should be audited and punished. But audits without disclosure are audits whose categories and results workers cannot inspect. Large firms have enough people to aggregate without identifying anyone; tiny categories can be combined, not hidden. Publish the structure and odds; then an employee in a dead-end role can ask why, employers can state reasons, and a suspicious pattern can be challenged before it hardens into a career.
[P2.8] Imperfect public rules are corrigible. Private discretion is not.

[C2.1] The defence makes the band elastic whenever it binds: widen it, explain the exception, or extend it to bonuses and equity. That destroys the promised clarity. A band wide enough for a scarce engineer, a turnaround hire, and an ordinary performer in the same title tells workers almost nothing. A band narrow enough to inform makes the good exception costly — not banned, but flagged, questioned by peers, anchored in negotiation, and preserved as evidence the firm departed from its own structure.
[C2.2] Managers need no formal cap to be capped; risk does the work.
[C2.3] If bands cover only salary, compensation migrates. If they must cover regular pay, variable bonuses and equity are forced into a range despite being contingent and sometimes worthless. The firm then publishes a range so broad it misleads, or standardizes the variable parts meant to reward results. Either way pay becomes simpler and flatter to compare, not better fitted to the work.
[C2.4] Rival bidding from published ceilings also turns the top of the band into the opening demand, raising fixed costs even for firms already paying fairly.
[C2.5] On promotion rates, the concession is decisive: the number alone cannot tell a junior whether a low rate means a stable senior team or a dead end. Disclosure supposedly forces employers to give causes, but the motion requires rates by role, not credible explanations — and any added narrative is the employer's unverifiable gloss. The predictable responses remain: split roles to manufacture movement, avoid transfers into slow teams, and time promotions to smooth the rate. Workers receive a distorted signal plus public relations.
[C2.6] Nor do audits need publicity to work. A regulator or independent auditor can inspect individual pay, tenure, performance, and demographics confidentially and order correction for bias. That targets discrimination directly. Public disclosure targets visibility and pays for it in anchoring, gaming, and compression — distortions that persist because the published number now drives hiring, titles, and negotiations.

No mandate does not mean no information: she receives an actual offer, can compare competing offers, ask about pay for her location and level, and negotiate from her market value.
[C4.2] That individualized information is more decision-useful than a public range stretched to survive every legitimate exception.
[C4.3] A2: A confidential audit is sufficient for its proper job—detecting and correcting bias using full individual context—and the published rate does not solve the career-choice problem either. The same low rate fits a stable senior team and a dead end. A numerator and denominator still omit vacancies, eligibility, performance, and whether advancement requires leaving the team. Before accepting, a worker can ask how many predecessors advanced, where they went, and how long incumbents remain, then judge evasiveness directly.
[C4.4] A false public signal is worse than a question because it creates confidence the data cannot support.
[C4.5] Those answers expose the mandate’s dilemma. To stop uselessly wide bands and boilerplate exceptions, regulators must judge acceptable widths and reasons. That is substantive pay control, not mere disclosure. To protect small teams, rates must be combined across roles or years, producing a stale aggregate rather than the role-specific odds workers were promised.
[C4.6] If neither safeguard is imposed, gaming and identification return.
[C4.7] So the choice is not a visible map versus ignorance. It is distorted public signals that reshape titles, hiring, negotiations, and pay versus targeted audits for discrimination plus concrete offers and answers for individual decisions. The mandate imposes its costs on every worker and firm, including fair ones, while still failing to tell a junior whether she faces a crowded ladder or a cul-de-sac.
