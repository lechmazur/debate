[P1.1] The smartphone is the most important device most people own, and yet two companies decide what software you may install on your own hardware and how much you must pay to transact through it. Keep those four words in mind, because they preempt the fear campaign my opponent is about to run.
[P1.2] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, competition. When a platform forces every developer through its own payment system and takes a roughly 30% cut, that is not a service fee—it is a toll extracted by a gatekeeper who controls the only road. That cost lands on consumers as higher subscription and app prices. Break the tollbooth's monopoly and prices fall, because developers can offer you a cheaper checkout the way a merchant offers a discount for paying by cash instead of card.
[P1.4] Second, ownership and innovation. On a desktop computer, you can install software from anywhere, and the world did not end—we got an explosion of tools that never had to beg a gatekeeper's permission. Sideloading simply restores to the phone the freedom the personal computer always had. Whole categories of apps that platform owners reject for competitive or ideological reasons would finally reach users.
[P1.5] Third—and this is where I meet CON head-on—security is not sacrificed; it is *governed*. The motion conditions everything on baseline security standards: notarization, malware scanning, sandboxing, permission controls, revocation of malicious signatures. A regulated open door with a lock is safer than the false comfort of a walled garden that also happens to be a monopoly. Regulators can mandate protections that no single company is obligated to provide today.
[P1.6] So the choice is clear. A closed system defended by one firm's self-interest, or an open one defended by public rules. CON must show that the walls protect *you* rather than the landlord's rent. They cannot.
