[P1.1] Fertility clinics routinely market elective egg freezing as reproductive insurance: a sleek, empowering way to pause the biological clock. Yet insurance guarantees a payout; egg freezing sells an expensive gamble. Behind subway ads and social media slogans about taking control of your future lies a steep biological cliff. Each cycle costs thousands of dollars, requires hormone injections and retrieval surgery, and often fails to yield a baby.
[P1.2] Today, clinic ads exploit hope by spotlighting flattering metrics, like thaw survival or young donor outcomes, while obscuring what patients actually seek: the realistic chance that freezing at their age produces a live birth.
[P1.3] Deferring honest numbers to a private consultation cannot cure a deceptive sales pitch. Elective egg freezing is a lucrative commercial market targeting healthy consumers paying out of pocket. Advertising sets the psychological anchor, framing the procedure as reliable peace of mind and bringing patients through the door already emotionally invested. Once someone has paid for initial diagnostics and latched onto a promised cure for fertility anxiety, caveats buried in consent forms struggle against sunk-cost bias.
[P1.4] We require pharmaceutical and financial ads to display prominent risk disclosures upfront precisely because sellers should not hook buyers on pure upside and hide the odds in the back office.
[P1.5] Requiring ads to foreground age-based probabilities and uncertainty directly fixes this distortion. Age is the steepest determinant of egg quality; touting peak or blended figures misleads a 39-year-old into thinking she is buying a 29-year-old’s odds. Prominence for uncertainty is equally vital. Because most elective egg-freezing patients have not yet returned to use stored eggs, real-world live-birth samples remain thin, and individual outcomes vary widely around any average.
[P1.6] Putting realistic age bands and clear uncertainty caveats front and center does not deter reproductive planning; it makes autonomy real, helping patients freeze earlier when odds are stronger, budget for multiple cycles, or refuse to spend savings on false security.
