[P1.1] A large, across-the-board minimum wage increase treats an extraordinarily diverse economy as a monolith. A blunt statutory hike imposes the exact same cost shock on a high-margin logistics hub, a razor-thin-margin rural nursing home, and an independent neighborhood diner. When a uniform mandate ignores industry-level economics, businesses operating on narrow margins or facing intense capital substitutability face an immediate crisis: they are forced to cut entry-level headcounts, slash operating hours, or accelerate abrupt automation. That is where disemployment happens.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining and wage boards avert this trap through targeted calibration. By bringing workers, employers, and public representatives together on an industry-by-industry basis, wage boards tie compensation directly to a sector’s specific productivity, profitability, and operational capacity. In sectors with high rents and resilient demand, like warehousing or commercial services, boards can aggressively raise wage floors with virtually zero risk of job loss. In fragile, labor-intensive sectors like elder care or early childhood education, standards can be phased in sustainably, paired with training benchmarks, or aligned with public reimbursement rates.
[P1.3] Because the parties who actually understand the balance sheet set the terms, pay increases are maximized without pushing firms into insolvency or mass layoffs.
[P1.4] Moreover, sectoral standards level the competitive playing field. When an entire industry moves together, individual firms are insulated from being undercut by race-to-the-bottom competitors. Rather than responding to wage pressures with defensive layoffs, businesses absorb costs through reduced turnover, operational efficiency, and modest, industry-wide price adjustments.
[P1.5] Blunt statutory minimums rely on political compromise to produce a single number that is either too low to help workers in prosperous sectors or too high for vulnerable ones to absorb without shedding staff. Wage boards replace that blunt instrument with economic precision, delivering robust wage growth while safeguarding the jobs low-wage workers rely on to survive.

[C1.3] A board that must weigh "sector-specific conditions" has been handed the perfect excuse to set floors below what a clean statutory increase would deliver. The minimum wage's bluntness is a feature: one number, one vote, no committee to lobby into paralysis.
[C1.4] Second, coverage. Minimum wages reach every low-wage worker — the hotel housekeeper, the home care aide, the clerk at the dollar store, the dishwasher. Sectoral boards only reach workers in sectors someone chose to organize. Low-wage work in America is fragmented across hundreds of small, diffuse sectors.
[C1.5] A fast-food council does nothing for the laundry worker or the farmworker. A policy that raises pay spectacularly for a covered few while leaving most low-wage workers behind does not beat an across-the-board increase; it loses on the very metric that matters.
[C1.6] Third, the employment claim cuts the wrong way. Wage boards don't impose less risk — they impose less scrutiny. Once compensation is negotiated per sector, there is no natural ceiling: a board can set $22 in one industry with none of the economy-wide discipline that constrains legislators setting one number for everyone. If large hikes threaten jobs, boards with narrower political accountability threaten them more, not less.
[C1.7] The minimum wage is simple, universal, and battle-tested. Wage boards are a promise that the same interests fighting fifteen dollars an hour will suddenly be generous behind closed doors. Low-wage workers cannot eat a process.
