[P1.1] Legislatures are structurally biased toward deficits. Politicians get the full political benefit of spending today and push the costs onto future taxpayers who cannot vote yet. Every election cycle rewards tax cuts and new programs; deficit reduction rewards nobody until long after the officeholder retires. That is not a moral failing of particular politicians—it is the incentive structure of democratic discretion itself.
[P1.2] Leaving fiscal discipline to legislatures is like leaving diet decisions to someone who only eats dessert and never steps on a scale.
[P1.3] Constitutional rules change that arithmetic. Germany's debt brake and Switzerland's debt containment rule show the mechanism: when borrowing requires supermajorities or triggers automatic correction paths, deficits shrink and debt ratios stabilize, without requiring legislators to be heroes. Rules also do what discretion cannot—they are credible. Markets, businesses, and households plan differently when they know the fiscal framework cannot be rewritten by the next majority.
[P1.4] Discretionary discipline evaporates the moment a legislature decides restraint is inconvenient; constitutional discipline survives exactly those moments.
[P1.5] The obvious objection is that rigid rules strangle economies in recessions. That is why the proposition includes automatic stabilizer exceptions. Unemployment insurance, progressive tax receipts falling, and similar countercyclical programs adjust without any vote—so when a downturn hits, the deficit widens automatically, cushioning demand, and when recovery comes, it narrows automatically, rebuilding room for the next shock. This is not a loophole; it is the difference between a rule targeting structural balance and a rule targeting accounting folly.
[P1.6] A balanced-budget requirement with stabilizer exceptions outperforms pure discretion on both dimensions: it binds during booms, when legislatures historically splurge, and it relaxes during busts, when austerity would be destructive.
[P1.7] The real choice, then, is not "rules versus flexibility." It is precommitment versus impulse. Odysseus tied himself to the mast because he knew his future self could not be trusted. Democracies deserve the same protection: a constitutional rule that makes fiscal discipline the default, with stabilizers that let the economy breathe when it must.
