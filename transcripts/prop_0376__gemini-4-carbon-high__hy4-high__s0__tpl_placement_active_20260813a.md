[P1.1] Uncapped credit card rates rest on a dangerous illusion: that access to punitive revolving debt helps financially fragile households. When a product routinely turns a temporary cash shortage into years of compounding servitude, shrinking its availability is not a policy failure—it is consumer protection working as intended. Lawmakers should cap credit card interest rates because for higher-risk borrowers, an ultra-high-rate credit line is worse to receive than to be denied.
[P1.2] In healthy lending, a bank profits only when a borrower can realistically repay. Uncapped rates sever that alignment. At thirty percent interest, issuers profit from a "sweatbox" model: if a struggling household carries a balance and pays minimums for a few years before defaulting, the bank still wins on interest alone. Uncapped pricing actively subsidizes reckless underwriting, rewarding issuers for pushing frictionless plastic onto families with no margin for error.
[P1.3] A legal cap restores market discipline, forcing banks to lend based on ability to repay rather than ability to bleed.
[P1.4] Weigh the actual stakes for a higher-risk borrower facing a sudden car repair or medical bill. Swiping a high-rate card feels like immediate relief, yet minimum payments barely touch the principal. Worse, revolving plastic invites incremental borrowing long before true emergencies hit. Soon, interest siphons off future paychecks needed for rent and groceries, culminating in default and a wrecked credit report that jeopardizes housing.
[P1.5] Denied that card, the upfront crunch is undeniably hard—forcing provider payment plans, family help, or tough budget cuts. Yet confronting a finite cash shortfall today is far less destructive than financing it through a compounding trap that cannibalizes years of future income.
[P1.6] Credit access is not an unconditional good regardless of price. Just as laws ban unsafe cars even when low-income buyers cannot afford pricier models, capping interest rates stops lenders from disguising a financial wrecking ball as a lifeline.

[C1.1] A rate cap does not make expensive credit cheap; it makes it disappear. The proposition accepts that and calls it protection. That reverses the relevant comparison. The claim that a high-rate card is “worse to receive than to be denied” assumes a safe world after denial, not the next-best options facing someone with bad credit and an urgent bill.
[C1.2] Those options are often worse. The listed alternatives are unreliable: landlords may refuse plans, relatives may be broke, and cutting back may mean missing work because the car stays broken. A “finite cash shortfall” can become eviction, a shutoff, lost wages, or delayed care. A card is destructive when a balance revolves for years, but that is not every use.
[C1.3] APRs are annualized; a brief bridge can beat an overdraft, late fee, payday loan, or lost job.
[C1.4] The “sweatbox” model flattens lender incentives. Default wipes out principal, adds collection costs, consumes capital, and brings scrutiny. High rates price unsecured loans where some borrowers never repay and no collateral can be seized. When law forbids price to reflect risk, rationing becomes non-price rationing: fewer approvals, lower limits, closed accounts, deposits, tougher screening.
[C1.5] That is not discipline; it is exclusion from the most transparent legal credit line many thin-file workers have.
[C1.6] Access is not an unconditional good, but it is a real good, including option value. An unused credit line is like a spare tire: valuable because it is there when something breaks. A cap bans the spare tire because some drivers crash, then pushes people toward informal lenders, arrears, employers, and landlords—less flexible and less dignified.
[C1.7] The unsafe-car analogy fails. An unsafe car is dangerous in ordinary use; a high-rate card is dangerous in one pattern of use: long balances. A blanket cap treats all higher-risk borrowers as incapable of choosing and punishes careful short-term users. Deception, hidden fees, and abusive collection can be targeted directly.
[C1.8] A cap buys a clean-looking number by pushing fragile households out of lawful credit.

[P2.4] Meanwhile, where caps push banks toward "lower limits" and secured "deposits" [C1.4], disciplined short-term users retain a modest bridge while runaway debt spirals disappear.
