[P1.1] Paying an actor to perform should not automatically buy permission to manufacture a materially different performance in their face or voice. A studio that makes a living actor appear decades younger, or synthesizes dialogue they never delivered, is using more than footage: it is using that person’s recognizable identity to create something new. That requires explicit consent and compensation.
[P1.2] The rule is practical. Before making a material alteration, the studio must describe the intended use, secure express agreement, and pay for that authorization. These terms can be negotiated in the original contract; compensation can be an expressly priced part of the overall deal. An actor could authorize de-aging throughout a flashback sequence without approving every frame.
[P1.3] Uses outside that agreement require fresh permission. Routine cleanup is not the target, and consent does not mean control over the entire film.
[P1.4] First, performers bear consequences that studios cannot fully absorb. Audiences associate an actor with the words, emotional delivery, and appearance presented onscreen. Synthetic voice can turn a restrained performance into an enthusiastic endorsement within a scene; de-aging can fundamentally change the character an actor appears to portray. A technically convincing alteration can still misrepresent their artistic choices.
[P1.5] Permission matters before release, because a later payment cannot undo that association.
[P1.6] Second, compensation prevents technological efficiency from becoming uncompensated appropriation. A synthetic replacement may save another recording session or enable a portrayal beyond the work originally commissioned. Studios may enjoy those efficiencies, but the performer whose identity makes them valuable deserves a negotiated share. Consent protects choice; compensation protects against treating that additional use as free.
[P1.7] The strongest objection is creative freedom. But filmmaking already coordinates budgets, contracts, and permissions. Requiring agreement does not ban these tools; it makes their use legitimate and predictable. The alternative gives studios unilateral power over a living person’s recognizable performance merely because alteration has become easier.
[P1.8] Convenience is not permission, and production savings are not an entitlement to someone else’s identity.
