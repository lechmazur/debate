[P1.1] A single national minimum wage is a blunt instrument swung at an economy with wildly different conditions. The same $17 floor that a Seattle hotel absorbs without blinking can shutter a diner in rural Mississippi where the median wage is barely above it. The research on minimum wages is actually fairly consistent on this point: employment losses are small when the floor sits well below the local median, and they grow as the "bite" rises. The danger of a large across-the-board increase is precisely that it imposes a high bite everywhere at once, including the places and industries least able to pay.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining and wage boards solve the bite problem by design. A wage board for fast food, home care, or warehousing sets pay with employers, workers, and public members at the table, calibrated to what that industry can actually bear. Non-tradable service sectors with pricing power can pass modest costs to consumers; tradable or thin-margin sectors get a different schedule or a longer phase-in. That is not weaker protection for workers; it is protection aimed where it works.
[P1.3] Two mechanisms make this safer for jobs. First, taking wages out of competition: when every fast-food chain in a state faces the same floor, no employer is undercut, so the rational response is slightly higher prices rather than layoffs. Second, information: employers at the table reveal real margins, so the standard reflects reality instead of a round number chosen in a political campaign.
[P1.4] This is not theoretical. New York's fast-food wage board raised pay sharply without the job collapse critics predicted. Australia's awards system and Nordic sectoral agreements deliver high low-end wages alongside strong employment. The question is not whether to raise pay for low-wage workers.
[P1.5] It is whether to do it with a scalpel or a sledgehammer, and the scalpel costs fewer jobs.

[C1.6] The scalpel often cuts workers out of protection, while a minimum wage guarantees a floor for everyone. Administrative complexity also delays implementation, leaving workers waiting for raises that a simple minimum wage increase could deliver immediately.
