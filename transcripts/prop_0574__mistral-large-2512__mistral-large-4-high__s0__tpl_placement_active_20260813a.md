[P1.1] The right to control one’s own artistic identity does not vanish at death—it is preserved by the same moral and legal principles that protect living creators. When studios train AI on decades of vocal tracks, mannerisms, and lyrical patterns, they are not merely sampling data; they are resurrecting a voice that never consented to be cloned, a style that was never licensed for posthumous replication. Without explicit permission from the artist’s estate or heirs, this practice amounts to digital grave-robbing.
[P1.2] If we accept that an artist’s work belongs to them in life, we cannot suddenly declare it fair game the moment they die.
[P1.3] Consider the emotional cost: families are forced to watch their loved one’s voice sell products they would have despised, perform lyrics they never wrote, or appear in contexts that betray their values. The estate of Prince has already fought to block AI-generated tracks mimicking his vocals; imagine the anguish of hearing a synthetic version of a parent or partner singing something they would never have endorsed. That harm is real, and it is preventable. Current copyright law treats the right of publicity as descendible in many jurisdictions—meaning the law already recognizes that personality rights outlive the person.
[P1.4] Extending that protection to AI-generated likenesses is not a legal leap; it is a moral necessity.
[P1.5] Opponents will argue that requiring permission stifles creativity or denies the public access to new art. But no one is banning AI—only demanding that it respect the same boundaries we apply to every other form of artistic borrowing. If a studio wants to use a dead artist’s style, they can negotiate, just as they would with a living collaborator. The alternative is a free-for-all where corporations profit from voices they never earned, while families are left powerless to defend the integrity of a legacy.
[P1.6] That is not progress; it is exploitation dressed in technological novelty.

[C1.1] The proposition commits a category error: it treats a dead artist’s style as inheritable property, when copyright law already draws a hard line at death—life plus seventy years—and deliberately releases works into the public domain. Extending control indefinitely to “likeness” abolishes that boundary and replaces it with a perpetual tax on culture, payable to distant heirs or corporate estates that never sang a single note.
[C1.2] You cannot copyright a style. No one owns the timbre of a voice, the rhythm of a drumbeat, or the aesthetic of a genre. If studios needed explicit permission to create work “in the style of” a deceased artist, we would need Hemingway’s estate to approve a sparse sentence, or Muddy Waters’ heirs to license a twelve-bar blues. That is not protection; it is a toll booth on influence itself, turning every homage into a licensing negotiation.
[C1.3] The practical consequences are chilling. Many deceased artists have fragmented estates, orphaned works, or rights-holders who are faceless corporations with no living connection to the creative vision. Requiring “explicit permission” means one dissenting cousin or a distant trust can veto a documentary, a tribute, or a new composition. We would freeze not just the voice, but the living culture that grows from it.
[C1.4] The PRO side invokes consent, but the dead cannot consent, and estates are not the artist—they are commercial managers of a catalog, not guardians of a sacred vision. The public loses nothing by keeping Beethoven or Nina Simone in the cultural commons; it loses everything if we lock them behind a permission slip that enriches lawyers and holding companies.
