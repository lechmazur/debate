[P1.1] Opening a food app tells it I want dinner. It does not tell it I want to pay extra to get dinner ten minutes sooner. That distinction should govern checkout: honor the service the customer selected, and when the customer hasn’t selected an upgrade, don’t put one in the basket.
[P1.2] Consider someone ordering lunch at the office. Standard delivery will arrive before lunch break; priority delivery offers an earlier window for a surcharge. If priority is already checked, the customer has to spot the charge, work out whether it’s optional, and find the switch just to buy ordinary delivery. Start with standard instead.
[P1.3] Put both arrival windows and full prices on the same screen. Someone with a meeting to catch can decide in one tap that those minutes are worth buying.
[P1.4] Pickup calls for judgment, not a blanket rule. If I regularly collect from the sandwich shop downstairs, start my next order there on pickup, clearly labeled before I pay. If I tapped “deliver to this address,” don’t make me undo that instruction just because walking is cheaper. A lower fee isn’t a bargain for someone who can’t leave home.
[P1.5] But that’s a reason to respect a delivery choice, not a reason to treat it as consent to the costliest delivery tier.
[P1.6] The telling comparison is what happens when a customer doesn’t touch the switch. Under the expensive default, inattention can produce a surcharge on order after order, and the app can mistake those purchases for proof of demand. Under the cheaper default, an upgrade has to earn its price from someone who sees a benefit. A customer choosing to pay extra is better evidence that the service matters to them than a customer failing to opt out.
