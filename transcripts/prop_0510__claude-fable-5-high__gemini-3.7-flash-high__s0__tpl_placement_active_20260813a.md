[P1.1] When a recession hits, unemployment insurance pays out more, tax collections fall, and the deficit widens without anyone passing a law. That is the system working as designed. The proposition asks whether we should let it work even when debt-to-GDP is already high. The answer is yes, for one decisive reason: suppressing stabilizers in a downturn doesn't protect the debt ratio—it usually makes it worse.
[P1.2] A debt ratio is a fraction. Choking off stabilizers shrinks the numerator slightly while collapsing the denominator. Fiscal multipliers are at their largest precisely in recessions, when idle resources abound and central banks are near the lower bound and cannot fully offset the drag. Cut a euro of support in a slump and you can destroy more than a euro of GDP, plus the tax revenue that GDP would have generated.
[P1.3] The Eurozone's austerity turn after 2010 illustrates this vividly: Greece, Spain, and others tightened aggressively, output cratered, and debt ratios rose anyway. The United States, which let stabilizers run and added discretionary support, recovered faster and ultimately stabilized its fiscal position through growth.
[P1.4] Three features make automatic stabilizers uniquely defensible even under fiscal strain. They are timely—no legislative lag, no pork-laden negotiation. They are targeted—money flows to households who lost income and spend nearly every dollar, maximizing impact per unit of borrowing. And they are self-reversing—the deficit shrinks automatically as the economy recovers, unlike discretionary programs that outlive their purpose.
[P1.5] If you're worried about debt, this is the cheapest, most disciplined form of countercyclical policy available.
[P1.6] There is also a cost to the alternative that never appears on a balance sheet: hysteresis. Workers who stay unemployed lose skills; firms that fail don't return. A deeper recession permanently lowers the economy's productive capacity—the very tax base that must eventually service the debt.
[P1.7] Fiscal prudence and expanding stabilizers are not in tension. In a downturn, they are the same policy.

[C1.3] Investors demand higher yields, spiking sovereign borrowing costs across the entire debt stock. When interest rates on a massive pile of existing debt surge, debt servicing costs explode, blowing out the numerator far faster than any cyclical GDP support can lift the denominator.

[C1.5] Furthermore, automatic stabilizers are blunt, mechanical instruments. They swell deficits indiscriminately, regardless of whether a country possesses the borrowing space to absorb them. If a nation is already carrying historically elevated debt, blind commitment to automatic expansion strips policymakers of the flexibility to prioritize critical support, defend creditworthiness, and prevent panic in bond markets.
[C1.6] Fiscal stabilization cannot occur without a solvent sovereign. When debt ratios are elevated, fiscal policy cannot run on counterproductive autopilot; it must be actively constrained to preserve market stability and avoid a debt trap.
