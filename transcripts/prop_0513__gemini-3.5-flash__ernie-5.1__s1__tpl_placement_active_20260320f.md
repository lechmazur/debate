[P1.1] For decades, antitrust enforcement has operated with a dangerous blind spot, obsessing over consumer-price effects while ignoring the quiet strangulation of labor markets. When major employers merge, they do not just gain leverage over shoppers; they gain monopsony power over workers. Competition policy must treat labor-market monopsony with the exact same gravity as consumer-price effects because workers and consumers are two sides of the same economic coin.
[P1.2] First, the economic harm of monopsony is structurally identical to that of monopoly. When a merger reduces the number of viable employers in a region or industry, the remaining firms gain the power to suppress wages, strip away benefits, and dictate working conditions. This is not market efficiency; it is the extraction of wealth from captive workers who have nowhere else to sell their labor. A specialized nurse in a city dominated by a single hospital system, or a logistics worker with only one local fulfillment center, suffers a direct, measurable loss of bargaining power.
[P1.3] Second, focusing solely on consumer prices creates a perverse policy loophole. Under a narrow consumer-welfare standard, a merger that lowers retail prices by ruthlessly squeezing worker wages is celebrated. This is economically incoherent. It permits firms to justify anticompetitive behavior in the labor market as long as they pass some of the spoils to the consumer.
[P1.4] We cannot measure the health of an economy solely by what people pay at the register while ignoring what they earn in their paychecks.
[P1.5] Finally, labor markets are inherently more vulnerable to concentration than retail markets. Workers face search costs, geographic constraints, and industry-specific skills that prevent them from easily switching jobs. Elevating labor monopsony in merger reviews is not an expansion of antitrust's mandate; it is the fulfillment of its core mission to stop the abuse of market power.

Treating wage effects as co‑equal would block such efficiencies, harming the patients the standard is meant to protect.
