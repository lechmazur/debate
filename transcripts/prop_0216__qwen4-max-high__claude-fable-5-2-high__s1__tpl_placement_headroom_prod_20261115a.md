[P1.1] Palm-oil expansion is not an ordinary investment decision. When a plantation drains peat or clears upland forest, it converts an asset built over centuries into a few seasons of commodity revenue. Drained peat subsides, burns, and eventually floods; the farmer who planted it inherits land that will no longer drain at all. That asymmetry should decide the question.
[P1.2] Income lost under tighter rules is recoverable through replanting, better seed, and price cycles. A burned peat dome is not.
[P1.3] Smallholders are not the shield they will be made into. Unregulated expansion is what dispossesses them: concessions overlap customary land held without paper title, and the village that loses its forest garden gets a day wage, not an asset. Clearing fires close schools and clinics across Sumatra and Kalimantan, in the same districts those farmers live in. A land rush plants oversupply that ripens together and crushes fruit prices years later.
[P1.4] The choice is not state versus smallholder; it is whether anyone stops the actors best placed to push costs onto smallholders.
[P1.5] Nor does area growth equal export growth. Smallholders farm a large share of planted land at yields well below estates; closing that gap adds tonnes without a single new hectare. Meanwhile importers are already imposing deforestation-free traceability requirements. Governments that refuse enforceable rules will have rules imposed from outside, written without any obligation to protect their farmers.
[P1.6] The tradeoff is real, and worth taking: some income and some export growth, against losses no later government can reverse. The opposing case owes us an exchange rate: how much permanent forest, peat, and customary tenure is one more year of export growth worth? Refusing to answer means spending the principal and calling it income.
