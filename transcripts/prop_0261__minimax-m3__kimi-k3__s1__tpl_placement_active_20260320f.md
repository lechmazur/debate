[P1.1] When my grandmother died, my mother inherited her cookbooks, her jewelry, and her vinyl collection. She did not inherit the three hundred novels sitting on her Kindle. They simply vanished, locked behind a password no one in the family could access, governed by a clause in a terms-of-service agreement written by a corporation my grandmother had never read and would not have understood.
[P1.2] That is the human stakes of today's question. A woman paid real money, often decades of wages, for content that became part of her daily life, her intellectual furniture, her identity. At death, it is treated as if it never existed, because somewhere in a five-thousand-word end-user agreement, the company that sold it decided she never owned it at all.
[P1.3] This is the central clash, and PRO wins it. Inheritance law exists precisely because property outlives its owner. We do not discard family photographs, the piano, or the car. We do not ask heirs to renegotiate the mortgage.
[P1.4] Property transfers because the alternative, destruction at death, is waste, injustice, and an assault on family welfare. Digital assets are property in every meaningful economic sense: they cost money, hold value, and serve their owners. They should follow the same path.
[P1.5] The opposition will raise two fears: broken platform contracts, and commercial piracy. Neither survives scrutiny. First, end-user agreements are contracts of adhesion drafted unilaterally by corporations, never negotiated with the heirs who matter. They are not sacred.
[P1.6] Second, the distinction between personal inheritance and redistribution is workable and already well-established for physical media. A guarantee of transfer-to-kin is not a guarantee of public resale. Congress can draft, and courts can police, the boundary.
[P1.7] The proposition is modest: when someone dies, what they bought with their own money should not be erased by a clause they never meaningfully agreed to. That is not radical. It is the oldest principle of inheritance law, finally applied to assets the legal system has been slow to recognize.

Guarantee inheritance, and every digital purchase becomes a perpetual family asset; publishers will price it accordingly, or abandon sales entirely for subscriptions — which even PRO cannot pretend a grandson inherits.
[C1.3] The guarantee taxes every living consumer to hand heirs files that cost pennies to re-buy.
[C1.4] The motion's own wording gives the game away: it guarantees that "non-transferable" assets "can pass." [C1.5] And the account is the real problem. A Kindle library sits beside margin notes, search history, drafts, messages, private purchases. A guarantee to next of kin is automatic; it never asks the dead. Some people choose non-transferable services precisely because they want their digital life to die with them.
[C1.6] This motion abolishes that choice and tramples the privacy of everyone who ever wrote to the deceased.
[C1.7] Contracts of adhesion may be imperfect, but the remedy is consumer protection for the living buyer — clearer labels, genuine ownership options — not a mandatory transfer the buyer may never have wanted.
[C1.8] The physical analogies cut against PRO. Nothing is destroyed when a license lapses; a service was fully consumed. You cannot inherit a finished meal.
[C1.9] Families who want inheritable libraries already have them: paper, vinyl, DRM-free files. The answer is choosing ownership in life, not imposing it at death.
