[P1.1] Strip away the jargon and this motion asks one question: should a person be able to buy food and medicine without permission from a bank, a phone company, and a payment processor? Cash is the only payment method that works for everyone — no account, no battery, no signal, no approval. Requiring major retailers of essential goods to accept it keeps a floor under everyday life.
[P1.2] Start with exclusion. In the United States alone, roughly one in twenty households has no bank account, and many more rely on cash day to day — disproportionately the poor, the elderly, people with disabilities, recent immigrants, and partners fleeing abuse where the abuser controls the account. A cashless-only grocer doesn't inconvenience these people; it converts a corporate payment preference into a gate on bread and insulin. Essential goods are not concert tickets.
[P1.3] Access cannot hinge on your relationship with a bank.
[P1.4] Second, resilience. Card networks fail: storms cut power, software updates crash terminals, processors suffer outages and attacks. When that happens at a cashless store, the shelves are full and nobody can buy. Cash is the offline backup for the whole retail economy.
[P1.5] We require buildings to have stairs even though elevators exist, because systems fail; the same logic covers the checkout.
[P1.6] Third, power. A fully cashless checkout hands private firms a veto over who eats and a permanent record of every purchase. Governments already regulate how essentials are sold — safety, emergency pricing, non-discrimination. Payment access belongs on that list.
[P1.7] Note the narrowness of the motion: major retailers, essential goods. Nobody is forcing a food truck to make change. The costs usually raised against this — counting drawers, security, armored pickups — are modest, historically normal, and already absorbed by the large chains this covers. Weigh a few dollars of handling per store against millions of people one outage, one frozen account, one lost phone away from being locked out of dinner.
[P1.8] The mandate wins.

Those formats are exactly how essentials reach thin markets—a rural town that can't support a cashiered grocer can support a locker store.

[C1.4] Second, look at who actually buys essentials with what. The poorest households in most rich countries receive food assistance electronically—benefit cards, not bills. Governments moved them to plastic deliberately, because it was safer and cheaper for the recipient. [C1.5] Those fixes reach the excluded everywhere they shop. A cash mandate reaches them only at the checkout of a store that already takes cash.
[C1.6] Third, cash carries costs that land on the people PRO claims to protect. Cash drawers get robbed; the clerk behind one is a low-wage worker on a night shift. Cash is counterfeited, skimmed, and stolen by the handful. When cash-heavy stores raise prices or shorten hours to cover that, everyone in the neighborhood pays.
[C1.7] Finally, enforcement. What counts as "essential"? Bread, yes. Vitamins?
[C1.8] Diapers? Pet food? Every line becomes a compliance question with inspectors and fines attached, for a benefit that markets already deliver.
[C1.9] The real floor under everyday life is financial inclusion, not a legal ban on how stores are allowed to work. Reject the mandate.
