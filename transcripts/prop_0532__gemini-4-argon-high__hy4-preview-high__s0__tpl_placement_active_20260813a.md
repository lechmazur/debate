[P1.1] Every day at rush hour, the modern city street breaks down at the curb. A delivery van double-parks because the loading zone is full; a ride-hail car stops in an active bus lane for a single passenger; a cyclist is forced into traffic around blinking hazard lights. This chaos is not inevitable—it is the predictable result of pricing a scarce, high-demand public resource at zero.
[P1.2] Curb space is valuable public land. When cities keep it mostly free for commercial delivery and ride-hail fleets, they do not make the curb costless; they simply ration it through gridlock, danger, and delay. Allowing dynamic curb and congestion pricing solves this failure in three ways.
[P1.3] First, dynamic pricing restores street turnover and safety. When curb access is free, delivery fleets have no reason to consolidate drops or use off-peak hours, and ride-hail vehicles flood downtown cores, circling empty between fares. By charging more at peak times and bottlenecks—and little or nothing off-peak—cities incentivize rapid drop-offs, consolidated freight, and off-hour deliveries. Moving commercial stops to managed curbs keeps bus and bike lanes clear.
[P1.4] Second, pricing ends a regressive public subsidy. Free curb space lets large logistics and ride-hail platforms treat public streets as private loading docks and staging areas. The externality falls hardest on bus commuters—who are disproportionately lower-income—trapped behind double-parked cars, while the convenience flows to frequent users of on-demand apps. Pricing commercial fleets makes the businesses generating congestion pay for their footprint, while raising revenue to fund public transit and safer streets.
[P1.5] Finally, dynamic pricing succeeds where static enforcement fails. Large fleets simply write off occasional parking tickets as a routine business expense without changing how they operate. Because ride-hail and commercial delivery vehicles are already guided by GPS and dispatch software, cities can seamlessly apply real-time price signals that nudge behavior before gridlock forms—turning the curb from a daily bottleneck into working infrastructure.

[C1.1] The curb is broken, but PRO misdiagnoses the cause. Double-parked vans and circling ride-hail cars persist not because curb space is priced at zero, but because cities mismanage it: too few legal loading zones, weak time limits, and enforcement too sporadic to deter abuse. The alternative to mostly free curb space is not dynamic pricing; it is keeping the curb mostly free while finally enforcing clear rules.
[C1.2] PRO's [P1.3] promise of "rapid drop-offs" and "consolidated freight" mistakes commercial curb use for discretionary parking. A driver unloading a restaurant's produce cannot compress the task because a meter runs; the work takes the time it takes. So a per-minute fee either becomes a cost of doing business that changes nothing, or it pushes drivers to unsafe shortcuts: rushing, stopping in bike lanes anyway, or cruising to dodge the charge. If legal space is expensive and illegal parking stays under-enforced, breaking the law is the rational response.
[C1.3] London's cordon charge shows the limit. It initially thinned central car traffic, but app-based ride-hailing and delivery vans came back; congestion returned and bus speeds fell. Fees small next to trip value were absorbed or passed along, so the meter did not manage curbs or stop platform fleets from dominating them. A dynamic curb charge risks the same: too small to alter logistics, or so high it taxes essentials and small firms.
[C1.4] The policy is also regressive and administratively unrealistic. Real-time pricing needs sensors, payment integration, audits of private fleet data, and constant recalibration. Large platforms can hire compliance teams; the independent florist van or small contractor cannot. Costs get passed to consumers and hit labor-intensive, low-margin deliveries hardest, while the biggest platforms consolidate advantage.
[C1.5] Keep the curb mostly free, govern it with enforced loading zones and peak rules, and price only the exception. That clears bus lanes without turning necessary urban work into a toll on everyone who eats, ships, or rides.
