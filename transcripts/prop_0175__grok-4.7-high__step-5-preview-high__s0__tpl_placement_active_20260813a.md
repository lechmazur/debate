[P1.1] These licenses fail the only test that matters: whether the rule tracks real danger or merely tracks who already has the credential. Hair braiding shows the mismatch in pure form. Braiders were barred unless they completed hundreds, sometimes well over a thousand hours, of cosmetology school — chemicals, cuts, and coloring that braiding does not use. That was not a response to injury.
[P1.2] It was a toll of time and tuition that salons and schools could impose and that newcomers, often immigrant women, could not pay. Where the requirement fell away, the predicted danger did not appear. The people the rule had locked out could finally work.
[P1.3] Interior design is the same fence in a nicer frame. Load-bearing work, wiring, and gas lines are already governed by building codes and by licenses for architects, engineers, and contractors. What an interior-design license polices is layout, finishes, and a job title. A bad sofa plan is not a public-health emergency.
[P1.4] A degree plus apprenticeship before someone may sell that advice does not stop structural failure. It stops competition.
[P1.5] Extend the point to eyebrow threading, basic floral work, and other low-risk trades, where the realistic downside is a poor job, not bodily harm. Fraud law, ordinary negligence, insurance, reputation, and voluntary certification already sort quality. A mandatory license adds exclusion those tools do not require.
[P1.6] What settles "primarily" is the map. The same occupation is tightly licensed in one state and barely touched in the next, without a visible safety crisis on the lighter side. If the license were a safeguard, the lighter states would be visibly dangerous. They are not.
[P1.7] What does track the rules is pressure from people already inside. Boards dominated by current practitioners write the hours, exams, and fees that raise rivals' costs. That is incumbent protection, not a guardrail. High-risk medicine is a different question.
[P1.8] For these fields, the license is a fence.

[C1.2] A license is not a toll on talent; it is the only reliable way to demand that a practitioner know scalp pathology, infection control, and when to refuse a damaging service.
[C1.3] Interior design carries the same logic into the built environment. Layout determines egress, ventilation, lighting, and accessibility for people with disabilities. A designer who omits a second means of escape or specifies finishes that trap moisture has not merely offended taste. They have created a hazard residents and buyers cannot see until it is too late.
[C1.4] The building code and the architect’s stamp do not cover every space a designer shapes. The license fills that gap by requiring demonstrated knowledge of safety codes before a professional is trusted with occupied space.
[C1.5] The other side treats high tuition and long hours as proof of incumbent protection. But competence has costs, and verification has costs. Every credible safety profession—nursing, electrical work, plumbing—requires sustained education. Singling out these fields because their tools look simple mistakes simple tools for the absence of risk.
[C1.6] Nor do reactive alternatives solve the problem. Negligence and fraud law compensate after harm, but they cannot unscar a scalp, un-break an ankle on a poorly lit stair, or guide a consumer who cannot judge safety in advance. Licensing is preventive. It sets the floor before the stranger is alone with your head or your home.
[C1.7] That is not a fence. That is the point.
