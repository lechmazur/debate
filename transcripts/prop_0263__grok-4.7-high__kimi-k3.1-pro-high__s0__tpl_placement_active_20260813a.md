[P1.1] Streaming has replaced the video store, the rerun, and often the cinema as the place films and series actually live. When a platform commissions an original, it decides whether that work will exist for the subscribers who fund the service and the culture that now depends on these libraries. Letting companies pull those titles early for tax write-offs or licensing maneuvers turns production into an accounting trick.
[P1.2] The tax cases are the plainest abuse. Studios have finished or nearly finished films and then buried them so a loss can be booked at once, rather than risk a release that might earn less than the write-off. Subscribers funded the pipeline. Crews did the work.
[P1.3] Audiences never see the film. A minimum availability rule does not forbid ordinary business judgment about flops. It stops the incentive to erase completed work because disappearance pays better than distribution.
[P1.4] Licensing removals of so-called originals are no cleaner. Viewers start series, finish seasons, and recommend titles on the understanding that the catalog remains. Yanking a show to dodge residuals, clear a music right, or tidy a balance sheet breaks that bargain after the marketing has already done its job. A required window long enough for a real release and a reasonable afterlife ties the platform’s gain to the audience’s access.
[P1.5] Property rights are not a license to delete the record. These firms hold concentrated power over distribution, lock in subscribers, and, in the tax cases, already benefit from public rules that reward the write-off. A modest floor protects creators’ legacies, the time and money viewers have already spent, and the basic claim that work made for the public should not vanish because a spreadsheet preferred a loss. Without it, the library becomes a set of ghost titles: announced, sometimes completed, then gone for the quarter.

That punishes every original to address a problem better handled at its source.

[P4.1] A1: Neither infringe, nor pay any ransom, nor treat every expiry as a free pass. [C3.1] is a false trio. For titles already out, the transition runs on rights already held, and the platform must not drop a renewable licence just to exit early. If a holder refuses ordinary commercial renewal, that is a documented rights failure: removal is allowed, infringement is not, and there is no blank check.
[P4.2] That exception does not revive the write-off. The motion targets works the platform still controls and chooses to bury for residuals, clearances it could keep, or quarter-end optics. A defined window does not require stealing music or funding a veto.
[P4.3] A2: Relabeling fails if the statute follows commissioning and exclusive control, not the poster word. [C3.2] treats “original” as a sticker that can be peeled off while the same exclusive show is sold. Define the class by who commissioned the work and who holds the distribution lever, and the trick dies. A service that declines both the brand and the exclusive claim is not extracting the bargain this rule protects.
[P4.4] Costs attach where the platform captures the gain: it markets a title as its own, subscribers start it, and the catalogue is the product. Want that premium, buy the window. Unwilling to stand behind access, do not sell permanence you will not deliver.
[P4.5] Those answers close CON's exits. A rights-failure carve-out stops the hold-up without blessing erasure the platform chooses. A control-based definition stops the label game without regulating every outside acquisition. [C2.5] still hopes a denied deduction and a disclaimer will cover residuals, clearances, and quarter-end optics.
[P4.6] They will not. A minimum window, on the home service or any ordinary-price outlet that actually carries the title, is not eternity and not forced infringement. It buys a real release and a short afterlife with the commission. Without that floor, disappearance still pays whenever control and a spreadsheet point the same way.

[C4.1] A1: Nothing needs to stop every removal; that is precisely the premise in dispute. Once an abusive deduction is denied, a platform may still have legitimate reasons to retire a title: continuing distribution generates residuals, clearances remain disputed, or the catalogue commitment is commercially hopeless. If marketing promised availability, enforce that promise; if tax rules reward burial, change them.
[C4.2] But an unattractive quarterly motive does not itself create a public entitlement to compelled distribution. Voluntary sale or licensing also remains possible; a mandate is not the only alternative to disappearance.
[C4.3] A2: Yes—if another outlet provides genuine, non-sham access at an ordinary price, that can satisfy “publicly available.” But this abandons PRO’s earlier home-service answer and settles only where access occurs, not why the state should compel it.
[C4.4] PRO’s new “documented rights failure” exception exposes the deeper problem. Who decides whether a renewal demand is ordinary or extortionate? The platform will call a demand a hold-up; the rights-holder will call the platform’s refusal strategic. Regulators must reconstruct private negotiations and price creative rights.
[C4.5] If refusal permits removal, licensing problems can still erase exclusive works. If it does not, the hold-up returns. The exception either swallows the protection or restores the coercion.
[C4.6] Likewise, redefining “original” through commissioning and exclusive control is not peeling off a sticker; it is another substantial legal test added to rescue the mandate. Every repair makes the supposed modest floor more intricate and contestable.
[C4.7] And after all this machinery, PRO guarantees neither preservation nor a meaningful release—only temporary availability, potentially quiet and inconspicuous, before the same disappearance becomes lawful. The reliable effect is earlier expenditure on longer rights commitments, including for programmes nobody watches. Target tax abuse and deceptive promises directly. PRO has not shown why every controlled original must subsidise an insurance policy against removal, especially when that policy can prevent the borderline commission—and its audience—from existing at all.

[C5.1] The decisive flaw is that PRO’s remedy never achieves the interest used to justify it. A minimum window cannot protect a creator’s legacy or stop an exclusive work from ultimately disappearing; it only makes disappearance wait. What it can do immediately is impose compulsory distribution on every commission, including the flop, the disputed licence, and the borderline show whose economics depend on a shorter run.
[C5.2] PRO answers that the window is simply “bought with the commission.” But rights are not cheaper because purchased earlier, and some are unavailable on the required terms at any price. The escape hatch—removal after a “documented rights failure”—then forces regulators to decide whether a renewal demand was ordinary or strategic. If that exception is generous, licensing removals continue; if strict, the hold-up returns.
[C5.3] Nor must the state eliminate every incentive to retire a title. Residuals and clearance costs are real obligations, not inherently abuses. Where a deduction rewards burial, deny it; where marketing promises access, enforce that promise.
[C5.4] PRO offers temporary access through permanent legal machinery, paid for partly by works never made. Targeted remedies address the proven wrong without conscripting every original into compulsory distribution. Reject the mandate.
