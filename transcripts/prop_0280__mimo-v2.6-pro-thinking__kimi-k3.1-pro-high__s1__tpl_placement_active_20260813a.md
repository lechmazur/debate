[P1.1] The modern wedding is a luxury product wearing a moral halo. That is the problem.
[P1.2] Consider what "traditional" now requires: a multi-vendor, multi-day production whose costs scale with the guest list, plus a new line item every few years — the engagement shoot, the rehearsal dinner, the videographer, the curated aesthetic that has to survive being photographed from every angle. A ceremony and a party have become a staged experience with an audience.
[P1.3] Two consequences follow. First, the price has detached from the couple's actual income and life stage. Even a modest traditional wedding can rival a used car or a down payment, and a substantial share of couples fund it with debt or raided savings — delaying homeownership, an emergency cushion, or having kids. This is a decade-shaped financial decision made under social pressure.
[P1.4] Second, the design increasingly serves observers rather than the marriage. Aesthetics chosen for photographs. Guest counts inflated by guilt invitations. Moments engineered for the feed.
[P1.5] When the event is built for an audience, the couple is performing a role, and nobody is owed a performance.
[P1.6] The obligation question is the whole debate. An obligation needs a creditor and a justification. Who is owed? Not the guests, who never asked for a lavish party.
[P1.7] Not the couple, who are often happier and wealthier with something smaller. The clearest beneficiary is an industry that has spent generations rebranding spending as virtue.
[P1.8] And "tradition" is weaker ground than it looks. The white dress, the diamond ring, the big reception are largely modern commercial inventions, not inherited moral law. A practice becomes traditional partly by being repeatedly sold.
[P1.9] I am not arguing that no couple should throw a big wedding. People may choose it freely and meaningfully. The proposition is narrower and stronger: given the cost and the performance, couples should feel little default obligation to comply. Anyone insisting otherwise must explain why a stranger's aesthetic expectations should sit on your credit card.
