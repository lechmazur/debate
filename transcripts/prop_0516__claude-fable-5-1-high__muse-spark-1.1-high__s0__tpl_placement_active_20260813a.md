[P1.1] A public university sits inside a town, not above it. When it decides how many students to admit, it is also deciding how many new bidders show up for the same finite stock of apartments. In a market with a healthy vacancy rate, that barely matters. In a market where vacancy has collapsed and rents are climbing double digits, every additional admission letter is a bid against a nurse, a bus driver, or a domestic first-year who cannot find a room within an hour of campus.
[P1.2] The motion asks a modest thing: when that shortage becomes severe, the university should throttle the one intake it fully controls rather than pretend capacity is infinite.
[P1.3] Notice the structure of the incentive. International tuition flows to the university's budget. The cost of the extra demand—higher rents, displacement, students packed six to a two-bedroom—lands on the surrounding community and on the students themselves. That is a textbook externality, and unconstrained recruitment is the university refusing to internalize it.
[P1.4] A conditional cap simply says: you may not book the revenue while exporting the bill.
[P1.5] Housing supply cannot respond fast enough to be the whole answer. Approvals, financing, and construction typically take years; a recruitment cycle takes months. Enrolment grows at the speed of marketing; housing grows at the speed of concrete. A cap is the circuit breaker that closes that gap.
[P1.6] It can, and should, lift automatically as vacancy recovers or as the university delivers new beds—which gives the institution a real reason to build rather than merely lobby.
[P1.7] This is not hostility toward international students. They are often the first victims of the shortage—arriving to garage conversions, illegal sublets, and rents they were never warned about. Canada's recent move to cap study permits, and Dutch universities' own retreat from unlimited English-language intake, came after exactly these conditions, not before them.
[P1.8] The choice is proportionate restraint triggered by measurable harm, or growth unmoored from the place that hosts it.

Local housing collapses because cities for decades refused to build, protected single-family zoning, and let investors convert rentals to Airbnb. A domestic sophomore from three hours away and an international sophomore need the same apartment. [P1.3] calls this an externality, but the externality is produced by every non-local student. [C1.6] PRO cites Canada and the Netherlands as models, but both saw university budgets crater, domestic programs cut, and rents keep rising because the non-student demand remained.
[C1.7] Housing policy belongs to housing authorities. Universities should be forced to house what they admit, to co-invest, to upzone campus land. Using the admissions office as a rent-control agency guarantees you damage research, talent, and town economies without moving vacancy an inch.
