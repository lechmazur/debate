[P1.1] Unabated coal is the central climate problem because it is carbon-intensive, long-lived, and still expanding in the very economies that must lead. Coal supplies roughly 40 percent of energy-related CO2. A single large unabated plant can emit millions of tonnes of CO2 a year, and a plant permitted today can operate for four decades. That is the trap: decisions made in the next few years lock in emissions into the 2060s, not merely to 2050.
[P1.2] Voluntary pledges have not solved it. Each major economy has an incentive to let others move first while enjoying cheap, familiar coal. The result is delay, life-extension, and new capacity, which raises the eventual cost of transition and deepens climate damages.
[P1.3] A binding agreement solves the coordination failure. A common timeline is not uniformity of policy; it is a synchronized destination for unabated coal. When investors know the endpoint is real, capital stops financing new unabated capacity and flows instead to renewables, storage, grids, and firm clean power. Binding force matters because voluntary timelines are revised whenever coal prices spike or growth slows.
[P1.4] A credible treaty turns a series of national ifs into a market signal: the asset stranding risk is shared, not dumped on first movers.
[P1.5] Differentiated support is what makes this just and politically durable. Major economies did not industrialize under the same constraints, so a common endpoint must be paired with finance, technology transfer, and grid investment, so that capacity—not loopholes—decides the pace. Support is not charity that weakens the common timeline; it is the mechanism that lets all members retire or skip unabated coal without sacrificing growth.
[P1.6] First movers are compensated, not punished, because the deadline is shared. Without it, the deadline would be ignored by those lacking means or watered down into a vague promise. The real contrast is not coercion versus freedom, but coordinated phaseout versus every country guarding its own coal while the atmosphere absorbs the cost.

[C1.1] A "common timeline" for phasing out coal sounds neat on paper [P1.3], but in reality, it treats radically different energy systems as if they face the same clock. Demanding that India, China, the United States, and Europe hit the exact same deadline is a recipe for diplomatic stalemate or severe energy insecurity.
[C1.2] First, a common timeline defies physical and economic reality. In mature Western economies, electricity demand is relatively flat, coal plants are decades old, and alternatives like natural gas or nuclear are already established. In rapidly growing major economies like India, power demand is surging and coal fleets are young—built to provide reliable baseload power and prevent blackouts. Forcing a synchronized endpoint [P1.3] means rich nations merely retire geriatric plants near the end of their natural lives, while emerging economies must prematurely scrap modern infrastructure and gamble their industrial growth.
[C1.3] Second, PRO’s fix—“differentiated support” [P1.5]—is a fiscal fantasy. Compressing a developing giant’s transition onto a Western schedule would require hundreds of billions of dollars in direct grants to buy out young plants and rebuild national grids. Yet wealthy nations have consistently struggled to deliver even modest climate finance, and recent transition deals have offered mostly debt rather than grants. No government in New Delhi or Jakarta will sign a binding legal obligation to shut down its primary baseload power in exchange for an uncertain promise of Western aid.
[C1.4] Finally, demanding a binding treaty [P1.3] guarantees deadlock. Because sovereign states do not sign up for domestic blackouts, forcing a rigid, binding deadline creates a fatal dilemma: either the timeline is aggressive and the biggest coal users refuse to join, or the timeline is stretched to accommodate the slowest country, giving faster economies an excuse to delay. Nationally tailored transitions driven by falling clean-energy costs work; a one-size-fits-all treaty fails before the ink dries.
