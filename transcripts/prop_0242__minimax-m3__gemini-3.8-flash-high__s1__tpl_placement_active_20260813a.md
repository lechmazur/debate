[P1.1] The polarization curve in Western democracies tracks social media adoption, not cable news viewership. Cable news peaked in the 1990s and early 2000s; affective polarization began its sharp climb only after Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube reached mass scale around 2008–2012. The timing is suggestive, but the mechanisms are decisive.
[P1.2] Cable news is a broadcast medium. A small number of producers push content to a passive audience. Viewers choose a channel, watch, and leave. Social media is participatory: every user is a potential broadcaster, and the platform's algorithm decides what gets seen.
[P1.3] That architectural difference matters.
[P1.4] Three mechanisms make social media more polarizing. First, algorithmic amplification. Engagement-optimized feeds disproportionately surface emotionally arousing content, which political extremes produce more reliably than the center. A cable network has to fill a schedule; a feed only has to keep you scrolling.
[P1.5] Second, homogeneous clusters. Friends-and-followers networks sort users into like-minded bubbles, creating epistemic isolation that professional cable news, with its cross-cutting coverage, never fully achieved. Third, micro-targeted political messaging. Cable news buys audiences in bulk; platforms sell them by the slice, letting campaigns and bad actors deliver bespoke polarization to narrow slices of voters.
[P1.6] The result is a different kind of polarization: identity-based, performative, and viral. The Capitol riot, the spread of false fraud narratives across platforms, and the radicalization pipelines on YouTube and Facebook did not require a cable set. They required retweets, recommendations, and group invites.
[P1.7] Cable news contributed to polarization. Social media restructured the terrain on which polarization happens. That is the more consequential effect.

[C1.2] Crucially, cable news captured the voters who actually determine democratic outcomes: older, reliable, high-turnout primary voters. If algorithmic feeds were the primary engine of political division, polarization would be concentrated among the digitally native young; the reality is the exact opposite.
[C1.3] Furthermore, cable news drove polarization by directly capturing the political elite. Cable news fundamentally rewrote the incentives of democratic governance, punishing bipartisanship and institutional compromise from Washington to state legislatures.
[C1.4] Social media certainly offers new tools for performative outrage, but cable news supplied the structural foundation: the ideological sorting of the public, the capture of governing institutions, and the radicalization of the actual primary electorate. Social media merely inherited an ecosystem whose battle lines cable news had already decisively drawn.
