[P1.1] Air pollution became a public problem when cities stopped treating it as an individual nuisance and started treating it as a regulated pollutant. Noise is the pollutant we have chosen not to see. It raises stress hormones, fragments sleep, and is linked to cardiovascular disease; chronic traffic and construction noise can impair children’s learning. That is not a lifestyle complaint.
[P1.2] It is a health exposure measured in decibels and duration, just as air pollution is measured in concentrations and time.
[P1.3] The current approach is complaint-driven, so relief depends on who knows how to file, who can wait, and who is not intimidated. Binding limits on traffic, construction, and commercial noise would work the way air-quality rules do: clear standards, monitoring, permits, penalties, and predictable compliance. Cities can set night-time construction bans, equipment standards, sound walls, low-noise pavement, route restrictions, and closing-time controls. None of that requires perfection.
[P1.4] Air quality rules also use thresholds and averages; noise can use time-of-day caps and zoning-based limits.
[P1.5] The opposition will call this expensive and subjective. But air rules were once called impossible too. The real question is who bears the cost of doing nothing: residents near highways, rail yards, and late-night venues, disproportionately poorer and minority neighborhoods. They absorb the sleep loss and disease while the noise producer externalizes the harm.
[P1.6] Same urgency does not mean identical rules. It means cities treat noise as an enforceable public-health limit, not a favor granted after complaints. If a city can require cleaner air, it can require quieter nights. The proposition simply asks for equal seriousness about a pollutant that enters homes through walls.

[C1.1] Elevating noise to the same urgency as air pollution sounds principled until you ask what would be enforced. Air pollutants linger, travel, and accumulate: particles drift across districts, build up in lungs, and can be measured as a stable concentration at a chimney or tailpipe. Noise is instant and hyperlocal. A truck passes and it is gone; a jackhammer stops and exposure ends.
[C1.2] The same level can be an ambulance, a delivery, wedding music, or unwanted construction. Context is not a detail — it is the harm.
[C1.3] That breaks binding, air-style limits. Air sensors can average pollution over hours in a way that tracks dose. Sound swings second by second with wind, building reflections, an open window, or listener sensitivity. Average it and you legalize the bursts that wake people; cap every peak and you criminalize normal urban life.
[C1.4] Enforcing either would need microphones everywhere, inspectors chasing transient events, and penalties on fleeting readings drivers and businesses cannot predict or contest.
[C1.5] The named targets — traffic, construction, and commercial activity — are not smokestacks that can add a filter. Restrict construction hours further and housing, schools, and road repairs take longer and cost more, worsening a shortage that already prices out the poor. Restrict traffic noise and you restrict buses, freight, waste collection, and emergency movement, or force expensive vehicle and road replacement. Restrict commercial noise and the burden lands first on small bars, restaurants, workshops, and vendors that provide entry-level jobs and neighborhood life.
[C1.6] That is why the equity promise reverses. Wealthy districts have setbacks, insulation, and lawyers to weaponize enforcement. Large developers can buy quieter equipment and absorb fines; a small contractor, immigrant-owned restaurant, or night-shift business cannot. Air-style rules would expand discretionary, subjective policing of everyday disputes, extracting fines from those least able to comply.
[C1.7] Cities should improve insulation, protect hospitals and schools, and punish reckless repeat offenders. That is proportionate nuisance control. Parity with air quality is a category error with regressive costs.
