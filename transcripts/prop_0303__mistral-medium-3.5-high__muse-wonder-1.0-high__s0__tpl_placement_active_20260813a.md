[P1.1] Meritocracy is the founding myth of modern elites, not their lived reality. The persistent illusion that elite educational and professional pathways select purely on talent and effort obscures how they systematically reproduce class privilege through both overt and subtle mechanisms. Legacy admissions at universities like Harvard openly reserve spots for the connected, while less visible barriers—expensive SAT prep courses, unpaid summer internships that only the wealthy can afford, and familial networking that opens doors closed to outsiders—stack the deck against those without inherited advantage.
[P1.2] At many top schools, children from wealthy families outnumber those from broad lower-income groups—a pattern too consistent to be accidental. These aren’t anomalies; they’re structural features intentionally designed to maintain existing social hierarchies. Even when working-class students gain entry to these institutions, they confront a hidden curriculum of norms and expectations—unspoken rules about dress, travel experiences, or cultural references—that implicitly favor the already affluent and can make outsiders feel like imposters. In the professional world, this pattern of privilege reproduction continues unabated: prestige firms in law, finance, and consulting recruit overwhelmingly from a handful of elite universities, while unpaid internships serve as gatekeepers for entire industries, effectively excluding those who cannot afford to work for free.
[P1.3] Successful people then compound the distortion by attributing their status solely to individual merit, ignoring the structural tailwinds of family wealth, elite socialization, or the financial safety net that allows for risk-taking and failure without catastrophic consequences. This self-serving narrative isn’t merely naive; it’s functionally essential to the system’s survival. It recasts structural privilege as earned desert, framing persistent inequality as the natural and just result of individual effort and talent rather than the predictable outcome of a system designed to reward inherited advantage.
[P1.4] The overstatement of meritocracy isn’t incidental—it’s the very mechanism by which privilege reproduces itself while appearing fair, ensuring that each generation of elites can sincerely believe their success was inevitable and deserved.

[C1.2] A century ago elite posts ran on patronage, family firms, and who your father knew at the club. Today entry to medicine, engineering, academia, law, finance, and technology runs through graded exams, transcripts, portfolios, publications, and structured interviews. A legacy name cannot pass organic chemistry, debug code under observation, or survive blind grading. That is why elite campuses now draw nationally and globally, fund low-income students heavily, and enroll many first-generation and immigrant students whose parents held no elite credentials.
[C1.3] Recruiting from a handful of universities is not mainly class loyalty. Tutoring and unpaid internships can help at the margin, but free preparation, paid internships, need-based aid, and work-study have eroded pay-to-enter gates, and none substitutes for the scores and performance needed to clear the threshold.
[C1.4] What remains is a real but upstream correlation: affluent parents can buy stability, books, and time that help children develop measurable skills earlier. Pathways that then reward those skills are not reproducing privilege as such; they are doing what meritocracy promises, albeit on unequal childhood inputs. Confusing that distinction underrates the mobility produced for talented outsiders who enter without connections and still rise.
[C1.5] Successful people today hardly deny advantage, either. Elite institutions constantly discuss privilege, luck, mentorship, and access, and their graduates fund scholarships, mentor strangers, and build pipeline programs. Elite university degrees, often secured through legacy preferences and expensive extracurricular opportunities, similarly advantage those with inherited capital. Prestige internships, many of which remain unpaid, exclude those who cannot afford to work for free.

The overrepresentation of the affluent at every stage of elite pathways isn’t a bug—it’s the inevitable outcome of a meritocracy that confuses purchased preparation with earned desert, and in doing so, naturalizes inequality as fair.
