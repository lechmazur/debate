[C1.1] The proposition frames a choice between a required course and "elective exposure or family background." That is a false dichotomy, and that is where the clash should sit.
[C1.2] First, opportunity cost. Graduation requirements are already a zero-sum game. Add a serious course and something must give. In most districts the casualty is art, music, foreign language, or an advanced science.
[C1.3] We do not improve a graduate's life by teaching them to read a W-2 if we have stopped teaching them to read a poem, write a coherent essay, or grasp basic economics already embedded in social studies. The proposition treats graduation slots as a wish list with infinite room. They are not.
[C1.4] Second, the effectiveness premise is weak. Ordinary background on consumer-education programs suggests financial knowledge decays fast and behavior barely moves. Students who ace a budgeting unit still take payday loans. Graduates who can recite Roth versus Traditional still underfund retirement.
[C1.5] A required course risks producing confident graduates who think one semester entitled them to expertise, which is worse than humble ignorance.
[C1.6] Third, the proposition ignores the actual third path: integration into existing math and economics curricula, credit-union partnerships, simulation programs, and targeted modules timed to real decisions. These cost less seat time, reach the same students, and adapt as markets change. A static required course written this year will be stale before half its first cohort graduates; the student-loan landscape alone has shifted multiple times in a decade.
[C1.7] Finally, "serious" is doing heavy lifting. Who teaches it? With what curriculum? Graded how?
[C1.8] Without answers, we are mandating an unmeasured course at scale, taught by teachers whose own financial experience may be limited, and tested by methods nobody has defined.
[C1.9] The motion is not "should students learn finance." It is "should we lock it into graduation requirements." That is a much narrower claim, and on that claim, the answer is no.
