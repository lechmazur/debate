[P1.1] The foundational premise of a constitutional democracy is that citizens are governed by laws they can actually understand. We hold people to the unyielding principle that ignorance of the law is no excuse. Yet legislatures routinely write statutes in an impenetrable dialect accessible only to a specialized guild of lawyers and judges. This is fundamentally unjust.
[P1.2] When a tenant cannot decipher their housing rights, or a small business owner cannot understand safety regulations without paying hundreds of dollars an hour for legal translation, the law ceases to be a public instrument and becomes a private barrier.
[P1.3] Opponents defend legalese in the name of precision, but hyper-technical drafting often creates an illusion of certainty. Labyrinthine clauses, archaic terms of art, and endless nested cross-references do not eliminate ambiguity; they multiply it. They create dark corners where well-funded litigants can exploit loopholes that ordinary people cannot see. Plain language, by contrast, forces lawmakers to articulate clear, principled rules that focus on core intent and practical application.
[P1.4] Even where drafting in plain language requires sacrificing marginal technical precision, that tradeoff is overwhelmingly justified. A legal code that achieves ninety-five percent precision but can be read, understood, and followed by the public produces far better social outcomes than a code that achieves ninety-nine percent precision on paper but is practically unintelligible to those it binds. Courts already resolve ambiguities and apply common-sense standards every day. What they cannot repair is the deep alienation that occurs when citizens are ruled by commands they cannot comprehend.
[P1.5] The law belongs to the public, not to the bar. Mandating plain language restores democratic accountability and ensures that legal compliance is a basic civic right rather than an expensive luxury.

[C1.1] The proposition treats statutes as public announcements rather than binding rules that must settle disputes. Precision is how a legislature limits official discretion and protects people from after-the-fact interpretation. When forced to make text immediately readable, drafters reach for broad words such as “reasonable” or “public interest.” Those words are plain, but they delegate real lawmaking to agencies and judges. The public reads a friendly sentence while unelected interpreters later decide what conduct is actually prohibited.
[C1.2] That weakens democratic accountability, not strengthens it.
[C1.3] “Plain language” is also not a stable standard. A clause that seems clear to a commercial landlord may be opaque to a tenant with limited literacy, limited English, or a disability. The mandate therefore creates a collateral test: was the statute plain enough? That question would be litigated in the same courts and billable-hour system the PRO side describes as a barrier.
[C1.4] It adds a procedural fight without producing universal comprehension.
[C1.5] Legal terms of art carry settled consequences. Replacing “consideration” or “joint and several liability” with everyday paraphrase can invite disputes over defenses, exceptions, and cross-references. In tax, property, criminal sentencing, and limitation periods, small wording shifts can determine who owns a home, whether a conviction stands, or whether a claim is time-barred. That is not losing “some” precision; it is changing substantive legal rights.
[C1.6] Accessibility can be achieved without rewriting the statute itself. Legislatures should publish plain-language guides, official examples, and translations along with the law. But the binding text must remain exact enough to constrain the state and settle cases. A mandate to simplify operative language offers readability while hiding the missing detail in later interpretation.
[C1.7] That is not legal access; it is uncertainty with better marketing.

[C2.1] PRO's rebuttal misses what the mandate actually changes. The choice is not between an opaque code and a readable one; it is between putting unavoidable technicality in the statute or hiding it in regulations, agency guidance, and judicial gloss. If a legislature must make every operative sentence accessible, it will not write a 30-day filing deadline with enumerated exceptions. It will say “promptly” or “without unreasonable delay.” That is plain.
[C2.2] It is also far less constraining. A citizen reads it, yet cannot know whether filing on day 31 survives until a court decides. That is not public control; it is delegation marketed as clarity.
[C2.3] PRO says hyper-technicality conceals discretion [P2.1]. The opposite is often true. Defined terms, numerical triggers, and explicit conditions tell an official what may happen. Replace them with broad plain words and you get precisely the “common-sense standards” PRO celebrates [P2.3]. “Good faith” and “due diligence” are familiar phrases, but they fuel some of the most expensive, unpredictable litigation in commercial and employment law.
[C2.4] If those are the model, the mandate buys readability by importing standards where rules once existed.
[C2.5] Nor does an explanatory guide create a two-tier system [P2.2]. It honestly separates the authoritative text from a reading aid. The worse tiered system is PRO’s: a “plain” statute whose surface meaning diverges from the technical meaning courts later enforce. The tenant who reads “reasonable notice” may believe she knows her rights, but the binding rule is whatever courts have layered onto that phrase.
[C2.6] A guide at least does not pretend to be the operative law.
[C2.7] Finally, PRO claims plain drafting preserves precision [P2.3]. It does not. Terms like consideration or joint and several liability are compressed references to settled doctrine. Paraphrasing them either creates a longer, no less technical explanation or substitutes a colloquial phrase whose boundaries courts must redraw.
[C2.8] That changes legal rights, not just readability. The mandate fails on its own terms: it offers the feeling of comprehension while reducing actual notice and constraint.
