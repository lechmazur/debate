[P1.1] A five-year-old cannot sign a contract, open a bank account, or consent to a medical procedure. She cannot consent to becoming the product in a business watched by millions. Yet in large family-influencer operations, that is exactly what happens: her meals, meltdowns, illnesses, and birthdays are scripted, filmed, sponsored, and sold.
[P1.2] This is not casual sharing with grandparents. Once channels reach commercial scale — brand deals, merchandise, and income the family depends on — the child is working. Child actors have long received basic protections, including trust accounts holding part of their earnings until adulthood. Family influencers exploit a loophole: because the set is the kitchen and the boss is a parent, the labor is relabeled as family life and left unprotected.
[P1.3] That loophole creates two harms. First, expropriation. The child's face and story generate the clicks, but parents control the revenue and can spend it all before she is old enough to ask for an accounting. A mandated share held in trust, with transparent records, is not punishment; it is recognition that the earner deserves part of what she earned.
[P1.4] Second, permanence. A child cannot foresee how a viral tantrum, bath video, health diagnosis, or awkward adolescence will follow her into school, dating, employment, and adult identity. Parents and platforms profit now, while she bears the reputational and safety costs for life. A right, on reaching adulthood, to demand removal of monetized content featuring her as a minor restores a minimum of control over a childhood she never chose to publish.
[P1.5] The conflict of interest is built in. Algorithms reward intimacy and crisis, so the incentive is to film more, disclose more, and stop later. Earnings and removal rights correct that incentive without banning sharing altogether. Above a commercial threshold, a child is not content, and parents should not build a business on her without protecting what she earned and who she gets to become.

[C1.3] The proposed protections are both unfair and unworkable. Mandating trusts for children’s ‘earnings’ assumes we can separate the child’s contribution from the parent’s work—an impossible division. [C1.4] The right to removal is equally impractical: the internet is permanent, and fair use would render it meaningless. Worse, it could let a child, at 18, unilaterally destroy a family’s business without compensation for the parent’s years of work. [C1.5] Consider the bureaucracy: auditing every video to assign a value to a child’s presence is absurd. Once content is shared, it’s copied and reposted; platforms can’t guarantee removal, and fair use exemptions gut the rule. The motion’s threshold for ‘large’ is undefined, leaving families in legal limbo.
[C1.6] Existing safeguards already cover abuse. The motion’s fear of ‘permanence’ is overblown. Child labor laws, privacy protections, and platform policies address genuine exploitation. Moreover, most children of influencers grow up without lasting harm, and the benefits often outweigh the risks.
[C1.7] The real harm is not the industry but the rare, already-illegal abuse that existing laws can address. We must not dismantle a thriving industry over speculative harms.
