[P1.1] A credential answers the question that actually matters to an employer, a patient, or a client: can this person do the work? Physical residency answers a different question: where has this person been sleeping? For a century we have made the second question the gate and treated the first as an afterthought. That ordering is backwards, and the motion simply asks us to fix it.
[P1.2] Consider a radiologist in Lagos who has completed an accredited online specialisation, passed remotely proctored board-style exams, and reads scans for a hospital network in Manchester every night. Nothing about that work requires her body to cross a border. Yet the system will spend eighteen months and thousands of pounds scrutinising her right to be physically present, while the credential that actually protects patients gets a rubber stamp or, worse, is dismissed because it was earned on a screen. Recognition effort should track risk, and the risk sits in competence, not in geography.
[P1.3] Second, online credentials are the easier thing to verify, not the harder. A digitally issued certificate can carry a cryptographic signature, a unique identifier, and an assessment record that a registrar can check in seconds. Residency documentation is a pile of utility bills, tenancy contracts and stamped passports, forged routinely and audited slowly. If we care about fraud, we should prefer the artefact that is designed to be checked over the one that is designed to be lost.
[P1.4] Third, gating opportunity on residency rations talent by wealth. Relocation costs a year of income for most of the world. Credential portability lets skill move while the person chooses whether to move, which also eases the brain drain that residency-first systems accelerate, because a country keeps its engineer while still exporting her expertise.
[P1.5] The opposition will say presence proves commitment and immersion. But presence is a proxy, and we now have direct measures. When you can assess the thing itself, clinging to the proxy is not caution. It is habit.
