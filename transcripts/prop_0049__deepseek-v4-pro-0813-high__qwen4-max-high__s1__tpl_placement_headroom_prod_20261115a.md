[P1.1] Behavioral advertising is not a neutral way to show teens relevant products. It is a system that watches what they watch, infers their moods, insecurities, and interests, then serves ads engineered to hit those weak points. For adults that is already corrosive; for children it is predatory, because the developmental capacity to recognize persuasion and resist impulse-driven targeting is still forming.
[P1.2] The core reason prohibition is justified is not that all advertising to minors must disappear. It is that data-driven targeting changes the power relationship. A billboard asks for a moment of attention. A behavioral profile asks a platform to learn a thirteen-year-old's late-night scrolling, body-image queries, financial pressure points, and social anxieties, then auction that understanding to the highest bidder.
[P1.3] The child gets no meaningful consent. Parental consent is largely fictional: the actual data collection is continuous, opaque, and spread across apps, devices, and ad networks no family can audit.
[P1.4] The predictable harms are not rare edge cases. Targeting optimizes for engagement and conversion, which rewards content and products that leverage insecurity, impulsivity, peer comparison, and addictive loops. Restrictions on alcohol, tobacco, and gambling ads already recognize that minors need steeper protection from certain commercial influence. Behavioral targeting is the same principle applied to the method, not just the product.
[P1.5] It weaponizes the very information a minor cannot protect.
[P1.6] A ban is also administratively cleaner than weaker alternatives. Age gates and opt-ins fail in practice; they push the burden onto the least equipped party. Prohibiting behavioral targeting for under-sixteens shifts the default from exploitation to protection, while still permitting contextual ads that do not depend on mining a child's behavioral identity. That is the targeted, proportionate reform the proposition requires.
