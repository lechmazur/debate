[P1.1] The motion does not defend every trophy hunt. It defends the narrow, testable model: legal, quota-limited hunting whose revenue is transparently audited and shared with conservation and local people. That distinction matters because the strongest practical objections—corruption and elite capture—are exactly what the transparency condition targets. The real choice is not between a morally pure world and trophy hunting; it is between a regulated channel that pays for habitat and an unregulated market or converted land that does not.
[P1.2] Wildlife survives where it outcompetes other land uses. A rural community facing crop damage, livestock loss, and danger from elephants or lions will not tolerate animals because distant donors feel warmly about them. Trophy hunting turns a small, quota-set offtake into a high-value, low-volume revenue stream. That money can pay game guards, compensate losses, and make habitat more valuable intact than converted to cattle or crops.
[P1.3] Namibia’s conservancy approach is the familiar illustration: community-managed quotas and benefit-sharing have given local people a reason to protect wildlife rather than eliminate it. I need not romanticize every case; the motion requires transparent use, so the model is conditional by design.
[P1.4] Banning it does not make the killing disappear. It shifts demand to black markets, unmanaged hunting, or simply removes the revenue that makes tolerance rational. Photo tourism is wonderful where it works, but it needs roads, lodges, security, and reliable wildlife viewing; many dry rangelands cannot attract enough visitors to replace hunting revenue. The moral discomfort of wealthy critics is not a conservation plan for the people who bear wildlife’s costs.
[P1.5] So the central question is comparative: does legal, transparent, community-linked hunting leave more habitat and more animals than a ban? Where the conditions hold, yes—and that is the proposition.

Laws are not written to apply only in the rare village where auditors show up; once trophy hunting is legal, it is legal everywhere in that jurisdiction, and licenses get issued long before anyone checks whether the money actually reached a ranger station or a herder's compensation fund. The condition in the resolution is a theoretical ideal; the policy it licenses is a blanket permission.
[C1.2] By the time transparency fails — and across much of the hunting industry, historically only a small sliver of trophy fees has ever reached local communities, with most captured by outfitters, brokers, and officials — the animal is already dead and the quota already filled. You cannot retroactively un-shoot a lion because the audit came back bad.
[C1.3] Even granting perfect bookkeeping, trophy hunting damages the thing it claims to fund. Hunters do not take random individuals; they target the biggest maned lions, the largest-tusked elephants, the most impressive-horned kudu — precisely the genetic stock that drives reproductive success and herd fitness. Decades of this selective pressure measurably shrinks tusk size and mane quality in hunted populations. That is not a side effect transparency can fix; it is baked into what the activity *is*.
[C1.4] You can launder the money perfectly and still be quietly degrading the species.
[C1.5] And the comparative case PRO needs — that hunting is the only revenue mechanism that works in these landscapes — is overstated. Community conservancies, non-lethal eco-tourism, international conservation payments, and certified wildlife credits have scaled in many of the same dry, low-infrastructure regions PRO cites, without requiring that the intact adult of the species be killed to pay for its own species' survival. If a non-lethal mechanism can deliver comparable local income, trophy hunting isn't a necessary trade-off — it's a convenient one we've chosen not to replace.
