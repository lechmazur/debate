[P1.1] Prophylactic antibiotic use in healthy livestock is not a marginal farming input; it is a systematic subsidy for drug resistance. The motion asks us to accept higher meat prices and lower yields, and that trade is worth making because the cheapest meat produced this way externalizes a catastrophic public-health cost.
[P1.2] When healthy animals are given routine low-dose antibiotics, the bacteria they carry are placed under constant selection pressure. Susceptible strains die, resistant strains thrive, and those microbes leave the farm on meat, in water, through waste, and through workers. Resistant infections then spread far beyond the farm. The result is not abstract: routine procedures—C-sections, hip replacements, chemotherapy, pneumonia treatment—become riskier when the drugs meant to prevent or cure infection fail.
[P1.3] The core principle is simple: antibiotics are a shared, exhaustible medical resource. Using them to compensate for crowded housing, poor sanitation, or low-cost production depletes that resource. A ban preserves the option value of effective medicine. No slight increase in meat prices compares to the costs of longer hospital stays, failed surgeries, expensive second-line drugs, and deaths from infections we once treated easily.
[P1.4] The ban is also feasible. It does not mean refusing antibiotics to sick animals. It means ending routine population-wide dosing of healthy animals, replacing it with vaccination, better hygiene, lower stocking densities, and treatment when disease occurs. Countries and producers can transition; the market adjusts, and governments can cushion vulnerable consumers.
[P1.5] What we cannot adjust away is resistance once it is entrenched.
[P1.6] If the price of meat reflects the true cost of preserving life-saving medicines, then that is not a failure of policy. It is the minimum honest accounting. For that reason, countries should ban prophylactic antibiotics in healthy livestock, even if meat becomes more expensive and yields fall.

[C1.1] The motion rests on a false equivalence: routine prophylactic use under veterinary direction is not indiscriminate growth promotion. Prophylaxis is preventive medicine. It is given when animals face a defined, high risk of disease—at weaning, during transport, after mixing—where an outbreak would mean suffering, death, and then mass treatment with higher doses. Ban it, and you do not remove antibiotic use; you delay it until animals are sick.
[C1.2] That is worse for welfare and often worse for resistance.
[C1.3] Take a pig barn after weaning. If a known pathogen is circulating, preventive medication can keep a manageable exposure from becoming pneumonia and mortality. Under a ban, the farmer waits. By the time animals are sick, the same drug may be needed at higher doses for longer, or a last-resort drug may be needed.
[C1.4] That is not reduced selection pressure; it is delayed and concentrated selection pressure.
[C1.5] Resistance is real, but the motion treats livestock prophylaxis as the main tap. Human prescribing and hospital transmission are enormous drivers, and growth promotion is the clearest misuse. A country can phase out growth promotion, require prescriptions, improve biosecurity, and monitor use without banning preventive treatment. The proposition skips that scalpel and reaches for a hammer.
[C1.6] Then comes the "even if" clause. Higher meat prices and lower yields are not rounding errors. They hit low-income households hardest and can push production to countries with weaker antibiotic rules. Resistant bacteria do not stop at customs.
[C1.7] A ban may make domestic statistics look clean while shifting the problem abroad.
[C1.8] The right policy is stewardship, not prohibition. Keep veterinary prophylaxis as a targeted tool, restrict it where evidence shows overuse, and invest in vaccines, hygiene, and better housing. The motion offers a symbolic ban with regressive costs and no guarantee of better public health. Reject it.
