[P1.1] Guaranteeing universal primary care is the least a government can do to make health real rather than theoretical. A checkup, a blood-pressure reading, treatment for an infection, prenatal visits, vaccinations, and management of diabetes or asthma are not luxuries. They are the early, inexpensive interventions that decide whether a minor problem stays minor or becomes a crisis.
[P1.2] Left to ability to pay, primary care fails predictably. People without coverage or with tight budgets delay care they cannot price in advance, skip medication reviews, and miss screenings. They do not disappear; they reappear later in emergency rooms with strokes, advanced cancers, uncontrolled diabetes, and infections that could have been treated weeks earlier for a fraction of the cost. That is not market efficiency.
[P1.3] It is the most expensive and cruel way to ration care — by delay and deterioration.
[P1.4] Universal primary care also creates benefits no private transaction captures. Clinicians who see a whole population can track hypertension, immunize children, spot outbreaks, coordinate referrals, and keep chronic conditions stable. Continuity matters: a doctor who knows a patient notices weight loss, depression, or a missed refill before it becomes hospitalization. A fragmented, pay-at-the-door system loses that information and shifts costs onto families, employers, and public hospitals anyway.
[P1.5] This proposition is deliberately modest and practical. It guarantees a floor, not a ceiling. Governments ensure everyone can access essential prevention, diagnosis, and ongoing care, while elective and supplementary services — cosmetic procedures, faster access to non-urgent extras, private rooms, expanded amenities — can remain in private markets. That preserves choice and innovation, relieves public budgets, and focuses public money where need and return are greatest.
[P1.6] The alternative is already a two-tier system, just an indefensible one: prevention for those who can pay upfront, catastrophe for those who cannot. A guaranteed primary-care floor reverses that logic, saves money downstream, and treats basic health as a condition of equal citizenship, not a perk of income.

[C1.1] A healthcare system is not a collection of disconnected services; it is a continuous pipeline. The proposition presents an attractive compromise: guarantee primary care as a modest, universal floor while leaving elective and supplementary care to private markets [P1.5]. But primary care cannot function as an island. By severing initial diagnosis from the specialized interventions needed to actually treat patients, this proposal creates a system that is clinically hollow, economically distorted, and profoundly unequal.
[C1.2] First, primary care is an entrance, not a cure. A routine checkup does not remove a tumor, and a blood test does not rebuild an arthritic joint. If governments guarantee only primary consultations while downstream specialty care, advanced diagnostics, and surgeries remain behind private market barriers, the guarantee becomes a cruel dead end. A patient receives an early diagnosis of heart disease or cancer, only to find the necessary treatment unaffordable.
[C1.3] Far from ending rationing by delay [P1.3], this model merely shifts rationing from the clinic door to the hospital ward. The poor are diagnosed; the wealthy are treated.
[C1.4] Second, it sparks a severe workforce drain. Thriving private markets in secondary and elective care pull physicians, nurses, and capital away from the public primary tier. With higher margins in the private sector, public primary care inevitably suffers chronic shortages, assembly-line appointments, and devastating waitlists. A legal entitlement to a family doctor is meaningless when clinicians have migrated into lucrative private clinics.
[C1.5] Finally, it misallocates finite public resources. Guaranteeing primary care universally subsidizes routine visits for wealthy citizens who can readily pay, while leaving low-income patients exposed to catastrophic costs for complex conditions. Healthcare cannot be cut in half: a universal primary floor without guaranteed follow-through is an empty promise that leaves the most vulnerable stranded.
