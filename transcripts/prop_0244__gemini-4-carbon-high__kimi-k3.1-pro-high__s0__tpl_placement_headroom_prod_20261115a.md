[P1.1] When body-worn cameras swept through policing a decade ago, civil rights groups, lawmakers, and reform advocates championed them as a game-changing fix. Following high-profile killings where officers' accounts went unchallenged, advocates expected a lens on every chest to transform accountability: cameras would sharply deter excessive force, end "word-against-word" cover-ups, and ensure reliable consequences for abuse. Measured against those high expectations, body cameras have plainly underdelivered, because advocates mistook visibility for accountability.
[P1.2] First, advocates underestimated institutional capture. Cameras do not operate independently; they are worn, triggered, and archived by the very agencies they are meant to police. In critical moments, cameras still conveniently fail to record due to late activation, muted audio, or supposed technical glitches, rarely resulting in serious discipline. Furthermore, departments control the vault.
[P1.3] When footage exonerates an officer or incriminates a civilian, agencies routinely release it within days. When footage looks damning for police, officials frequently withhold it for months or years behind "ongoing investigation" exemptions, blunting public scrutiny. Selective transparency is public relations, not accountability.
[P1.4] Second, even when crystal-clear footage emerges, it collides with an unchanged legal fortress. Advocates assumed undeniable video would yield undeniable justice. Yet graphic recordings of questionable force routinely end without indictments or firings because deferential use-of-force laws, union arbitration rules, and qualified immunity shield officers anyway. Worse, chest-mounted cameras literally adopt the officer's physical perspective.
[P1.5] Shaky, close-range video exaggerates a civilian's sudden hand movements while hiding the officer's own posture, often making split-second fear easier to claim in court.
[P1.6] Consequently, as cameras expanded across major departments, the dramatic, uniform drops in police violence that advocates predicted largely failed to materialize. Instead, prosecutors gained massive evidence troves against everyday citizens, while entrenched police impunity persisted. Cameras changed what we see far more than what policing answers for.
