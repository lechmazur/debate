[P1.1] The Constitution places the power to tax and regulate commerce squarely in Congress. Tariffs are taxes on imported goods, and the Framers deliberately assigned taxing authority to the legislative branch precisely because it is the most accountable to the people. When Congress allows the President to impose sweeping tariffs unilaterally under open-ended "emergency" statutes, it does not merely delegate authority; it abandons its constitutional duty.
[P1.2] The consequences are immediate and severe. Presidents have invoked national security to slap broad tariffs on allies and adversaries alike, triggering retaliatory measures that devastate American farmers and manufacturers. Statutes like Section 232 and the IEEPA were designed for narrow contingencies, not for restructuring global trade relationships. A single executive can roil markets, spike consumer prices, and rupture supply chains without a congressional hearing, a committee markup, or a recorded vote.
[P1.3] That is not agile leadership; it is arbitrary economic coercion made possible by statutory loopholes that no emergency ever intended.
[P1.4] Restoring sole congressional authority would inject stability. Businesses invest on timelines measured in years, not news cycles. When broad tariff policy requires legislative deliberation, outcomes reflect balanced regional interests rather than the impulse of one administration. Steel tariffs justified on national security grounds, for example, raise costs for automakers and builders nationwide—costs imposed without legislative consent.
[P1.5] Congress can still act with speed when genuine crises demand it, but sweeping, long-lasting trade barriers should survive the scrutiny of representatives from agricultural, industrial, and consumer states alike.
[P1.6] Democratic accountability demands no less. The Framers separated powers to prevent exactly this kind of unilateral economic control. Congress should reclaim its constitutional role, eliminate presidential emergency powers over broad tariffs, and return trade policy to the branch the people actually elect to tax them.

[C1.6] The constitutional case is also overstated. The nondelegation doctrine has never required Congress to handle each tariff itself.
