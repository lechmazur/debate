[P1.1] When drought hits one country and a harvest holds next door, a truck of maize crossing the border is not abstract commerce. It is whether prices in a deficit market stay survivable. Export bans cut that lifeline at the exact moment it matters most.
[P1.2] An enforceable AfCFTA limit solves a classic collective trap. A single government banning maize, rice, or wheat exports may calm politics at home for a few weeks, but it instantly raises prices for import-dependent neighbours, especially landlocked and Sahelian states with few alternatives. Those neighbours then hoard and ban in turn. During COVID disruptions and after global grain shocks, this cascade across Africa turned local shortages into regional price spikes.
[P1.3] Each ban was individually rational and collectively disastrous. Only a binding rule, not a voluntary pledge, breaks that race to close borders first.
[P1.4] Limiting bans also protects the banning country itself over time. Farmers and traders will not plant for surplus, invest in storage, or build cross-border supply chains if any good harvest can be trapped by decree. Predictable access to regional markets is what turns a bumper crop into income and a deficit year into an importable solution. Without enforceable discipline, AfCFTA promises integration in normal times and permits fragmentation in crises — precisely when integration is supposed to prove its value, and precisely when private actors will discount its promises.
[P1.5] This is not a demand for unconditional exports while people starve at home. Limiting rules can allow narrow emergency measures: short duration, advance notification, exemption for existing humanitarian shipments, and proof that less harmful tools — releasing reserves, targeted cash support, temporary import easing — are being used. What enforceability adds is a ceiling, a clock, and consequences through AfCFTA dispute processes. That keeps the exception from becoming an open-ended ban.
[P1.6] Staple-food security in Africa is regional because weather, pests, and conflict are local. A continent that shares risk survives shocks; a continent that bans exports exports hunger instead.

[C1.7] The alternative is not isolation but resilience: coordinated strategic reserves, regional early warning systems, and voluntary solidarity mechanisms that respect sovereignty while building trust. Many export restrictions are not last-resort responses to famine; they are pre-emptive insurance, imposed early and kept long after prices ease. [P2.6] Notification, time limits, and later justification are administrable even for low-capacity states, and clearer rules help them resist pressure to extend bans indefinitely.
[P2.7] Finally, reserves and early warning [C1.7] are not alternatives; without an anti-cascade rule they fail for the same reason.
