[P1.1] Public schools are not monuments. They are where a country decides whether the next generation will inherit its myths or its memory. Requiring students to study their own country's major atrocities and abuses is essential because a democracy cannot be defended by citizens taught to misunderstand it.
[P1.2] First, omission is itself a curriculum. If students learn independence, progress, and wartime sacrifice but not slavery, colonial dispossession, internment, forced sterilization, or state violence against dissenters, they do not get a neutral history. They get a usable past: heroic, innocent, and therefore entitled to repeat itself. Abuses thrive on that innocence.
[P1.3] A student who never learns how laws, courts, and bureaucracies enabled cruelty is equipped to recognize those mechanisms when they return in new language.
[P1.4] Second, honest history strengthens rather than weakens attachment. Loyalty built on flattery collapses at the scandal, documentary, or protest. Loyalty built on truth can withstand criticism because it does not require denial. Germany's reckoning with the Holocaust did not make civic commitment impossible; it made postwar democracy more self-conscious and harder to corrupt with nostalgia for dictatorship.
[P1.5] By contrast, societies that center only victimhood teach students that wrongdoing is what others do, making every accusation feel like betrayal rather than information.
[P1.6] Third, a requirement matters because local discretion fails under pressure. Without a mandate, the most uncomfortable chapters are the first to be softened, postponed, or framed as aberrations by a few bad actors. A requirement does not dictate shame. It dictates inclusion: major atrocities must be studied with evidence, causes, victims, perpetrators, and resistance.
[P1.7] Students can learn achievements, courage, and reform. Indeed those achievements mean more when reformers are shown confronting power, not decorating a spotless story.
[P1.8] A country confident enough to teach what it did wrong is teaching students how to keep it right.
