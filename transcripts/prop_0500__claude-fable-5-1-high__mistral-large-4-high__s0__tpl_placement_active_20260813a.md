[P1.1] Every honest accounting of a tunnel begins with the wrong comparison. The question is never "tunnel versus free." It is "tunnel versus what a city can actually build on its surface," and in the places that matter most, the surface option is either gone or ruinously expensive in ways nobody puts on the invoice.
[P1.2] Start with land. A new surface rail corridor or highway through a dense city means buying or seizing hundreds of parcels, demolishing homes, and litigating for a decade. Elevated lines blight everything beneath them. Street-level utility trenching closes the same arterials every few years for water, then gas, then fiber, each closure spraying delay costs across thousands of businesses and commuters that never appear in the project budget.
[P1.3] Tunnels internalize what surface works externalize. When you compare full costs, the "expensive" tunnel is frequently the cheap option.
[P1.4] Second, tunnels are century assets. London's deep tube lines from the early 1900s still carry millions daily. A high upfront bore amortized over a hundred years of uninterrupted service is a bargain next to surface infrastructure that is rebuilt, widened, and re-trenched repeatedly. Boston's Big Dig was famously over budget, yet it returned a strip of downtown to parks and reconnected the waterfront; the alternative was a rusting elevated highway in perpetuity.
[P1.5] Third, resilience. Buried power and water lines shrug off hurricanes, ice storms, and wildfires that flatten overhead networks, and storm-management tunnels under cities like Tokyo have kept whole districts dry. As climate risk grows, undergrounding becomes insurance, not luxury.
[P1.6] Finally, the cost objection is partly self-fulfilling. Boring is expensive where countries tunnel sporadically, re-learning everything each time. Countries that keep a steady pipeline, like Spain, drive per-kilometer costs far lower because crews, machines, and permitting mature. Sustained investment is how the cost comes down; refusing to invest keeps it high.
[P1.7] The motion asks whether necessity outweighs cost. On land, on lifespan, on resilience, and on the learning curve, it does.

[C1.1] The proposition asks governments to pour capital into the most expensive infrastructure available while treating the price tag as an accounting illusion. That is fiscal fantasy. When a kilometer of subway costs several times a surface line, "investing heavily" means choosing tunnels over hospitals, schools, and maintenance of existing roads. The externalities PRO cites are real but finite; they do not justify bankrupting budgets for marginal convenience gains.
[C1.2] Resilience is not a tunnel monopoly. Buried infrastructure drowns. Subways flood in New York, London, and Zhengzhou; when tunnels inundate, they become death traps with no easy evacuation and repair bills that dwarf surface alternatives. Earthquakes crack concrete tubes; fires in enclosed spaces are catastrophic.
[C1.3] Surface networks are visible, accessible, and repairable in hours rather than months. We need adaptable systems that fail visibly and fix quickly, not ones that fail catastrophically underground.
[C1.4] The learning-curve argument assumes every nation can sustain the perpetual construction treadmill Spain enjoys. Most face stop-start funding and geological surprises that turn tunnels into white elephants. The Channel Tunnel and Boston's Big Dig show how quickly "necessary" projects become budgetary disasters. Capital locked underground for decades cannot be redeployed when priorities shift.
[C1.5] Finally, surface constraints are often policy failures, not physics. Coordinated utility corridors, night-time roadwork, and better zoning reduce disruption without geological surgery. Not every country is Manhattan; most have space to build outward before they must dig down. Heavy tunneling is a luxury for dense, wealthy enclaves, not a universal necessity.
