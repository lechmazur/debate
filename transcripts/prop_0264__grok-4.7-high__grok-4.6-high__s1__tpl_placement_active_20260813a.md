[P1.1] Taxpayers currently underwrite police misconduct. Settlements and judgments for excessive force, false arrest, and other civil rights violations are paid by cities through general funds or municipal insurance pools. Individual officers rarely contribute anything. Qualified immunity and union contracts keep personal financial exposure minimal, so the cost lands on residents who neither ordered the conduct nor had a realistic way to prevent it.
[P1.2] That externality squeezes city budgets and diverts money from other public services.
[P1.3] Requiring personal professional liability insurance ends the subsidy. Coverage would be a condition of keeping the badge. Insurers would price premiums by actual risk: complaint history, prior payouts, force patterns, and whether the department trains and supervises seriously. Officers with clean records would pay ordinary rates.
[P1.4] Officers with repeated problems would pay much more, and the highest risks would be unable to buy coverage. Without coverage, they could not work. That market screen is faster and less politicized than internal systems that routinely delay or dilute consequences.
[P1.5] The incentives then run toward prevention. An officer who knows misconduct can raise next year's premium has a direct reason to choose restraint. A department that knows better practices will lower members' premiums has a reason to adopt them. Physicians already carry malpractice coverage that prices risk instead of billing general taxpayers.
[P1.6] Police exercise broader coercive authority. There is no good reason to exempt them.
[P1.7] Risk is not evenly spread. A minority of officers accounts for a disproportionate share of lawsuits and payouts. Personal coverage prices that concentration instead of averaging it across every household. It aligns payment with control and supplies an outside check where internal discipline has fallen short.
[P1.8] The financial cost of misconduct should not fall mainly on municipal taxpayers. Requiring officers to carry their own professional liability insurance is the direct way to stop that.

[C1.1] Requiring personal professional liability insurance does not take the cost of police misconduct off municipal taxpayers. It disguises it. Cities already indemnify officers for acts within the scope of employment and already fund judgments through general revenues or insurance pools. Make personal coverage a condition of the badge, and departments will reimburse the premium or raise pay to keep positions filled.
[C1.2] The bill still lands on residents. The subsidy is not ended; it is routed through a paycheck.
[C1.3] The market this proposal wants cannot price this risk fairly. Officers cannot choose their calls, refuse violent neighborhoods, or stop a lawsuit after a lawful but messy arrest. Claims are sparse, noisy, and political. Insurers will overcharge for high-complaint assignments or walk away from cities that need staffing most.
[C1.4] The supposed screen does not isolate a few bad actors. It taxes officers willing to work where risk concentrates, then prices them out.
[C1.5] That is not prevention. It is hesitation. An officer who knows a justified use of force or a nuisance settlement can spike next year's premium has a reason to hang back. High-crime neighborhoods then lose proactive work, not just payouts.
[C1.6] The motion trades a visible settlement line for an invisible drop in street-level deterrence. Public safety is the larger budget the motion ignores.
[C1.7] A minority of officers generating most claims is an argument for firing them and fixing supervision, not for outsourcing a public license to private underwriters. Physicians carry malpractice insurance because patients choose them and practice is largely individual. Police must respond to everyone, under policy, in teams, under commanders who control training and deployment. The city—not an insurer—is the actor that can actually change the risk.
[C1.8] Personal insurance fails its own goal and adds a worse one: a quieter, more expensive, less present police force, still paid for by the same taxpayers.
