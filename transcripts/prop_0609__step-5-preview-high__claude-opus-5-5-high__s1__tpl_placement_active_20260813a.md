[P1.1] Debt relief that ignores climate risk is not generosity; it is a write-down of a failing balance sheet while leaving the hazard intact. When a low-lying coastal state or a drought-prone agricultural economy borrows, creditors are not only betting on fiscal policy—they are betting on the physical survival of the tax base. If the port floods or the harvest fails, the debt becomes unpayable regardless of restructuring terms. Conditioning relief on climate-resilience investment is therefore not an ideological add-on.
[P1.2] It is basic credit-risk management.
[P1.3] The current system fails because it treats climate adaptation as a discretionary development goal, separate from fiscal stability. Vulnerable countries routinely spend more on servicing external debt than on protecting the roads, water systems, and farmland that generate the revenue needed to repay that debt. Unconditional relief breaks this trap only temporarily. Without dedicated resilience spending, the next cyclone or heat wave wipes out the fiscal space the relief created, and the country defaults again.
[P1.4] A dollar spent servicing old debt cannot rebuild the bridge that connects farms to markets.
[P1.5] Automaticity matters because discretionary conditions become bargaining chips for geopolitical favors or policy packages that have little to do with survival. A fixed, transparent rule—relief is released only against verified resilience spending—removes that arbitrariness. It gives debtor governments a predictable path and gives creditor nations a credible commitment that their concessions will not be eroded by the next disaster. It also prevents a race to the bottom, where the most climate-exposed borrowers receive the least credible relief because their collateral is literally underwater.
[P1.6] The objection that this violates sovereignty misses the point. A government that cannot protect its coastline or its water table does not enjoy meaningful sovereignty; it manages decline. The proposition aligns creditor interests with debtor survival: relief becomes an investment in continued repayment capacity rather than a transfer into a storm.

[C1.2] Start with the trap PRO describes in [P1.3]: vulnerable countries spend more servicing debt than protecting roads and water systems. Correct. But that is an argument for relief, not for conditions. These governments do not skip seawalls because they lack the will.
[C1.3] They skip them because the money is gone. Relief frees that money. Making relief contingent on resilience spending demands the investment first and releases the cash afterward, which asks a government to spend money it cannot access until it has already spent it.
[C1.4] Second, conditionality means delay, and delay is the real killer in a debt crisis. Under the heavily indebted poor countries initiative, relief was tied to policy milestones, and some countries waited years to reach completion while debts compounded and social spending was squeezed. Every verification audit and every dispute over whether a drainage project "counts" as resilience is another quarter of interest payments and another downgrade.
[C1.5] Third, PRO claims in [P1.5] that automatic rules remove arbitrariness. They move it. Someone must define qualifying investment and certify compliance, and that someone is the creditor. The bargaining chip survives under a greener label.
[C1.6] And rigidity has its own cost: when a country hit by a cyclone needs cash for food, clinics, and salaries this month, an automatic rule says relief waits until its adaptation ledger checks out.
[C1.7] Finally, consider who pays. The countries most exposed to climate damage contributed least to causing it. This motion tells them to earn relief from wealthy creditors by funding their own protection against harm those creditors' economies largely produced. That is not credit-risk management.
[C1.8] It is liability shifted onto the victims.
[C1.9] Resilience matters. But it gets built by restoring fiscal space quickly and funding adaptation directly, not by holding a solvency lifeline hostage to a compliance checklist.
