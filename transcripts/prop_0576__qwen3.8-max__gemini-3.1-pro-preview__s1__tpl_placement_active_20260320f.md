[P1.1] Tax advantages are not a universal privilege; they are a public subsidy for institutions that serve broadly charitable, educational, or spiritual purposes. The moment a religious organization stops functioning primarily as a house of worship or charity and becomes an overt partisan machine, it no longer satisfies the public-benefit rationale that justifies that subsidy.
[P1.2] The proposition is narrow. It does not say religion must be silent, and it does not punish belief. It says an institution that openly endorses candidates, mobilizes voters for one party, or makes electioneering a central activity should not have taxpayers underwriting that work. If a church, mosque, synagogue, or religious nonprofit wants to act as a partisan political actor, it may do so, but it should do so without special public support and under the ordinary rules that apply to political organizations.
[P1.3] Three harms follow from the opposite. First, compelled subsidy: citizens who disagree with a party or candidate are still forced, through lost tax revenue and deductible donations, to support religiously branded partisan campaigns. Second, unfair advantage: a partisan church becomes a subsidized campaign vehicle, able to attract contributions with tax benefits that secular political groups cannot claim.
[P1.4] Third, corruption of religion: when clergy become party operatives with tax privileges, spiritual authority is converted into political leverage, and public trust in both religion and government erodes.
[P1.5] The right line is functional, not theological. We ask what the organization does. Does it feed the hungry, educate, comfort the dying, and sustain communal worship? Then its exemption is defensible.
[P1.6] Does it operate primarily to elect allies, defeat opponents, and channel partisan identity? Then it has chosen a different role and should lose the advantages attached to a different one. The state should subsidize charity and spiritual care, not partisan power.
