[P1.1] Public universities are funded on a promise: public money turns into completed credentials and usable skills. In low-completion degree programs, that promise routinely breaks. Students enroll, accumulate credits and debt semester after semester, then leave with neither a degree nor a marketable license. The university has spent on instructors, advising, facilities, and financial aid; the student has spent years.
[P1.2] Neither gets the return.
[P1.3] Shift that marginal dollar to shorter vocational and technical pathways and the arithmetic changes. A one-year certificate in industrial maintenance, welding technology, cybersecurity support, or licensed practical nursing is built around a defined job, a defined skill set, and a finish line students can actually reach. Programs are typically cohort-based, sequenced, and tied to employers or clinical placements, which is exactly the structure that helps working adults, parents, and first-generation students persist.
[P1.4] The relevant comparison is not a graduate in engineering versus a welder. It is the student who would otherwise drift for three years in a program most entrants never finish versus that same student earning a credential in twelve months, entering work, and keeping the option to stack credits toward a later degree. One path leaves debt and a transcript employers cannot use. The other leaves income, experience, and a portable qualification.
[P1.5] This is also fairer. Prolonged non-completion falls hardest on low-income students who can least afford lost time, while well-off students can absorb an extra year or a changed major. Shorter pathways respect students as adults with rent and families: they offer speed, clarity, and lower exposure if life intervenes. Designed with transferable credits and advising, they are on-ramps, not dead ends.
[P1.6] Public universities should not abandon broad education, but they cannot defend subsidizing prolonged failure while hospitals, manufacturers, and infrastructure projects wait for trained workers. When a program consistently enrolls many and graduates few, resources should follow completion and employment — toward pathways that turn public investment into finished credentials.

[C1.1] PRO has built their entire case on a false and unstated assumption: that students who do not complete a low-completion degree program would instead succeed at a vocational pathway if only the original program was taken away. There is no good reason to believe this. Most students who drop out of these programs do not enroll in any other credential. They just leave higher education entirely, with exactly the same debt and lost time PRO decries, and now no future path to come back.
[C1.2] Public universities were never designed to be efficient credential factories. They exist to be the only public space where someone can show up uncertain, try things, take a semester off when a kid gets sick, change their mind twice, and still eventually build the life they want. A 31 year old warehouse worker who takes one sociology class a year for four years before graduating will count in your statistics as a bad completion rate. Under this policy, that program gets cut before he ever signs up.
[C1.3] This will not make the system fairer. It will tier it. Well-off students will still get the full university experience, the right to wander, the space to take longer. Working class students will be shunted into 12 month tracks, told they cannot be trusted with the option to aim for something more, that speed and completion matter more than what they actually want for their lives.
[C1.4] We do not fix bad student support by eliminating the things people are trying to reach. That is not reform. That is surrender.

[P2.2] That answers the transfer objection. Of course many students who drift three years and leave never re-enroll; that is the cost of intervening too late. Offer a maintenance certificate, LPN pathway, or IT credential at entry, and again when a student goes off-track in semester two, and you are not asking an exhausted dropout to restart. You are giving a still-enrolled student a reachable finish line.
[P2.3] Fixed sequences, cohorts, and placements remove the guesswork that makes open-ended programs hardest for students without a family roadmap.
[P2.4] The warehouse worker taking one sociology class a year is not the target, nor is he helped by preserving a program that fails most entrants around him. Slow progress can be banked. A stackable pathway lets him earn something usable along the way, keep studying sociology, and finish later — with income and protection if life interrupts year three, instead of credits that pay nothing until a distant graduation.
[P2.5] The tiering already exists. Affluent students can afford to wander and recover; poor students pay for wandering with debt and no credential. [P2.6] Equality is real choice with a credential at the end of each route, not being locked into the longest route to prove ambition.
[P2.7] Cohort advising, clear sequencing, and placement are support — built into a program designed to be finished. More money for the same structure is not loyalty to students; it is loyalty to the arrangement losing them.
