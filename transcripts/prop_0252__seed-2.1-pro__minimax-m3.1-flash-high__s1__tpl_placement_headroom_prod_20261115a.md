[P1.1] Unpaid internships outside strictly defined, genuinely educational placements operate as a discriminatory, market-distorting pay-to-play gatekeeper that governments have a clear obligation to heavily restrict. The narrow carveout for legitimate educational placements—supervised clinical rotations for health care workers, structured student teaching, accredited program placements with formal faculty oversight, clear learning objectives, and no replacement of regular paid staff—preserves the only scenarios where unpaid work delivers tangible, equitable educational value without exploiting workers or locking out marginalized candidates. All other unpaid internships rig entry-level hiring across high-growth, high-wage fields like media, policy, tech, and corporate services in favor of applicants with family wealth to cover months of rent, transit, and living costs with no income.
[P1.2] A talented working-class student who cannot afford to work for free in an expensive major city is shut out of the very career pipelines that lead to stable, middle-class work, no matter their skill or effort, while peers with parental subsidies collect the resume credentials needed to land permanent roles. That entrenchment of class stratification is not an accidental side effect; it is the predictable, documented outcome of unregulated unpaid internship systems. These roles also actively erode labor standards for all workers.
[P1.3] For decades, weak rules have let employers label almost any routine, menial role an “internship” to skirt minimum wage and overtime protections, with no requirement to deliver actual training or mentorship. When employers can tap an endless stream of young, eager candidates to do routine filing, research, administrative support, and client coordination for free, they face no pressure to create paid entry-level positions, or to pay entry-level hires a living wage when they do hire. The vague promise of “experience” does not justify a system that excludes low-income candidates, replaces paid work, and treats young workers as disposable free labor.

[C1.1] The proposition’s central problem is the word “heavily.” It treats unpaid work as acceptable only when it fits a narrow educational template, then invites governments and employers to police that boundary. Such classification will favor organizations large enough to hire lawyers and build compliance systems, while smaller nonprofits, start-ups, and community organizations face disproportionate barriers. Restrictions aimed at exploitation could therefore entrench established firms rather than open opportunities.
[C1.2] Unpaid trials and project work can serve a legitimate function even when they are not attached to formal course credit. A laid-off accountant might complete a short, supervised finance project to demonstrate current skills; a recent graduate might join a small conservation team; a small publisher might test an editor’s ability on a defined manuscript. These placements can involve genuine learning without replacing established workers. Yet under a strict reading of “genuinely educational,” they are vulnerable to prohibition simply because no institution formally certifies them.
[C1.3] The defensible abuse is not every unpaid arrangement. It is the use of the label to conceal productive labor, displace paid employees, provide no meaningful instruction, or indefinitely avoid compensation. Governments should prohibit those features and require clear terms, mentoring, and reasonable duration. They should not presume that a young person needs a formal classroom affiliation before an employer may offer a low-risk trial.
[C1.4] Class barriers remain a serious concern, but the opponent’s remedy may worsen access by shutting out the very small organizations that offer unconventional routes into work. A rule is not equitable merely because it removes every unpaid internship. It must distinguish exploitation from opportunity, and “heavily restrict outside genuinely educational placements” does not make that distinction. It replaces one broad discretion with another: the discretion of deciding which experiences officially count as education.

Small community groups rarely have the cachet to pull that off, so the burden of compliance falls heaviest on the bad actors already exploiting the system, not the tiny, community-focused placements CON holds up.
[P2.2] CON’s proposed alternative—targeting only the worst abuses while letting employers self-certify that roles offer mentorship, clear terms, and reasonable duration—is the failed status quo we already live with. The short, low-stakes trials CON cites—a quick manuscript edit, a discrete finance project to demonstrate current skills—are not internships at all, and no reasonable restriction would bar paid probationary shifts or bounded interview tasks.
[P2.3] What does get barred is the open secret of unpaid roles that replace paid entry-level work, stretch on for months with no guaranteed job offer, and only accept applicants who can cover rent and food without a paycheck. Framing that loss as a blow to unconventional opportunity ignores who is actually excluded when unpaid work passes as access: every qualified candidate without family wealth to subsidize their labor, who never even gets a foot on the career ladder. The “discretion” CON warns of in accrediting legitimate educational placements is trivial next to that unaccountable employer power.

A small community publisher, clinic, or charity may have no payroll system and cannot convert every exploratory placement into paid employment without abandoning the project. [C2.3] Equal formal treatment would not produce equal opportunity.
[C2.4] P2.2 likewise evades the distinction by saying short trials “are not internships at all.” The motion concerns unpaid internships, but even genuine internships can involve productive work; the relevant dividing line is whether a defined educational relationship exists, not whether every task has immediate market value. [C2.5] PRO correctly identifies unpaid work that replaces regular jobs, provides no instruction, or lasts indefinitely. Governments should require evidence of training, supervision, reasonable scope, and protection from displacement, while allowing genuinely educational placements judged by their substance. “Heavy restriction” gives governments an undefined mandate and invites enforcement against the least powerful organizations. The better policy polices exploitation directly instead of making credentialization the price of opportunity.
