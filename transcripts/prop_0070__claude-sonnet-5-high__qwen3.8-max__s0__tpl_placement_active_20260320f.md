[P1.1] Retreat from climate-vulnerable land is coming whether or not any government plans for it. The only real choice is between an orderly, funded, humane version of that retreat and a chaotic, expensive, unjust one triggered by the next flood or fire. That is the choice this debate is actually about, and it is why mandatory long-term relocation planning should begin now.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Planning does not mean bulldozing neighborhoods tomorrow. It means mapping which zones will become uninhabitable on a twenty- or thirty-year horizon, pre-negotiating land swaps, buyout formulas, and receiving-community infrastructure, and building the legal and financial scaffolding so that when a triggering disaster hits, a city can execute a plan instead of improvising one under emergency conditions with panicked residents and collapsed property values.
[P1.3] Compare the two counterfactuals. Louisiana's coastal parishes and Pacific island nations show what unplanned retreat looks like: people leave piecemeal, after their homes are destroyed and their insurance has vanished, with no coordinated place to go and no compensation mechanism ready. Communities fracture. Property values crash for those who leave last, wiping out the retirement savings of whoever couldn't sell early.
[P1.4] Contrast that with what happens when a city studies floodplain retreat in advance, zones new development away from the highest-risk areas, and lines up funding before the crisis: displaced people land somewhere with schools, jobs, and infrastructure instead of scattering into ad hoc diaspora.
[P1.5] Timing is the whole argument. Land use planning, infrastructure financing, and community buy-in all take years. If a city waits until repeated disasters force the issue, it forfeits exactly the lead time that makes relocation survivable rather than traumatic. Planning early is strictly cheaper too: pre-positioned buyouts cost less than post-disaster reconstruction-then-buyout cycles, which many flood-prone towns have already repeated two or three times.
[P1.6] The proposition doesn't ask cities to abandon mitigation or resilience spending. It asks them to stop pretending permanent occupation of doomed land is a real long-term plan. That single admission is what CON has to defend against, and it's indefensible.
