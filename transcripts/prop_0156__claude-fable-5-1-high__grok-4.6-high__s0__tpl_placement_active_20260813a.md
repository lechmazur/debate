[P1.1] The euro survived. That is the strongest thing anyone can say for the crisis response, and it is not the same as solidarity. A marriage that survives because divorce is too expensive is not a marriage that has grown closer. The question is what the response built, and the answer is resentment—in both directions.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. The response was structured as creditors lending to debtors under conditions, policed by a Troika, sold at home with morality tales: prudent northerners rescuing feckless southerners. That framing was politically useful in Berlin and Helsinki and poisonous everywhere else. Greeks, Portuguese, and Irish watched wages, pensions, and health budgets cut on instructions from unelected officials, while much of the "rescue" money flowed straight back out to repay French and German banks.
[P1.3] Meanwhile northern voters were told they were paying for others' sins. Both sides learned to distrust the other. Neither learned to trust Brussels.
[P1.4] Then the democratic bypass. Papandreou floated a referendum in 2011 and was gone within days. Berlusconi was replaced by a technocrat under market and Frankfurt pressure. In 2015, Greeks voted roughly three to two against the bailout terms, and within a week their government signed something harsher.
[P1.5] The Eurogroup, which dictated all this, kept no minutes and answered to no parliament. The lesson taught to millions of citizens was simple: your vote does not bind the machine.
[P1.6] The political returns came fast. Syriza, Podemos, Five Star, and a resurgent Front National on one side; the AfD, founded in 2013 explicitly against bailouts, and the True Finns on the other. Trust in EU institutions in the southern periphery fell from majorities to minorities and stayed there. Brexit campaigners pointed at Greece as the future.
[P1.7] Here is the decisive weighing: the euro was ultimately saved by the ECB's "whatever it takes," not by intergovernmental generosity, which arrived late and grudgingly. And when the pandemic hit, leaders deliberately designed the recovery fund to look nothing like the 2010–2015 playbook. They knew what that playbook had cost. So should we.

[P2.2] But membership retained under threat is not solidarity preserved. Greece stayed in 2015 because the alternative on offer was expulsion, with the German finance ministry openly floating a "time-out" from the euro. A partner who stays because the exit was made unaffordable has not experienced solidarity. He has experienced leverage.
[P2.3] The counterfactual in [C1.5] is rigged. The real choice was never rescue-as-delivered versus disorderly collapse. It was this response versus a different one: earlier debt restructuring instead of two years of lending into insolvency, an ECB backstop in 2010 rather than mid-2012, conditions negotiated with parliaments rather than dictated by a body that kept no minutes. Every alternative that would have cost less trust was rejected, usually because creditor politicians preferred the morality tale.
[P2.4] Look at what the "new common instruments" [C1.3] actually are. The ESM sits outside the treaties, answers to finance ministers, and lends only against conditionality. The fiscal compact added automatic sanctions. Banking union stopped short of common deposit insurance because northern governments would not trust southern banks.
[P2.5] These are not monuments to solidarity. They are distrust written into institutions—machinery built precisely so that no one would have to rely on anyone's goodwill again.
[P2.6] Then [C1.8] and [C1.9]: the vote "still mattered for governments" but "did not get to unwind the currency." Read that back. Citizens could change faces, never policy. That is exactly the lesson that manufactures distrust, and it is being offered as reassurance.
[P2.7] Finally, "whatever it takes" was Draghi's, opposed by the Bundesbank and denounced by a new party founded specifically to fight it. Governments tolerated it after the fact. If the euro's survival is the trophy, it belongs to Frankfurt—and the intergovernmental response is left holding only the resentment it created.
