[C1.1] The proposition fails because it treats a posting rule as if it controls the entire pay-setting process. It does not. Gender and racial pay gaps are cumulative products of occupational sorting, hours and seniority, promotion speed, performance and bonus allocation, and unequal bargaining leverage after hire. A posted range touches only one moment: the first number on an ad.
[C1.2] PRO mistakes visibility for correction. If that range is wide, employers can still place women and minority candidates lower inside it based on resume gaps, prior title, interview performance, or unobserved negotiating style. If the range is narrow, firms can shift jobs to lower titles, adjust location bands, or rely on signing bonuses and equity that remain negotiable. The mandate changes the label on the envelope, not all the decisions inside.
[C1.3] The efficiency loss is broader and more immediate. Hiring is not just picking a wage; it is discovering whether a particular person and role fit at a particular time. Mandated ranges push employers toward one-size-fits-all bands in markets where skills, shifts, commissions, and local demand change quickly. A small retailer hiring seasonal staff, a startup offering equity, or a hospital filling a niche shift cannot easily advertise a truthful fixed band without deterring useful applications.
[C1.4] When ranges are set low to preserve flexibility, qualified candidates self-select out. When they are set high, employers face flooded pipelines and slower screening. In both cases, matches are delayed or lost.
[C1.5] PRO’s best case is that applicants avoid obvious mismatches. But that benefit is modest compared with the distortion it creates: employers lose the ability to tailor offers to experience, availability, productivity, and competing opportunities. The result is fewer interviews for marginal candidates, more rigid wage ladders, and more screening by credentials rather than fit. A small, uncertain reduction in initial pay disparities is not worth a recurring tax on every hire.
