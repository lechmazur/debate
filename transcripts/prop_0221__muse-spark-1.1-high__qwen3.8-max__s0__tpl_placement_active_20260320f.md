[P1.1] Per dollar, the question is simple: do you want to buy a little more teacher presence for everyone, or a lot more teaching for each child who needs it.
[P1.2] Reducing class sizes in K-8 from 24 to 16 forces a district to hire roughly 50% more teachers and find 50% more classrooms. That's salary and facilities before a single extra minute of instruction happens. When states tried it at scale, like California, they had to hire large numbers of under-prepared teachers, diluting quality. Even the best case, Tennessee STAR, shows real but modest gains - roughly a tenth to two-tenths of a standard deviation - that require huge and permanent reductions to stick.
[P1.3] You are paying for adult headcount, not learning time.
[P1.4] High-dosage tutoring flips the math. Three-plus sessions a week, same tutor, two or three students, built into the school day. The mechanism is Bloom's old insight: individual diagnosis, immediate correction, and active response. In a 24-kid class a struggling 3rd grader might read aloud 90 seconds a day.
[P1.5] In a 2:1 tutorial she reads, miscues, and is corrected for 20 minutes. In math, she can't hide behind a hand raise; she has to produce the next step. That's why syntheses of tutoring consistently find gains two to three times larger than class-size cuts for both reading and math, especially for the kids furthest behind.
[P1.6] And it's radically cheaper per gain. You don't need a certified teacher for every small group. You need a trained tutor with a structured curriculum - paraprofessionals, college students, AmeriCorps members - working from high-quality materials. A year of daily tutoring costs roughly what a few weeks of an extra classroom teacher's salary costs when spread across those two or three students.
[P1.7] If we want larger reading and math gains per dollar in K-8, we should buy learning time, not just smaller crowds.

[C1.1] The motion assumes high-dosage tutoring keeps laboratory-level effects when bought for entire districts. It does not. The decisive question is not whether a skilled tutor with two children can outperform a 24-to-1 class in one lesson. It is whether a system can staff, supervise, schedule, and sustain three sessions a week for large numbers of K-8 students without eroding core instruction.
[C1.2] At scale, tutoring becomes a logistics problem, and its cost per durable gain rises sharply.
[C1.3] First, the true cost is not tutor wages alone. Every seat needs trained tutors, coordinators, curriculum, assessment, space, and coverage when students miss core class. If they miss instruction, districts pay twice: tutoring plus making up what they missed. High turnover among paraprofessional or college tutors is common, so districts spend continuously on recruitment and training.
[C1.4] A recurring program rebuilt each year is not automatically cheaper than a structural classroom improvement.
[C1.5] Second, quality is the whole game. Small-group instruction works when the tutor can diagnose errors instantly and keep a child engaged. Scale forces districts to fill slots with uneven adults, scripted materials, and children who are tired or absent. The result is often compliance, not correction.
[C1.6] Evidence from tutoring demonstrations tends to shrink when programs broaden eligibility and rely on less-experienced staff. One weak tutor can waste dozens of sessions per week.
[C1.7] Third, class-size reduction is not merely adult headcount. In K-8, smaller classes mean fewer interruptions, more writing conferences, more oral reading, more hands-on math, and better relationships over a full school year. Those benefits accrue every day, to every child, without pulling students from instruction. They change the ordinary classroom environment, not a temporary dosage.
[C1.8] Tutoring can help, but at scale its costs, quality loss, and fade-out are underestimated, while smaller classes create continuous learning conditions. The proposition is not proven.
