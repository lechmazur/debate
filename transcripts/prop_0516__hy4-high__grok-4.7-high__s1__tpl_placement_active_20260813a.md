[P1.1] Public universities do not merely admit students; they place new demand into a housing market. In severe shortage—near-zero vacancies, rents outrunning wages, families pushed out, shelters strained—another recruited cohort does not arrive into empty space. It competes for the same finite rooms. Leaving international recruitment unconstrained is not neutral: it lets a publicly supported institution expand enrollment and tuition while the host city absorbs the housing shock.
[P1.2] The governing duty is capacity. A public university should not promise an education it cannot house without shifting predictable harm onto the community that sustains it. Housing supply is slow; recruitment is fast. A university can add a large cohort in one admissions cycle, while a town cannot build comparable housing by September.
[P1.3] In that gap, demand raises rents, pushes family homes into shared lets, overcrowds units, and squeezes low-income renters least able to outbid newcomers. International students suffer too: weaker local networks can leave them in exploitative or unsafe accommodation.
[P1.4] A cap is proportionate because the trigger is severe local shortage, not nationality as such. It is a temporary planning brake: recruit only to verified housing capacity, protect current students, and restore growth when conditions improve. Public universities already cap numbers when labs, clinical placements, or safety limits bind. Housing is another binding constraint, more urgent in crisis because the cost falls on people’s homes.
[P1.5] International students bring academic and cultural value; the cap says only that recruiting cannot outrun the city’s ability to shelter the people invited.
[P1.6] The opposing defenses duck that timing problem. “The market will respond” ignores that apartments cannot be produced before the next intake. “We need revenue” does not buy instant housing. “Any cap is unfair” reverses fairness: it asks local renters, poorer students, and working families to bear displacement so a university can keep recruiting. Unconstrained growth is not hospitality; it is cost-shifting. When scarcity becomes severe, a cap is basic public stewardship.

[C1.1] Public universities should not ration international enrollment to paper over a housing shortage they did not create and cannot cure by turning students away. The proposal treats a supply failure as an admissions problem and singles out the group easiest to exclude.
[C1.2] Severe shortages appear when cities underbuild. Restrictive zoning, slow permitting, and resistance to density keep vacancies near zero whether or not the university recruits abroad. Domestic students, staff, and ordinary in-migration compete for the same rooms. A cap aimed only at international recruitment leaves those sources of demand untouched.
[C1.3] It changes the passport mix of tenants, not the number of people who need shelter, and it produces no new unit before the next intake.
[C1.4] International students are the wrong fiscal lever. They commonly pay full tuition that subsidizes resident instruction and capital projects, including residence halls. Cutting their numbers in a crisis reduces the revenue public universities use to expand beds, while the city still refuses to let builders catch up. Revenue is not a dodge; it is how institutions finance housing the motion would wait for someone else to provide.
[C1.5] The cap is not nationality-neutral in effect. Labs and clinical placements limit everyone because the constraint is internal and academic. Housing is external and civic. Selecting international students—less enfranchised, often more willing to accept shared or purpose-built accommodation—shifts blame onto outsiders for a shortage produced by local land-use choices.
[C1.6] A family squeezed by rents is not helped if a domestic transfer or a short-term let still takes the unit the excluded student would have occupied.
[C1.7] Stewardship means matching growth to real academic capacity, partnering on student housing, and pressing cities to build and protect low-income renters. It does not mean freezing a globally recruited cohort so unconstrained domestic demand and failed permitting can continue undisturbed. A nationality cap is misdirection, not public duty.
