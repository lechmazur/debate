[P1.3] The central mistake is treating private digital spaces as separate territory. Exclusion through group chats does not stay in the chat. It becomes the seating chart, the group project, the lunch table, the whispered “you had to be there.” It decides who belongs to the school community and who stands outside it. Calling this “purely off-campus” ignores the place where the harm is daily enforced.
[P1.4] Bullying includes relational aggression, not just public name-calling. Digital exclusion is power over belonging: gatekeeping invitations, information, and support. For an adult, an exclusive chat may be annoying; for a child in compulsory school, there is no exit. The excluded student must return every day to the same peers and the same invisible boundary.
[P1.5] Schools already address off-campus conduct when it creates a hostile environment or disrupts learning. Digital exclusion does both. Excluded students lose access to class coordination, study groups, and team planning. They may withdraw, stop participating, or skip school.
[P1.6] The relevant question is not where a chat is hosted, but whether the exclusion reaches the school. That is an educational harm, not a private matter.
[P1.7] This does not require monitoring every group chat. It requires that when exclusion is reported or visible in school, schools investigate, support the target, and treat the dynamic as a serious anti-bullying issue rather than dismiss it as off-campus drama. If schools ignore private group chats, they ignore the primary machinery of modern peer exclusion and abandon students to harm that follows them into the classroom.
