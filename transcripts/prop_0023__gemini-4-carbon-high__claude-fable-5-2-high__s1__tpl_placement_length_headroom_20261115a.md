[P1.1] Financial regulation must track economic reality, not technological labels. Across the cryptocurrency industry, most trading platforms operate as centralized intermediaries listing hundreds of tokens marketed to fund ventures with the expectation of profit from a founding team's efforts. In substance, those assets are securities, and the venues matching buyers, routing orders, and holding customer balances perform the exact functions of securities exchanges and broker-dealers. Carving out a distinct, lightly supervised category merely rewards regulatory arbitrage, letting firms evade protections built over decades of market crises.
[P1.2] The decisive failure of light supervision is the bundling of incompatible financial roles. Securities law separates exchanges, broker-dealers, clearinghouses, and custodians, or enforces strict structural walls, net-capital buffers, and segregated custody, because when one company runs the marketplace, holds customer cash, and trades for its own account, investors get fleeced. Major crypto platforms routinely combine order matching, retail brokerage, custody, and affiliated market-making under one roof. Collapses like FTX were not mysterious blockchain glitches; they were classic disasters of commingled customer assets and conflicted proprietary trading that exchange and broker-dealer rules are specifically built to prevent.
[P1.3] A bespoke, lighter regime leaves core market pathologies intact. Without securities-grade trade surveillance, best-execution duties, and mandatory disclosures, retail traders face rampant wash trading, insider front-running, and opaque token promotions. Crucially, regulating most platforms this way does not require forcing autonomous open-source code or a pure commodity coin into an ill-fitting box; the vast majority of crypto platforms are centralized, for-profit marketplaces listing dozens or hundreds of speculative enterprise tokens. Genuine financial innovation competes by making settlement faster and cheaper, not by winning legal permission to commingle client funds or bet against uninformed customers.
[P1.4] Same economic risk demands the same regulatory standard: most crypto platforms belong under securities exchange and broker-dealer law.

The bulk of crypto trading is in Bitcoin, Ether, and dollar stablecoins. These assets have no issuer to file disclosures, no board, no earnings, and no founding team whose efforts holders rely on.
[C1.2] A platform should be regulated according to what it mostly trades, and by that measure most platforms are not securities markets.
[C1.3] Securities exchange law is also the wrong tool for the job. It is built on issuer registration: a company files audited statements, and exchanges list only what is registered. Apply that to Bitcoin and there is no one to register it. Mandatory disclosure would tell a buyer nothing about a decentralized network's real risks, which are code, token supply, and concentration of control.
[C1.4] The framework also assumes retail customers reach exchanges through brokers, with settlement run through separate clearing agencies and transfer agents. That layering exists because legacy settlement takes time. A blockchain settles the trade itself, so forcing the layers back in adds cost without adding protection.
[C1.5] FTX does not carry PRO's argument. It was an offshore firm that stole customer money, which is fraud under any regime. The fix is specific: segregated custody, proof of reserves, capital requirements, and limits on affiliated trading. A distinct crypto regime can impose every one of those directly, and Europe's bespoke framework already does something very like it.
[C1.6] PRO needs to show that these protections require the entire securities apparatus. They do not.
[C1.7] The practical result of PRO's rule is that major tokens cannot be listed lawfully on compliant domestic venues. Users will not stop trading. They will move to offshore platforms beyond any regulator's reach, which is where FTX operated.
[C1.8] So the real choice is between a tailored regime that platforms can actually comply with and an ill-fitting one that drives them offshore. Fitted rules protect investors, and forced rules export them.
