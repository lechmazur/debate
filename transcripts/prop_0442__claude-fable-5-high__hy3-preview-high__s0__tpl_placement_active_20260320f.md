[P1.1] Every year, international shipping pumps out roughly three percent of global greenhouse emissions—more than Germany—and that share is growing while almost every other sector decarbonizes. Yet shipping sits in a regulatory blind spot: it happens on the high seas, beyond any single nation's jurisdiction, deliberately excluded from national carbon accounting. There is exactly one body capable of governing it: the IMO. If the IMO does not set a binding timetable for low- and zero-carbon fuels, no one will, and the sector free-rides indefinitely.
[P1.2] The case rests on three pillars. First, only a binding mandate breaks the chicken-and-egg deadlock. Fuel producers won't build green ammonia, methanol, or hydrogen plants without guaranteed demand; shipowners won't order dual-fuel vessels without guaranteed supply. Voluntary pledges cannot break this loop—a binding timetable can, by making investment in the old technology the risky bet.
[P1.3] We have a proof of concept: the IMO's 2020 sulfur cap was attacked as impossible and ruinous, yet compliance arrived on schedule, fuel markets adapted, and trade did not collapse.
[P1.4] Second, the clock is merciless. Ships last twenty-five to thirty years. A conventional vessel ordered today will still be burning heavy fuel oil in the 2050s. Every year of delay locks in decades of emissions.
[P1.5] Mandates work precisely because they shape today's order books.
[P1.6] Third, the cost objection is far weaker than it sounds. Ocean freight is a sliver of retail prices—a few cents on a pair of sneakers, perhaps a percent or two on most consumer goods. A modest, predictable rise in freight rates, applied equally to every carrier so no one gains by polluting, is a trivial price for decarbonizing an entire global sector.
[P1.7] The real question is not whether freight gets marginally more expensive. It is whether the one industry no country can regulate alone should be allowed to opt out of climate physics. The answer must be no.
