[P1.1] When nearly every visitor is funneled into the same square mile in the same eight weeks, a city does not get more prosperity. It gets a bottleneck: crowded transit, inflated rents, souvenir shops replacing grocers, and residents who start to see tourism as extraction rather than exchange. Venice, Barcelona's old town, and Kyoto's most famous lanes all show the same pattern — the core becomes harder to live in, harder to visit, and easier to resent.
[P1.2] Spreading demand breaks that bottleneck in two ways. Geographically, it moves spending to neighborhoods that already have cafes, workshops, parks, and local businesses but lack foot traffic. A food market, walking route, museum pass, or festival placed one district over can keep visitor money in the city while giving a second neighborhood a reason to invest and hire. Temporally, promoting shoulder seasons — spring exhibitions, autumn food weeks, winter lights — smooths the brutal peak-trough cycle that forces businesses to over-hire in August and lay off in January, while leaving hotels and venues idle most of the year.
[P1.3] This is also how a city protects what tourists came to see. A historic center preserved as a living neighborhood, with residents, schools, and ordinary shops, is more attractive than a stage set emptied by short-term rentals and queues. Dispersal does not dilute the icon; it relieves it. The famous cathedral, canal, or old street can be enjoyed with timed entry and calmer streets, while visitors discover that the city is larger than its postcard.
[P1.4] The alternative — keep concentrating — is not neutral. It subsidizes landlords and chains best positioned in the core, pushes housing costs onto residents, strains water, waste, and transit built for locals, and makes the whole economy vulnerable to a single protest, heatwave, or downturn in peak season. Spreading demand is not anti-tourism. It is how popular cities keep tourism economically broad, socially tolerated, and worth taking for decades.

[P2.1] Containing tourism sounds tidy until you look at the contained district. It is not sealed: rents, short-term rentals, and day crowds spill into adjacent streets precisely because everyone is told to stay in one place, while inside that place residents and ordinary shops are already priced out. Concentration does not prevent intrusion; it intensifies it to the point of displacement, then leaks anyway without planning, rules, or benefits for the next neighborhood.
[P2.2] That answers the claim that dispersal merely adds trips [C1.2]. Addition happens when a city promotes a new market while leaving core access unlimited. Substitution happens when it pairs timed entry, hotel licensing, and tour routing in the core with passes, events, and overnight stays elsewhere. Visitors have finite days and budgets; an evening in a promoted district, or a hotel outside the old town, is not automatically on top of the same crowded itinerary — it replaces part of it.
[P2.3] The duplication argument reverses the real cost. Neighborhoods are not empty stages needing new policing, transit, and cleaning from scratch; they already have streets, buses, cafes, and shops used below capacity. What is expensive is squeezing another peak-season surge through the same stations, waste routes, and water systems, and building core capacity that sits unused in winter. Using slack that already exists is efficiency, not waste.
[P2.4] Seasonal spreading likewise does not require abolishing school holidays or weather. It targets travelers who are flexible — retirees, remote workers, conferences, food and culture visitors — and moves movable events to shoulder months. Even if August stays busy, fuller March and October mean steadier jobs and revenue, not necessarily another peak; and a city can still protect a quieter deep off-season for repairs and rest.
[P2.5] Finally, caps and pricing in [C1.5] need dispersal to work. Without somewhere else to go, they make the core more exclusive and push demand informally outward; with alternatives, limits relieve the center while the city keeps the spending.
