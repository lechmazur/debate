[P1.1] The motion asks one thing: when the costs a city's residents bear clearly outweigh the money tourists bring, should the city be allowed to limit arrivals? Put that way, opposing it means arguing that a city must keep accepting a deal it is losing. That is the burden CON now carries.
[P1.2] Three reasons the answer is yes.
[P1.3] First, cruise ships and large tour buses are the lowest-value, highest-impact form of tourism. Passengers sleep and often eat on board, then are released in waves of thousands into a few narrow streets for a few hours. They buy a gelato and a magnet. Overnight visitors fill hotels, restaurants, and museums, spreading their spending across days.
[P1.4] The day-trip surge brings the crowding of a festival with the spending of a lunch break. When revenue is thin, it takes far less harm for costs to win.
[P1.5] Second, the costs land on residents and nobody prices them. Ships idling at berth burn heavy fuel beside homes. Buses clog roads built for carts. Most damaging, the city reorganizes around the surge: grocers become souvenir stalls, apartments become short-term rentals, and families move out.
[P1.6] Venice's historic center has lost a large share of its population over recent decades. A historic city emptied of residents becomes a stage set, and stage sets eventually lose even their tourist appeal.
[P1.7] Third, caps work and they are precise. Venice pushed the biggest ships out of the Giudecca Canal. Dubrovnik limited how many cruise ships may dock each day. Amsterdam has moved to reduce cruise calls in its center.
[P1.8] None of these cities banned tourism. They metered the most concentrated, least lucrative flows and made room for visitors who stay and spend.
[P1.9] CON must show why a city should keep subsidizing a losing bargain with its air, streets, and homes. Saying no to that is simply sound governance.

They do not allocate scarce visitor slots to people who value the city most; they hand them to the largest, highest-bidding operators, pricing out working-class families, student groups, and budget travelers who can only afford short day visits, while doing nothing to target the idling private vehicles, luxury yachts, and unlicensed short-term rentals that actually impose the largest unpriced costs on everyday residents.
