[P1.1] Obesity is a chronic disease—recognized as such by the AMA for over a decade—that drives diabetes, heart failure, stroke, kidney disease, and more than a dozen cancers. Yet insurers routinely carve out the one class of medicine that actually treats it. No insurer would dare exclude statins for high cholesterol or insulin for diabetes. The exclusion of anti-obesity medications is not medicine; it is a legacy prejudice that treats obesity as a character flaw rather than a physiology problem.
[P1.2] The motion simply ends that anomaly.
[P1.3] Three pillars carry this case. First, clinical effectiveness. Modern GLP-1 medications produce sustained weight loss of roughly fifteen to twenty percent—results previously achievable only through surgery. Large cardiovascular outcomes trials have shown meaningful reductions in heart attacks and strokes even in non-diabetic patients.
[P1.4] When a drug is FDA-authorized, clinically indicated, and prevents the most expensive events in American healthcare, denying coverage isn't prudence—it's rationing effective care from the sickest patients.
[P1.5] Second, equity and coherence. Today, access tracks employer generosity and wealth, not need. A wealthy patient pays a thousand dollars a month out of pocket; a working-class patient with severe obesity gets a pamphlet about diet. Meanwhile insurers happily pay for the amputations, dialysis, and bariatric surgeries that untreated obesity produces.
[P1.6] Covering the disease's consequences but not its treatment is fiscally and morally incoherent.
[P1.7] Third—and this is where the motion is smarter than the caricature—it pairs the mandate with value-based pricing and outcomes-based contracts. If a patient doesn't respond, or a drug underdelivers, manufacturers absorb the cost, not premiums. That mechanism directly disciplines the sticker prices everyone worries about, aligns payment with real-world results, and gives insurers leverage they currently lack.
[P1.8] So the question for this debate is narrow: should a proven treatment for a disease afflicting over forty percent of American adults be covered, with built-in cost guardrails? Denying that requires defending discrimination the rest of medicine abandoned long ago.
