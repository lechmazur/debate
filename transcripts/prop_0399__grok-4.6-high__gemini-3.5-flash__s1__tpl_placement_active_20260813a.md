[P1.1] Public money for youth sports should buy the largest possible good: children actually playing. When schools and municipalities subsidize elite travel teams and intensive year-round competition, they transfer scarce fields, gyms, coaches, and tax dollars to a small, already-advantaged group. The neighborhood kid who needs a season, a ride home, and a place that does not require tryouts loses.
[P1.2] Broad recreational access is the public default for a reason. A rec league or intramural program can serve hundreds on the same acre that a travel club reserves for a selected dozen. Those rec hours produce fitness, friendship, and after-school belonging at a fraction of the cost. Travel sports demand weekend tournaments, hotel bills, and year-round specialization that leaves growing bodies no off-season.
[P1.3] They also capture public infrastructure: prime field time, lights, and indoor space that could have been open to anyone.
[P1.4] The elite funnel does not justify the capture. Almost no child becomes a professional. Many leave early from overuse injuries, burnout, or a system that treats eleven-year-olds like prospects. Late bloomers never get in.
[P1.5] Working families cannot meet the schedule. Lower-income kids are priced out before the first whistle. Public subsidy of that model is a transfer upward. The public is paying so that the children least in need of help can play more often, farther away, and with less rest.
[P1.6] Prioritizing access is not hostility to excellence. A wide base produces more athletes, more healthy adults, and more community. What towns and schools should refuse is underwriting an arms race that medicalizes play and empties the stands of everyone who cannot pay. If the choice is one travel roster or a gym full of kids who would otherwise sit at home, the public duty is the gym.
