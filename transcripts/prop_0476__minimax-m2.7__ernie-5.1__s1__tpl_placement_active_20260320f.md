[C1.1] The proposition sounds clean, but it solves a problem that already has workable solutions—and creates new ones that are far worse. Deorbit bonds are a blunt instrument that misprices risk, chokes market entry, and ultimately fails to guarantee safety.
[C1.2] First, bonds punish before any harm occurs, yet they do not guarantee that the money will be available when needed. A bond is a static sum set today for a mission that may last fifteen years. Technology changes, orbital environments shift, and disposal costs can rise dramatically. If the bond is too low, operators walk away with profit while the public bears the clean‑up cost.
[C1.3] If set high enough to be meaningful, it prices out small and emerging operators, concentrating the market in a few incumbents who can absorb the upfront hit. That reduces competition, slows innovation, and paradoxically increases debris risk because fewer players mean less redundancy and less pressure to design for end‑of‑life.
[C1.4] Second, the bond mechanism creates perverse incentives. Operators will focus on meeting the bond’s checklist rather than ensuring safe retirement. They may select the cheapest disposal trajectory that technically satisfies the bond while leaving the object in a graveyard orbit that still threatens collisions. The bond rewards paperwork, not performance.
[C1.5] Third, we already have a liability framework that works better. Under the Outer Space Treaty and national space laws, operators are liable for damage caused by their objects. Coupled with mandatory launch insurance, this creates a financial incentive to avoid debris without the need for a pre‑launch escrow that governments must manage.
[C1.6] Finally, enforcement is a nightmare. Governments would have to monitor thousands of objects for decades, adjudicate forfeiture claims, and litigate across jurisdictions. That administrative burden dwarfs any benefit the bond might deliver.
[C1.7] In short, bonds are a costly, inefficient, and poorly targeted fix. We should strengthen liability and insurance, not impose a blunt financial levy that harms the very industry we need to keep space sustainable.
