[P1.1] Sanctuary cities don't declare themselves above immigration law; they declare local police are not immigration agents. The policy is simple: cops don't ask status on a traffic stop, jails don't hold people past their release date on a civil ICE request without a judge's warrant, but they still arrest, prosecute, and share fingerprints for any crime.
[P1.2] That distinction is what makes community policing possible. Public safety depends on people picking up the phone. If a mother believes calling 911 about domestic violence will get her deported, she doesn't call. If a day laborer robbed at knifepoint thinks the responding officer will check papers, he disappears.
[P1.3] If a teenager sees a shooting and fears his undocumented parents will be questioned if he testifies, he stays silent. When police are seen as a pipeline to ICE, predators learn which neighborhoods won't report.
[P1.4] Restore the wall between local policing and civil immigration enforcement and that calculation flips. Victims report, witnesses cooperate, detectives can actually close cases. That benefit accrues to everyone — citizens included — because violent offenders don't stay inside an immigrant community; they exploit the silence.
[P1.5] What does this cost immigration enforcement? Very little. ICE retains full federal authority to investigate, obtain judicial warrants, and arrest anywhere. What sanctuary denies is a taxpayer-funded subsidy: local jails acting as indefinite holds on warrantless civil detainers, and patrol officers diverting time from violent crime to do federal paperwork.
[P1.6] Detainers are civil, often error-prone, and courts have repeatedly found extended holds violate the Fourth Amendment.
[P1.7] So the trade is unbalanced. On one side, tangible, daily public safety gains from trust that lets police do their core job. On the other, a marginal, inefficient inconvenience to a federal agency that never depended on locals to begin with.
