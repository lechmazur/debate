[P1.1] When a merger creates a dominant buyer of labor, the resulting wage suppression can be as damaging to overall welfare as any price increase faced by consumers. Competition law has long prioritized consumer‑price effects because they are easy to observe, but that convenience has blinded regulators to a parallel harm that is equally measurable and far more pervasive. A monopsonistic employer can cut wages by 5‑10 % without losing workers, because alternatives are scarce. Multiply that across millions of employees and the aggregate loss in income dwarfs the modest price hikes that trigger most antitrust actions.
[P1.2] Consider the wave of hospital consolidations in the United States. In many regions a single health system became the sole major employer of nurses and technicians. Studies have shown that wages in those markets fell sharply after consolidation, even though patient prices rose only modestly. The workers who bore the brunt were low‑skill, often women and minorities—precisely the groups consumer‑price analysis tends to overlook.
[P1.3] The economic logic is identical to the consumer side. A merger that raises the marginal cost of labor is a distortion of the same market that a price‑raising merger creates for goods. Ignoring labor effects means we permit firms to capture surplus from workers while we police surplus extraction from shoppers. That double standard cannot be justified by measurement difficulty; modern econometric tools can estimate wage elasticity and counterfactual wages with reasonable precision.
[P1.4] Treating labor‑market monopsony with the same rigor forces firms to internalize the full cost of concentration. It deters deals that would depress wages, preserves bargaining power, and ultimately sustains aggregate demand—because workers who earn more spend more. In short, a merger review that looks only at consumer prices leaves half the story untold, and the policy cost of that omission is a systematic transfer of wealth from labor to capital. Competition policy must close that gap.

[C1.2] The most glaring flaw in the proposition is that it forces an artificial, zero-sum conflict between consumers and workers. Mergers frequently generate efficiencies—such as consolidating redundant corporate headquarters or streamlining supply chains—that lower the cost of production. In a competitive market, these savings are passed on to consumers as lower prices. If regulators must treat potential labor-market concentration with the same severity as consumer price hikes, they will be forced to block mergers that would lower prices for millions of shoppers simply to shield a specific pool of workers from corporate reorganization.
[C1.3] Under this dual mandate, the consumer always loses.
[C1.4] Furthermore, labor markets are notoriously difficult to define and analyze compared to product markets. Attempting to measure "labor elasticities" with the same precision as consumer demand is an econometric mirage that will lead to regulatory paralysis, endless litigation, and arbitrary enforcement.
[C1.5] Protecting workers is a vital societal goal, but antitrust is the wrong tool. Labor laws, collective bargaining, and minimum wage regulations are specifically designed to address wage disparities. Weaponizing merger review to solve labor market frictions will not save jobs; it will simply stifle the corporate restructuring necessary for economic growth, leaving consumers to pay the bill.
