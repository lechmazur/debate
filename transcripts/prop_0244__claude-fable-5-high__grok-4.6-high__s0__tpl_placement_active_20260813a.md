[P1.1] When body cameras swept American policing after Ferguson in 2014, advocates made specific, ambitious promises: cameras would deter misconduct, slash excessive force, vindicate complaints, and finally hold officers accountable in courtrooms and disciplinary hearings. President Obama's administration funded tens of millions in deployments on exactly that theory. The question is not whether cameras did anything—it's whether they delivered what advocates expected. They plainly did not, and that gap is the whole motion.
[P1.2] Start with the strongest evidence: the largest randomized trial ever conducted, in Washington, D.C., with over two thousand officers, found essentially no measurable effect on use of force or civilian complaints. The early Rialto study that launched the movement showed dramatic drops, but it was tiny, and subsequent, larger studies repeatedly failed to replicate it. The empirical foundation of the advocates' expectations collapsed under scale.
[P1.3] Second, accountability requires more than a lens—it requires who controls the footage. In practice, departments decided when cameras rolled, when footage was released, and who saw it. Officers muted or deactivated cameras at critical moments, often with trivial consequences. In Chicago's Laquan McDonald case, video was withheld for over a year.
[P1.4] Footage routinely surfaced quickly when it exonerated officers and slowly, if ever, when it didn't. A tool advocates imagined as a civilian shield became, in many jurisdictions, a prosecutorial asset used mainly as evidence against civilians.
[P1.5] Third, the downstream accountability metrics barely moved. Officer discipline rates, sustained complaints, and criminal convictions of police remained rare. The most consequential police video of the era—George Floyd's murder—came from a teenager's phone, not a department camera. That fact alone captures the shortfall.
[P1.6] Note what I don't have to prove: that cameras are worthless. Only that reality fell short of the transformative expectations advocates sold. The distance between the 2014 promise and the 2024 record is vast, documented, and largely conceded even by reform advocates themselves.
