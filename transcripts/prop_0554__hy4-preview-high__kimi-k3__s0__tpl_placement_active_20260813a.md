[P1.1] Local opposition is real, but the question is whether local objection should stop infrastructure the whole country needs. Onshore wind and grid are among the cheapest, fastest tools the EU and UK have to cut bills, gas imports, and emissions. Strong local resistance should improve a project, not veto it.
[P1.2] That is why relaxing restrictions means a national presumption for onshore wind and priority grid corridors, not rule by distant technocrats. Local voices should shape siting, mitigation, and community benefit; they should not hold a veto. Delay is brutal. After the gas shock, Europe saw that imported fuel is a security and cost-of-living emergency.
[P1.3] Onshore wind can be deployed far faster than nuclear, is among the lowest-cost sources of new power, and avoids the imported fuel that made the gas shock so painful. Yet tight local-consent rules in England throttled onshore wind for years, wasting some of Europe's best wind.
[P1.4] Grid expansion is the missing half. Cheap wind is useless if the wires cannot carry it. When remote turbines are constrained off while households pay for gas backup, consumers pay twice: once for power they do not get, once for fuel they should not need. That cost lands on bills and credibility.
[P1.5] Decarbonisation looks expensive when the bottleneck is the grid, not the technology.
[P1.6] CON will dress a veto as "local democracy". But many objections come from small, unrepresentative meetings, while those who most need cheaper power—renters, younger households, energy-intensive industry—are rarely in the room. We do not let a parish council veto a motorway, a hospital, or flood defences because neighbours object. Energy security deserves the same national-interest test.
[P1.7] So relax the restrictions: consult early, compensate communities, protect genuinely sensitive sites, then decide on overriding public interest. If opposition remains strong after that, the project should still proceed. A narrow local majority should not hold a continent's security, competitiveness, and climate commitments hostage.

[C1.2] First, coercion is slow. Strong opposition does not vanish when a minister signs an order; it relocates to the courts, the public inquiry, the protest camp, and the ballot box. Britain tried this with fracking: declared a national priority, planning tilted its way — and it collapsed within years because communities refused it. Scotland's Beauly–Denny power line spent years in inquiry fighting objections one by one.
[C1.3] The fastest megawatt is the one with consent; the imposed one arrives late, litigated, and reversible.
[C1.4] Second, the backlash is systemic, not local. Onshore wind and grid are a thirty-year construction programme that must return to the same rural regions again and again. Override one community and you poison the well for every project after it — and hand every anti-green populist party a ready-made grievance. Dutch farmers' protests and German turbine-scepticism did not come from nowhere; they grew where governments treated rural consent as an administrative inconvenience.
[C1.5] Third, coercion is unnecessary. Repower existing, already-consented wind sites with taller turbines. Build offshore, where Britain leads the world. Put solar on warehouses before farmland.
[C1.6] Reconductor existing grid corridors to carry more power on the same towers. The project that requires steamrolling strong opposition always has a substitute; the trust it destroys does not.
[C1.7] PRO frames this as parish councils vetoing hospitals. But a hospital must stand where its patients are; a turbine can go offshore, a cable can go around. Where alternatives exist, riding over strong opposition is not national interest — it is national impatience. Consent is not the enemy of the transition; it is the only transition that lasts.
