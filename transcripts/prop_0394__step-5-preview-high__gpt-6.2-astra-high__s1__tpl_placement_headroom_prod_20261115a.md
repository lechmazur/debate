[P1.1] Access is not an add-on to education; it is education. A seminar where one student cannot reliably hear a classmate's question, a chemistry lecture whose equations the board camera misses, and a recording with no transcript do not merely disadvantage disabled students—they exclude them from the same course everyone else is taking. The proposition says provision should be default because learning is the institution's core function. A student should not have to prove, semester after semester, that she deserves access to the material the university already requires her to master.
[P1.2] Presumptive provision does not mean every accommodation in every context; it means the burden of justification sits where it belongs. A strong pedagogical reason—such as an assessed oral-language exercise whose transcript would defeat the learning objective—can justify withholding captions for that exercise. But administrative convenience, unfamiliarity, or a preference for live-only teaching is not a pedagogical reason. A lecturer's improvisation is not a pedagogical reason either; unplanned teaching can still be captioned afterward.
[P1.3] These exceptions are narrow and rare; inaccessible defaults are not.
[P1.4] The default also benefits students no one had in mind. Captions help learners with hearing loss, auditory processing differences, unstable internet, noisy dorm rooms, or English as an additional language. Recordings allow students recovering from illness or managing chronic pain to participate without dropping out. Accessible slides help students with low vision, dyslexia, or limited course familiarity.
[P1.5] Universal design improves learning rather than diluting standards.
[P1.6] Finally, an individualized-access regime is expensive and unreliable. It forces students to disclose disabilities, negotiate each time, and wait while deadlines pass. A presumptive standard prevents avoidable exclusion, reduces repeated administrative friction, and lets faculty focus on genuine teaching constraints. Default to access; justify only real pedagogical departures.

[C1.1] Access should be a standing obligation. Creating a replayable record of a classroom should not. A caption can disappear when a sentence ends; a recording can leave the room. Putting both under one presumption treats two different decisions as though the same reason settled them.
[C1.2] Consider a nursing lecture in which a patient describes living with a colostomy. She is happy to answer questions, happy for her words to be captioned live, but unwilling to have her story recorded. Nobody needs to show that replay would impair learning; it might actually help. Her refusal is sufficient.
[C1.3] Consent is not a pedagogical exception; it is an independent condition. A password controls who gets the first copy, not who gets the last. The school owes absent students an accessible account of the clinical lesson, not a permanent record of her testimony.
[C1.4] Even in ordinary lectures, the scarce resource is often not storage but skilled attention. In the chemistry example, a recording of an unreadable equation with a garbled caption gives a student two bad versions of the same explanation. An hour spent repairing yesterday’s archive is an hour the same specialist cannot spend making tomorrow’s equations readable before class. That is a choice between students’ access needs, not a matter of administrative convenience.
[C1.5] None of this requires sending students back to negotiate with each lecturer. Require courses to publish access arrangements before enrollment, supply accessible core materials without a request, and let students flag unmet needs confidentially to one office responsible for resolving them promptly. Record conventional lectures where replay is a reliable way to reach absent students. Put the burden on the school to show how students can learn the material, not why it declined to produce a particular file.
