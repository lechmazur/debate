[P1.1] A blanket minimum wage treats a Manhattan pharmacy and a rural gas station as if they can absorb the same mandated cost at the same speed. They cannot. Employment losses come not from raising pay but from raising it uniformly where capacity to pay is wildly uneven. Wage boards and sectoral bargaining fix exactly this defect.
[P1.2] Three mechanisms do the work.
[P1.3] First, differentiation. A wage board sets rates by industry, often by region or firm size. Fast food in high-cost metros gets a higher floor; a thin-margin sector in a depressed county gets a slower phase-in. A single statutory number cannot do that.
[P1.4] It sets one price of labor for a labor market that has many.
[P1.5] Second, and decisive: sectoral standards bind every firm in the industry at once. The classic disemployment channel runs through competition — the firm that raises pay while its rival doesn't loses business and sheds workers. Remove undercutting, and employers compete on service and productivity, not on who pays the least. US industry-committee wage orders and wage-board systems abroad were built on precisely this logic.
[P1.6] Third, boards can write in phase-ins, carve-outs, and productivity-linked adjustments, so the effective cost shock tracks what firms can actually absorb. An across-the-board number has no such dial.
[P1.7] The claim is comparative, not utopian: fewer losses than large across-the-board hikes for the same or greater pay lift. And losses under big uniform hikes cluster in low-cost, low-margin labor markets — exactly where boards would set gentler schedules. Boards can also reach tipped, piece-rate, rural, and misclassified workers that a statutory floor misses.
[P1.8] To win, CON must show one-size-fits-all is the low-cost tool for raising low-end pay. It is the opposite. Uniformity causes the damage; discretion and coverage deliver the raises.

[C1.2] Instead of preventing undercutting, sectoral boards create cross-sector undercutting, shrinking employment in the targeted industry and flooding uncovered sectors with displaced job-seekers, which depresses pay elsewhere.
[C1.3] Furthermore, PRO compares an idealized wage board against a caricature of statutory minimum wages [P1.1]. US statutory floors are not trapped in one-size-fits-all uniformity [P1.8]; legislatures routinely build in regional tiers, small-business phase-ins, and predictable indexing. [C1.5] Finally, sectoral systems face an inescapable Catch-22. Low-wage US workers churn constantly between retail, food service, warehousing, and care work. Where boards set timid rates for "thin-margin" sectors [P1.3], or stall in bureaucratic gridlock, they leave the poorest workers behind. Where they impose aggressive sector-specific spikes—as in recent US fast-food and hospitality mandates—firms respond immediately with layoffs, cut hours, eliminated delivery roles, and kiosk automation.
[C1.6] A broad statutory floor lifts the entire low-wage baseline cleanly; sectoral boards balkanize the labor market, delivering either narrow raises or concentrated job losses.
