[P1.1] Modern policymaking repeatedly fails not from a lack of data, but from structural flaws in how governments aggregate insight. Relying primarily on internal bureaucracies and appointed expert panels institutionalizes groupthink, political deference, and career-risk aversion. Experts face broken incentives: echoing conventional wisdom protects status, whereas offering an accurate but inconvenient dissent invites professional marginalization. Furthermore, committees produce consensus by trading in vague qualitative language—like "plausible risk" or "moderate likelihood"—which obscures uncertainty and lets decision-makers evade accountability.
[P1.2] Subsidized prediction markets resolve this epistemic crisis by creating a transparent, incentive-aligned mechanism for truth discovery. By funding market liquidity, the state overcomes thin trading volumes and engages dispersed, ground-level knowledge across society.
[P1.3] First, markets enforce skin in the game. Unlike panel members who suffer no tangible penalty for mistaken advice, market participants risk capital on their convictions. Overconfident bloviating is penalized; contrarian accuracy is rewarded. This filters out political posturing and privileges verifiable forecasting competence over institutional credentials.
[P1.4] Second, prediction markets bypass bureaucratic censorship. An agency analyst often cannot safely tell a minister that a signature infrastructure project will double its budget or that a procurement timeline is unrealistic. Markets, however, aggregate anonymous, distributed intelligence into a single price. They reveal uncomfortable truths immediately and adjust continuously in real time, rather than delivering stale, politicized committee reports months too late.
[P1.5] Crucially, using prediction markets as a formal input does not replace democratic leadership with an algorithm; it provides an unvarnished probabilistic baseline. When leaders choose to act against market odds, they are forced to explicitly defend why their assumptions should override the collective intelligence of incentivized forecasters.
[P1.6] Closed panels protect bureaucratic comfort; subsidized prediction markets protect policy outcomes by making accuracy the only currency that matters.

[C1.1] The failure of policy is rarely that no one knew the risk. It is that the government lacked a causal model, a legal path, or the capacity to act. Replacing panels with betting pools misidentifies what policy advice is for.
[C1.2] Prediction markets work when an event is crisp, exogenous, and soon verifiable - who wins an election, whether it rains tomorrow. Major policy decisions are the opposite: long-horizon, endogenous, and counterfactual. What is the settlement contract for "this industrial policy succeeds" or "this war was a mistake"? You must either write a vague proxy that traders then game, or pick a narrow metric that collapses the policy's real goals into GDP blips.
[C1.3] The market will not reveal uncomfortable truth; it will reveal whatever is cheapest to make true.
[C1.4] Make that market subsidized and formally binding and you create a direct attack surface. [P1.3] praises skin in the game, but when price moves policy, the return to manipulating price dwarfs the trading loss. A defense contractor can short the success of a procurement reform, a foreign lobby can pump the odds of sanctions failing. Government would be paying speculators to corrupt its own signal.
[C1.5] Finally, numbers without reasons are useless for governing. A minister does not just need a 62% chance that a bridge overruns; she needs why - soil geology, contracting law, labor supply - and what lever changes it. Expert panels and internal forecasts supply models, tradeoffs, distributional impacts. Markets supply a ticker with no memorandum.
[C1.6] Weighting dollars over deliberation replaces accountable, fireable expertise with anonymous capital. Subsidizing that with public money is regressive and corrosive. It tells career analysts whose memos are audited that their judgment matters less than a hedge fund's position.
[C1.7] That is not truth discovery. It is outsourcing judgment to those rich enough to bet.
