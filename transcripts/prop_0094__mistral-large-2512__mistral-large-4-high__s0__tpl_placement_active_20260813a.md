[C1.1] The proposition solves a regulatory problem by freezing supply, which is the wrong lever. When curb, labor, or safety rules are unclear, cities should write outcome-based rules—priced curb zones, pickup points, incident reporting, certification, wage floors, retraining funds—not cap how many vehicles may operate. A numerical limit does not make a robotaxi safer or a curb more orderly; it only reduces the number of vehicles subject to the rules that exist.
[C1.2] Geofences and caps protect incumbents more than the public. Legacy taxi medallions show what permit-count rationing produces: shortages, inflated fares, and fights over scarcity rents. A robotaxi cap would recreate that scarcity, shielding existing operators from competition while denying dense-center residents—often low-income, disabled, or transit-poor—the cheapest pooled rides. The cap becomes permanent not because safety requires it, but because permit holders lobby to keep it.
[C1.3] The congestion argument cuts the other way. A capped fleet can still block intersections and circle for passengers; if set below efficient size, vehicles cruise longer, waits lengthen, prices rise, and riders return to private cars. A larger, coordinated fleet under dynamic pricing and pooling can replace several private vehicles and cut parking searches, a major source of urban traffic. Rationing supply does not ration congestion; it makes the remaining vehicles pricier and scarcer.
[C1.4] Labor policy should target wages and transition directly. A cap is not a wage floor, benefit mandate, or retraining program; it is a slowdown that preserves some driving jobs while preventing the operations, maintenance, charging, and oversight jobs that scale creates. Cities can require displacement disclosure, fund transitions, and set standards while allowing deployment to proceed.
[C1.5] Safety improves through exposure, reporting, and iteration, not by keeping vehicles out of hard places. The motion lets cities postpone clear rules indefinitely while pretending a cap is caution. Reject it: regulate outcomes, price the curb, protect workers, and let fleets compete under those rules.
