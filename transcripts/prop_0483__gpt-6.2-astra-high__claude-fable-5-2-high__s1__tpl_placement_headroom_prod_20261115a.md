[P1.1] A reasoning score can be worth consulting without being worth a veto. If a school uses it to keep a student out of advanced science, or an employer uses it to discard an application before anyone sees the applicant’s work, the claim is no longer just that the score predicts something. It is that the score deserves priority over evidence the gatekeeper may never see.
[P1.2] IQ-style tests ask people to recognize patterns, hold information in mind, and reason through problems that someone else has specified. Those are genuine cognitive demands. But consider a technician facing a machine that fails intermittently. The crucial insight may be to ask the operator what changed between the last good run and the first bad one.
[P1.3] Knowing what information is missing, eliciting it, and revising an explanation when it arrives are acts of reasoning, not personality traits. A good performance on a puzzle may predict some success at diagnosis; it is not a demonstration of diagnosis.
[P1.4] The distinction matters especially at the cutoff. Even if higher scorers do better on average, that does not tell a school to prefer the student just above its threshold to one just below who designed a careful experiment to distinguish two rival explanations. Excluding the latter student also denies them the instruction through which the school might discover its mistake.
[P1.5] Standardization need not mean standardizing on puzzles. Give applicants the same diagnostic scenario, let each request information, and score the reasoning against common criteria. Give students a common investigation and evaluate how they use evidence. Keep the test where it adds information.
[P1.6] What needs justification is letting it silence better evidence, and neither the convenience of a common scale nor its ability to predict average outcomes supplies that justification.
