[P1.1] The motion sets its own test: population health per dollar. That means the health of everyone, set against total spending, and it does not mean the best hospital available to the best-insured patient. On that test the evidence runs one way.
[P1.2] Start with the natural experiment. The United States, the wealthy world's clearest case of predominantly private insurance, spends roughly a sixth of its national income on health, which is about double what the typical rich country spends per person. In return it gets shorter life expectancy, higher infant and maternal mortality, and more deaths from treatable conditions than Britain, Canada, France, Japan, Australia or the Nordic states, all of which are publicly financed. The US pays twice the price for a worse result.
[P1.3] That is a gap far too large to be a rounding error.
[P1.4] Three mechanisms explain it, and they are structural.
[P1.5] First, private systems waste money on overhead. Multiple insurers mean spending on billing departments, claims denials, marketing, and profit margins. None of this spending heals a patient, and a single public payer avoids most of it.

[C1.1] The proposition fails because it treats “public” as automatically efficient and “private” as automatically wasteful, while the decisive variable is not the insurer’s ownership but the rules around coverage, prices, and access.
[C1.2] PRO’s centerpiece is the United States. But the U.S. is not a clean test of private insurance; it is a high-price, fragmented system with weak universal enrollment, heavy cost-sharing, and administrative complexity created by thousands of plans and provider markets. The bad outcomes per dollar come from those design failures, not from private financing as such. If the lesson is “cover everyone, regulate prices, and simplify administration,” then many private-insurance systems already do that without becoming single-payer.
[C1.3] Switzerland and the Netherlands are the crucial counterexamples. Both use mandatory private or quasi-private insurers with universal enrollment, strong risk equalization, subsidies, and regulated benefits. They spend more than the leanest public systems but far less than the United States, while delivering high life expectancy, low infant mortality, and broad access. If PRO’s model were right, these predominantly private insurance systems should collapse under overhead and poor prevention; they do not.
[C1.4] Public systems also have costs that crude dollar comparisons miss. Budget control often becomes rationing: longer waits for hips, knees, mental health, and elective cancer care; delayed diagnoses; and pressure to underinvest in new technology. When a diabetic waits months for a specialist or a patient postpones surgery, the system may look cheap this year, but it can produce worse health and higher downstream costs. Those harms are real health outcomes, not accounting quirks.
[C1.5] Finally, population health is shaped by demographics, poverty, diet, education, and violence, not only clinics. Public financing cannot claim credit for every advantage in healthier societies while blaming private systems for factors outside medicine.
[C1.6] The better claim is that good regulation beats bad design—not that public systems beat private insurance per dollar.
