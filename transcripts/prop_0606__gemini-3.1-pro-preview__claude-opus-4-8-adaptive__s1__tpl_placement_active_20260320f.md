[P1.1] A patent is a temporary, government-granted monopoly designed to reward innovation, but it was never intended to be a death warrant for those who cannot pay. Essential medicines are, by definition, non-negotiable for survival. When these vital treatments are priced beyond a population's capacity to pay, the market has fundamentally failed. By treating access to essential medicines as grounds for compulsory licensing whenever prices exceed a defined affordability threshold, governments can correct this deadly inefficiency and fulfill their primary duty: protecting human life.
[P1.2] The traditional pharmaceutical business model frequently relies on low-volume, high-margin pricing, which structurally excludes vast segments of the population. Tying compulsory licensing to an objective affordability threshold stops monopolies from holding societies hostage.
[P1.3] Opponents will inevitably claim this policy destroys the incentive to innovate. This relies on a false dilemma. First, compulsory licensing is not expropriation; originators still receive reasonable royalties for their patents. Second, setting a defined threshold actually creates market predictability.
[P1.4] If pharmaceutical companies know exactly where the licensing trigger is, they have a built-in incentive to price their medications just below that line to maintain total market exclusivity. This smoothly shifts the market toward a high-volume, broad-access model that sustains business without sacrificing public health.
[P1.5] We have already witnessed the devastating consequences of prioritizing absolute patent rights over human lives. During the early HIV/AIDS epidemic, millions died in developing nations because antiretrovirals were priced at exorbitant monopoly rates. It was only the threat and application of generic competition—via mechanisms like compulsory licensing—that forced prices down to accessible levels and saved millions of lives. A universal affordability trigger prevents such tragedies from ever repeating.
[P1.6] Innovation means nothing if the resulting medicines only exist behind an impenetrable paywall. A standardized affordability threshold guarantees that when science produces a life-saving cure, the law ensures patients can actually afford to survive and use it.

A threshold pegged to objective local metrics—such as a defined percentage of per capita income or the national health budget—scales perfectly to local purchasing power. [P2.4] In [C1.4], CON warns that companies will simply price "just below the line," treating the threshold as a floor. [P2.5] Regarding the claim in [C1.5] that complex breakthroughs will vanish, CON ignores a crucial premise: compulsory licensing is not outright theft. Pharmaceutical companies still profit from high-volume global sales and continuous royalty streams; they simply lose the ability to artificially restrict volume to exploit desperation.
[P2.6] The status quo "scalpel" CON defends [C1.8] requires years of agonizing political negotiation and trade pressure—time during which vulnerable patients die.
