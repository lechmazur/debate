[P1.1] The mobile OS duopoly is infrastructure. Two firms control 99% of phones. When they set their own wallet as the only tap-to-pay, their own browser as the unchangeable engine, their own assistant as the long-press on the power button, that is not product design, it is self-dealing at the bottleneck.
[P1.2] Defaults decide markets. Behavioral evidence is overwhelming: fewer than 10-15% of users ever change a default browser, let alone dig into NFC settings. On iOS you could not even pick another default browser until 2020, and even now Apple forces all browsers to use WebKit. On Android, Google pays to be default and uses scare screens - "this browser may not protect you" - when you try to leave.
[P1.3] In wallets, Apple blocked third-party access to the NFC chip for a decade, so every bank had to go through Apple Pay and its fee. For AI, the side button now summons Gemini or Siri with no neutral setup choice. Preference is not winning on merits; it is winning by being preinstalled.
[P1.4] That kills the mechanism that disciplines tech: contestability. Why build a better browser if you can never reach rendering engine parity? Why build a better wallet if you cannot tap? Why build a specialized medical or legal AI assistant if the OS intercepts every voice trigger for its own general model?
[P1.5] The result is higher take rates, slower web app innovation, and fragile single-point control of payments and information.
[P1.6] Security and integration are not excuses. An OS can set neutral security standards for NFC access or browser engines without requiring its own product win. The EU browser choice screen and the recent opening of NFC to third parties prove it can be done without breaking phones.
[P1.7] Barring default preferencing does not ban Apple Wallet, Chrome, or Siri. It bans using OS control to make them win automatically. If they are truly better, they will win a fair choice screen.

[C1.2] Defaults are starting points, not final orders. Users can install rival browsers, wallets, and assistants, and often set them as defaults. [C1.3] Satisfied default users are not evidence of foreclosure.
[C1.4] Much of what the other side calls self-dealing is consumer-desired integration. A default wallet tied to hardware security and biometrics is safer than a third-party bolt-on. [C1.5] Choice screens are not neutral either. Someone must choose which apps appear, in what order, and with what warnings. Mandating them invites user fatigue, regulatory capture, and weaker security defaults. And actual foreclosure—denying an API or NFC access—can be handled by targeted enforcement.
[C1.6] A permanent design ban on successful integration is a disproportionate remedy.
[C1.7] The strongest check on defaults is competition between ecosystems. Apple and Google compete hard on privacy, performance, and integration. If one makes its default stack genuinely abusive, users and developers push back or leave. Treating both as regulated distribution pipes may please rival app vendors, but it does not serve phone buyers.
[C1.8] We should reject a rule that penalizes preinstalled excellence while weakening security and user experience.
