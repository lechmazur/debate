[P1.1] If a central bank digital currency is designed to replace cash, then taking away anonymous peer-to-peer use below a modest threshold strips away the quiet privacy that cash always gave ordinary people. A CBDC that logs every transfer, even five dollars handed to a friend, turns daily life into a permanent financial record open to state inspection. That is not a technical detail; it is a transformation of the relationship between citizens and power, replacing spontaneous trust with architectural surveillance.
[P1.2] The proposition's fixed legal threshold solves the legitimate concern about large-scale abuse. Above the line, identity and oversight apply; below it, the user enjoys the same anonymity as a banknote passed hand to hand. This is not loophole economics—it mirrors the cash system we already have and that has not collapsed society. Religious or political donations below the line equally deserve shielding from retaliation, something only anonymity can guarantee.
[P1.3] Consider a simple case: a neighbor pays a teenager ten dollars for shoveling snow, or a passerby donates to a street musician. Requiring verified identities for such moments chills routine generosity and excludes those without perfect documentation. In many communities, elderly people uncomfortable with smartphones rely on cash-like anonymity; a CBDC without it alienates them. When cash disappears, only a CBDC with anonymous small-value P2P preserves financial inclusion and civic freedom.
[P1.4] The contrary position demands total traceability with no private sphere. That exaggerates the risks of minor anonymous payments while ignoring the proven reality that major laundering and tax evasion operate through large, traceable channels, not pocket-change transfers. Forcing universal surveillance for the sake of marginal enforcement gains is a disproportionate cure that converts a public convenience into a lever for control. The burden is on those who would abolish the private sphere to show why the state needs to watch pocket change.

[P1.2] says the threshold “mirrors the cash system we already have.” It does not: it mirrors the privacy while discarding the constraints. Even a modest $50 cap on anonymous transfers does not stop a $500,000 laundering operation; it merely turns it into ten thousand automated taps, cheaper and safer than any suitcase of bills.
[C1.3] That is not “pocket change”; it is laundering, sanctions evasion, and ransomware payouts at industrial scale.
[C1.4] The required anonymity also harms the very people it claims to help. Anonymous P2P is final and untraceable. [C1.5] Nor is the choice anonymity versus total surveillance. Privacy can be protected through data minimization, strong access controls, and warrants—privacy from merchants and marketers, not immunity from lawful investigation. [C1.6] We should reject the proposition. A CBDC can respect privacy without being forced to be blind. The burden is on PRO to explain why automation-age anonymity below a line must be mandatory—and why the rest of us should bear the fraud, abuse, and enforcement costs of that mandate.
