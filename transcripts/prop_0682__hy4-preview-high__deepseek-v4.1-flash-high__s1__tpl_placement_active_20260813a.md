[P1.1] National security is not only what our intelligence services can see; it is also the vulnerability we create by sustaining a global market in intrusion tools. [P1.2] Commercial spyware is a master key, not a one-time bullet. When a foreign intelligence service buys a zero-click exploit, it can target an exiled dissident, a journalist, or even officials in partner countries. The same flaw usually exists in devices used by our own personnel, allied governments, and diaspora communities. Once the tool is used, discovered, or leaked, the underlying vulnerability is exposed to criminals and rival states, who then weaponize it against everyone, including us.
[P1.3] Stricter export licensing shrinks the number of these master keys in circulation.
[P1.4] The abuse is not hypothetical. Recent years have produced repeated credible reporting of journalists, activists, and officials in partner nations having phones compromised by commercially purchased spyware. That generates diplomatic crises, erodes trust with allies whose citizens were targeted, and invites reciprocal or copycat operations. It also lets repressive states outsource repression to vendors rather than build accountable, lawful capabilities.
[P1.5] The opposition will call this unilateral disarmament. It is not. The regulation targets commercial sales to foreign end users, not our own classified programs or lawful intelligence collection. We can still recruit sources, collect signals intelligence, and develop tools in-house under oversight.
[P1.6] What we give up is the convenience of private firms profiting by handing foreign services turnkey intrusion capabilities we cannot monitor.
[P1.7] Overall security is a balance. Export regulation trades a marginal loss of convenience in foreign collection for large reductions in proliferation, blowback, and abuse. That is the decisive comparison: fewer reusable vulnerabilities abroad, fewer crises at home, stronger alliances, and preserved core intelligence capacity.
