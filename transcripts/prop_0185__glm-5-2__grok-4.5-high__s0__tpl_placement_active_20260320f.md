[P1.1] Legislatures have a built-in asymmetry: spending wins votes now while fiscal pain lands later, on someone else's watch. Every democratic government feels this pull. The question is whether to keep trusting that pull to self-correct, or to bind fiscal policy to a rule that survives the political cycle. Constitutional balanced-budget provisions with automatic stabilizer exceptions do exactly that—imposing discipline when the economy is near or above potential, while freeing countercyclical spending precisely when it is needed.
[P1.2] The case against pure discretion is not theoretical. Discretion failed spectacularly during the 2000s expansion. Many advanced economies ran structural deficits even while growth was healthy and unemployment was falling. Politicians spent windfall revenues rather than banking them, leaving no fiscal space when the financial crisis hit.
[P1.3] Discretion does not mean "flexibility used wisely." It means deficits in good times and deficits in bad times, because the political reward structure never reverses.
[P1.4] A rigid balanced-budget amendment would indeed be dangerous—procyclical austerity during downturns would deepen recessions. But that is not what the proposition proposes. The automatic stabilizer exception is the critical design feature. Unemployment insurance, food assistance, and revenue shortfalls would operate without legislative approval, expanding naturally during recessions and contracting during recoveries.
[P1.5] The rule targets the real problem: structural deficits built during expansions, not the temporary deficits that come automatically during downturns.
[P1.6] The constitutional layer matters because statutory rules arewaivable by the same legislature that benefits from waiving them. Statutory pay-as-you-go rules in the United States were routinely bypassed with emergency designations and exceptions. A constitutional provision raises the cost of evasion. It creates judicial review, supermajority requirements, and a normative anchor that shifts the burden of proof onto those who want to borrow during good times.
[P1.7] This is the core comparison: discretion has been tried and has produced persistent structural deficits. A constitutionally embedded rule with stabilizer exceptions disciplines the political cycle where it fails, while preserving automatic responses where fiscal policy actually works. That is the safer bet.

[C1.1] Legislatures do face short-term spending pressure. That is no reason to hand fiscal architecture to a constitutional clause and the courts that would police it. The proposition assumes the political bias is fixed and permanent while a cleverly written rule will be precise, enforceable, and costless. Both halves fail.
[C1.2] Automatic stabilizers already function under ordinary discretion. Revenues fall and safety-net payments rise in recessions without needing a constitutional green light. What the rule actually constrains is everything else: large-scale response to banking crises, pandemics, wars, or multi-year public investment that cannot be classified as “stabilizers.” Drafting an exception list that anticipates every genuine emergency is impossible. Either the list is narrow and the rule becomes procyclical, or the list is expansive and the constitutional constraint evaporates under creative reclassification.
[C1.3] Experience with statutory pay-as-you-go and debt-ceiling statutes shows politicians will invent designations; a constitutional version merely relocates the fight to courtrooms and supermajority votes rather than eliminating it.
[C1.4] Courts lack both the technical capacity and democratic warrant to decide when an economy sits “above potential” or whether a spending item truly is automatic. Embedding that judgment elevates litigation over legislation and invites partisan appointments of judges as fiscal arbiters. Markets and voters already punish chronic profligacy through higher interest rates and electoral turnover. Those feedbacks are imperfect, but they adapt to new shocks; a rigid clause freezes yesterday’s definition of fiscal virtue.
[C1.5] The safer path is legislative responsibility itself—transparent multi-year frameworks, independent forecasting offices, and the electoral risk of visible debt dynamics—rather than a constitutional straitjacket that will either bind too tightly or leak through every engineered exception. Discretion is not the absence of discipline; it is the only mechanism that can still adjust when reality refuses to fit the drafting committee’s model.
