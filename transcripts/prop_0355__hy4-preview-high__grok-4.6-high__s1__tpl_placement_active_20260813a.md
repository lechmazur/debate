[P1.1] The motion asks who controls clean transportation for renters: the resident who needs it, or the landlord who can stall. Today the renter asks, and the owner has the power to delay, refuse, or ignore. That is not a balanced property right; it is a private veto over whether an electric vehicle is usable at all. A stronger right to request does not force owners to pay.
[P1.2] It flips the default: a clear request, a duty to respond on a deadline, and refusal only for narrow reasons such as verified capacity or code conflicts.
[P1.3] This matters because assigned-space charging is where EVs actually work. Public charging is pricier and less reliable overnight, and many renters park in the same spot every night. If that space cannot have a charger, whole households are locked out of cheaper fuel and maintenance, and climate policy treats renters as second-class. Picture a tenant in a forty-unit building willing to pay for a licensed, separately metered installation who is stopped not by wiring or cost but by management never approving.
[P1.4] The obstacle is permission, not physics.
[P1.5] Landlord objections are manageable. Wiring capacity, fire risk, cost, and aesthetics can be handled by conditions the tenant meets: payment by the requester, licensed contractor, permits, insurance, load management, and restoration on move-out. California’s right-to-charge rule proves the model: tenants install in their own assigned spaces at their own expense, while owners avoid cost and liability. Owners may gain, because EV-ready buildings are more attractive.
[P1.6] What they lose is not property; it is the power to block a zero-emission choice through inertia.
[P1.7] The clash is between administrative convenience and access to essential infrastructure for a legal, mainstream product. If the tenant pays and the connection is feasible, “I’d rather not” is not a legitimate denial. Make the process real—timely response, written reasons, independent review—and renters gain the mobility the clean-energy transition assumes they should have.

[C1.1] The proposition treats a landlord's caution as illegitimate power. It is not. Parking, panels, and the service entrance are shared capital the owner remains responsible for after the lease ends. A request for EV charging is a request to alter that system.
[C1.2] The owner's ability to delay or refuse follows residual risk, not a veto over clean cars.
[C1.3] Home charging is convenient, not essential. Public and workplace charging already make EVs usable. Picture a four-unit walk-up with a 1960s panel. One tenant offers to pay a licensed installer.
[C1.4] The work still changes load calculations, insurance, fire-code exposure, and what the next occupant inherits. If the charger later causes an outage or a claim, the landlord—not the departed renter—answers. "I'll pay and restore on move-out" does not follow the tenant into court or to the insurer. Assigned space does not privatize the feeder.
[C1.5] A forty-amp circuit is capacity other units cannot use. Delay is often the time needed to learn whether that trade is tolerable.
[C1.6] A stronger right to request, with deadlines and narrow refusals, is a mandate with extra forms. Engineering studies and permits take time because they must. A tenant-favoring clock does not create capacity. It shifts the cost of error onto neighbors who never asked.
[C1.7] There is no limiting principle. If a leaseholder can commandeer common electrical capacity because an EV is mainstream, the next demand is a heat pump or a workshop circuit. Right-to-charge statutes do not erase those tradeoffs; they litigate them. Small owners eat legal cost and delayed occupancy; larger ones fold the friction into rent.
[C1.8] The default belongs with whoever owns the wires. Markets already reward EV-ready buildings. Elevating one consumer product over an owner's duty to keep the whole building safe and fairly allocated is the wrong swap. Climate goals are better served by public chargers and construction standards than by litigating each older building on a tenant's clock.
