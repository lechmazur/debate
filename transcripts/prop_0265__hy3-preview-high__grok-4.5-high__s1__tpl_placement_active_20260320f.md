[P1.1] Hidden fees are a classic bait-and-switch that erodes trust the moment a diner sees the final bill. When a restaurant advertises a price but adds a mandatory service charge at the checkout, they are not providing a price; they are providing a deceptive estimate. Displaying the full cost upfront is a fundamental requirement of honest commerce.
[P1.2] The current practice of revealing surcharges late in the process creates a "gotcha" moment that ruins the dining experience. Even if the service was excellent, the final total feels like a penalty because it exceeds the mental budget the customer set when ordering. This "bill shock" is a manufactured frustration that turns a loyal patron into a disgruntled critic. By including all mandatory costs in the menu price, restaurants eliminate this friction and ensure the final bill matches the customer's expectations.
[P1.3] Critics often argue that menu prices would look unappealingly high if they included these charges. This is a concession that the industry relies on obscuring the true cost of dining to attract customers. If a surcharge is unavoidable, it is part of the price of the meal. Hiding it is a deliberate attempt to make the business appear more competitive than it actually is.
[P1.4] A customer cannot make a truly informed choice if the price tag is a lie.
[P1.5] Mandatory fees also create an administrative and cognitive burden. Diners must perform mental math to calculate tips or compare venues, leading to frustration and slower table turnover. Transparent pricing streamlines the payment process and respects the diner’s time and intelligence.
[P1.6] The burden of action should not be on the customer to hunt for footnotes or ask servers about hidden fees. Ethical business practices demand that the price on the menu is the price on the bill. Full transparency is not just a courtesy; it is the only way to ensure a fair transaction in the hospitality industry. We must move past the era of deceptive pricing and require that the "mandatory" price be the "displayed" price.

[C1.3] Mental budgets are rarely formed in total isolation from the service reality of restaurants.
[C1.4] Bundling creates its own distortions. A venue that must carry a 18 percent service charge would simply inflate every printed price. [C1.5] The result is clumsier pricing, not cleaner honesty.
[C1.6] True transparency means showing the structure of the price—base cost plus any mandatory service line—before the order is locked, not collapsing everything into one opaque sticker that conceals how much covers food versus labor. Customers can then evaluate value as they already do, and restaurants can price service fairly without artificial packaging. The proposition mistakes a standard, disclosed element of hospitality for deception and replaces it with a rigid rule that reduces useful information.
