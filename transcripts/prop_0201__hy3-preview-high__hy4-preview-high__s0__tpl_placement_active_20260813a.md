[P1.1] When a state commits aggression, it acquires an international legal obligation to make reparation for the harm caused. That obligation does not evaporate because the wrongdoer's assets sit in foreign vaults under sovereign immunity. The proposition simply asks courts to close the gap between liability and remedy: where sovereign assets are already frozen, judges should be empowered to channel them toward rebuilding the schools, hospitals, and homes destroyed by the aggressor.
[P1.2] This is not a radical assault on property rights. The assets are already immobilized by lawful sanctions; the only question is their eventual use. A court-order route gives the affected country a structured, evidence-based claim, with the aggressor free to contest liability. That is more, not less, consistent with the rule of law than leaving trillions frozen in limbo while executive branches dodge the hard question.
[P1.3] Judicial oversight also prevents politically motivated misallocation.
[P1.4] The practical stakes are decisive. Reconstruction after modern aggression costs sums the victim state cannot raise alone, and the aggressor rarely pays voluntarily. To forbid courts from touching frozen wealth is to sentence civilians to generation-long deprivation while the perpetrator's reserves gather dust. That imbalance rewards violation of the most basic prohibition in international law.
[P1.5] Opponents will lean on sovereign immunity as an absolute shield. But immunity was never meant to protect a state from the consequences of its own internationally wrongful acts. Permitting targeted reconstruction remedies reinforces, rather than weakens, a rules-based order: it tells potential aggressors that borders and treasures will not insulate them from accountability. History shows impunity breeds repeat aggression.
[P1.6] Consider a simple analog: if an arsonist's bank account is frozen pending trial, no one argues the victim cannot later seek those funds to rebuild. At state scale, the logic is identical and the justice more urgent. Scale does not cancel fairness.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to mistake a freeze for a forfeiture. Sovereign immunity is not a loophole for impunity; it is the barrier that stops national courts from unilaterally redistributing another state’s wealth. Frozen sovereign assets are preserved precisely because title has not been transferred and because a negotiated settlement may yet restore them. Letting domestic courts convert those reserves into reconstruction spending collapses that distinction and turns judges into engines of confiscation.
[C1.2] PRO’s arsonist analogy fails because the asset is not the aggressor’s private loot. Central-bank reserves back a currency, secure trade, and belong to a state whose ordinary citizens did not individually commit aggression. Freezing them is a pressure tool; spending them is permanent expropriation.
[C1.3] Nor do courts add the neutrality PRO promises. A judge in the victim’s state cannot fairly declare a foreign government an “aggressor” while the executive has already branded it an enemy and immobilized its property. The defendant will default or boycott; the promise in [P1.2] that the aggressor can “contest liability” is theatre. What PRO calls “more, not less, consistent with the rule of law” is really victor’s justice with a gavel.
[C1.4] The practical stakes also cut against the motion. After the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, hundreds of billions in Russian central-bank reserves were frozen. If domestic courts simply transferred them to reconstruction, Moscow would not quietly absorb it: it would seize remaining Western-linked assets, accelerate de-dollarization, and treat future talks as hostage to a precedent it now fears. Frozen assets are leverage for a peace settlement; spent assets are a missed one.
[C1.5] Reconstruction should be funded by legislatures, multilateral institutions, and a final settlement—tools with democratic accountability and diplomatic purpose. The motion replaces that sober, lawful path with ad hoc judicial confiscation that is legally unstable, financially reckless, and strategically self-defeating.
