[P1.1] High public debt is a reason to choose better stabilizers, not to switch them off in a slump. Unemployment benefits, income support, lower tax bills under progressive schedules, and earnings-linked transfers arrive without legislative delay, reach cash-strapped households, and fade with recovery. Because they are temporary and rule-based, their value rises when debt is high. This is not permanent spending; it is targeted support protecting the tax base before recession hollows it out.
[P1.2] The debt-ratio objection confuses a stock with its denominator. Debt/GDP rises when GDP falls; slumps crush output, wages, and receipts. Withholding support to improve that ratio can be self-defeating. A worker whose extended benefit is denied spends less; a shop cuts hours; tax receipts fall and other safety-net costs rise.
[P1.3] The numerator may rise anyway while the denominator shrinks. A ratio damaged by recession is not repaired by making recession longer.
[P1.4] Sustainability objections get timing backwards. Consolidation in a slump is least credible: weaker growth makes adjustment harsher. Durable consolidation is easier after employment, profits, and revenues recover. Automatic expansion can use triggers and sunsets: benefits rise only while unemployment or output gaps breach preset conditions, then taper.
[P1.5] That is more disciplined than discretionary packages, and signals creditors that support is cyclical, not a ratchet.
[P1.6] High debt also raises the return on speed. In a downturn balance sheets are impaired, monetary policy may be constrained, and many households cannot self-insure against job loss. Delay can turn a temporary shock into evictions, business failures, long-term unemployment, and lower future output—the base from which debt is serviced. Protecting that base is asset preservation.
[P1.7] Debt-first restraint would withhold support precisely when private income collapses because a ratio looks uncomfortable. That buys a show of discipline at the price of weaker receipts, deeper recession, and often a worse ratio later. Expanding stabilizers answers debt risk with a temporary, self-reversing instrument and reserves consolidation for recovery, when the economy can carry it.
