[P1.1] Irregular migration is a labor market with the legal channel switched off. Farms in Almería, care homes in Lombardy, meatpacking plants in Iowa need workers every season, and employers hire whoever arrives. Border enforcement attacks the supply of crossings while leaving the demand untouched. That is why it fails: it makes the trip harder and more expensive, but as long as a job waits on the other side, someone will make it.
[P1.2] The clearest natural experiment is the US–Mexico border. When the Bracero program expanded in the 1950s, apprehensions fell sharply—Mexican farmworkers took the legal route because it existed. When Congress killed the program in the mid-1960s, the same workers, the same farms, and the same demand remained, and irregular crossings climbed for decades. Then came the enforcement era: fencing, tripled agents, billions per year.
[P1.3] The result was not fewer migrants but a changed migrant. Circular workers who once went home each winter stopped leaving, because the crossing had become deadly and cost thousands in smuggler fees. Enforcement converted seasonal movement into permanent undocumented settlement—the exact outcome it promised to prevent.
[P1.4] Legal pathways do the opposite. Spain's contracting-at-origin scheme with Morocco brings agricultural workers to Huelva each harvest, and return rates are high precisely because a worker who goes home is invited back. A visa you can lose is the strongest compliance tool ever devised. Compare that to a smuggler's route: once someone has paid for it, they have every incentive to stay forever.
[P1.5] Notice too what enforcement measures. Displacing a route from Arizona to the Sonoran desert, or from Lampedusa to the Canaries, counts as success on a local dashboard while total flows and deaths rise. Pathways actually reduce the irregular count, because the same person crossing with a permit is, by definition, no longer irregular.
[P1.6] Enforcement has had thirty years and unlimited budgets. The proposition asks which tool reduces irregular migration more effectively. Only one of them touches the reason people move.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a seductive idea: people cross borders for jobs, so give them a legal way to take those jobs. But the syllogism breaks at every step, because legal pathways do not substitute for irregular flows—they sit on top of them, and often feed them.
[C1.2] First, the demand mismatch. Seasonal and sectoral visas are capped, employer-tied, and small. Huelva's berry harvest absorbs tens of thousands; the populations seeking entry to Europe and North America number in the millions, and most are not seasonal farmhands at all. Families fleeing Venezuela, Syria, or Afghanistan are not candidates for a nine-month strawberry permit.
[C1.3] Legal pathways serve a thin slice of the flow while the rest keeps walking. Enforcement, by contrast, applies to everyone crossing without papers, whatever their motive.
[C1.4] Second, pathways create their own irregularity. A visa holder who disappears into the interior becomes an overstayer—and in the United States, a large share of the undocumented population entered legally and stayed. Every new pathway is a new pipeline of future irregular residents, complete with documents, networks, and a foothold. Worse, visible legal channels act as a magnet: they signal that entry is possible and reward those who position themselves, legally or not, at the border.
[C1.5] The Huelva model works only because it is small and tightly policed—which is to say, because enforcement is doing the heavy lifting.
[C1.6] Third, when enforcement is actually credible, it delivers. The EU–Turkey arrangement in 2016 collapsed Aegean crossings within months. Australia's turnback and offshore policy ended maritime arrivals. These were not route shifts; total flows fell.
[C1.7] That is the empirical signature of deterrence, and no visa program has produced anything comparable at scale.
[C1.8] The proposition's own examples concede the point: Bracero-era calm and Huelva's return rates both depend on controlled, enforced, finite channels. Strip away the enforcement architecture and the pathway becomes a doorway. Enforcement is the tool that reduces irregular migration; pathways are, at best, its supplement—and at worst, its accelerant.
