[P1.1] Every regulator faces two possible errors: approving something harmful, or blocking something beneficial. The first produces victims with names, lawsuits, and headlines. The second produces victims nobody ever sees: the patient who died waiting, the child who went without the better crop, the lungs that breathed coal smoke because the cleaner plant was never built. The precautionary principle, as commonly applied, builds that blindness into law.
[P1.2] Notice what the principle does in practice. It puts the burden on the innovator to prove safety, treats uncertainty as a reason to stop, and almost never asks what the status quo is costing. But the status quo is not safe. It is where people currently die of disease, hunger, and pollution.
[P1.3] A rule that scrutinizes only the risks of the new, and never the risks of the old, will systematically err in one direction.
[P1.4] Three examples show the pattern.
[P1.5] - **Nuclear power.** Precautionary licensing and public fear froze construction across much of the West for decades. The substitute was largely fossil fuel, whose air pollution kills vastly more people per unit of energy than nuclear ever has, accidents included.
[P1.6] - **Genetically modified crops.** Decades of broad scientific agreement on safety have not ended near-total cultivation bans in Europe, and those bans have pressured African governments to refuse drought- and pest-resistant varieties. Golden Rice, engineered to fight vitamin A deficiency, sat in regulatory limbo for roughly twenty years while children went blind.
[P1.7] - **Medicine.** Delayed approval of drugs already saving lives abroad has cost lives at home, and nobody was ever held accountable for those deaths.
[P1.8] Here is the test this debate should turn on. The question is not whether regulation ever prevents harm; of course it does. It is what the precautionary principle adds beyond ordinary evidence-based risk assessment. Thalidomide-style disasters are caught by requiring testing and weighing evidence, which cost-benefit regulation already does.
[P1.9] What precaution adds is delay and prohibition when the evidence of harm is speculative, and the forgone benefits there are large, compounding, and uncounted.

[C1.1] As commonly applied, the precautionary principle is not a demand that novelty halt until proved innocent. Where serious or irreversible damage is plausible, regulators need not wait for certainty before taking proportionate, reviewable action. That constrains timing and tail risk. It is not a veto on innovation.
[C1.2] The motion still needs a showing that blocked benefits exceed prevented harms. It does not have one.
[C1.3] Forgone benefits are called large because they go uncounted. Uncounted is not larger. A delayed drug, crop, or design can still arrive. Blindness from a teratogen, a poisoned aquifer, a collapsed fishery, or lead in a child's blood cannot be approved back into existence.
[C1.4] A rule built for that asymmetry will sometimes slow a good thing. It does not thereby block more good than it prevents harm.
[C1.5] The record is not a catalog of buried progress. Precautionary pressure helped remove lead from gasoline, asbestos from buildings, and ozone-depleting chemicals from commerce, and the industries involved kept innovating toward the substitutes those limits made worth building. Evidence was incomplete. Denial was profitable.
[C1.6] Waiting would have multiplied exposure that does not wash out.
[C1.7] The examples do not reverse that. Nuclear slowdown was cost, politics, and accident risk; strict rules did not stop countries that chose to build. Drug lag is mostly ordinary evidence-based approval, already conceded as justified, and haste has its own body count. Caution on modified crops did not stop the technology where it was wanted.
[C1.8] Delay is not children blinded by a principle when trade policy, product performance, and existing nutrition programs share the causal chain.
[C1.9] Common application refuses to treat uncertainty as a free pass when damage may be serious and hard to undo. It binds dangerous incumbents as well as newcomers, and it is meant to stay proportionate. On the comparison the motion actually requires, prevented harm still outweighs blocked innovation.
