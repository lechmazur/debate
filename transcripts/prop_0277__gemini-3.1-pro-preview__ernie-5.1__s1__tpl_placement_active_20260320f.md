[P1.1] Popular tourist cities are buckling under the weight of hyper-concentrated tourism. Funneling millions of visitors into a few historic blocks or peak summer months creates an unsustainable model that degrades the city for locals and tourists alike. Spreading demand across neighborhoods and seasons is the only viable path forward.
[P1.2] First, concentrated tourism destroys local livability. When a single district absorbs the vast majority of visitors, it turns living, breathing neighborhoods into unlivable theme parks. Residents face crushed public transit, severe noise pollution, and the rapid displacement of essential services by souvenir shops and short-term rentals. Distributing this footprint reduces the localized stress on infrastructure and housing.
[P1.3] It prevents the hollowing out of city centers, allowing locals to reclaim their hometowns while mitigating the severe physical wear and tear on fragile heritage sites.
[P1.4] Second, spreading demand promotes economic equity. Under the current model, a fraction of businesses in a central hub capture the vast majority of tourist revenue. By guiding visitors to diverse, residential neighborhoods, cities can inject capital into local businesses and restaurants that traditionally miss out on the economic upside of tourism. A distributed model transforms visitors from a localized nuisance into a city-wide economic benefit.
[P1.5] Finally, the seasonal and geographic dispersal of tourists radically improves the visitor experience and the local labor market. The opposition might argue that tourists naturally congregate because they only want to see the main attractions, but nobody travels to shuffle through gridlocked plazas or stand in hours-long queues. Smoothing out the demand curve geographically alleviates these miserable bottlenecks. Furthermore, spreading tourism across the calendar stabilizes employment in the hospitality sector, replacing the precarious boom-and-bust cycle of peak seasons with reliable, year-round jobs for residents.
[P1.6] We must transition from an exploitative, highly concentrated model to a sustainable, distributed one. Tourism should enrich a city, not overwhelm its center.
