[P1.1] Universities do not have an information problem; they have a belonging problem. A lonely first-year can already find a meditation video, a counselling booking page, and three engagement apps on her phone. What she cannot download is a table where people expect her on Thursday, notice her absence, and save her a seat. Funding in-person clubs, common spaces, and recurring events first treats the cause of distress — isolation — rather than monetizing its symptoms through another dashboard.
[P1.2] Digital wellness platforms are weakest where need is greatest. They rely on the isolated, exhausted student to opt in, dismiss notifications, and self-report to a screen. Use fades after novelty wears off, leaving licences paid for students already resourceful enough to seek help. An app can nudge you to breathe; it cannot give you a climbing partner, pull you to rehearsal, or notice over coffee that you have gone quiet this week.
[P1.3] Physical infrastructure works through repetition, not reminders. Friendships form from repeated, unplanned contact: the same faces in the union lounge, the society that meets every week, the five-a-side team that needs you to show up. Those ties become study groups, flatmates, and the informal safety net that catches problems before they become crises. Common spaces also work without a login, a diagnosis, or being tracked — vital for commuters, international students, and anyone exhausted by online life.
[P1.4] Order matters because space is scarce and hard to restore. Once a common room becomes offices or a club budget is hollowed out, rebuilding that culture takes years; adding app features later takes a contract. Universities already have portals, email, and learning systems. The next pound spent expanding them buys duplication, while the same money keeping a room open late or funding a weekly low-cost social buys hundreds of real encounters.
[P1.5] Fund where community actually happens first, and digital tools can then support — not substitute for — a campus worth belonging to.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes physical presence equals belonging, but it ignores who gets left out of the room. A student working thirty hours a week, a disabled student navigating inaccessible buildings, or an international student in a different time zone cannot attend Thursday society meetings—but they can access counselling at 2 AM through a digital platform or join a peer group anonymously. Prioritizing brick-and-mortar first locks the most vulnerable, time-poor students out of support.
[C1.2] Digital wellness scales where physical space cannot. A teletherapy contract or moderated mental health app serves thousands for a fraction of the cost of renovating one common room. When budgets are finite and counselling waitlists stretch for months, spending capital on lounge furniture while students wait is a misallocation. The pound funding a weekly pizza night reaches fifty students; the same pound expanding crisis chat reaches five hundred, including those who never enter the student union.
[C1.3] Anonymity matters. Students with severe social anxiety, LGBTQ+ students not out to their families, or those fleeing abusive homes often cannot walk into a physical wellness centre without being seen. Digital platforms offer private, low-stakes entry to care that in-person spaces structurally cannot provide. Sequencing physical infrastructure first treats visibility as a prerequisite for help, excluding those who need discretion.
[C1.4] The dichotomy is also false. Digital tools do not replace community—they coordinate it. Event apps and virtual study groups extend the reach of physical spaces and connect commuter students to campus life. Expanding these first builds the infrastructure that makes any future in-person investment more inclusive.
[C1.5] Fund the digital lifeline first. It reaches further, costs less, protects the vulnerable, and includes those the lounge chair leaves empty.
