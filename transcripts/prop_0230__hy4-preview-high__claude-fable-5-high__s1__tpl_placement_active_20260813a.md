[P1.1] The case for mandatory national service is not nostalgia. It is a response to two shortages Europe can no longer paper over: a shortage of trained people for defense, and a shortage of shared civic experience. Volunteer forces and optional civic programs have been tried and found too thin. The European answer is not to drag society back to 1914, but to update the citizen-duty model: short, fairly shared, gender-neutral where possible, with military and civilian tracks.
[P1.2] Defense readiness starts with mass. Professional armies buy excellence but not scale; they are expensive, hard to recruit, and brittle in a prolonged crisis. Conscription builds a trained reserve and a wider base of citizens who know logistics, communications, first aid, and civil defense—exactly the skills that make deterrence credible and societies resilient. Finland kept universal male conscription and can mobilize a reserve far larger than its active force, while maintaining high social trust.
[P1.3] Sweden abolished conscription, discovered volunteers would not fill its units, and brought it back; Norway made service gender-neutral. Where it already exists, expansion should mean broader intake, civilian tracks, and gender-neutral service, not simply more of the same. Reintroduction is not fringe; it is what serious states do when volunteer recruiting falls short.
[P1.4] Social cohesion cannot be downloaded. Schools and media increasingly sort young Europeans into separate social circles, regions, and information bubbles, and optional programs mostly attract the already-engaged. A compulsory year—whether in uniform, firefighting, elder care, environmental repair, or cyber defense—forces a cross-section of a generation to live and work together under common rules. That shared effort builds trust and a felt national identity in a way no civics class can.
[P1.5] Critics will call this illiberal or costly. But a short, universal obligation tied to training, pay, and education credit is not forced labor; it is the price of a defense and a democracy that belong to everyone. Europe needs readiness and cohesion at scale. Mandatory service is the one instrument that can deliver both at that scale.

[C1.2] Start with readiness, because PRO's theory of war is outdated. Modern deterrence rests on precision weapons, air defense, drones, ammunition stockpiles, and highly trained professionals who serve long enough to master complex systems. A conscript on a nine-month rotation is barely trained before discharge; German and French generals spent years arguing exactly this before their countries suspended conscription. Every euro spent housing, feeding, and supervising reluctant draftees is a euro not spent on artillery shells, air defense batteries, or retaining experienced sergeants.
[C1.3] Ukraine's hardest lesson is that undertrained mass gets people killed; its most effective units are motivated volunteers with serious equipment. If Europe's problem is readiness, the answer is defense spending on capability and a well-paid volunteer reserve—not a mass intake that dilutes training budgets.
[C1.4] PRO's own examples cut against the motion. Finland and Sweden are small states with a long border-threat tradition; their model does not transfer to Spain, Portugal, or Ireland. And Sweden's "reintroduction" is telling: it drafts only a small, selective fraction of each cohort—closer to targeted recruitment than the universal obligation PRO defends.
[C1.5] On cohesion, the mechanism is asserted, not shown. Historical conscription coexisted with deep class division; the wealthy have always found exemptions, deferments, and cushy postings, which breeds cynicism, not trust. Forcing a diverse generation into barracks does not manufacture solidarity—resentment at a year of lost wages, delayed education, and coerced labor is at least as likely.
[C1.6] Finally, the principle: conscription is a massive tax paid exclusively by the young, in years of their lives, to solve problems adults refuse to fund through budgets. A democracy that needs coercion to feel cohesive has diagnosed its illness wrong—and prescribed a treatment that weakens both liberty and defense.
