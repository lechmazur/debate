[P1.1] Countries should reintroduce native apex predators into suitable parts of their former range, even when rural communities object. The governing principle is straightforward: people facing concentrated costs deserve protection and compensation, but not an unconditional veto over ecological recovery. A damaged ecosystem is not merely a private land-use preference; its consequences extend downstream and across generations.
[P1.2] Predators restore functions that degraded landscapes often lack. Wolves can reduce or redistribute large herbivores, relieving browsing pressure on young trees and riverbank vegetation. Recovering vegetation can support other wildlife, stabilize soils, and improve habitat. Predation also supplies carrion and influences competition among species.
[P1.3] These effects vary by ecosystem; predators are not magic. But removing a major ecological relationship and then permanently prohibiting its return can lock degradation in place.
[P1.4] The alternative is not cost-free coexistence without predators. It can mean repeated culling, fencing, planting, and other human interventions to compensate for missing ecological processes. Those measures sometimes remain necessary, but they are not always substitutes for predators operating across landscapes and generations. Restoration should rebuild ecological capacity, not assume indefinite human maintenance is preferable.
[P1.5] Farmers’ objections nevertheless identify real burdens: killed livestock, disrupted husbandry, anxiety, and additional work. Governments should therefore fund prevention before releases, including suitable fencing, livestock protection, and husbandry changes; compensate verified losses promptly; and pay for reasonable additional labor. Reintroductions should be staged and monitored, with targeted intervention when particular animals create serious problems. These obligations belong to the public purchasing the ecological benefit, not solely to the farmer living beside it.
[P1.6] But consultation must shape implementation, not become a requirement for unanimous permission. Otherwise, those nearest a restoration site can block benefits shared by everyone, including other rural residents and future generations. The decisive comparison is between accountable restoration that reduces and shares its costs, and continued degradation because objections are treated as absolute. Protect livelihoods while restoring predators; do not make ecological recovery hostage to consent.
