[C1.2] Consider what "enforceable" actually requires. Unlike a fire code, which can be inspected annually, indoor air quality is dynamic. CO2 shifts hourly. Particulate matter varies with occupancy, weather, and season.
[C1.3] Enforceable means continuously measurable, which means permanent sensor networks, ongoing reporting, and an inspection regime unlike anything in existing building codes. Governments that struggle to maintain school roofs will now audit HVAC performance across every public building. That is not a minimum obligation. It is an administrative fantasy.
[C1.4] Then consider who pays. Schools are public institutions. When government mandates ventilation upgrades, the school district pays—with money that would have gone to teachers, textbooks, or special needs programs. Older buildings, concentrated in poorer districts, require the most expensive retrofits.
[C1.5] The result is regressive: communities with the fewest resources divert the most from actual education to satisfy a uniform standard. Small businesses in older commercial spaces face the same squeeze—comply at a loss or close.
[C1.6] PRO calls poor ventilation an externality. But the real externality is when a mandate forces an underfunded school to choose between a new air-handling system and keeping its art teacher. Better alternatives exist: tax credits for HVAC modernization, tenant disclosure requirements that let families and workers choose, targeted grants for under-resourced districts. These flex with building age, type, and use.
[C1.7] A single enforceable standard cannot.
[C1.8] The fire-code analogy fails because fire codes address discrete, inspectable hazards. Ventilation is a continuous system interacting with climate, occupancy, and architecture. Collapsing that distinction produces compliance theater—sensors installed to satisfy inspectors while real air quality barely changes.
[C1.9] Clean air matters. But this proposal trades practical improvement for bureaucratic satisfaction.
