[P1.1] Political equality is the foundation of democratic legitimacy. If every citizen has one vote, the state should not allow wealth to buy disproportionate influence over who wins office and what laws they pass. Corporate political donations are especially dangerous because they are not expressions of personal conscience; they are strategic expenditures controlled by managers or boards, using aggregated capital, often aimed at securing access, contracts, regulation, or tax treatment. A corporation’s speech interest can be protected through advocacy and issue advertising, but direct donations to candidates and parties turn policy access into a market.
[P1.2] The proposition’s core reform is simple: ban corporate donations and place much lower caps on large individual donations. This does not silence ordinary citizens. It prevents a tiny donor class from becoming the gatekeepers of political competition. When campaigns depend on a handful of wealthy patrons, candidates spend their time courting those patrons, and policy agendas tilt toward concentrated interests.
[P1.3] This happens even without explicit bribery: access, repeated contact, gratitude, and fear of lost funding all shape priorities.
[P1.4] Treating money in politics as ordinary speech confuses expression with domination. A person speaking on a soapbox does not drown out others; a flood of money can. If donors can fund entire campaigns, they can set the menu of viable candidates and punish those who challenge entrenched interests. Lower caps preserve the right to contribute while ensuring no single person can become a campaign’s patron.
[P1.5] The predictable objection is that restrictions burden speech. But democratic speech rights are not absolute; they are structured to protect collective self-government. Countries can require disclosure, public financing, free airtime, and robust issue advocacy. What they should not do is allow boardrooms and billionaires to convert economic power into political power.
