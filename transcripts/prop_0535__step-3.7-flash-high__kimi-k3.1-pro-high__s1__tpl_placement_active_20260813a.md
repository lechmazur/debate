[P1.1] The right to control one’s own medical care does not vanish the moment a person loses decision-making capacity to dementia. Advance directives exist precisely to extend autonomy into that exact scenario, and the proposed standard—binding force unless there is clear evidence of a material value shift—strikes the only workable balance between respecting prior autonomy and honoring genuine changes in a person’s core values.
[P1.2] Without this strong default, advance directives become empty promises. A person who plans for years to avoid the undignified, dependent state they associate with advanced dementia—refusing life-sustaining food, hydration, or antibiotics when they can no longer recognize loved ones or engage with the world—would have that plan discarded the moment a caregiver interprets a reflexive smile or moment of apparent pleasure as a sign their values have changed.
[P1.3] But dementia impairs judgment, insight, and the ability to act on long-held core values; most apparent "preference shifts" in advanced dementia are symptoms of the disease, not evidence of a deliberate change in what the person cared about.
[P1.4] The clear evidence standard is not a rubber stamp. It requires demonstrable, consistent proof that the person’s core priorities shifted independent of cognitive decline. For example, if a person wrote an advance directive refusing permanent institutional care, but years before diagnosis repeatedly told family they had come to see long-term care as a dignified option and intended to revise their directive, that would count as clear evidence of a value shift. This standard respects both the person’s lifetime of choices and the reality that dementia robs people of the ability to make new, authentic choices—while the alternative substitutes the preferences of impaired individuals for the autonomous decisions the person made when they were fully competent.

[C1.1] An advance directive deserves great weight. It does not become morally omnipotent because its author once had capacity. The proposition converts respect for past autonomy into a nearly irreversible license to override the living person before us.
[C1.2] Its “clear evidence” exception is structurally rigged. Someone with advanced dementia is least able to articulate a consistent account of transformed values, while relatives may disagree and clinicians may dismiss enjoyment as mere reflex. The result is not neutrality: silence, confusion, and family conflict all favor enforcing the old document. A safeguard the vulnerable person cannot realistically activate protects the document, not the person.
[C1.3] Strong binding also ignores dementia’s diversity. Capacity can be decision-specific and fluctuating; someone may be unable to manage finances yet still understand a simple choice about antibiotics or hospitalization. The motion treats cognitive impairment as if it erased every present preference, while demanding proof of an almost philosophical conversion before those preferences matter.
[C1.4] Consider a directive refusing “life-prolonging treatment” in severe dementia, written by someone imagining constant suffering. Years later she is comfortable, sociable, and visibly engaged, but develops pneumonia. Antibiotics might return her to that life. Under the proposed default, her contentment proves nothing; unless someone demonstrates a material change in her values, the old refusal can prevail.
[C1.5] That is not autonomy extended. It is a past self’s projection enforced against present welfare.
[C1.6] PRO calls weakened directives “empty promises.” But a promise about unknown future circumstances cannot responsibly mean “ignore everything we later learn.” Vague wording, unforeseen treatments, uncertain prognosis, and family disputes all require interpretation. Making that interpretation strongly binding shifts power from the patient to whoever construes the text.
[C1.7] Specific, informed, repeatedly reaffirmed refusals should ordinarily govern when directly applicable. But where present welfare, current preferences, or changed circumstances create serious doubt, decision-makers must be able to respond without proving a value conversion. Otherwise fidelity to paper becomes abandonment of the person.

[P4.1] A1: CON’s Q1 assumes we can objectively identify when a directive’s “factual premise” (like anticipated dementia suffering) has collapsed without sliding into caregivers’ subjective judgments about which lives are worth living. If override is permitted based on “premise collapse” alone, decision-makers can substitute their own views of a good life for the incapacitated person’s clearly stated prior wishes.
[P4.2] The clear evidence standard avoids this by only allowing override when the person themselves, while still competent, explicitly revised their values, not when others judge their current state as acceptable.
[P4.3] A2: CON’s Q2 misstates the safeguard: it does not only look for pre-dementia value changes, but for any authentic, competent revision of core priorities, even in early mild cognitive impairment before full incapacity. Uncertainty resolves in favor of the prior directive because it is the only uncoerced, clear record of the person’s values, unclouded by dementia’s progressive erosion of judgment, insight, and consistent preference formation. What appears to be a “sustained preference” post-diagnosis is often confabulation, emotional lability, or the disease overriding prior core commitments, not a genuine shift.
[P4.4] Defaulting to the prior directive when authenticity is uncertain is not abandonment of the living patient—it is the only way to ensure their own choices, not others’ projections, govern their care. This standard is the only workable balance between respecting lifetime autonomy and honoring genuine change. CON’s alternative offers no guard against subjective caregiver bias, leaving people with dementia vulnerable to having their explicit prior wishes discarded based on fleeting, unreliable impressions of their current state.
