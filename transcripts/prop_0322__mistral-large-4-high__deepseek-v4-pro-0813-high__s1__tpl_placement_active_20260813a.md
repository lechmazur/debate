[C1.1] A synthetic voice is not automatically a counterfeit, and treating it that way is the proposal’s central mistake. Creators already use AI for benign ends: repairing damaged audio, translating a segment, producing an obvious synthetic character, or voicing text the creator wrote but could not record. A mandate tied to “AI-generated voices or scripted likeness replication” sweeps all of those into a legal disclosure regime. The relevant question is whether the audience is materially misled, not which tool generated the sound.
[C1.2] The rule is also unworkably vague. Would a noise-repair plugin trigger it? A single synthesized word replacing a stutter? A licensed model trained on the creator’s own voice?
[C1.3] Small podcasters and independent video creators cannot reliably classify their production stack. The cost of guessing wrong—fines, lawsuits, platform removals—falls hardest on those least able to bear it, while large studios hire compliance teams. That is not the trivial burden the proposal assumes.
[C1.4] Existing law already reaches the real harm. Unauthorized voice cloning is actionable under false endorsement, fraud, and right-of-publicity doctrines. Paid-endorsement rules require disclosure of the commercial relationship because that affects a listener’s judgment. But once the endorsement is authorized and the material connection is disclosed, whether the delivery involved an AI tool adds no useful information for evaluating the message.
[C1.5] If there is no consent, the problem is theft and deception, not missing labeling.
[C1.6] Finally, disclosure does not fix the scenario the proposal imagines. A label warning that a voice is AI may inform the buyer, but it does not compensate the host whose identity was taken and does not stop the unauthorized use. And if consent exists, a mandatory AI label is compelled speech attached to truthful commercial expression. The better framework is targeted enforcement of existing deception and consent laws, not a broad new mandate that punishes AI use itself.
