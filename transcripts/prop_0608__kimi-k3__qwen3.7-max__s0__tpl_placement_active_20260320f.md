[P1.1] A company that declares itself "net-zero" is making a factual promise to investors, customers, and regulators: my operations no longer add carbon to the atmosphere. The question is what that promise is made of. Our case is simple: a promise built mostly on offsets is mostly fiction, and the law should say so.
[P1.2] First, the integrity problem. Offsets systematically overstate their benefit. Analyses of major voluntary registries have found that large shares of forest-protection credits likely never represented real avoided emissions — the trees were never going to be cut. Permanence is worse: forests sold as offsets have literally burned, releasing carbon a buyer had already claimed as neutralized.
[P1.3] When the credit is phantom, the net-zero claim is phantom. Law already polices misleading environmental claims; capping offsets is how "net-zero" becomes a claim that can actually be true rather than a marketing license.
[P1.4] Second, the incentive problem. Offsets are cheap — often a few dollars per ton — while genuine decarbonization costs far more upfront. Leave offsets uncapped and the rational CFO buys credits and postpones the boiler replacement, the fleet transition, the supplier overhaul. Corporate climate action becomes an accounting exercise instead of an industrial one.
[P1.5] A legal cap reverses that: reductions must happen in the value chain, where they compound into technology, infrastructure, and supply chains that stay decarbonized. And offsets are finite — there is not enough credible land or removals for every firm to make them the plan. They cannot be the main tool for everyone.
[P1.6] Third, the design is proportionate. We are not banning offsets. Genuinely residual emissions — cement process emissions, some aviation — may need neutralization for years. A small permitted share reserves offsets for exactly that role: last, limited, and only after real cuts, which is how serious standard-setters already treat them.
[P1.7] The cap writes best practice into law because voluntary practice has failed.
[P1.8] Net-zero should describe a company that transformed itself, not one that rented someone else's forest. Limit the offsets; require the transformation.

[C1.3] We cannot afford to bottleneck climate finance with arbitrary quotas when public funding is already vastly insufficient.
[C1.4] Furthermore, the demand for strict in-value-chain reductions ignores physical and economic reality. A dollar spent on a verified, high-quality offset often reduces far more atmospheric carbon than a dollar spent on a forced, marginal in-chain adjustment.
[C1.5] The answer to phantom credits is rigorous quality verification, not a blunt legal cap. Independent standard-setters are already establishing strict integrity frameworks to root out bad actors. Let the market and evolving standards mandate quality. A legal limit arbitrarily restricts corporate climate ambition, penalizes hard-to-abate sectors, and defunds global mitigation projects.
[C1.6] We must regulate the quality of the carbon ton, not legally choke the quantity of the investment.
