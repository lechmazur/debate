[P1.1] A driver finishes a Saturday of rides, then the app goes dark. A warehouse picker supplements rent with evening deliveries, then both streams collapse in a downturn. Unemployment insurance and social insurance exist to replace lost earnings from work. Platform labor is work.
[P1.2] When governments refuse to count it toward eligibility, they do not preserve flexibility—they leave a growing class of people paying into the economy but standing outside the insurance pool when income disappears.
[P1.3] The proposition is precise. It does not require reclassifying every gig worker as an employee. It requires that platforms' own payout data become standardized earnings records, so contribution bases, work weeks, and eligibility can be calculated the way wages already are. Platforms already know every fare and transfer.
[P1.4] In many systems they already report that income for tax. The missing piece is a legal requirement that those same records count for insurance.
[P1.5] That requirement closes an arbitrary gap. Two people can lose the same dollars and receive opposite protection solely because one paycheck ran through payroll and the other through an app. Mixed earners are hit hardest: they can work substantial hours yet fail recency or earnings tests because a large share of their labor is legally invisible. Coverage thins, later emergency spending rises, and firms get a standing invitation to shift social costs off the books by routing work through platforms.
[P1.6] Counting platform earnings does not freeze schedules or kill matching algorithms. Workers can still choose hours. Platforms can still allocate demand. What changes is that income volatility is no longer a private disaster financed by everyone else.
[P1.7] If the labor is real enough to tax, it is real enough to insure. Require the records. Count the work. Anything less is a policy of uncovered labor sold as innovation.
