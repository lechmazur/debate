[P1.1] Financial exchanges exist to match buyers and sellers fairly so capital flows to productive enterprise. Instead, modern exchanges run a lucrative side business: auctioning time itself. By selling ultra-low-latency server space mere yards from their matching engines, exchanges grant private trading firms a microsecond head start over everyone else. Prohibiting co-location sales stops institutionalized rent extraction, cures a corrupting conflict of interest, and curtails a socially wasteful arms race.
[P1.2] Look at how bought proximity distorts trading. When a pension fund routes orders to invest retirees' savings, or economic news breaks, co-located servers inside the exchange's facility detect the earliest order-book ripples and fire trades microseconds before outside messages even clear the building's perimeter. Speed traders use that purchased time to snipe resting limit orders and reprice shares right ahead of incoming institutional buyers. That is not genuine risk-taking; it is latency arbitrage, a hidden tax extracted from ordinary savers through worse execution prices.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, legitimate market makers get trapped in a protection racket, forced to pay steep co-location rents merely to defend their quotes against snipers occupying the adjacent rack.
[P1.4] Worse, selling co-location corrupts the referee. Exchanges are not ordinary commercial landlords; they are regulated market utilities entrusted with fair price discovery. Turning physical proximity into a high-margin product addicts exchanges to structural asymmetry. An exchange profiting handsomely from microsecond advantages has every incentive to preserve predatory speed races, rather than equalizing access at its external gateways.
[P1.5] Banning these sales realigns exchange revenues with fair trading instead of privileged access.
[P1.6] Finally, prohibiting co-location sacrifices zero real economic value. Discovering a stock price microseconds faster builds no factories, lowers no corporate borrowing costs, and funds no innovations. Even if speed firms rent offices nearby, exchanges stripped of co-location profits lose their financial incentive to cater to nanosecond predation. Exchanges should maintain level playing fields, not sell cuts in line.
