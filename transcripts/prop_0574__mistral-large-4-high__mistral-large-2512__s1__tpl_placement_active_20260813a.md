[P1.1] The dead cannot consent. Yet studios and labels now deploy AI to manufacture "new" performances from artists who never agreed to lend their voices, faces, or creative personas to synthetic works. This is not innovation; it is digital grave-robbing that treats human identity as raw material to be strip-mined after death, generating revenue from the vulnerable silence of the tomb.
[P1.2] Our legal systems already recognize that identity interests outlive the body. Right of publicity laws in many jurisdictions protect a person's name, image, and likeness for decades post-mortem precisely because economic value and dignitary harm persist beyond the grave. If a studio must negotiate a license to use a living artist's voice in a commercial, death must not become a loophole that converts human creativity into a consent-free zone where estates have no veto and families watch their loved one's likeness monetized without recourse.
[P1.3] The damage is immediate and irreversible. When AI generates a "new" recording in a deceased artist's style, it fabricates an endorsement, manufactures consent that was never granted, and extracts revenue from an identity that cannot object, negotiate, or revoke permission. Estate managers and heirs—who hold the legal responsibility to steward an artist's legacy—are bypassed entirely. The artist's moral rights to protect the integrity of their work and reputation are annihilated by algorithms trained on their life's work, creating content the artist might have found abhorrent.
[P1.4] We require explicit permission for samples, covers, and biographical films. Why should AI-generated performances—often indistinguishable from authentic recordings and capable of flooding markets with synthetic catalogs—be exempt from the same fundamental requirement? The proposition simply extends an established principle: you cannot appropriate someone's identity without asking. Death is not a waiver of rights; it is precisely when those rights require the strongest protection against exploitation.
