[P1.1] A corporation that claims net-zero while buying cheap credits for most of its pollution is not reporting a reduction; it is outsourcing its responsibility. The proposition sets a simple integrity rule: offsets may supplement corporate climate action, but only for a small residual share, while most emissions must be cut inside the company’s own operations and supply chain.
[P1.2] The core problem is that an offset is not equivalent to eliminating a ton from the claimant’s value chain. Many offsets depend on avoided deforestation, soil carbon, or renewable projects that are difficult to verify, may not be additional, and can be reversed by fire, policy change, or market shifts. When a firm can meet a net-zero label by purchasing credits cheaper than changing its energy use, logistics, materials, or product design, the rational business response is to buy the label rather than make the transition.
[P1.3] That is how net-zero becomes a marketing category instead of a decarbonization pathway.
[P1.4] Legally limiting offsets forces companies to confront the emissions they control or cause. In-value-chain reductions—electrifying heat, switching to clean power, redesigning products, cutting transport emissions, demanding low-carbon materials—create durable changes in capital stock and procurement. They also reveal the true cost of emissions to boards, investors, and regulators. A small offset allowance can still cover genuinely hard-to-abate residual emissions and support high-quality removals, but it cannot become the loophole that swallows the target.
[P1.5] This is especially important because net-zero claims influence consumers, investors, and policy. If those claims can be built mostly on third-party promises, they distort competition against firms making real capital investments. The question is not whether carbon finance can exist; it is whether the phrase “net zero” should be legally allowed to mean “we paid someone else to claim our reduction.” It should not.
