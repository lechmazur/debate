[P1.1] Proactive takedowns of political content fail on their own terms. A deleted post does not delete the belief; it relocates it, angrier and harder to answer. The user sees a blank screen labeled misinformation and learns nothing except that a distant moderator disapproved. The claim then resurfaces as a screenshot, a coded euphemism, a grievance on another app — now armored with proof of censorship.
[P1.2] Community notes reverse that dynamic by keeping the speech visible and attaching the correction to it, so refutation travels as far as the rumor.
[P1.3] This matters most in politics because truth is often contested, evolving, and interpretive. A takedown demands false certainty: remove or leave up, true or false, within hours. Notes allow proportion. A misleadingly edited video of a protest, a cherry-picked crime statistic, an exaggerated claim about a bill can be annotated with missing context, original footage, full numbers, with sources readers can check themselves.
[P1.4] That preserves voter autonomy instead of substituting a moderator's judgment for it.
[P1.5] Notes are also institutionally more legitimate. Centralized takedown teams, however well-intentioned, will always be accused — often plausibly — of partisan bias because someone in an office decides what millions may see. A system that only publishes context when contributors who normally disagree agree on it builds credibility across divides. People accept correction from an unlikely coalition in a way they never accept deletion from a corporation.
[P1.6] None of this denies that some narrow categories like direct incitement or voter suppression about time, place, and manner of voting need fast removal. But those are the exception. For the vast bulk of political argument, exaggeration, spin, and disputed claims, addition beats subtraction: more speech with shared context, not less speech on secret authority.
