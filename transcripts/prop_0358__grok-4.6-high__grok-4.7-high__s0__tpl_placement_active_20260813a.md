[P1.1] Traffic deaths in cities are not mainly a failure of punishment. They are a failure of physics and design. A person struck at about twenty miles an hour often lives. Struck at thirty or forty, they often die.
[P1.2] Kinetic energy rises with the square of speed, stopping distances stretch, and the driver’s chance to recover a mistake collapses. If the goal is fewer deaths, the first job is to keep ordinary trips from reaching lethal speed.
[P1.3] Lower default urban limits do that directly. Street redesign does it more reliably still: narrower lanes, tighter corners, raised crossings, and protected space for walking and cycling. Those features make high speed uncomfortable and sometimes physically difficult. The street itself becomes the enforcement.
[P1.4] Drivers do not have to remember a future sentence while they are late, distracted, or treating a wide arterial as if it were built for thirty-five.
[P1.5] Harsher post-crash penalties cannot substitute for that. They arrive after the body is already on the pavement. Deterrence needs people to expect detection, conviction, and a real cost. Urban speeding is common, enforcement is patchy, and many fatal crashes involve people who did not set out to kill anyone.
[P1.6] Raising the penalty on a rare, late event leaves the everyday geometry of death untouched.
[P1.7] Worse, punishment strategies hunt “bad drivers” while the lethal system stays legal. Wide, fast streets with high defaults invite the speeds that turn a lapse into a funeral. Lower limits and redesign change the system that produces those lapses. Penalties, at best, try to scare a subset of people into driving as if the street were already safe.
[P1.8] If cities want fewer traffic deaths, they should make lethal speed rare before the crash, not merely expensive after it.

[C1.1] Deaths do not fall evenly across ordinary urban trips. They concentrate in impairment, extreme speeding, distraction, and repeat offending. Severe post-crash penalties deter those behaviors before the trip and remove the people who commit them afterward. Favoring lower defaults and redesign over that response treats a concentrated behavioral problem as if it were mainly a geometry problem.
[C1.2] Kinetic energy is real, but it does not settle the ranking. A sober, attentive driver near a reasonable speed is rarely the one who kills. A drunk, racing, or red-light-running driver can still kill after the posted limit drops and the corner is tightened. Narrower lanes do not sober anyone, and they do not take the keys from someone who has already shown they will endanger others.
[C1.3] Long license loss, vehicle forfeiture, and substantial prison terms for fatal or serious crashes caused by clear violations do both. They raise the expected cost in advance and stop the same driver from doing it again.
[C1.4] Redesign is also slower and blunter. Rebuilding streets takes years and large sums, and calming one corridor often shifts volume onto the next untreated road. Unrealistically low defaults, set below the speed most drivers will actually choose, breed noncompliance. Enforcement then becomes patchier, not sharper.
[C1.5] A harsh, predictable consequence for the crash that follows speeding, impairment, or reckless disregard is a clearer rule than a limit treated as advisory.
[C1.6] If the goal is fewer traffic deaths, cities should not spend their first political and fiscal capital on slowing every trip. They should make killing or seriously injuring someone with a vehicle costly in the currency dangerous drivers understand: freedom, licenses, and cars. Design can support that. It should not outrank it.
