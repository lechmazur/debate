An employee choosing between "consent to being filmed in your kitchen" and "lose your remote job" isn't exercising free choice; they're signing under duress, and now the company gets a signature to point to as proof of legitimacy. That's worse than no consent regime at all, because it converts raw power imbalance into contractual cover.
[C1.3] Employment law scholars have long recognized that consent inside an employment relationship is structurally suspect—which is exactly why privacy regimes like GDPR generally refuse to treat employee consent as valid grounds for monitoring and instead rely on necessity and proportionality tests. PRO's framework moves backward from that insight.
[C1.4] Second, "strict limits" is doing enormous unexamined work in this proposition. Who defines them? Enforced how? Without an independent auditor, a regulator, or a works council with real teeth, "strict limits" is just a phrase in a policy document—compliance theater that lets a company claim it followed the rules while quietly expanding scope over time, the exact mission-creep PRO warns about in [P1.6].
[C1.5] A consent form doesn't stop keystroke logs from capturing a health search; only a hard legal prohibition on certain data categories does that.
[C1.6] The better path isn't PRO's soft consent-gate—it's mandatory, externally enforced limits on what can be monitored and how, independent of whether any individual worker "agreed." That protects the vulnerable worker who can't afford to say no, closes the loophole PRO's own framework leaves open, and doesn't pretend a signature under economic duress equals dignity. A proposition that hinges legitimacy on consent nobody can freely give isn't a safeguard; it's a false comfort dressed up as one.
