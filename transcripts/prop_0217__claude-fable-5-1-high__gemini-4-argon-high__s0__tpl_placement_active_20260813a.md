[P1.2] It squeezes the family that was never the problem and barely dents the estate that is. That is the core failure of blunt restrictions: they ration the wrong water.
[P1.3] Sharply rising prices fix the targeting. Give every household a cheap lifeline block for essential use, then make each additional tier steeply more expensive. The apartment never leaves the first tier and feels nothing. The estate faces a bill that climbs toward hundreds or thousands of dollars a month, and suddenly the lawn gets replaced, the leak gets fixed, the pool gets a cover.
[P1.4] Nobody had to write a rule about lawns or pools. The price finds the cheapest conservation that each household can actually make, which is something no city inspector can know in advance.
[P1.5] Restrictions also rely on enforcement theater: neighbors reporting neighbors, patrols checking sprinklers, fines that wealthy owners simply pay. [P1.6] Then there is the utility's own survival. When a ban cuts consumption, revenue collapses, and the utility raises base rates on everyone—including the frugal family—to cover fixed costs. Tiered pricing keeps the system solvent and funds the leak repairs and recycled-water projects that end the recurring shortage rather than merely surviving this one.
[P1.7] Recurring shortages demand durable behavior change, not emergency scolding. The question for the round is simple: whose water should be cut? Prices cut the heavy user's water. Blunt restrictions cut everyone's, and still leave the estate green.
