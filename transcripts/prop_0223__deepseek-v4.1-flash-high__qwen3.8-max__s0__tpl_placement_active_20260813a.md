[P1.1] Speed is the variable that turns ordinary mistakes into fatalities. A crash at 25 miles per hour is survivable; the same mistake at 45 often is not. The metric that matters, then, is whether enforcement changes the speed distribution across the whole road network, not whether it produces dramatic stops.
[P1.2] Automated speed enforcement does that better. Cameras can be placed at every school zone, work zone, and high-crash corridor. They detect violations consistently, at all hours, without an officer deciding who looks suspicious, who gets a warning, or who gets handcuffed. Deterrence comes from certainty, and certainty scales.
[P1.3] When drivers know speeding is detected everywhere, not just where a cruiser happens to be parked, compliance spreads beyond the camera itself. That is how average speeds fall, and lower average speeds reduce crash severity across the board.
[P1.4] Traditional stop-based surges cannot match that. They are labor-intensive, geographically sparse, and discretionary. Their safety gain is concentrated where officers happen to be. Their externalities are not.
[P1.5] A minor traffic stop is an armed state encounter: it can become a search, a pursuit, an injury, a death, or a humiliating viral scene, and it falls disproportionately on minority and low-income drivers. It also pulls police away from violence and theft, and it puts officers on the roadside in traffic.
[P1.6] Automated enforcement has real governance problems: fines can be regressive, cameras can be used as revenue machines, and privacy needs rules. But those are design choices, not inherent costs of face-to-face policing. The proposition is comparative. One tool can enforce speed everywhere without a gun, a badge, or a pretext.
[P1.7] The other cannot. That is a larger safety gain with fewer negative externalities, not because cameras are perfect, but because the alternative is both under-inclusive and coercive. If we want fewer deaths and less collateral damage, choose the tool that tickets the offense rather than the person, before the crash instead of after.
