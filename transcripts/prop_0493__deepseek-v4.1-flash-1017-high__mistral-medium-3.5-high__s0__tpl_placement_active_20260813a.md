[P1.1] Water is not a soft drink input like any other. It is the condition of life, public health, and local ecology. Once a corporation owns or trades a municipal source or aquifer for bottling, the decision that matters most—how much to extract, when, and for whose profit—has already been handed to an actor with a legal duty to maximize returns. That is the central failure the ban addresses.
[P1.2] The harm is not hypothetical. A bottling plant can draw heavily from an aquifer that also supplies household wells and small farms; the profit is captured privately, while the depleted aquifer, subsidence, dry wells, and lost future supply are socialized. Regulation may slow this, but it cannot erase the incentive to pump more, sell more, and treat the aquifer as an asset to be liquidated. And “trading” makes it worse: it creates a market in extraction rights, inviting speculation and turning a public resource into a financial instrument whose price rises as scarcity deepens.
[P1.3] Ownership also corrupts democratic accountability. A cash-strapped municipality may sign a long-term extraction contract that looks attractive today and becomes nearly impossible to undo later. Residents then face a corporation with contractual rights and political resources, while the public retains the liability but loses the lever. That is not a partnership; it is a transfer of sovereignty over a necessity.
[P1.4] Notice how narrow this proposition is. It does not ban private water use, farming, or ordinary commerce in bottled drinks. It bans corporate ownership, trading, and extraction of municipal water sources and aquifers specifically for commercial bottling—the case where the profit motive most directly collides with the need to preserve a shared, often irreplaceable source.
[P1.5] The opponent will say regulate instead. But regulation presumes the public can monitor, renegotiate, and enforce against a powerful extractor. A ban is clearer: it keeps the allocation decision where it belongs, with the public and its accountable institutions. When the choice is between bottled profit and a community’s water future, the water future must win.

Food can be grown elsewhere and shipped; energy has substitutes; a depleted municipal aquifer often does not.
[P2.2] That is why the motion targets commercial bottling from municipal sources and aquifers, not private water use in general.

Caps, reviews, and short contracts only work if the public can monitor, renegotiate, and enforce against a well-resourced extractor. [P2.5] Flint [C1.3] cuts against CON. Public mismanagement is an argument for democratic accountability, not for handing the source to an actor whose fiduciary duty is to sell more water. [P2.6] Finally, offshoring and property rights [C1.5] do not rescue them. If bottling moves elsewhere, this aquifer is spared—that is the point.
