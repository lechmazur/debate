[P1.1] The proposition is narrow: when a rule is highly restrictive and mainly reflects a family’s personal lifestyle preference—not safety, health, or basic rights—parents should not be able to turn teachers into enforcers. That matters because schools are shared institutions, not extensions of one household’s habits.
[P1.2] Three points decide this.
[P1.3] First, schools must serve many families with limited staff. If one parent can demand that teachers police lunchboxes, friendships, hairstyles, or clothing because of personal preference, the school either obeys and burdens everyone, or refuses and faces conflict. A teacher cannot fairly spend instructional time inspecting snacks or measuring skirt lengths for idiosyncratic demands. Less power means schools can set reasonable, generally applicable boundaries.
[P1.4] Second, children have an independent interest in developing autonomy. A highly restrictive rule imposed at home is one thing; when the school enforces it, the child learns that adults may use institutions to control ordinary social life. Consider a parent who wants a school to ban birthday treats because of a family diet, or prevent a child from working with classmates of another gender. Those may be sincere preferences, but using teachers to impose them teaches conformity, not judgment.
[P1.5] The school should protect the child’s ability to encounter ordinary diversity and make age-appropriate choices.
[P1.6] Third, parental authority is real, but it is not unlimited command over shared institutions or public resources. Parents may guide their own homes. They may choose private options where feasible. What they should not have is a veto that converts personal taste into school policy.
[P1.7] The proper line is simple: parents control their own household; schools control common rules; and when the two conflict, schools should not be forced to enforce restrictive personal preferences.
[P1.8] The decisive question is who gets to use coercive institutions for private lifestyle preferences. The answer should be: less power to commandeer schools; more space for children, teachers, and pluralism.

[C1.3] Parents are not ordinary lobbyists. They bear legal and moral responsibility for the child, and schools act in their place for much of the day. Weakening that default makes public schooling less trustworthy for families who already worry their convictions will be dismissed as “personal preference.”

Honoring a few parental restrictions is not commandeering the school; it is ordinary accommodation. [C1.5] That invites more conflict, not less.
[C1.6] The child-autonomy argument cuts the other way. Autonomy develops best when parents remain the primary moral educators and schools cooperate where possible.
[C1.7] The real fault line is not health versus preference; it is whether public schools must accommodate the plural families they serve. They should. Reject a proposal that would reduce parents to clients whose deepest commitments can be ignored whenever a teacher finds them restrictive.
