[P1.1] Heritable germline editing is not a stronger version of gene therapy; it is a different act. Somatic therapy changes non-reproductive cells in one existing person; if it fails, the harm stops with that consenting patient. A heritable edit alters the embryo’s germline, so the change is carried in that child’s sperm or eggs to future generations. Those descendants never consent to being part of an open-ended genetic experiment.
[P1.2] That alone justifies a ban: people exposed to a risk must be able to say yes or no.
[P1.3] Off-target effects may stay invisible for decades and can interact with other genes only in later generations. Once a heritable change enters the population, it cannot be recalled. No monitoring system can follow the descendants indefinitely. A cautious society does not ban all gene therapy; it bans an unconsented, irreversible change to the shared human gene pool.
[P1.4] The medical necessity claim is weak. Parents who carry serious single-gene conditions can already use IVF with preimplantation genetic diagnosis to select an unaffected embryo without editing. The unique added benefit of heritable editing is narrow; the unique added risk is permanent and transgenerational. What remains is often a preference for a genetically related child, not a last-resort treatment.
[P1.5] Legalization also invites a market. Once the line between treating disease and enhancement blurs, clinics will face pressure to offer optimized traits, and disability is reframed as an avoidable genetic defect. The result is engineered inequality sold to those who can pay. A bright-line ban is easier to enforce than a fuzzy boundary and stops the country from becoming a destination for genetic tourism.
[P1.6] The proposition is therefore consistent: countries can allow non-heritable therapies for consenting patients while banning heritable reproduction. The dividing line is consent, reversibility, and the difference between treating a person and redesigning the human future.
