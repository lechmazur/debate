[P1.1] A parking space is public land. When a full-size pickup overhangs the stall lines, forces the city to repaint wider spaces, blocks sightlines at crosswalks, and grinds down pavement faster than a sedan, the driver is consuming more of a shared resource — and right now, everyone else picks up the tab. The proposition asks only that cities price that difference honestly. That is not punishment; it is the same user-pays logic we accept everywhere else: heavier trucks pay more at tolls, bigger houses pay more property tax, freight pays by weight.
[P1.2] The safety costs are the sharpest edge. Vehicles have grown dramatically taller and heavier over two decades, and tall, blunt hoods are far deadlier in pedestrian collisions — they strike the chest and head rather than the legs, and their height creates blind zones in which a small child standing in front of the vehicle is simply invisible. Pedestrian deaths in American cities have risen steeply over this same period. When one consumer's vehicle choice raises the risk of death for everyone outside it, a modest fee is the gentlest possible corrective.
[P1.3] Nobody is banned from anything.
[P1.4] The street-use costs are just as real. Oversized vehicles occupy more curb length, meaning fewer parking spots per block. Road wear scales steeply with vehicle weight, so a three-ton SUV imposes maintenance costs far beyond a compact car's while paying identical fees. Cities from Paris to Washington, D.C. have already moved toward size- and weight-based pricing precisely because flat fees quietly subsidize the largest vehicles.
[P1.5] Note what the proposition does not do. It doesn't touch tradespeople who genuinely need a truck any more than a downtown parking meter "bans" commuting — they can pay the true cost of the space they use, and cities can exempt commercial fleets if they choose. The choice here is simple: either large-vehicle owners pay for the costs they impose, or pedestrians, cyclists, and small-car drivers subsidize them. Fair pricing wins that comparison.
