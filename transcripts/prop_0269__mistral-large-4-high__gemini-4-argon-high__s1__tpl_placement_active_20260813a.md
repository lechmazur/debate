[P1.1] The secondary luxury market runs on a simple fiction: that buyers can outsmart sophisticated counterfeiters with a grainy photo and a thirty-day return window. Resale platforms pocket substantial commissions on every transaction while pushing the authentication burden onto consumers who lack the expertise, tools, and economies of scale to verify authenticity. This isn't merely unfair—it's economically irrational and ultimately self-defeating.
[P1.2] Platforms occupy a privileged position in the transaction. They control listing standards, set fee structures, and collect revenue from every sale—including the counterfeit ones that slip through their filters. When a buyer receives a fake designer bag, they bear the full loss: the purchase price, the dispute friction, and the eroded confidence. The platform, meanwhile, has already secured its commission and insulated itself with "as-is" clauses and narrow return policies.
[P1.3] This creates a perverse incentive: the easier it is to list fakes, the more inventory flows, the more fees the platform collects. The cost of fraud is externalized onto buyers while the profit is privatized by the intermediary.
[P1.4] Authentication is a fixed cost that scales with volume. A platform can train specialists, deploy machine-learning detection, and negotiate collective insurance at rates no individual buyer can access. Forcing every consumer to become an expert in stitching patterns, date codes, and hardware engraving is grossly inefficient—like requiring every airline passenger to inspect the jet engine before boarding.
[P1.5] Furthermore, buyer confidence is the platform's actual product. If consumers believe the marketplace is a minefield of fakes, they stop bidding, stop listing, and the liquidity that makes resale valuable evaporates. Platforms have both the strongest incentive and the only realistic capacity to guarantee authenticity. Shifting that burden to buyers isn't consumer protection; it's risk laundering that destroys the market everyone depends on.

[C1.4] Requiring buyers to shoulder the evidentiary burden within a strict dispute window is the only defense against this moral hazard. If platforms absorb that risk, they inevitably pass the losses onto honest casual sellers and shoppers through punitive fee hikes.

Shoppers who want zero-risk luxury can pay high fees on consignment sites; bargain hunters use peer-to-peer platforms precisely because they prefer lower prices over expensive institutional guarantees.
[C1.6] Forcing luxury-grade overhead onto everyday resale destroys the circular economy.
