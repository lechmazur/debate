[P1.1] Cities should remove urban freeways more often than widen them because widening treats a symptom—vehicle demand—while removal addresses the deeper failure: placing high-speed regional traffic through the heart of city life. The core test is not whether a freeway carries cars today, but whether a city can allocate scarce land, money, and public health capacity better.
[P1.2] Widening usually fails its own test. Added lanes invite more driving. Induced demand means the new capacity fills, congestion returns, and the city is left with a broader barrier between neighborhoods. The construction is extraordinarily expensive, displaces homes or parks, and locks in emissions and noise for decades.
[P1.3] Even if congestion improves briefly, the long-run effect is more vehicle miles traveled, harder-to-meet climate goals, and worse crashes at high-speed merge points.
[P1.4] Removal is counterintuitive but often works because urban freeway traffic is partly discretionary and redistributable. When a freeway comes down, trips do not simply gridlock. Drivers shift routes, times, or modes; some trips disappear; surface boulevards can move people with signals, transit lanes, and safer crossings. San Francisco’s Embarcadero and Boston’s elevated removal show that replacing freeways with boulevards or parks can restore street grids, increase taxable land, and reconnect downtowns to waterfronts.
[P1.5] The point is not that every removal is easy; it requires transit, demand management, and freight planning. But that is precisely why it should be chosen more often: it forces cities to solve mobility, not merely pour concrete.
[P1.6] The decisive comparison is opportunity cost. A widened freeway consumes public money and land to move vehicles faster for a few years. A removed freeway can return that land to housing, local commerce, parks, and safer streets. Because cities are dense, space-constrained, and responsible for health and economic vitality, the presumption should favor removal.
[P1.7] Therefore, more often than widening, removal is the better urban choice.

[C1.2] For that vast majority, widening, retrofitting, or converting lanes to managed/transit use is the safer, more broadly applicable tool. Removal is the exception that works only under a narrow set of preconditions—redundant capacity, a nearby parallel route, mature transit, and political will to fund alternatives for a decade before the freeway comes down. Most American and even most global cities do not have those preconditions today.
[C1.3] PRO's showcase examples prove this narrowness rather than refute it. San Francisco's Embarcadero freeway did not come down because a city deliberately chose removal as policy; it came down because the 1989 earthquake damaged it beyond repair, and only then did the city discover it could absorb the loss. Boston's "removal" was the Big Dig—one of the most expensive infrastructure projects in American history, which did not eliminate the highway's capacity at all but buried it in a tunnel. That is not evidence that removal is cheap and liberating; it is evidence that when regional demand is real, cities pay enormous sums to preserve capacity rather than lose it.
[C1.4] There's also an equity cost PRO ignores. [C1.5] The presumption should track the on-the-ground reality: most freeways carry indispensable trips. Widening, managing, or safety-retrofitting is therefore the more responsible default, and removal should remain the careful exception, not the more frequent choice.
