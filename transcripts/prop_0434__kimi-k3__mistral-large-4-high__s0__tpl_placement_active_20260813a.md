[P1.1] Picture a family living beneath a neighbor who drums at 2 a.m. They complain; the landlord asks for proof. They call the council; the officer visits at noon, hears nothing, closes the file. Repeat for two years.
[P1.2] That is the standard lifecycle of a noise complaint, and it fails for one structural reason: the victim cannot prove anything.
[P1.3] Noise is the perfect offense for bad actors — invisible, intermittent, gone before any authority arrives. The entire burden rests on a diary of "it was loud, honestly," evidence no adjudicator can weigh. The noisy party has to prove nothing. That asymmetry is the real dignity violation in the status quo.
[P1.4] Certified monitoring fixes the hole, and the word "certified" does the heavy lifting. We already trust calibrated, tamper-evident instruments everywhere: breathalyzers decide prosecutions, smart meters decide bills, speed cameras decide fines. A certified noise monitor is the same idea — a calibrated device logging decibel levels against the clock, with a chain of custody a tribunal can actually test. Not a neighbor's edited iPhone clip; a standard.
[P1.5] Three gains follow. First, access to justice: renters, shift workers, and parents of newborns currently choose between suffering silently and funding a legal war; objective logs make complaints cheap to bring and cheap to resolve. Second, deterrence: behavior changes when it becomes measurable — the venue that knows its levels are logged turns the music down before the second warning.
[P1.6] Third, fairness to the accused: objective evidence exonerates as readily as it convicts. The thin-walled flat blamed for next door's subwoofer finally has a defense.
[P1.7] The obvious objection is privacy. But decibel logging records how loud, not what was said — no speech content, no camera, no listening in. The motion asks only that certified evidence count for more than it currently does. Against a status quo where the honest party is structurally helpless, that is the least a dispute system owes people.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes instrumentation for justice. It asks us to trust that a decibel log, privately purchased and privately operated, is equivalent to a breathalyzer administered by police. It is not. A neighbor with a grudge can press a monitor against the shared wall to capture vibration, generate readings by slamming their own doors, or submit data with no chain of custody.
[C1.2] Certification of the hardware does not certify the operator, the placement, or the context. We would be replacing impartial adjudication with a surveillance arms race.
[C1.3] Worse, the motion reduces nuisance to a number. Nuisance law asks whether interference is unreasonable—whether the sound is a crying baby at 3 p.m. or a drum kit at 3 a.m., a renovation or a midnight party. A certified log records volume, not reasonableness. It captures the decibel spike of a toddler’s tantrum identically to a subwoofer rave, stripping away the temporal and qualitative judgment that distinguishes tolerable life from genuine harassment.
[C1.4] By elevating raw data over contextual wisdom, we hand the gavel to an algorithm.
[C1.5] Then there is the social architecture. The proposal invites every aggrieved neighbor to become a private investigator, logging their neighbor’s presence through ambient noise. Apartment living already strains under thin walls; it does not need thin trust. Once monitoring becomes the expected standard of proof, the neighbor without a monitor becomes the neighbor with something to hide.
[C1.6] We trade a false complaint for a permanent culture of mutual surveillance, where the hallway becomes a courtroom and every door slam is potential evidence.
[C1.7] The motion promises access and deterrence, but deterrence works only when the observer is neutral. A weaponized decibel meter in the hands of the angriest resident deters quiet living, not noisy living. We already have noise ordinances, mediation services, and landlords for a reason. Expanding private evidentiary power does not close the justice gap; it moves the fight from the council office to the shared wall—permanently, and at the cost of community.
