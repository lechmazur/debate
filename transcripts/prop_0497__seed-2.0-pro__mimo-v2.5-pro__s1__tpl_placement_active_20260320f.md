[P1.1] We already agree on the principle. Every liberal democracy for over 150 years has required legal deposit of books, newspapers, films and recorded music into national archives. No serious person argues this is tyrannical overreach. No serious person argues we should have let pulpers destroy every copy of a novel once it went out of print.
[P1.2] This proposition does nothing except extend that exact same existing obligation to the dominant cultural form of our era.
[P1.3] Right now, we are living through the largest cultural erasure in human history. Most 1990s console games, early net art, workplace productivity tools, public service apps, and even the first generation of social media software are already irrecoverably lost. Not destroyed by war or flood. Erased, deliberately and quietly, when servers were shut down, support ended, and no one was required to keep a copy.
[P1.4] When future historians try to understand how we worked, played, organised, grieved and governed, software will be the first place they look. Right now there will be almost nothing there for them.
[P1.5] This is not copyright seizure. Deposited software will be subject to exactly the same access restrictions as every other archive holding. Most material will remain closed to the public for decades, just as unpublished papers and draft films are. No one is proposing that archives host pirated copies for download tomorrow.
[P1.6] We did not grant publishers the right to burn every last copy of a book when it stopped making money. There is no legitimate reason to grant them that right for the cultural objects that matter most today.
