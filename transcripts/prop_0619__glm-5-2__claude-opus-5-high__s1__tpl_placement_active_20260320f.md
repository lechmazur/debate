[P1.1] Coordinated harassment campaigns don't stay online. That's the entire point. A swarm of thousands targeting one person—amplifying their home address, their employer's contact information, their family details—produces offline consequences that are predictable, severe, and increasingly routine. Journalists driven into hiding.
[P1.2] Election workers facing death threats. Ordinary people losing jobs after targeted pile-ons. These campaigns are organized on platforms, amplified by platform algorithms, and executed through platform infrastructure. Platforms are not bystanders—they are the operating system.
[P1.3] The question is whether a legal duty to mitigate foreseeable offline harms is justified. It is, for three reasons.
[P1.4] First, platforms have unique visibility. They see coordination forming before anyone else can—the spike in mentions, the sock-puppet accounts, the brigading patterns. Law enforcement typically learns about these campaigns from victims after harm has occurred. Platforms know in real time because the abuse is happening on their servers.
[P1.5] A legal duty leverages that information advantage toward prevention rather than after-the-fact apology tours.
[P1.6] Second, the economic incentives point the wrong way. Engagement metrics reward outrage and pile-ons because conflict drives attention. Without external legal pressure, the rational business decision is to tolerate harassment until it becomes a PR crisis. Self-regulation has had years and produced a familiar cycle: scandal, followed by promised reform, followed by quiet regression.
[P1.7] Third, the legal standard is deliberately modest. "Foreseeable" means what any reasonable observer would recognize as likely to cause offline harm—direct threats, doxxing, swatting coordination. "Mitigate" means reasonable steps, not perfection. We already impose duties on landlords, employers, and manufacturers to address foreseeable harms arising from their systems.
[P1.8] Social media platforms built the infrastructure; duties follow infrastructure.
[P1.9] The opposition will predictably invoke free speech. But a duty to mitigate coordinated harassment aimed at producing real-world harm protects speech—the speech of targets forced into silence. The motion doesn't ask platforms to police opinion. It asks them to take reasonable steps against foreseeable, organized offline harm.
[P1.10] That is both possible and overdue.
