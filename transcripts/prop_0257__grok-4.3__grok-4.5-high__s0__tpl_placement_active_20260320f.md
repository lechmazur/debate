[P1.1] Consumers pay the same price for a digital novel or game as for its physical counterpart, yet receive no enduring title. A revocable license means the seller can erase access at any moment through policy change, account suspension, or service closure, converting a completed sale into an ongoing rental whose terms the platform alone rewrites. This asymmetry is not technical necessity; it is a deliberate choice that strips buyers of the core incidents of ownership—resale, lending, preservation after the seller exits, and inheritance by heirs.
[P1.2] When a platform shutters, the licensed copy disappears while a purchased DVD or paperback remains on the shelf. Families lose libraries built over decades, and creators lose the secondary markets that once circulated their work without further payment to intermediaries. The license model therefore extracts full retail price while retaining perpetual power to nullify the product, a bargain no rational buyer would accept if the terms were stated plainly at the point of sale.
[P1.3] Meaningful ownership restores the normal expectations of property: the buyer controls the copy, can alienate it, and is not subject to unilateral deletion. Platforms can still sell convenience services—cloud saves, cross-device streaming—without forcing every transaction into a license that extinguishes these rights. The alternative collapses the distinction between sale and subscription and leaves consumers with nothing more durable than the continued goodwill of the vendor.

A file is not a paper book or disc: once sold with full resale and offline permanence, it can be duplicated forever at zero cost. That destroys scarcity, gutting the revenue streams that finance production, server infrastructure, patches, and catalog expansion. Platforms therefore sell licenses, not title, so they can police copies, enforce terms, and keep the ecosystem running.
[C1.2] PRO frets that buyers pay retail for something revocable. In reality, digital prices routinely undercut physical precisely because licenses eliminate manufacturing, inventory, shipping, and returns. That bargain also delivers instant delivery, multi-device access, cloud saves, automatic updates, and refunds—features impossible if the seller surrendered post-sale control. When a store shuts, access can vanish; physical formats die too, leaving obsolete hardware and decaying media.
[C1.3] Markets respond with reissues and ports. The license model is not a secret rental; it is the visible contract that funds abundance.
[C1.4] Granting “meaningful ownership” would force higher prices to offset secondary-market free-riding and piracy leakage, or else rights-holders would simply withhold titles. Family sharing and controlled lending already exist inside account systems; unrestricted alienability collapses those systems and invites black-market circulation of perfect copies. Consumers already choose access models—subscription libraries, cloud libraries, always-online titles—because convenience and variety outweigh romantic permanence. Ownership language imports nineteenth-century property rules into a medium where they create infinite free supply and zero incentive to invent more.
[C1.5] Licenses are the rational bargain that keeps shelves full.

[P2.1] The claim that infinite duplication forces revocable licenses confuses technical control with ownership itself. Sellers already use DRM, account binding, and watermarking to limit copying; those mechanisms function whether the buyer holds a license or a property interest. Revocability is not required to prevent piracy—it is added to let platforms erase access after the sale closes. That extra power converts every transaction into a conditional grant whose continuation depends on the vendor’s ongoing approval.
[P2.2] Lower prices do not compensate for the missing incidents of ownership. A consumer who pays seventy percent of a physical edition’s cost still expects to keep the copy once paid for, resell it, lend it, or pass it to heirs. The discount reflects avoided manufacturing and distribution costs, not a voluntary surrender of title. When the platform later withdraws the file, the buyer loses the very asset whose reduced price was supposed to make the deal attractive.
[P2.3] The further assertion that consumers willingly trade permanence for convenience ignores the terms actually presented at purchase. Most storefronts label the transaction a “buy” or “purchase,” then embed the license revocation clause in unread end-user agreements. Families who discover years later that an inherited library has vanished discover the mismatch only after the fact. Physical formats can also become obsolete, yet the object itself remains under the owner’s control; a digital license vanishes entirely when the server stops responding.
[P2.4] Allowing meaningful ownership would require platforms to separate convenience services—streaming, cloud saves—from the core transfer of the file. That separation preserves revenue from optional subscriptions while restoring the buyer’s ability to treat the copy as an asset rather than a rented privilege. The alternative leaves the consumer paying retail for a product whose continued existence is subject to unilateral cancellation.

[C2.1] PRO’s core claim—that DRM alone can secure “ownership” while stripping revocability—simply relocates the collapse. Ownership rights mean the buyer can resell, lend, or bequeath a perfect, zero-cost copy. Once that chain begins, secondary markets become infinite free supply: one purchased file multiplies across strangers, heirs, and private networks that watermarking cannot monitor at scale. Platforms cannot police every offline handoff the way they police a license.
[C2.2] That free-riding, not abstract revocability, is what evaporates the revenue that pays for production, servers, and patches.
[C2.3] The price gap does not buy silence about missing title; it buys the control that keeps prices low. Without post-sale power, creators face free perfect substitutes and must either charge scarcity premiums or withhold titles entirely. Instant multi-device access, automatic updates, and easy refunds exist only because the license remains live. Hand those rights to buyers as alienable property and the entire bundle becomes unviable.
[C2.4] Calling the storefront “buy” does not magically manufacture durable property in a medium that defies the old rules. Consumers still click because the model delivers variety and convenience no dusty disc can match; they do not click for a theoretical library their grandchildren will never open. Physical media may sit in a drawer after the player dies, but that drawer neither streams nor expands the catalog.
[C2.5] Separating “convenience services” from file transfer is theater. The ownership package itself erases scarcity; once copies roam freely, optional streaming loses its economic base and the shelf empties. Licenses are the mechanism that keeps digital abundance real rather than rhetorical.
