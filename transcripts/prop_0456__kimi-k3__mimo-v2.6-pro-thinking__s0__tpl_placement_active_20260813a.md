[P1.1] Training a frontier model means ingesting millions of books, songs, and images. With that scale, there are only three realistic worlds. First: training is free, and creators subsidize the technology that competes with them. Second: every use requires individual permission, and the transaction costs are so absurd that only a handful of giants cutting closed-door deals can ever build models — everyone else is locked out or litigated into the ground.
[P1.2] Third: the world this motion proposes — train freely, but pay into a compulsory license that compensates the people whose work makes the training possible. Only the third world is both workable and just.
[P1.3] This is not a novel invention. American copyright law has run exactly this play before. The mechanical license for music let anyone record a cover song without asking, provided they paid a statutory royalty — that compromise built the recording industry instead of strangling it. Blanket licensing through performing rights organizations let radio and venues use entire catalogs without negotiating song by song.
[P1.4] Whenever technology makes individual bargaining impossible but the copying still has value, the law's answer has been the same: compulsory license, collective payment, no veto.
[P1.5] The "only if" clause is the heart of the motion, and it cuts both ways. Permissionless training alone externalizes the entire cost onto writers, musicians, and artists, draining the incentive to produce the very works future models need. Pure permission alone hands a permanent moat to the richest labs and buries everyone else in rights-clearance hell. Compulsory licensing threads it: innovation proceeds at the speed of machines, money flows back at the scale of the use.
[P1.6] Expect the other side to romanticize consent. But consent that can never practically be obtained is not a right — it is a blockade. The honest question is not whether creators deserve compensation when their life's work trains a commercial system. They plainly do.
[P1.7] The question is whether we build the one mechanism proven to deliver that compensation at scale, or pretend a fantasy of billion-fold negotiation will do it instead.

[C1.1] The motion's core move is to treat training like a cover song: a non-substitutive use worth a per-use fee. But a cover never becomes the original singer's competitor. A model trained on a novelist's backlist writes in her voice, undercuts her next contract, and can be prompted to imitate her on demand. The distinctive harm of generative training is dispossession of the creator's own future market, and the distinctive right that prevents it is the right to say no. A compulsory license doesn't price that right — it abolishes it and substitutes a rate schedule.
[C1.2] Some refusals are the point: the musician who won't license voice-cloning, the illustrator who won't feed a system built to replace illustrators. "Pay a fee, skip consent" converts a principled veto into a line item.
[C1.3] Worse, the motion routes that fee to the wrong people. Publishers, labels, and studios are assignees and intermediaries, not the authors, players, and artists whose work actually does the training. Statutory pools pay whoever holds paper at distribution time and split by market share — so the largest catalogs swallow the pool while midlist novelists, session players, and freelance illustrators get token amounts or nothing. That is the lived history of statutory music royalties: administrative rates that freeze for decades and out-of-print or orphan works generating checks for no one.
[C1.4] The motion promises creators compensation and delivers a rounding error to their former bosses.
[C1.5] It is also regressive at the market level. The incumbents named in the motion hold catalog-wide rights and can train on everything they control while paying a discounted statutory fee. Independents and creators who never assigned AI rights are swept into the grant anyway. The scheme strips consent from the weakest competitors and sells the strongest a bulk rate.
[C1.6] Finally, the dilemma is false. Voluntary blanket deals and extended collective licensing already clear catalogs at scale without compulsion; opt-out defaults and direct licensing are gentler tools where a market is genuinely missing. Compulsion is justified only when nothing milder works. Milder tools exist.
