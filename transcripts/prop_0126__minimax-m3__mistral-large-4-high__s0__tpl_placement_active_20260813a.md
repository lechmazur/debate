[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a pricing problem for a funding problem. Prisons are not telecommunications utilities; they are institutions of confinement where every communication channel carries security costs that someone must pay. Declaring calls "free" ignores basic arithmetic: taxpayers still fund the infrastructure, the monitoring, the bandwidth, and the staffing. What the PRO side calls eliminating vendor fees is actually creating an unfunded mandate—an open-ended subsidy with no usage caps, no infrastructure budget, and no mechanism to prevent system abuse.
[C1.2] The recidivism argument cuts both ways. Yes, family contact correlates with better outcomes, but the evidence suggests connection quality matters more than unlimited quantity. A regulated vendor model with strict rate caps—already implemented in multiple jurisdictions—preserves meaningful contact without bankrupting the corrections budget. When departments face finite resources, "free" calls don't displace vendor profits; they displace drug treatment programs, educational programming, and mental health services.
[C1.3] Every dollar diverted to unlimited video minutes is a dollar not spent on the rehabilitation that actually reduces reoffending.
[C1.4] Security risks are concrete, not hypothetical. Video visits, especially when uncapped, become conduits for contraband coordination, gang communication, and witness intimidation. Each minute of "free" video time requires supervision from already overstretched staff, diverting attention from unit security and rehabilitative programming. The vendor model at least internalizes infrastructure and monitoring costs; the "free" alternative socializes them across the public while offering no limiting principle.
[C1.5] The real choice isn't between families paying and families not paying. It's between a regulated market that funds necessary oversight and a blank check drawn on public safety budgets. The proposition asks us to treat communication as a welfare entitlement rather than a privilege within a punitive institution. That misallocates scarce resources away from victims, officers, and the genuine rehabilitation the system exists to provide.
