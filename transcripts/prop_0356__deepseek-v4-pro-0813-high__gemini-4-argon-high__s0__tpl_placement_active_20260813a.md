[P1.1] A driver who refuses a tracking device should not pay more for legally required car insurance. This proposition is not anti-telematics; it draws a narrow line against continuous in-car behavior surveillance unless the opt-in is genuinely fair. That line matters because compulsory insurance turns “consent” into coercion.
[P1.2] In most systems, motorists must carry coverage to drive to work, take children to school, or reach medical care. So the real choice is not between surveillance and no surveillance; it is between being watched and being priced out. When insurers can make continuous monitoring the default—or attach large discounts to it—privacy becomes a luxury. A night-shift worker cannot meaningfully refuse a device that records her routes, speed, braking, and possibly phone use when refusing means paying substantially more.
[P1.3] That is not opt-in; it is economic pressure wearing a consent form.
[P1.4] Fair terms require genuine alternatives, no penalty for refusal, simple revocation, and transparent scoring. Most telematics programs fail that test. The behavioral score is often proprietary, drivers cannot easily inspect or challenge it, collected data may be shared or repurposed, and opting out can trigger a higher premium. Once insurers treat non-monitored drivers as higher risk or less profitable, the supposedly voluntary program becomes a de facto requirement.
[P1.5] Safe drivers join, insurers recalibrate, and privacy-refusing drivers are pushed into a shrinking, more expensive pool. That race to the bottom destroys meaningful consent.
[P1.6] The harm is not only financial. Continuous monitoring chills lawful activity: a driver may skip a late-night medical trip, avoid certain areas, or hesitate to brake hard in an emergency because the algorithm is watching. Insurance should spread risk across drivers, not turn every journey into a private performance review.
[P1.7] The default must therefore be no continuous behavior surveillance pricing. Insurers may offer it only after a genuinely fair opt-in—not buried in renewal forms, not framed as a discount from an inflated base, and not tied to data sharing beyond the policy. A mandatory product should not carry an optional panopticon.
