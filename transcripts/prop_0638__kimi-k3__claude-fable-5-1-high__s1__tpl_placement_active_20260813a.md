[P1.1] Fundamental science is the search for things nobody yet knows how to find. You cannot hold a researcher to milestones on a path that does not exist; you can only judge, over a long horizon, whether excellent work happened. So the real question is which funding model produces more discovery per decade. The short-cycle competitive grant fails that test three times over.
[P1.2] First, it selects against the very science it exists to fund. A panel ranking three-year proposals funds what is legible: extensions of known results with predictable outputs. Transformative work reads as speculative and dies in consensus review. Katalin Karikó spent years demoted and defunded because her mRNA research looked like a dead end; it later underpinned the COVID vaccines.
[P1.3] A system that starves its future breakthroughs to guarantee short-cycle deliverables is not accountable — it is myopic.
[P1.4] Second, the treadmill tax. Senior researchers commonly report spending a third or more of their time writing, reviewing, and reapplying for grants; labs live in perpetual boom-bust. Each of those hours is scientific talent converted into paperwork. Block grants return that capacity wholesale.
[P1.5] Third, the accountability objection measures the wrong thing. Per-dollar milestone reporting tells you a scientist filed their forms, not that the field advanced. Block funding does not mean no accountability; it means accountability matched to the timescale of the work — periodic deep institutional review, the way the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology or the Max Planck Institutes have long operated, with records of outsized discovery to show for it.
[P1.6] Ask what stops waste and the answer is the same retrospective judgment that already decides tenure, prizes, and reputation — applied to institutions every seven to ten years rather than to projects every three. Fund people and places for the long term, judge them at the long term, and you get Karikó instead of another thousand incremental follow-ons.

[C1.1] The motion asks you to trade the one mechanism that lets money follow ideas for one that lets money follow buildings. That is the choice, and it should be refused.
[C1.2] Start with Karikó, because she is my example, not PRO's. She was not killed by a grant panel in the abstract; she was demoted by her own institution, which had ample resources and chose to spend them on people its leadership favored. That is what a block grant is: a department chair or institute director deciding who inside the walls deserves the money. Institutional hierarchies protect incumbents, and the outsider with a strange idea loses to the colleague who plays golf with the dean.
[C1.3] Competitive project grants are the only door that does not require permission from the people already in the building.
[C1.4] Second, block funding freezes the map of science at the moment the grants are written. CRISPR did not emerge from a CRISPR institute; the microbiome, machine learning, and gravitational-wave astronomy all pulled money toward them because competitive systems can move funds to a new field in a single cycle. Under block grants, the 1985 organizational chart is still eating the 2025 budget, and every reallocation becomes a political war with a rector.
[C1.5] Third, "less accountability" is not paperwork. It means dead labs stay funded. PRO invokes Max Planck and the LMB, but these are hand-picked elite exceptions with fixed-term directors and brutal periodic review—the Max Planck model works because it is small and selective. Extend block grants system-wide and you fund the median department at the level of the exceptional one, forever.
[C1.6] Finally, PRO's real complaint is cycle length, not competition. Five- to seven-year investigator awards, renewal based on track record, and lotteries for near-tie proposals fix the treadmill tax while keeping the door open. You do not need to sacrifice accountability to buy stability; PRO's motion demands you give up both the outsider's chance and the taxpayer's visibility to get something competitive systems already can deliver.

[C2.5] On the frozen map [P2.3]: a director shifts teams within the institute's mandate. [C2.7] PRO's accountability is nominal.
[C2.8] Lotteries [P2.5] are not an obituary for competition; they are competition with an honest tiebreak, still external and applicant-initiated.
