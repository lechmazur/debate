[P1.1] Conventional agriculture’s high yield is an accounting illusion. Every year, governments pour massive subsidies into synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and monocrop inputs. This artificially lowers the price of production today by liquidating the natural capital—topsoil, aquifers, and pollinators—that tomorrow’s harvests require. Demanding decades of conclusive yield data before giving regenerative agriculture equal support is not fiscal caution.
[P1.2] It is a decision to keep subsidizing a known ecological collapse while holding the solution hostage to an impossible standard.
[P1.3] Farmers do not cling to chemical-intensive monoculture because they want to degrade their land; they are trapped by a rigged market. Transitioning to regenerative practices—like cover cropping, no-till drilling, and diverse crop rotations—requires upfront equipment investments and a three-to-five-year biological rebuilding period where yields often temporarily dip. When the state heavily subsidizes synthetic inputs, it makes short-term soil extraction artificially safe and long-term stewardship financially perilous. Matching those subsidies at scale builds a financial bridge across that transition valley, allowing farmers to rebuild organic matter without facing bankruptcy.
[P1.4] Furthermore, waiting for conclusive long-term data is a self-defeating Catch-22. Farming is hyper-local. You cannot gather conclusive, multi-decade yield data across diverse climates and commercial scales until millions of acres actually make the transition—and those acres will never transition while government policy pays farmers to stick with chemicals. Scale must precede certainty.
[P1.5] Finally, weigh the asymmetric risks. The worst-case scenario of equal subsidies is that public funds support farms whose peak yields in ideal weather are marginally lower, offset by drastically reduced chemical runoff and lower input costs. The worst-case scenario of waiting is irreversible topsoil exhaustion and systemic crop failures as extreme weather accelerates. Because carbon-rich soil acts like a sponge, regenerative fields consistently endure droughts and floods that devastate conventional neighbors.
[P1.6] In an era of climate volatility, resilience is the ultimate yield. Governments must level the subsidy playing field now, while we still have topsoil left to save.
