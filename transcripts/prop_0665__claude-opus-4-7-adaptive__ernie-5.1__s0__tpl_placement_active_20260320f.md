[P1.1] Mitochondrial disease is one of the cruelest categories of illness in medicine. A child born with severe mitochondrial DNA disease can suffer seizures, blindness, muscle failure, organ failure, and death in infancy or early childhood. There is no cure. Treatment is palliative.
[P1.2] Parents who carry these mutations face a brutal lottery every pregnancy, and adoption or donor eggs require giving up genetic parenthood entirely.
[P1.3] Mitochondrial replacement therapy — pronuclear transfer or maternal spindle transfer — offers something almost unique in medicine: it doesn't manage the disease, it prevents the disease from existing in that child at all. The nuclear DNA of both parents is preserved; only the faulty mitochondria, which carry just 37 genes governing cellular energy production, are replaced. This is not "designer babies." Nobody is selecting eye colour or intelligence.
[P1.4] We are swapping a broken battery.
[P1.5] Public funding is the right mechanism for three reasons.
[P1.6] First, cost-effectiveness. Preventing even a handful of cases per year pays for the programme. This is exactly the kind of high-upfront, high-return intervention public systems exist to deliver.
[P1.7] Second, equity. If MRT is available only privately, wealthy carriers escape the disease and poor carriers do not. Their children pay the price for that gap in seizures and funerals. A public health system that treats cystic fibrosis but refuses to prevent Leigh syndrome when it can is making an indefensible distinction.
[P1.8] Third, the regulated public route is the safe route. Driving families to unregulated clinics abroad — which is what happens when public systems abstain — is worse on every axis: worse oversight, worse follow-up, worse data.
[P1.9] The proposition is narrow: severe inherited mitochondrial disease, a defined population, a targeted technique. The alternative is to keep watching preventable children die to preserve a squeamishness the science no longer warrants.
