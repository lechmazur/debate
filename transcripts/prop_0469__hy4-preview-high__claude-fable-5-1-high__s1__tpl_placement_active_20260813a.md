[P1.1] Ecosystems are not scenery; they are the life-support systems every human society depends on. When damage to them becomes widespread, long-term, and severe, it is not a regulatory mishap—it is a crime against the conditions of survival. Countries should recognize ecocide as an international crime because the current order lets the worst environmental destruction be priced as a cost of doing business.
[P1.2] The standard should be strict, not vague: intentional or reckless destruction that is widespread, long-term, and severe to the natural environment. That threshold is no less administrable than the wartime environmental standard already familiar in international law, which asks whether damage is clearly excessive. Today, though, that protection is largely confined to armed conflict and is weighed against military advantage. Peacetime mass destruction—clearing a rainforest until a basin loses its rainfall, poisoning a river delta so fields and fisheries die, strip-mining a watershed into permanent toxicity—escapes individual criminal liability.
[P1.3] It becomes a permitting dispute, a fine, a deferred cleanup.
[P1.4] That gap is decisive. Civil regulation and national environmental agencies cannot do the job, because ecosystems cross borders while regulators do not, and because captured or under-resourced states often host the very extraction that destroys the environment. A minister who authorizes a project that ruins a water supply, or a board that approves dumping to protect margins, should not answer only with a corporate penalty. Individual criminal responsibility changes the calculus: it stigmatizes, deters, and reaches the people who decide.
[P1.5] This is not anti-development. It is anti-expropriation of the future. The communities sacrificed first are usually poor, rural, and politically weak; their land and water are converted into export profit while the ecological collapse remains. International recognition sets a red line: destroy the basis of life, and you can be prosecuted, not merely taxed.
[P1.6] If law already condemns killing a person, it cannot stay silent when the act destroys the systems that keep a people alive.
