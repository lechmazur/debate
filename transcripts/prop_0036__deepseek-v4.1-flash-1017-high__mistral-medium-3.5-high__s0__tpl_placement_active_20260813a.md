[P1.1] Insurance is not a gym membership, and a smartwatch is not a medical record. The proposition draws the right line: insurers may not turn consumer wearable telemetry into a price tag or a coverage verdict unless the customer affirmatively opts in under limits that keep the bargain real.
[P1.2] The core problem is coercion disguised as choice. People do not buy health or life insurance the way they buy a streaming service; it is often tied to a mortgage, a job, or a family’s security. If a discount, a policy, or a claims decision hinges on sharing step counts, heart-rate traces, and sleep patterns, “opt in” becomes “pay more or surrender your data.” A default prohibition prevents that trap. Strict limits then ensure any opt-in is narrow: specific data, specific purpose, specific time, no secondary use, no penalty for refusal, and no use in denying claims.
[P1.3] Second, wearable data is not trustworthy enough for underwriting. A wrist device can mistake a bumpy bus ride for exercise, miss a cardiac event, or reflect a disabled person’s limited mobility. It captures behavior, not medical diagnosis. Pricing on noisy proxies invites error and discrimination, and coverage decisions based on a missed step goal are plainly unjust.
[P1.4] Insurers already have claims history and medical evidence; they should not substitute gadget surveillance.
[P1.5] Third, the sensitive inferences are exactly where the harm lies. Sleep, heart rate, location, and activity can reveal pregnancy, illness, mental-health struggles, or poverty. Once that data enters underwriting, the customer cannot see or contest the model. The proposition does not ban voluntary wellness programs; it makes them prove consent is genuine and bounded.
[P1.6] The question is who bears the risk of ambiguous data. It should not be the person whose premium rises because their watch was charging.
