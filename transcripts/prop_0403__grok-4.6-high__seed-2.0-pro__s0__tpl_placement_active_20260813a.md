[P1.1] When a city writes a check for a stadium, it is not buying souvenirs. It is buying a stake. A team that takes major public subsidies should not pocket that investment and leave as if the public were a disposable landlord.
[P1.2] Public money changes the deal. Stadium subsidies routinely reach hundreds of millions, drawn from taxes, bonds, and opportunity costs: schools delayed, roads neglected, neighborhoods underserved. Cities are sold permanence and civic identity. If the owner can later relocate on a private timetable, the public has financed an asset with no residual claim.
[P1.3] That is not a market. It is a one-way transfer.
[P1.4] The pattern keeps repeating. Oakland, St. Louis, San Diego, and Seattle watched franchises leverage or abandon publicly supported facilities when a richer offer appeared. Taxpayers were left with debt service, empty venues, and no voice at the exit. Workers around the stadium lost jobs.
[P1.5] Civic identity was treated as a bargaining chip. Unrestricted departure after subsidy rewards hostage-taking: threaten to leave, extract another deal, or dump the bill on the city that already paid.
[P1.6] A binding community approval process does not ban relocation. It requires the people who paid to consent before the team walks, by referendum or another legally binding local mechanism. Existing lease clauses have proven too easy to game or outlast. A public vote or equivalent binds the exit, not just the ribbon-cutting.
[P1.7] Owners can still argue a failing market or genuine hardship. They cannot treat the subsidy as a no-strings gift.
[P1.8] If an owner wants total freedom to move, do not take the public's money. If they take it, the community that financed the franchise gets a binding say before it is stripped away. Reciprocity is the point. Public risk without public consent at the exit is not a partnership.
[P1.9] It is extraction.
