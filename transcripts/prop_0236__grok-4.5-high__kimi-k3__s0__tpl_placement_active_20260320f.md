[P1.1] Schools that ban generative AI by default are fighting a tool students already own and will keep using after graduation. The educational duty is to make that use disciplined, visible, and skill-building. Treat generative AI as permissible by default; require citation of when and how it was used, plus process transparency—prompts, revisions, what was accepted or rejected. That policy beats broad bans on every metric that actually matters: learning, fairness, and integrity.
[P1.2] Bans are unenforceable theater. Detection tools misfire, students route around them, and the honest ones self-limit while others hide the help. The result is inequity and distorted assessment. Transparency flips the incentive: students document the AI step, then show their human judgment layered on top.
[P1.3] The grade can reward that judgment—the critique, the selection, the rewrite—rather than a polished surface that may or may not be theirs.
[P1.4] This is how schools already handled calculators and search engines. We taught when to use them and how to check the output. AI is no different; it is simply faster and more generative. Forcing citation and process logs builds the exact literacy employers and universities already expect: knowing AI’s strengths, its hallucinations, its bias, and the difference between prompting and thinking.
[P1.5] Ban the tool and you ban the practice.
[P1.6] Integrity improves under disclosure. Fabricated sources and wholesale paste-ins are harder to hide when the workflow is on the table. Instructors can still forbid AI for pure skill checks, but that prohibition must be narrow and purposeful, not the default. Default bans waste enforcement time, erode trust, and leave graduates unprepared.
[P1.7] The path forward is permission with accountability. Schools win when they teach students to own their process with AI instead of pretending they can stop them from using it.
