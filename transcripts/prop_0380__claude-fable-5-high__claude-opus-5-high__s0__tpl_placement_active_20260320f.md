[P1.1] Democracies do not demand wisdom before granting the vote. We do not test adults on policy, screen out the impulsive, or disenfranchise the uninformed. The franchise rests on a simpler principle: those bound by the laws should have a voice in making them. Sixteen-year-olds work, pay taxes on their wages, drive in many countries, can be tried in adult courts in some, and will live longest with today's decisions on climate, debt, and pensions.
[P1.2] Excluding them requires a special justification—and none survives scrutiny.
[P1.3] Start with competence. Voting is what psychologists call a "cold cognition" task: deliberate, unhurried, done with time to reflect. The developmental research consistently distinguishes this from "hot" split-second judgment, and on cold reasoning, sixteen-year-olds perform comparably to adults. Where the vote has actually been extended—Austria nationally, Scotland and Wales for devolved elections, parts of Latin America—the sky did not fall.
[P1.4] Austrian sixteen-year-olds turned out at healthy rates and cast votes that tracked their stated preferences as coherently as older cohorts did.
[P1.5] Second, timing matters enormously for democratic health. Voting is habit-forming: cast your first ballot and you are far likelier to keep voting for life. At sixteen, most people live at home, attend school, and sit inside civic institutions that can support a first vote. At eighteen, many are mid-move—new city, new job, university—precisely the churn that suppresses first-time turnout.
[P1.6] We currently schedule the first vote at the worst possible moment.
[P1.7] Third, exclusion distorts policy. Electorates skew old, so governments over-serve the retired and under-serve the young: housing, education, and climate perennially lose to short-term promises. Enfranchising sixteen-year-olds forces politicians to answer to the people inheriting the consequences.
[P1.8] The question for this debate is not whether every sixteen-year-old votes wisely—no age group does. It is whether there is a principled reason to silence citizens with real stakes, adult-level deliberative capacity, and everything to lose. There is not.

[C1.3] Eighteen is not an arbitrary line in a range of numbers; it is the hinge where a whole package of legal adulthood turns over at once. [C1.4] Dependency has a practical edge, too. Sixteen-year-olds live inside two institutions with enormous influence over them: the household and the school. [C1.5] And notice what PRO's best arguments actually are. Habit formation and youth-friendly policy are not claims about justice — they are engineering claims. [C1.6] PRO must show exclusion is unjustified. Dependency justifies it — and their remedy for turnout is available without it.
