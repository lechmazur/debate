[P1.1] Emergency is not the moment a democracy should check its accountability at the door. Broad executive powers—curfews, expanded surveillance, seizure of private capacity, suspension of ordinary procurement—are justified by urgency, yet urgency fades while the legal architecture it built remains. Without automatic sunsets, temporary exception becomes permanent rule.
[P1.2] The core problem is asymmetric retreat. An executive branch asked to confront a genuine crisis has every incentive to retain every tool it has been handed, while legislators who voted to grant those tools face far less pressure to revisit them once the immediate threat has passed. Emergency powers do not self-limit; they compound. A statute that begins as a narrow data-access authority quietly becomes the template for the next crisis, and the next.
[P1.3] The political cost of leaving a dormant power on the books is near zero, while the cost of appearing weak in a crisis is enormous. Sunset hearings and renewal votes interrupt that ratchet. They force a recurring question: does this specific power still match this specific threat?
[P1.4] The evidence is in the pattern. Authorities rushed through after the September 11 attacks included provisions that outlived the immediate emergency by years. Pandemic-era restrictions and procurement shortcuts similarly persisted long after hospitalization curves flattened. In each case, the powers themselves may have been defensible at the outset.
[P1.5] The failure was structural: no automatic moment compelled a democratic re-decision.
[P1.6] Skeptics claim sunsets will leave leaders empty-handed during a true catastrophe. But renewal votes do not strip a government of capacity—they require it to justify continuation to the very body that represents the governed. If the emergency is real, the legislature can act quickly. If it is manufactured or fading, the burden should rest on the executive to prove it, not on citizens to discover it.
[P1.7] Democracy’s promise is that power is temporary and conditional. Sunsets make that promise operational.

[P4.2] That is better accountability than CON’s default, where broad powers continue because inertia and scheduling favor the executive. If legislators block renewal despite a real threat, they must answer on the record; if the executive cannot prove necessity, the burden stays where it belongs.
[P4.3] A2: [C3.2] assumes bundling is unavoidable. Under the proposition, each broad grant requires separate renewal, and the statute can prohibit omnibus packages. [P4.4] An executive who bundles is not exploiting sunsets; that executive is exploiting a legislature willing to abandon single-subject discipline. PRO’s design creates the clarity that forces each power to justify itself.

[P5.1] The debate reduces to what should happen when politics fails. CON says a delayed hearing or unsuccessful repeal leaves lawful operations intact; PRO says silence must not leave broad emergency power intact. That difference matters. Under CON, the ordinary result of legislative silence is that temporary authority quietly becomes permanent: reports are filed, courts defer, committees meet, and the ratchet remains.
[P5.2] Under PRO, silence cannot renew. After a short certified bridge, the legislature must either grant the power again on the record or explain why it did not.
[P5.3] CON treats overreach as reviewable in principle, but the lesson of post-9/11 and pandemic powers is that review without a deadline waits for a political moment that never arrives. A court can strike one unlawful use while the legal architecture survives; a repeal can be filed and never scheduled. A sunset moves the burden back to the executive to prove that this specific power still matches this specific threat.
[P5.4] The hazard PRO adds is visible and temporary; the harm CON accepts is invisible and cumulative. Emergency authority should be a renewable grant, not an inheritance. PRO preserves capacity where necessity is shown and forces overreach to answer where it is not.

PRO’s answer is a ritual that may either launder overreach through a whipped renewal or disable necessary authority through inaction. Neither outcome tracks necessity or rights.
[C5.2] PRO calls its hazard “visible and temporary.” But expired authority cannot be revived retroactively, and harm during the gap may be irreversible. A bridge lasting days merely postpones that risk; executive certification of “indispensable” powers restores the very discretion PRO distrusts. Continued authority, by contrast, is not continued impunity: reporting, judicial review, narrowing, and repeal remain available without making emergency operations depend on a punctual floor vote.
[C5.3] Most importantly, PRO never escapes its own political diagnosis. Legislators too timid to repeal can be too timid to reject renewal, except now they can call the result fresh democratic consent. Silence is not the only danger; compelled approval under deadline pressure can entrench power more securely.
[C5.4] A calendar cannot supply institutional courage. It can only choose a default. CON preserves lawful, reviewable capacity while scrutiny continues. PRO risks indispensable capacity for an accountability benefit it cannot guarantee.
