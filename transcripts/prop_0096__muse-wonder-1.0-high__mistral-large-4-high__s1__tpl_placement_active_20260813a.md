[P1.1] An actor's voice is not a costume a studio can keep after shooting wraps. It is recognizably, biometrically them — accent, age, emotion, and cultural identity compressed into sound. AI dubbing does not merely translate dialogue; it manufactures a new performance in that person's voice, saying words they never spoke, in a language they may not speak, with inflections they never chose. A right that personal cannot be signed away by implication.
[P1.2] Broad boilerplate fails the basic test of consent. Performers, especially day players and early-career actors, sign standard contracts on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, often before anyone explains that a brief recording session will become a trainable voice model. Clauses granting rights 'in any medium now known or hereafter devised' were built for distribution — putting a finished performance on a new format — not for generating endless new performances. That confuses owning a recording with owning the person who made it.
[P1.3] The risk is concrete. A cloned voice can deliver rewritten lines after the actor has left set, carry a character into sequels and promotions, or produce a foreign-language version the actor finds embarrassing or politically objectionable. It can outlive the job, undercut their ability to sell their own voice work, and attach their reputation to choices they had no chance to refuse. Once a model exists, broad language leaves almost no practical way to pull it back.
[P1.4] Opt-in does not ban AI dubbing. It asks for what consent should always have been: a separate, specific yes naming the project, use, duration, and pay. Studios offering fair terms will get that yes, and audiences can still get faithful translations. What opt-in stops is only the shortcut — taking a lifelong voice replica because a performer once signed page nine to get a day's work.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false premise: that "broad contract boilerplate" means silence on AI. It doesn't. After the 2023 labor agreements, standard performer contracts now explicitly address AI voice replication—requiring notice, consent, and additional compensation within the existing contractual framework. The choice isn't between consent and boilerplate; it's between negotiating AI terms once, collectively, or forcing individual opt-ins for every dub.
[C1.2] Opt-in transforms routine post-production into a permission minefield. When a film releases in forty countries, requiring separate performer consent for each language track means forty negotiations, forty potential holdouts, and indefinite delays. Consider accessibility: a deaf viewer in São Paulo waits months for a Brazilian Portuguese track while a day player—who already granted voice rights for the performance—must be re-located and re-paid for a dub they never performed. A documentary about a deceased civil rights leader cannot use archival audio without hunting down heirs for each foreign release.
[C1.3] That isn't protection; it's paralysis.
[C1.4] The deeper error confuses owning a recording with owning the person. Studios don't "own" the actor; they own the performance captured under contract. Dubbing translates that performance into another language—a derivative use within the original grant. If an actor signs away rights to their performance "in all media," that logically includes synthetic reproduction of that same performance.
[C1.5] When a studio colorizes a black-and-white film or upscales it to 4K, nobody demands fresh consent. AI dubbing is the audio equivalent: a technical transformation of existing performance, not the creation of a new one. Demanding opt-in treats every translation as a brand-new performance requiring fresh authorship.
[C1.6] The proposition's real target isn't consent—it's leverage. By fragmenting consent into per-use opt-ins, it converts standard distribution into perpetual renegotiation. The solution is better boilerplate: clear AI riders, collective bargaining minimums, and residuals for synthetic use—not a veto that strangles international cinema, accessibility, and archival preservation.
