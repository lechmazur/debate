[P1.1] If your pay depends on audience size, you get to see the audience size. That is not a radical regulation, it is the minimum condition for a valid contract.
[P1.2] Streaming platforms today ask filmmakers and performers to accept audience-based pay while hiding the audience. A show triggers a bonus at a certain threshold, residuals scale with views, backend deals depend on performance - yet the only party with the meter is the platform itself writing the check. No other industry would accept an employer who says trust our secret count. That structure invites underpayment and makes good-faith bargaining impossible.
[P1.3] The second problem is definitions. One service calls two minutes of viewing a view. Another counts household completion at 70 percent. Another uses hours viewed divided by runtime.
[P1.4] Without a standard, a "million views" means nothing, and compensation can be gamed by changing the ruler. Standardized viewing data means agreed metrics - complete views, total hours, reach and retention over a set window - reported the same way across services, auditable and comparable. Box office has always been public. Broadcast had Nielsen.
[P1.5] Music streaming reports play counts to rights holders. You cannot negotiate tomorrow's deal if you never learn how today's work was actually watched.
[P1.6] This is narrow and feasible. The rule does not demand public release or individual user data. It demands that when a contract ties pay to reach, the worker whose pay is at stake gets standardized, aggregated proof of that reach under confidentiality. The data already exists; platforms track it obsessively for recommendations and advertising.
[P1.7] Sharing it costs almost nothing and reveals no trade secret, only whether the work delivered the audience it was supposed to. It protects performers without banning performance pay; it just makes that pay honest.
[P1.8] Without that, audience-based compensation is an illusion.

[C1.2] Three reasons this mandate fails on its own terms.
[C1.3] First, it confuses disclosure with fairness. Standardized viewing numbers would tell a creator how many accounts streamed their work, but not whether the deal was fair. Real compensation disputes turn on backend definitions, expense allocations, and contract interpretation — issues a data mandate doesn't touch. A hit with a bad contract is still a bad contract.
[C1.4] The proposition asks us to regulate the meter when the actual problem is in the contract.

[C1.6] The proposition treats per-title reach as if it were atomized, but viewership is interconnected, and aggregation across a creator's portfolio leaks the underlying business.
[C1.7] Third, the rule is overbroad. It sweeps in every agreement where reach is even partly relevant, including deals that already carry negotiated audit rights. It forces small platforms and indie producers into a one-size-fits-all compliance regime, raising costs and pricing out experimental work. A "narrow" rule that touches every audience-based contract is not narrow.

Box office is public because it is a single transaction at a point of sale, and it tells you almost nothing about whether any individual creator was paid correctly.
