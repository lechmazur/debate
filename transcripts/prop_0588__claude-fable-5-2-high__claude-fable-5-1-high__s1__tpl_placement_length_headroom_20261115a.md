[P1.1] The motion turns on two words: "near-term." On that clock, methane wins on physics, on price, and on speed.
[P1.2] Start with physics. Methane traps roughly eighty times more heat than carbon dioxide over its first twenty years, and largely breaks down within about a dozen years. That means a tonne of methane kept in the pipe this year shows up as avoided warming within this decade. Carbon dioxide captured this year barely moves the thermometer before mid-century, because it chips away at a stock that has been accumulating for two centuries.
[P1.3] Now price. A leak is lost product. When an inspector with an infrared camera finds a stuck valve or a failing compressor seal, the repair is often a few hours of labor, and the gas saved is gas sold. A large share of oil and gas methane abatement is widely estimated to cost little or nothing net, and much of the rest remains cheap per tonne of CO2-equivalent.
[P1.4] Carbon capture runs the other way: tens to well over a hundred dollars per tonne, plus an energy penalty that burns extra fuel to run the capture unit itself. Same budget, many times the tonnes.
[P1.5] Then speed. Tightened inspection rules bite within months: crews, cameras, aircraft and satellites already exist, and a small number of super-emitting sites account for an outsized share of leakage. A capture project needs years of permitting, pipelines, and geologic storage characterization before it stores anything, and flagship projects have a record of delays and capturing less than promised.
[P1.6] So weigh it plainly: a cheap, proven fix whose cooling arrives before 2040, against an expensive, slow build whose benefits land later. Carbon capture may earn a place in the long game. But the motion asks about the near term, and there the wrench beats the megaproject.

[C1.1] The motion does not ask whether fixing leaks beats carbon capture in the abstract. It asks whether *tightening* leak-detection requirements beyond today's rules delivers more near-term benefit than the same money put into capture. That word "tightening" is where the proposition collapses.
[C1.2] Start with the cheap-tonne argument. If a repair pays for itself in sold gas, operators already do it; it needs no new rule. Those tonnes are also already being claimed by existing regulation, methane fees, and industry pledges. What a tighter mandate buys is the residue: more frequent inspections of thousands of low-producing wells that each leak little, where an inspector's visit costs far more than the gas recovered.
[C1.3] The marginal cost of methane abatement climbs steeply once the easy valves are turned. Compare apples to apples: marginal leak-rule dollars against marginal capture dollars, not the free first tonnes against the dearest capture plant.
[C1.4] Second, scheduled inspection is the wrong tool for the leaks that matter. The super-emitters that dominate the inventory are episodic: a blown-out tank, a stuck unlit flare, a liquids-unloading event. A quarterly camera visit mostly finds them gone. Tightening the visit schedule drives cost up without driving those tonnes down.
[C1.5] Third, verification. Methane reductions are modelled, not measured; satellite surveys repeatedly find emissions well above reported figures, which means a tighter rule's paper savings may not exist in the atmosphere. A tonne captured from a gas-processing or ethanol stream is metered at the stack and injected into rock. It is countable, permanent, and already happening at a handful of sites, for roughly the price of a few dozen dollars per tonne on high-purity streams, with build times of two or three years.
[C1.6] So the real comparison is uncertain, expensive, diminishing methane tonnes against verifiable, durable, affordable capture tonnes. Even on a twenty-year clock, the honest ledger favours capture.
