[P1.1] The copyright system is collapsing under foundation models. Rightsholders get nothing while suing, AI labs face multi-billion-dollar infringement claims for training on the open web, and courts are asked to decide the economics of an entire industry under fair use doctrine never meant for this. Both sides lose.
[P1.2] There is a tested fix: compulsory licensing. We did it for player pianos, for radio playing songs, for cable retransmitting broadcasts, for cover versions. Whenever a new technology needs to use millions of works at once, where any single work is low-value but the whole corpus is essential, and individual bargaining would mean gridlock, Congress sets a standard price and lets everyone use, but pay. Training is the textbook case.
[P1.3] That solves the incentive problem CON will ignore. A newsroom photo or novel is not valued by a model for its expression, but the model would not work without millions of them. Today under de facto fair use, the creator gets zero despite commercial use at scale. A compulsory pool — funded by a training fee or revenue share, distributed by a collective like ASCAP — creates money where now there is none, preserving human creation without giving any one publisher a kill-switch over AI progress.
[P1.4] It also rescues competition and innovation. Without a statutory path, only OpenAI can cut a deal with the New York Times, only Google can afford to litigate Getty. A startup or university lab cannot. A single clearinghouse gives legal certainty at a known price.
[P1.5] The alternative CON offers is either continued piracy or a data blackout that makes models worse, more biased, and monopolized. Payment with permission by law is how copyright has always resolved such crises. That is progress for creators and builders alike.
