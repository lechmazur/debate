[P1.1] Social contributions are not a tax on having employees. They price the risks work generates: injury, unemployment, old age without savings. That price does not vanish when a job is wrapped in an app or handed to a subcontractor. It moves — onto the worker's thin pension record, and onto the general budget that catches them when the platform deactivates them or the subcontractor dissolves and reopens under a new name.
[P1.2] The question is not whether this work should be funded. It is already funded, partly by everyone else. It is whether the firm that designed the chain and keeps the margin should pay.
[P1.3] A warehouse picker on a direct contract and a picker on the same shift, same scanner, same rate, through three layers of labour subcontracting, generate identical risk. Today the second arrangement is cheaper for the company at the top — not because it is more productive, but because the contribution base has been moved somewhere it cannot be collected. That is a subsidy paid by firms that employ people normally.
[P1.4] Raising the employer share with the share of labour a large company procures this way is not exotic. Injury and unemployment schemes already price premiums to the risk employers push into the pool. We apply that logic at the top of the chain, where the power to choose the structure sits. Small firms stay untouched; the levy follows procurement power.
[P1.5] It must be an EU floor: these chains cross borders, and contribution arbitrage is single-market competition.
[P1.6] And it works without a courtroom. Reclassification fights take years, worker by worker, and are outrun by a new contract template. A contribution rate applies immediately, to everyone, and still lets you subcontract — you simply stop getting it for free. Flexibility survives.
[P1.7] The opposition must defend the discount.

[C1.1] The warehouse image [P1.3] is incomplete. Two pickers with the same scanner may create similar risk; it does not follow that a surcharge on the buyer's procurement method repairs that risk. Social contributions attach to wages, insured status, and national benefit promises. The motion instead prices the use of a platform or chain by a large firm.
[C1.2] That is contract-form taxation, not risk pricing.
[C1.3] “The opposition must defend the discount” [P1.7] assumes the discount exists. A compliant contractor or consultancy already pays employer contributions; some platform work is inside national schemes. Add a higher charge at the top and the same wage is priced twice, while the worker gets no extra pension, sick cover or unemployment claim. If abuse is shell contractors, bogus self-employment, or unpaid dues, enforce those obligations.
[C1.4] A blanket levy does not distinguish evasion from lawful specialization.
[C1.5] “The levy follows procurement power” [P1.4] mistakes who writes the check for who bears the cost. A large buyer can squeeze supplier rates, demand indemnities, shorten contracts, bring work in-house, or automate. Small firms are not untouched; they are where the bill lands. Gig workers may see fewer shifts and lower rates.
[C1.6] Worse, transparent subcontractors pay, while evasive operators relabel, split entities, or contract through a non-EU vendor.
[C1.7] Nor is the scheme “without a courtroom” [P1.6]. Officials must define the labor share, trace layers, separate services from labor supply, police groups, and reconcile different national contribution bases. An EU floor stacks one procurement penalty over systems financing different promises.
[C1.8] The decisive test is not whether risk exists. It is whether this levy links payment to risk. It does not. Liability should follow actual unpaid obligations and worker entitlements, not the size of the buyer or the shape of the contract.
