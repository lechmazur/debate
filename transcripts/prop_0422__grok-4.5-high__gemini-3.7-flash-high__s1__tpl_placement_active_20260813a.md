[P1.1] Young children cannot navigate terminals, handle turbulence, reach restrooms, or evacuate without the adult who brought them. Seating them with accompanying adults is ordinary child protection, not a luxury upgrade. Airlines and rail operators should be required to provide that adjacency without add-on seat fees or premium-fare penalties.
[P1.2] The present system converts parental duty into inventory. Basic fares scatter seats; families then pay extra merely to restore the arrangement any reasonable person expected at booking. A parent with a toddler faces a coerced choice: absorb the surcharge or risk the child three rows away among strangers. That is not choice.
[P1.3] It is extraction from travelers who cannot leave a young child unsupervised.
[P1.4] Safety makes the obligation non-negotiable. In an emergency the adult who knows the child must be beside them. On long hauls the adult manages fear, sickness, and basic needs. Separation is preventable harm, not a scheduling quirk.
[P1.5] The same logic applies to rail: assigned cars and capacity constraints must not erect a fee wall between a five-year-old and the parent.
[P1.6] Markets fail because family demand is inelastic. Carriers know parents will still fly or ride, so they monetize anxiety. Voluntary “family seating” programs remain uneven, limited, or quietly gated behind higher fare classes. A clear rule ends the games: when a young child travels with an accompanying adult, operators must seat them together at the ordinary fare for the seats occupied—no adjacency surcharge, no forced upgrade to a pricier bucket.
[P1.7] The rule does not give free tickets or unlimited upgrades. It simply forbids treating the bond between a young child and caregiver as a revenue lever. Child safety and family integrity are not upsells. Require the seating; ban the penalty.

[C1.1] Mandating free adjacent seating sounds compassionate, but a rigid statutory rule ignores transport economics and harms the broader traveling public. Forcing operators to guarantee adjacent seating without fare differentials dismantles affordable unbundled travel, imposes unfair cross-subsidies, and disrupts transit operations.
[C1.2] First, unbundled pricing democratized travel. Discount fares exist precisely because optional services—including advance seat selection—are unbundled, allowing price-sensitive consumers to fly or ride at historic lows. When travelers choose basic economy, they accept unassigned seating in exchange for that lower price. If families can purchase the cheapest basic tickets and legally demand guaranteed adjacent blocks, the incentive structure collapses.
[C1.3] To recover lost revenue and manage restricted inventory, carriers will inevitably raise baseline fares for everyone, forcing solo travelers, students, and budget passengers to cross-subsidize family seat selection.
[C1.4] Second, a blanket mandate creates severe operational friction. On heavily booked flights or high-demand trains, guaranteeing adjacent seats to late-booking groups requires forcibly bumping or reseating passengers who planned ahead, booked early, or paid specifically for aisle or window preferences. Displacing compliant travelers to accommodate late bookings is fundamentally unfair. Moreover, applying this across rail systems—many of which rely on open, unreserved seating—would require burdensome reservation technology that slows boarding, complicates regional transit, and inflates ticket prices across the network.
[C1.5] Third, this mandate is unnecessary for safety. Transport operators already manage child supervision effectively. Gate agents, conductors, and flight attendants possess dynamic flexibility to seat families together through automated assignment algorithms and voluntary swaps without rigid statutory mandates tying their hands.
[C1.6] Child safety is already secured in practice. What PRO proposes is not a safety rule, but an economic distortion that increases base ticket prices, penalizes other passengers, and disrupts mass transit. CON firmly opposes the motion.
