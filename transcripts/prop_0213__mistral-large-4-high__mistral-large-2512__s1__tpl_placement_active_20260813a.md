[P1.1] Post-authoritarian societies face a choice between two models of transition: one that treats peace as the mere absence of prosecution, and one that treats peace as the presence of accountability. The evidence favors the latter. Truth commissions with public testimony should be the default mechanism because they transform fragile ceasefires into durable democratic legitimacy, whereas broad amnesties purchase temporary quiet at the price of permanent institutional distrust.
[P1.2] First, amnesties institutionalize impunity. When outgoing regimes grant themselves and their enforcers blanket immunity, they signal that power remains permanently above the law. This does not stabilize societies; it teaches successor governments and military officers that violence is a viable political tool. South Africa’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, whatever its operational limitations, created an official, public record that apartheid was a crime against humanity.
[P1.3] By contrast, broad amnesties—such as those enacted in Argentina in the late 1980s to halt military rebellions—left unanswered questions that resurfaced with explosive force years later, ultimately undermining the very stability they were designed to secure.
[P1.4] Second, public testimony serves victims and the polity simultaneously. Silence imposed by amnesty does not heal collective trauma; it privatizes it, allowing perpetrators to deny, victims to doubt their own memories, and societies to drift into conspiracy and rumor. A public transcript restores dignity to victims, names the dead, and gives citizens shared facts upon which to build a new constitutional order. Without that shared narrative, post-conflict societies fracture along the lines of competing myths and unresolved grievances.
[P1.5] Third, truth commissions generate deterrence where amnesties generate temptation. Knowing that atrocities will be documented, confessed, and condemned—even if not always punished—changes the calculus of future elites. Amnesties remove that shadow entirely, inviting repetition.
[P1.6] The proposition asks what societies should *usually* prefer. The answer is clear: the difficult, public work of truth over the false comfort of silence.
