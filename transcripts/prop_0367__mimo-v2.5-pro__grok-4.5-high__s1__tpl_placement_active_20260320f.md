[P1.1] The streaming revolution sold consumers one core promise: you're in control. No more paying for hundreds of channels you never watch. No more two-year contracts with punitive early termination fees. No more calling a retention department and negotiating like you're buying a car just to cancel a service.
[P1.2] That promise is eroding, and we should insist that streaming platforms honor it.
[P1.3] The case for flexible, reconfigurable subscriptions rests on a simple principle: in a competitive market, companies should earn loyalty every month through quality, not trap customers through friction. When a platform lets you pause your subscription easily during months you're not watching, it must compete for your return with compelling content. When bundling is transparent and adjustable, you pay for what you actually value. This is how healthy markets function.
[P1.4] Contrast this with the cable model that streaming was supposed to replace. Cable companies thrived on opacity—bundled channel packages hiding the true cost of individual networks, long-term contracts creating switching costs, and deliberately cumbersome cancellation processes. These practices served the provider, not the viewer. When streaming platforms adopt similar tactics—burying cancellation behind multiple clicks, offering annual plans that punish monthly flexibility, or bundling services in ways that obscure individual value—they betray the model's founding premise.
[P1.5] The technology already supports seamless subscription management. These are digital platforms with user accounts and payment systems. There is no technical barrier to letting someone pause HBO in July when nothing interests them, restart Netflix in November for a flagship release, and add a sports package only during playoff season. The barrier is purely strategic: platforms calculate that friction reduces cancellation.
[P1.6] Consumer welfare demands we reject this trajectory. Households managing tight budgets shouldn't subsidize months of unused access because reactivation feels daunting. Viewers shouldn't need a spreadsheet to decode what a bundle actually costs per service.
[P1.7] Streaming can remain genuinely better than cable. But only if we demand that flexibility be a feature, not a casualty.
