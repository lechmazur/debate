[P1.1] Blanket pet bans solve the wrong problem. The landlord's legitimate concern is money: scratched floors, ruined carpet, chewed doorframes. A standardized damage insurance policy or deposit answers that concern directly and completely — it converts an uncertain loss into a guaranteed, priced one. Once the compensation mechanism is in place, the ban has no remaining justification.
[P1.2] It's a crude proxy standing in where a precise tool already exists.
[P1.3] That's why the proposition says "default," not "unconditional right." Under a default-permit regime, buildings can still set behavioral rules: leash requirements, noise provisions, weight or species limits where a specific risk is documented, and pet agreements that let a landlord act against a particular animal that causes problems. What they cannot do is refuse every tenant with a pet because some hypothetical pet might someday damage a unit. We don't ban children despite spilled paint, or forbid cooking despite grease fires; we price the risk and set conduct rules.
[P1.4] Pets deserve the same ordinary treatment.
[P1.5] The status quo also produces harms that land squarely on housing stability. Pet-owning tenants in ban-heavy markets face shrinking options, higher rents for the few pet-tolerant buildings, and pressure to surrender animals to shelters when they move or face eviction. People hide pets, avoid maintenance calls, and stay in bad units rather than risk discovery. None of this protects property; it just degrades the landlord-tenant relationship and pushes costs onto renters and communities.
[P1.6] A deposit or insurance requirement keeps everyone honest — the tenant pays upfront for the risk they introduce, and the landlord is made whole.
[P1.7] Standardization is the key word. When coverage is a uniform, portable policy rather than a case-by-case negotiation, landlords face less administrative burden, insurers pool the risk across many buildings, and tenants can show proof the way they show renters insurance today. The transaction becomes simple: here is my coverage, here is my deposit, here is my pet agreement. Landlords keep their money.
[P1.8] Tenants keep their homes. The only thing we lose is a ban that was never doing the work [clipped]

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a convenient fiction: that a pet is nothing but a priced risk to a landlord's flooring. Follow that logic and the ban looks obsolete. But a building is not a warehouse of floorboards; it is a shared home, and the real question is who absorbs the costs when animals and strangers live behind thin walls.
[C1.2] A damage policy covers a scratched doorframe. It does not cover the neighbor's sleepless nights when a dog barks at 3 a.m., the child who learns to flinch at loose dogs in the hallway, the tenant whose allergies close off the elevator, or the bite that triggers a liability exclusion. A deposit is paid once; the daily burden of living with an animal falls on people who never signed a pet agreement and receive no premium. They did not choose the animal; they should not subsidize its upkeep.
[C1.3] The proposal converts the landlord's loss into a price, then calls the harm solved. It simply moves the externality onto everyone else in the building.
[C1.4] Nor does 'standardized' insurance standardize behavior. A certificate proves a tenant bought coverage, not that the pet is quiet, healthy, or under control. Landlords must still verify policies, police noise and nuisance rules, and litigate whether a stain is pet damage or ordinary wear—costs the proposal waves away as risk pooling. Worse, a permissive default invites adverse selection: those most likely to own destructive animals are drawn to buildings where they cannot be refused.
[C1.5] The children-and-cooking comparison fails. Children are not optional amenities, and a stove does not roam a hallway or trigger anaphylaxis. A blanket ban is not a crude proxy for money. It is a bright-line boundary that preserves the quiet, safe, and allergen-controlled enjoyment of the many against the discretionary preferences of the few.
[C1.6] Pets are a choice; the right to avoid them is not. Keep it.
