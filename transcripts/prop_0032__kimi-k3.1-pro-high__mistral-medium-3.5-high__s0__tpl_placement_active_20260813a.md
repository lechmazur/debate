[P1.1] A high-impact predictive result is not ordinary consumer information. A report suggesting greatly elevated risk for hereditary breast cancer, Huntington’s disease, or a serious cardiac condition can change medical decisions, family relationships, insurance anxieties, and a person’s sense of future. Companies that profit from delivering such information should not be allowed to treat a downloadable disclaimer as adequate preparation. They should be required to provide access to certified genetic counseling before those results are released.
[P1.2] First, counseling protects people from consequential misunderstanding. Genetic risk is probabilistic, test coverage varies, and a consumer report is not always a clinical diagnosis. A positive finding may require confirmatory testing; a negative finding may not rule out inherited risk. Without expert interpretation, customers can make opposite but equally dangerous errors: pursuing drastic screening or treatment based on an incomplete result, or ignoring family history because a limited test found nothing.
[P1.3] Certified counselors are trained to explain what was tested, what the result means, and what should happen next. This requirement therefore prevents avoidable harm at precisely the moment when fear and confusion are most likely.
[P1.4] Second, counseling makes consent meaningful. Clicking “I understand” beside technical language does not establish understanding. Genuine autonomy requires a realistic opportunity to ask whether one wants irreversible knowledge, what it could reveal about relatives, and what options exist after disclosure. Access before delivery respects choice better than counseling offered only afterward, when the psychological impact has already occurred and the result cannot be unseen.
[P1.5] Third, this is a proportionate obligation, not a ban on testing. The motion targets high-impact predictive results, not ancestry reports or trivial traits. Companies can integrate certified counseling through telephone or video services, schedule it before result release, and build the cost into a business model already monetizing sensitive health data. Convenience and modest expense cannot justify shifting interpretive and emotional risks onto the least informed party.
[P1.6] The central principle is simple: the power to deliver life-altering genetic information carries a duty to make expert help available before delivery. Direct-to-consumer access should expand genetic knowledge, [clipped]

[C1.1] Genetic information belongs to the individual, and adults have a fundamental right to access their own data without mandatory professional gatekeeping. Requiring pre-result counseling treats consumers as incapable of autonomous decision-making, undermining personal agency over health information they already own.
[C1.2] The practical burden would be severe. Mandatory counseling adds significant cost—often hundreds of dollars per session—and scheduling delays that can deter testing for serious, time-sensitive conditions. For someone with a strong family history of early-onset Alzheimer’s, weeks of waiting for a counseling appointment before accessing their APOE status could mean unnecessary anxiety and delay in taking preventive action. In rural areas with counselor shortages, access could be impossible, effectively denying some people their own genetic information.
[C1.3] The result could be fewer people getting tested overall, reducing early detection of treatable conditions. Moreover, requiring counseling before results could discourage companies from offering these tests at all, limiting consumer choice.
[C1.4] Mandatory counseling also fails to guarantee better outcomes. Genetic risk is inherently probabilistic, and even certified counselors cannot ensure every client fully grasps nuances like penetrance, variable expressivity, or the limitations of direct-to-consumer tests. Forced sessions risk becoming perfunctory checkboxes that provide false reassurance rather than genuine comprehension. Some may simply rush through to access results, while others may be deterred by the requirement itself.
[C1.5] Better alternatives exist. Current industry practice already includes prominent risk warnings, educational materials, and optional access to counselors. This respects autonomy while supporting those who want guidance. Consumers can choose to consult professionals after receiving results, when they have context to ask meaningful questions and can better understand what they need from counseling.
[C1.6] Many may find post-result counseling more valuable precisely because they have actual results to discuss, rather than abstract probabilities.
[C1.7] Most critically, this mandate sets a dangerous precedent. If pre-approval is required for genetic data, what comes next? Blood test results? Imaging scans?
[C1.8] The principle that individuals control access to their own health information would be systematically eroded by paternalistic requirements that assume people cannot handle their own data responsibly. This undermines the very foundation [clipped]

Thus the warnings about “mandatory professional gatekeeping” [C1.1], “forced sessions” [C1.4], and “pre-approval” [C1.7] rest on an invented mandate. A counselor is not a gatekeeper when the customer retains the keys.
[P2.2] That distinction also answers the access argument. Release need not depend on completing an appointment: companies can offer telephone or video counseling and allow customers to decline it. Rural shortages argue for remote provision, not for leaving rural customers without expert support. If a business chooses delays or withdrawal rather than compliance, that demonstrates the need to regulate its delivery model; it does not prove that unaccompanied disclosure is safe.
[P2.3] Nor should speculative reductions in testing outweigh preventable misinterpretation without any reason to think deterrence would exceed the harm prevented.
[P2.4] The Alzheimer’s example actually exposes CON’s weakness. A predictive APOE result is not an emergency diagnosis, and a strong family history does not turn a consumer report into a reliable basis for immediate action. Counseling helps distinguish risk information from clinical confirmation. Where results genuinely have urgent implications, prompt access can be built into the service; urgency is a reason for competent support, not an exemption from it.
[P2.5] CON is right that counseling cannot guarantee understanding. But the obligation is to provide access, not to certify perfect comprehension. Optional warnings and post-result counseling do not supply the same safeguard: educational text cannot respond to a particular customer’s misconceptions, and counseling after disclosure cannot restore the choice not to know. A person shocked by a Huntington’s-risk result may need help afterward too; that is an argument for both, not afterward alone.
[P2.6] Finally, there is no slide toward controlling every blood test. The motion draws a specific line around high-impact, probabilistic, potentially irreversible predictive results sold directly to consumers. CON protects instantaneous delivery by imagining coercion. [P4.1] A1: The constraint falls on companies, not customers. Before releasing a high-impact predictive result, they must make a timely, genuine offer of access to a certified counselor—not merely provide a disclaimer or counseling after disclosure. Customers may decline and proceed. That obligation changes what companies must arrange and fund without creating a counselor’s veto.
[P4.2] CON’s weeks-long wait follows from assuming compulsory attendance, not from this motion.
[P4.3] A2: Prevention does not require guaranteed understanding. The mechanism is concrete: expert help can correct the belief that a limited negative result eliminates inherited risk, explain why a positive consumer result may need clinical confirmation, and clarify the choice not to receive irreversible information. Those misunderstandings can drive false reassurance or premature medical action. That is a function of certified counseling, not of a static warning.
[P4.4] Demanding proof of perfect outcomes while calling cost, delay, and reduced testing “certain” applies evidence asymmetrically. CON has not shown that a declinable offer must cause those harms.
[P4.5] This also exposes the false choice between pre-result and post-result counseling. Counseling before disclosure protects deliberation; counseling afterward helps interpret and act on the findings. Nothing in the motion removes the latter. A checkout click begins a transaction; it should not extinguish the customer’s opportunity to reconsider before the company delivers information that cannot be unseen.
[P4.6] The decisive comparison is therefore not “professional control versus freedom.” It is a guaranteed opportunity for expert support versus leaving customers to find help only after the most consequential moment has passed. PRO’s burden is narrow and tied to an exceptional category: high-impact predictive results, not every blood test or scan. Companies bear a manageable service obligation; customers retain the right to decline, the right to know, and access to their data. On CON’s account, autonomy means instant delivery.
[P4.7] On PRO’s, autonomy includes a real chance to make an informed decision. That is the stronger protection of both safety and choice.
