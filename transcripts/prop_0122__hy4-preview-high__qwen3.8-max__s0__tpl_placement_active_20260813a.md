[P1.1] Punishment is not merely imposed suffering; it is the community’s solemn act of blame. Criminal responsibility must track culpability, not just harm. A child of ten, twelve, or thirteen is still becoming a moral agent. Treating such a child as a fully punishable offender mistakes immaturity for guilt.
[P1.2] Early adolescence is exactly when the capacities blame requires—impulse control, future orientation, resistance to peer pressure, appreciation of consequences—are most unstable. A twelve-year-old may know stealing is forbidden and still be unable to resist an older accomplice, desperation, or impulse. That gap is why full penal blame is disproportionate. Very low ages also fall hardest on children already failed by poverty, family breakdown, or care systems—those least able to defend themselves in court.
[P1.3] For children under fourteen, the state’s first response should be welfare, education, mental-health care, and family support, not charge, conviction, and criminal record. Arrest and stigma do not manufacture responsibility; they brand the child, remove them from school, and immerse them in the deviant networks that harden offending. If the goal is public safety, the punitive route is not just cruel but self-defeating.
[P1.4] Fourteen is not a magic switch; older teens can still deserve leniency. But before fourteen the developmental excuse is general rather than exceptional, so fourteen is the honest floor. Human-rights guidance has long urged a floor of fourteen, and many jurisdictions already set the line there or higher. The norm is achievable; the outliers are the very low ages.
[P1.5] The clash is therefore narrow. To defend very low ages, one must show either that under-fourteens already possess adult-like culpability, or that full penal condemnation is just despite diminished capacity. Neither is defensible. This is not impunity; it is proportionality—intervention and repair without lifelong conviction.
[P1.6] Reduced culpability means reduced punishment; when punishment cannot be proportionate, it should not be full penal punishment at all.
[P1.7] Raise the age to at least fourteen, and answer children’s deeds with accountability that fits: therapeutic, educational, and civil before it is criminal.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that any age below fourteen must mean treating children as miniature adults. That is false. A low minimum age can coexist with a youth justice system that presumes diminished capacity, uses secure children’s courts, rehabilitation orders, and reserves custody for the gravest cases. The question is not whether a nine-year-old shoplifter should be imprisoned like an adult.
[C1.2] It is whether the law may ever hold an under-fourteen accountable through criminal process when the act is grave and the child’s understanding is proven.
[C1.3] A flat fourteen-year floor removes that possibility. It substitutes biology-by-calendar for moral judgment. Thirteen-year-olds differ enormously. Some act from childish impulse; others plan serious violence, recruit others, conceal evidence, and understand exactly what they are doing.
[C1.4] If the state must treat every person under fourteen as categorically non-responsible, victims of serious harm are told the wrongdoer owes no public answer, and the state may lack tools to manage genuine risk. Welfare agencies can help, but they may lack authority, resources, or coercive safeguards when a child has killed or seriously injured another child.
[C1.5] This matters because accountability is not only punishment. Criminal responsibility can trigger supervision, mandatory education, restitution, family intervention, psychiatric treatment, and, in extreme cases, detention in a youth facility. Those are not the same as adult prison. By raising the floor to fourteen, the proposition narrows the menu of responses and makes it harder to combine public protection with structured intervention.
[C1.6] The better principle is rebuttable presumption, not absolute immunity. Where jurisdictions set low ages, they can still require proof of understanding, exclude minor misconduct, and prioritize welfare. That approach respects childhood without surrendering judgment in cases where a child’s conduct causes severe harm. The motion’s error is turning a common tendency toward leniency into an irrebuttable age bar.
[C1.7] We should keep low ages possible, constrain them with safeguards, and judge capacity case by case.

[C2.1] PRO’s strongest claim is that civil welfare powers can manage dangerous children without a criminal label. But that concedes the real issue: the state will still restrain, supervise, and institutionalize a child. The question is whether that coercion is governed by legal standards or by opaque welfare discretion. Criminal process, even in children’s courts, provides proof of intent, legal representation, appeal, proportionate limits, and a public finding that the harm was wrong.
[C2.2] A civil order can lock a child away “for care” with fewer rights, less transparency, and no clear endpoint. If the worry is stigma, the answer is child-centered procedure, not replacing accountability with unaccountable confinement.
[C2.3] PRO also says a bright fourteen line avoids unequal discretion. But the line does not remove discretion; it moves it earlier and darker. Police, teachers, and child-protection workers will still decide which under-fourteen is “dangerous” or “in need of care.” Those judgments are often more class- and race-inflected than a courtroom finding of capacity. A rebuttable presumption, requiring proof of understanding and excluding trivial conduct, is the narrower safeguard: it says no child is automatically adult-like, but no grave harm is automatically invisible.
[C2.4] The deeper flaw in PRO’s case is its treatment of criminal responsibility as only blame. For rare under-fourteen cases involving planned killing, serious sexual violence, or repeated severe assault, responsibility is also recognition—of the victim, of the community’s judgment, and of the child’s capacity to make amends. Welfare support alone can say the act was a symptom; criminal responsibility can say the act was wrong and must be answered. That distinction matters most when harm is greatest.
[C2.5] Fourteen as an irrebuttable floor does not prevent cruelty by accident; it prevents judgment by design. The better rule is low entry into a protective youth system, with capacity proven case by case.
