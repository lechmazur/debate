[P1.1] The question is not whether controlled human infection studies are risky. They are. The question is whether regulators should forbid a tool that can give humanity earlier, cleaner answers when animals cannot tell us what we need to know. They should permit it more broadly, under strict ethical conditions.
[P1.2] Animal models fail predictably. Mice, ferrets, and monkeys do not share human immune architecture, receptor distribution, or disease progression. A vaccine can provoke strong antibody titers in macaques and still fail in people; a treatment can rescue mice and miss human pathophysiology entirely. When the model is inadequate, human challenge is not a reckless shortcut—it is the most direct way to learn whether a vaccine or drug actually prevents infection or disease, and which immune markers correlate with protection.
[P1.3] That knowledge can rescue a program or kill a false hope before thousands are enrolled in field trials that cannot answer the key question.
[P1.4] Serious ethical objections remain. But serious is not unmanageable. Regulators can require independent ethics review, truly informed consent from competent adults, no exploitative inducements, rigorous monitoring, guaranteed rescue treatment, pre-specified stopping rules, and restriction to pathogens whose illness is typically self-limited or treatable. These safeguards already govern challenge studies for malaria, cholera, and influenza.
[P1.5] Expanding the category does not abandon them; it applies them to more cases where the alternative is worse.
[P1.6] And there is an ethical cost on the other side. Delay is not neutral. Waiting years for inadequate animal models and blunt field trials means people die in outbreaks, families wait for vaccines, and ineffective products get deployed at scale. The risk borne by consenting volunteers must be weighed against the risk imposed on millions by ignorance.
[P1.7] Permit human challenge studies more broadly when animal models are inadequate, with strict review. Ethical objections are a reason to regulate carefully, not to protect ourselves by staying ignorant.
