[C1.1] The proposition assumes organized crime is a cannabis company. In Latin America it is not. Cartels, gangs, and armed groups are poly-criminal enterprises: they earn from cocaine transit, extortion, illegal mining, fuel theft, and human smuggling. Taking one agricultural commodity out of that portfolio may change their bookkeeping, but it does not reliably weaken their power.
[C1.2] In many places, cannabis has already become less important than protection economies that legalization cannot touch. A smaller budget does not automatically mean fewer guns, less territory, or less corruption.
[C1.3] PRO’s market logic also assumes the state can run a legal market that undercuts illegal suppliers. That is the hardest part, and it is exactly where many Latin American states are weakest. A legal regime needs licensing, taxation, quality controls, banking, inspections, and police able to confront illegal operators without being corrupted or outgunned. If taxes and compliance costs are high, the illicit market remains cheaper, as seen in legal cannabis jurisdictions with persistent black markets.
[C1.4] If taxes and rules are too low, the state gains little revenue and little control. In weak institutional settings, legalization can create new targets: licensed farms, dispensaries, transport routes, and municipal permits become assets for extortion, money laundering, and takeover.
[C1.5] Uruguay is not the template PRO suggests. It is a small, high-capacity country with less exposure to major cocaine transit routes and a domestic market that can be closely monitored. Latin America’s major cannabis and cocaine producer zones are rural, contested, and often beyond effective state reach. National legalization there does not simply replace a cartel with a pharmacy; it risks forcing cultivation into informal channels, empowering local armed actors, and leaving export incentives intact because neighboring markets and the United States remain illegal.
[C1.6] The real question is whether this policy weakens organized crime. Legalizing cannabis under these conditions is more likely to shrink one commodity margin while leaving criminal governance, violence, and diversified illicit revenue intact.
