[P1.1] Gig workers currently suffer from extreme, artificial lock-in. When a driver completes thousands of trips and earns a top-tier rating on a dominant platform, that reputation capital is held hostage. If the platform slashes pay, increases commission fees, or worsens conditions, the worker cannot leave for a competitor without starting from absolute zero. This lack of portability transforms a supposedly flexible labor market into digital serfdom, where workers are effectively trapped by the sunk cost of their own reputations.
[P1.2] Mandating standardized data-transfer tools for ratings and verified work history shatters this trap. Portability restores genuine labor mobility. When workers can seamlessly take their proven track record to a rival app, they gain actual bargaining power. This shifts the power dynamic, ensuring platforms earn worker loyalty through fair treatment rather than data captivity.
[P1.3] Platforms will finally be forced to compete on pay, safety, and working conditions to retain their experienced workforce, rather than relying on the artificial moat of trapped, non-transferable reputation data.
[P1.4] This policy also rescues market competition and directly benefits consumers. Currently, dominant platforms enjoy a massive first-mover advantage because new apps cannot attract experienced workers who understandably refuse to abandon their hard-earned ratings. By standardizing data portability, we drastically lower the barrier to entry. New platforms can immediately onboard vetted, highly-rated workers, which in turn gives consumers more choices, shorter wait times, and consistently better service.
[P1.5] The incumbent’s monopoly is broken not by heavy-handed price controls, but by freeing the specific data that makes the labor market function efficiently.
[P1.6] The reputation a worker builds through their physical labor is a portable professional asset, not the proprietary property of a single corporate app's database. Requiring standardized portability aligns the gig economy with basic free-market principles. True market competition requires the freedom to exit, and workers cannot meaningfully exit if their professional identity and verified history are held hostage.

[C1.1] The central premise of this motion is that ratings function as portable professional credentials. They do not. A driver's 4.95 on a delivery platform reflects performance within that platform's specific routing, customer mix, and evaluation culture. That number has little predictive validity when pasted onto a competing app serving a different city, client base, and service format.
[C1.2] Mandating transferability doesn't liberate workers; it imports a misleading credential into a context where it doesn't belong.
[C1.3] The restaurant analogy exposes the flaw. If a waiter earned "Top Server" status at one restaurant, should they walk into a Michelin-starred competitor and demand the same status? No, because the new house has its own standards, clientele, and judgment about fit. Gig platforms are no different.
[C1.4] Their ratings are house-built evaluations, calibrated to their own service, and forcing them to honor foreign assessments corrupts their quality control.
[C1.5] The lock-in narrative also misreads the market. The vast majority of active gig workers already run two, three, or four apps simultaneously. They multi-home precisely because switching costs are low. The "digital serfdom" framing, with its feudal vocabulary, applies to perhaps a sliver of single-platform power users; it cannot justify a sweeping industry-wide mandate built on an exaggerated premise.
[C1.6] Worse, this policy invites systematic fraud. A worker facing termination for misconduct could export a clean 4.9 rating, then re-enter the new platform as a "verified" top performer while the misconduct record stays behind. Standardized portability, without a parallel misconduct registry, becomes a laundering mechanism for bad actors and a liability bomb for receiving platforms asked to trust data they did not generate.
[C1.7] Finally, the mandate would chill innovation in reputation systems. Platforms currently experiment with multi-dimensional ratings, contextual scoring, and anti-fraud weighting. Forcing them to honor a competitor's output discourages the very differentiation that benefits workers and consumers alike, locking the industry into a lowest-common-denominator standard set by the most permissive evaluator.
