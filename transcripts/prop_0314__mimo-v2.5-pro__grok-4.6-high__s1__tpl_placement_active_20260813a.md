[C1.1] The proposition would forbid a city from writing a stadium deal unless the team signs one of two prescribed contracts. That is not responsible governance. It is a gag rule on local judgment.
[C1.2] Cities subsidize stadiums for reasons checklists do not capture. Residents want the team to stay. A downtown needs an anchor that fills hotels on game nights and gives a mid-sized city a national profile. Those benefits are uneven and still real.
[C1.3] Even if much of the spending is reshuffled entertainment money, a city is allowed to buy an amenity its people actually use. Forcing every deal into a revenue-share or community-benefits box pretends the only legitimate public interest is a contractual rebate.
[C1.4] The "unless" clause misunderstands bargaining. Teams can leave. Rival cities will still offer cash with fewer strings. A city that unilaterally demands enforceable kickbacks does not become a tougher negotiator; it becomes the city that loses the franchise.
[C1.5] Saying a deal that cannot survive these conditions was never worth having only works if civic identity, fan welfare, and district revitalization count as nothing unless they appear as a line item.
[C1.6] Enforceable conditions are not free. Revenue-sharing formulas invite accounting games. Neighborhood packages can become slush for connected groups, or they inflate costs until the public pays twice—once in subsidy, again in delay and litigation. Paper protections do not halt land-market displacement; they do create monitors and hold-up after the ribbon-cutting.
[C1.7] Voters already punish bad stadium deals. The remedy is a better local bargain or a no vote, not a blanket rule that treats every market as the same extractive transfer. The cities most constrained by this rule are the ones that already have the least leverage. Cities should remain free to reject giveaways—and free to accept a deal whose value is not reducible to a mandated split.
