[P1.1] Every rich economy on earth got rich by making things and selling them to its neighbors. Africa does the reverse: it digs up wealth, ships it raw across oceans, and buys back the finished product at ten times the price. That is not a trade strategy. It is a supply chain built by colonial powers to serve colonial powers — and sixty years after independence, it still runs on the same rails, laid from mine to port, never from one African market to another.
[P1.2] The proposition asks Africa to break that pattern, and the economic logic is overwhelming.
[P1.3] First, value capture. Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire grow most of the world's cocoa yet keep only a sliver of the global chocolate industry's value; the grinding, branding, and retailing happen elsewhere. Exporting raw materials means exporting the jobs, the skills, and the profit margins, while keeping the price risk. Manufacturing and regional trade keep those margins at home.
[P1.4] Second, volatility. Commodity dependence means building a national budget on a price you do not control. When oil or copper crashes, Nigeria and Zambia crash with it. Diversified manufacturing economies bend; mono-crop exporters break.
[P1.5] Every debt crisis on the continent traces back to this fragility.
[P1.6] Third, jobs. Africa adds millions of young workers every year. A mine employs a few thousand and exports billions; a textile or agro-processing sector employing millions is what actually absorbs that labor and builds a middle class whose spending then fuels more local industry. That is the only ladder any country has ever climbed from poverty to prosperity.
[P1.7] The African Continental Free Trade Area finally gives this a continental market to stand on — the scale that made Asian and European manufacturing possible.
[P1.8] The CON will say "comparative advantage." But comparative advantage is built, not inherited. South Korea's advantage was once rice and tungsten; it chose to build shipyards and chip fabs. Africa's supposed advantage in raw exports is simply the advantage others assigned it.
[P1.9] Time to choose its own.

[C1.1] The motion isn't "also build manufacturing." It's *prioritize over* — divert scarce capital, foreign exchange, tariff policy, and political attention away from the sectors that actually earn money today. That is where this case collapses.
[C1.2] Start with PRO's own example. South Korea did not get rich selling shipyards to its neighbours. It sold to America and Europe — distant rich markets. So did Taiwan, Vietnam, Bangladesh, and China.
[C1.3] The lesson of every successful industrializer is not regional self-reliance; it is ruthless access to high-income consumers wherever they live. Telling Ethiopia's garment workers that Lagos matters more than Los Angeles is not decolonization. It is shrinking their market.
[C1.4] Because the arithmetic of intra-African demand is brutal. A continent of low incomes cannot absorb manufactures at the volumes that make factories efficient. You need scale to get down the cost curve, and scale lives where the money is. AfCFTA is useful precisely as a complement to global exports — not as a substitute for them.
[C1.5] Second: what pays for industrialization? Machinery, turbines, fertilizer, port cranes — all imported, all priced in dollars. Those dollars come from copper, cocoa, oil, gas, lithium. Botswana built one of Africa's best-run states on diamonds sold to distant rich markets and then invested the proceeds.
[C1.6] Deprioritize the export earner and you don't get factories; you get a foreign exchange crisis, which is how the 1970s–80s import-substitution experiments ended — protected plants running at a fraction of capacity, subsidised by revenues that eventually ran out.
[C1.7] Third: the timing argument runs against PRO. Global electrification has made African minerals more valuable, not less. Walking away from a seller's market to chase politically directed manufacturing is trading a real income stream for a hoped-for one.
[C1.8] And note who the buyers now are. China, India, the Gulf — not "former colonial powers." PRO is fighting a 1965 map.
[C1.9] Build industry where costs actually permit it. But do not make the export sector the sacrifice.
