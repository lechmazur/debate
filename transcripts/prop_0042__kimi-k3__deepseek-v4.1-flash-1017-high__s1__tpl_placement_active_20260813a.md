[C1.1] The motion is not a modest carbon price or a labeling rule. It is a demand that governments compute “full” environmental and health externalities, convert them into a production tax, and impose it even when the predictable result is higher food prices for lower-income households. That last clause is not a footnote; it is the concession that decides this debate.
[C1.2] Start with the arithmetic problem. “Full externalities” has no stable, agreed boundary: methane and land use, manure and water, antibiotics and zoonotic risk, saturated fat and cancer risk, rural livelihoods and nutrition. Each requires contestable valuations, varying by region and method. In practice, the rate will be set by lobbying and revenue appetite, not truth. A tax that claims scientific precision while resting on arbitrary monetization is not “prices telling the truth”; it is a planner’s guess with legal force.
[C1.3] Then the incidence problem. PRO says the poor already pay in damage, and that is true. But it does not follow that the remedy is a charge at the till. A tax on production raises the price of a nutrient-dense staple for households that spend a large share of income on food, including children and people who rely on meat for iron and protein.
[C1.4] Beans are cheaper per gram of protein, but they are not drop-in substitutes for every family, cuisine, or food environment. The tax-and-dividend reassurance [P1.5] is doing enormous work: rebates must be generous, timely, and reach informal and rural households; none of that is in the motion. “Even if it raises food prices” means the policy is justified even when compensation fails.
[C1.5] Finally, taxing production invites leakage. Domestic producers face the charge; imports do not. Damage can relocate, while domestic livestock sectors and the rural poor absorb the cost.
[C1.6] If the goal is lower emissions and better diets, set standards, fund alternatives, and target support. Do not sell an unmeasurable, regressive production tax as fairness.
