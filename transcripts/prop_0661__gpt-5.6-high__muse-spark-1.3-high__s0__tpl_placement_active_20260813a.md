[P1.1] When software helps decide who is disciplined, scheduled, promoted, or fired, a secret scoring rule becomes a form of private law. Workers are governed by it but cannot see, question, or correct it. The proposition restores a basic principle: consequential decisions require accountable reasons.
[P1.2] Disclosure does not mean publishing source code or handing workers a manual for gaming quotas. Employers should tell workers that monitoring exists, what data are collected, the principal factors used in scoring, how scores affect employment decisions, how long data are retained, and how errors can be challenged. Regulators should receive more technical information under confidentiality protections. That design preserves legitimate trade secrets while exposing harmful practices.
[P1.3] The first benefit is accuracy and due process. Algorithms turn proxies into judgments. Minutes without scanner activity may reflect a broken device or a bathroom break, not laziness. Low keyboard activity may reflect time spent helping a customer.
[P1.4] Location data may be wrong. If workers never know what is measured, they cannot supply context or correct false inputs. Errors then become self-validating: a bad score triggers fewer shifts, and fewer shifts are later treated as evidence of poor commitment.
[P1.5] The second benefit is meaningful privacy. Monitoring can reach messages, movements, voice, video, biometrics, and activity outside ordinary work tasks. Employment dependence makes “just quit” an empty safeguard. People deserve to know when they are watched and for what purpose.
[P1.6] Regulators need the same visibility to determine whether collection is excessive, discriminatory, or unrelated to legitimate business needs.
[P1.7] Disclosure also improves management. Workers can identify flawed metrics that executives never experience, while regulators can detect patterns that isolated complaints cannot reveal. Sound systems gain legitimacy; unsound systems face pressure to improve.
[P1.8] CON may invoke compliance costs or gaming. But employers already know what systems they deploy, and standardized notices make disclosure manageable. Sensitive thresholds can remain protected. The decisive asymmetry is simple: employers risk explaining their tools; workers risk their income, reputation, and privacy.
