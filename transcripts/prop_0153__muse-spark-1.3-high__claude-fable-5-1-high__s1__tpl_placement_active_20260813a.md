[P1.1] Outlawing paid surrogacy does not stop surrogacy. Infertility, medical risk, and same-sex parenthood do not disappear when a law bans payment. What disappears is oversight. Couples travel abroad, arrangements go underground, and the surrogate loses legal counsel, medical standards, and any guaranteed payment.
[P1.2] The question is not surrogacy or no surrogacy. It is safe, visible surrogacy or risky, hidden surrogacy.
[P1.3] Payment, properly regulated, respects women rather than using them. Pregnancy is months of physical strain, medical risk, lost wages, and emotional labor. An altruistic-only system tells a woman to undertake all that for free while agencies, clinics, and lawyers still get paid. That is exploitation dressed as virtue.
[P1.4] Fair compensation recognizes the work and gives women reason to demand enforceable terms.
[P1.5] Legalization makes protection possible. A regulated market can require independent legal counsel for the surrogate, comprehensive medical and psychological screening, full informed consent, health insurance and life insurance, escrowed funds she controls, limits on embryo transfers and number of surrogacies, and an uncoerced right to make medical decisions during pregnancy. Bans cannot impose any of that. They simply abandon surrogates to private deals where the intended parents hold all the power.
[P1.6] Finally, this is about who gets to form a family. For women without a uterus, cancer survivors, couples with repeated loss, and gay men, surrogacy is often the only path to a biological child. A ban reserves parenthood for those wealthy and mobile enough to evade it abroad, while poorer women at home are left with fewer safe options and poorer intended parents with none. Regulated compensated surrogacy does not celebrate inequality.
[P1.7] It confronts it with contracts, clinics, and courts in the open.

[C1.1] The motion contains its own refutation. It asks for a market in gestation with "strong protections against coercion," but in this market, the payment is the coercion. Nobody with comfortable alternatives rents her womb to strangers. When surrogacy is legal and paid, the women who sign are the ones for whom the money is not optional: military spouses, single mothers, women behind on rent.
[C1.2] A protection against coercion that leaves the price intact protects nothing. Strip out the financial pressure and you no longer have a compensated market; keep it and you have exactly the pressure the motion pretends to guard against.
[C1.3] Second, the contract cannot be both enforceable and humane. Intended parents pay for a child, delivered, healthy, on their terms. That is why real surrogacy contracts contain clauses about selective reduction, abortion, caesareans, diet, travel. Now hold that next to the promised "uncoerced right to make medical decisions."
[C1.4] If the surrogate genuinely retains that right, the buyer has purchased nothing enforceable and the market unravels. If the contract binds, the state is enforcing a stranger's authority over a woman's body and compelling her to surrender a newborn she has carried for nine months. Pick one. There is no regulation that dissolves this dilemma, only regulation that hides which side it has chosen.
[C1.5] Third, the child. Every other area of law treats a newborn's parentage as a matter of the child's interest, not of who paid. Compensated surrogacy makes a birth certificate the receipt for a transaction. When a baby is born disabled, or twins arrive when one was ordered, we have seen what happens: children left behind, refused, disputed like defective goods.
[C1.6] The alternative is not prohibition of surrogacy but prohibition of payment for it: altruistic arrangements with full expense reimbursement, medical care, insurance, and legal support, as several countries already run. That answers every fairness concern about unpaid labor without turning pregnancy into a product and poverty into a qualification.

[P2.1] The claim that payment itself is coercion proves far too much. Nurses, home aides, soldiers and construction workers also take bodily risk for money because they need it. We do not call that trafficking and ban their wages. We regulate hours, safety, consent and pay.
[P2.2] Coercion is a threat that removes options. A regulated offer adds one, with screening to reject women in acute debt or housing crisis, independent counsel, medical review, cooling-off periods and escrow she controls. Banning paid surrogacy leaves the poverty intact and removes one of the safest, best-paid options, pushing the same women toward unregulated deals, riskier work, or foreign brokers where no one screens for desperation at all.
[P2.3] The supposed contract dilemma is false. Good law already splits what is enforceable from what is not. Escrowed compensation, health and life insurance, single-embryo limits, and the intended parents' duty to take the child whatever its health can be strictly enforced. Forced abortion, forced caesarean, forced diet or travel cannot.
[P2.4] The surrogate keeps full medical decision-making, just as an employee can quit a job without being forced under the knife, while still owed wages and care. Buyers are not purchasing a body. They are assuming legal parenthood and financial responsibility, with the risk that pregnancy cannot be controlled.
[P2.5] That also answers the child. Babies refused or left stateless, twins rejected, disabled newborns disputed — those scandals came from cross-border, unclear-parentage markets that bans create. A regulated system requires pre-birth parentage orders: the intended parents are the parents from conception in law, liable for support, unable to reject a child as defective goods.
[P2.6] So-called altruistic-only surrogacy is not an answer. Generous expense reimbursement plus lost wages for nine months is payment by another name, only lower, vaguer, and without labor rights. It does not remove pressure, it shifts it to family obligation and quiet under-the-table cash, while creating shortages that send intended parents straight back to the exploitative abroad the opposition deplores.
