[P1.1] The decisive question is whether a short waiting period changes incentives enough to undermine asylum integrity, or whether it mainly changes whether applicants survive on aid or pay their own way. Sixty days is not open-ended admission; it is a narrow bridge from filing to lawful work.
[P1.2] First, public assistance is largely a function of enforced idleness. When asylum applicants cannot work for months, savings run out, and they then need housing support, food aid, or charitable help. A permit issued within two months lets them enter the formal labor market quickly, often in sectors with persistent vacancies such as hospitality, warehousing, caregiving, and agriculture. They earn wages, pay sales and payroll taxes where applicable, and reduce the cash transfers that would otherwise be needed.
[P1.3] The fiscal difference is simple: a person earning wages is far less likely to need emergency assistance than a person barred from earning.
[P1.4] Second, this does not materially increase fraudulent claims because work authorization is not asylum. It does not grant permanent status, does not stop removal, and can be revoked if the claim fails. Fraud is controlled by the mechanisms already used for work eligibility: identity checks, biometric screening, employer verification, and penalties for misuse. A sixty-day permit adds no new pathway to remain permanently; it only moves people from informal work or public support into monitored, taxable employment.
[P1.5] Finally, the fraud incentive is weak. People fabricate asylum claims mainly to avoid persecution or exploit long-term status, not to get a temporary job permit that ends if the claim is denied. If anything, legal work makes applicants more traceable and less dependent on exploitative underground labor. That is why the proposition wins: it cuts public assistance by allowing self-support while leaving fraud controls essentially intact.

[C1.5] Third, temporary authorization can still be a powerful incentive.
