[P1.1] Elder care is not a normal asset. Residents are frail, often cognitively impaired, and dependent on others for feeding, medication, and dignity. Families cannot easily switch providers; staff cannot simply walk away. That makes ownership structure a safety issue, not a financial footnote.
[P1.2] Private equity’s core playbook is buy, lever, extract, exit. In elder care it produces recognizable harms: sell the building to an affiliated landlord and lease it back at high rent; load debt onto the operating company; squeeze staffing, training, and supplies to hit margin targets; then exit before the consequences fully mature. Even a decent manager inside that structure answers to investors and lenders, not to a ninety-year-old with dementia.
[P1.3] Government already regulates staffing and safety. Why restrict ownership too? Because ordinary rules police incidents and minimum inputs, not financial architecture. A facility can satisfy a ratio while cutting training, therapy, and dignity.
[P1.4] When the operator is a thinly capitalized shell and the real estate sits elsewhere, fines and lawsuits hit an entity with little left. The public absorbs the failure; residents absorb the harm.
[P1.5] Restrictions can be calibrated: licensing conditions on sale-leasebacks, debt limits, ownership transparency, capital-maintenance requirements, and accountability for controlling investors. If private capital can genuinely improve facilities, it can do so under transparent ownership, stable debt, and care-first conditions. What it cannot demand is the right to financial engineering that offloads risk onto residents and taxpayers.
[P1.6] The objection will be: regulate behavior, not ownership. But ownership determines who controls behavior and what they are rewarded for. We do not hand the keys of a nursing home to an owner whose best exit is to strip it. Restrict private-equity ownership because elder care’s time horizon is a human lifespan, not a fund cycle.
