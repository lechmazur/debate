[P1.1] Retreat planned early is a choice; retreat improvised after catastrophe is triage. The motion requires cities to begin mandatory long-term relocation planning now—not to evict residents tomorrow, and not to declare every vulnerable neighborhood doomed. A serious plan maps risk, sets triggers for staged withdrawal, protects housing and livelihoods, budgets land and infrastructure needs, and gives residents enforceable roles in decisions. Making planning mandatory ensures it happens while choices still exist.
[P1.2] First, time creates options disasters destroy. After repeated floods or fires, households must decide under trauma, insurance and emergency funds favor rapid rebuilding, and governments face pressure to restore what was there. Before disaster, cities can phase action: stop adding exposure in the highest-risk areas, preserve safe receiving land, align school and transport investments, and offer relocation assistance before emergency conditions. Waiting does not preserve the status quo; it lets mortgages, roads, and new development lock communities into a costlier future.
[P1.3] Early planning also protects public money. Repeatedly repairing assets expected to become unsafe crowds out adaptation elsewhere. A plan distinguishes where defenses can work from where they merely postpone larger losses, avoiding the cruelest sequence: encourage rebuilding, withdraw support later, then compensate too late.
[P1.4] Mandatory planning is essential to justice. Without it, affluent residents and mobile firms leave first while renters, elderly people, and poorer households remain with declining services and unaffordable insurance. A planned process can fund comparable housing, protect tenants as well as owners, maintain community networks, and require genuine consultation rather than treating consent as a press release.
[P1.5] Uncertainty and attachment to place justify staged triggers, regular review, and community power—not having no plan. They argue against irreversible eviction, not reversible preparation. The choice is not planning versus a risk-free home, but accountable decisions versus choices made in shelters and fiscal panic after the neighborhood is lost. Cities must plan while residents still have safe destinations and time to shape the journey.

[C1.3] PRO claims this protects vulnerable residents [P1.4], but it actually traps them. [C1.6] Finally, state-mandated retreat plans inevitably reproduce the ugly history of top-down urban renewal. When bureaucrats decide which zones to save and which to surrender, politically connected enclaves will secure defensive seawalls while marginalized communities are designated for managed abandonment, gutting community cohesion and local democracy.

[P2.5] Rejecting the plan because one tool might be chosen is not resilience; it is refusing to compare tools.
