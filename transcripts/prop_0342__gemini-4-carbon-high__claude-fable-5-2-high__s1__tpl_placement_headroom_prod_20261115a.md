[P1.1] Core subjects exist for capabilities so foundational that no student can safely navigate adulthood or citizenship without them. Literacy and numeracy earned that status because industrial society demanded them. Today, generative AI and algorithmic media have transformed the baseline conditions of knowing, working, and voting. Every student now inhabits an environment where synthetic voice clones execute scams, engagement algorithms exploit psychological vulnerabilities, and language models fabricate plausible falsehoods on command.
[P1.2] Navigating that landscape is not a niche hobby like robotics; it is modern civic and cognitive survival.
[P1.3] Relegating AI and manipulation-resistant media literacy to an optional module fails the exact students who need protection most. Optionality creates severe selection bias: students from tech-fluent households or high-achieving tracks opt in, while overloaded or less-supported students opt out. Yet synthetic disinformation, predatory financial targeting, and workplace automation do not ask whether a graduate took the elective. Making epistemic self-defense optional turns basic resilience against manipulation into an inequality multiplier.
[P1.4] Nor can schools rely on brief add-ons or vague cross-curricular integration. Understanding how probabilistic models hallucinate, how persuasive feeds optimize for outrage, and how to verify digital provenance requires cumulative instruction across grade levels. In real schools, optional modules are the first casualties of budget cuts, scheduling conflicts, and exam pressure, while making a skill "everybody's responsibility" across existing classes quickly makes it nobody's priority. Overstretched math or history teachers cannot improvise rigorous curricula without dedicated time, specialized training, and clear standards.
[P1.5] Elevating this domain to a core subject aligns schooling with modern reality. Core status guarantees universal reach, sustained teacher preparation, and regular updates as manipulative technologies evolve. Schools mandate reading so citizens are not helpless before the printed page; in an era of automated persuasion and synthetic reality, defending one's own mind cannot be an elective.

[C1.2] First, the content will not hold still. [P1.5] promises "regular updates," but core subjects run on standards, textbooks, teacher certification, and exams, the slowest machinery in education. Lessons on spotting deepfakes by counting fingers were outdated within months. A light module can be rewritten every summer; a core subject fossilizes what was true when the committee met.
[C1.3] Second, the core timetable is zero-sum. Every weekly hour handed to a new subject comes out of reading, mathematics, science, or history. PRO must name which one loses, because those subjects are the manipulation resistance. A student detects a fabricated historical claim by knowing history, sees through a misleading statistic by understanding statistics, and doubts a miracle cure by knowing some biology.
[C1.4] Skepticism without knowledge is just a posture. Teach "question everything" to a student who knows little and you get cynicism, not discernment: someone who distrusts true and false alike and then believes whichever source feels right.
[C1.5] Third, PRO's strongest argument, equity in [P1.3], is an argument for universality, not for core-subject status. Schools reach every student with many things that are not core subjects, among them health units, civics requirements, and required short courses. A compulsory, focused, frequently refreshed module, reinforced inside existing subjects, reaches the vulnerable student just as surely, and it does so without conjuring a specialist teaching workforce that does not exist or cutting that same student's reading instruction.
[C1.6] So the question is not whether students need defenses against manipulation. It is whether a decade-long standalone subject is the best way to build them. Deep knowledge plus an agile module beats a bloated new core that is obsolete on arrival.
