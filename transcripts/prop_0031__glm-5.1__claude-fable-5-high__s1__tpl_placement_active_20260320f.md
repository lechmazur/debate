[C1.2] Start with the supposed proof case. Minneapolis eliminated single-family-only zoning, and the result was a trickle—by most accounts, only a few dozen triplexes a year materialized, because zoning labels were never the binding constraint. Lot sizes, setbacks, financing, and construction costs were. Meanwhile, the parts of the Minneapolis 2040 plan that actually moved the needle were corridor upzoning and parking reform—exactly the kind of targeted, incremental measures the resolution dismisses.
[C1.3] Banning the district type is a press release; it is not a housing strategy.
[C1.4] Second, density bonuses do something a ban never can: they extract public benefit. When a city says "you may build six stories instead of three if a fifth of the units are affordable," it converts land value directly into below-market housing. A ban legalizes market-rate duplexes and asks nothing in return. In hot markets, that often means tearing down the cheapest existing house on the block to build two expensive units—density without affordability, and sometimes with displacement.
[C1.5] Third, durability. Sweeping mandates provoke sweeping backlash: lawsuits, ballot referenda, state preemption fights, and council majorities elected specifically to repeal them. Incremental reforms that let neighborhoods see fourplexes work next door build the political coalition that makes density stick. A ban won citywide by one vote can be unwound by one election; a hundred successful infill projects cannot.
[C1.6] Finally, uniformity is a bug, not a feature. Wildfire-prone hillsides, neighborhoods on septic systems, and blocks atop failing water mains are not "preserved in amber"—they are places where infrastructure must lead growth. Cities need a scalpel. The resolution hands them a sledgehammer and calls the wreckage justice.
