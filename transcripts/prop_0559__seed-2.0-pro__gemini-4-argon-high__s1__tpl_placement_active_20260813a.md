[C1.2] You are exporting the identities, relationships, and private communications of every parent, friend, and child connected to you—without their knowledge or consent. You may own your relationships [P1.6], but you do not own the other people in them.
[C1.3] Relying on a single user's "active, one-time consent" [P1.4] is not a privacy safeguard; it is a massive security vulnerability. Under this mandate, a predatory app, data broker, or scammer does not need to hack a platform. They only need to trick one user into clicking "transfer" to legally harvest the social map of everyone in that user's orbit. Today, platforms are expected to block bulk data scraping and shut down shady third-party access.
[C1.4] A legal interoperability mandate flips that duty upside down, forcing platforms to build open pipelines for third-party harvesting while stripping them of the ability to vet where shared data goes.
[C1.5] Finally, the claim that no mainstream entrant has succeeded in a decade [P1.5] ignores reality. TikTok and Discord attracted hundreds of millions of users without importing anyone's legacy social graph. They succeeded because they invented new ways to connect—around shared interests and communities—rather than cloning an old friend list. Worse, legally requiring "interoperable" social graphs means regulators must enforce a standardized template for how online relationships work.
[C1.6] That freezes today's incumbent design into law, while burying small startups under complex engineering and liability burdens that only tech giants can afford.
[C1.7] We should not force platforms to compromise everyone else's privacy just to make switching apps convenient for one user.
