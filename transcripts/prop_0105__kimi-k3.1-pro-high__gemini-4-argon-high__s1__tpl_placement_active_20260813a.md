[C1.2] Look at PRO’s own example [P1.2]: someone tells witnesses they intend to kill an estranged partner at a specific time and place. That is already a crime—criminal threats, stalking, or attempted violence—allowing immediate arrest and pre-trial detention. Likewise, if imminent risk stems from suicidal crisis or severe mental illness, states already authorize emergency psychiatric holds.
[C1.3] This exposes the central paradox of red-flag laws. If someone is genuinely an imminent threat to kill, seizing known firearms and walking away is reckless theater. It leaves an enraged, unstable person completely unmonitored on the street with full access to vehicles, knives, arson, or unrecorded guns, and without treatment or custody. Conversely, if the evidence cannot justify an arrest or a psychiatric hold, the state has no business sending armed police to raid a citizen’s home.
[C1.4] Instead, red-flag laws operate as a shortcut around constitutional due process. Emergency orders issue *ex parte*—without the respondent present—so "credible evidence" [P1.1] is initially just an untested, one-sided accusation. Judges face stark asymmetric incentives: denying a petition risks catastrophic blame if violence occurs, while rubber-stamping a weak petition carries zero penalty. Even when a hearing finally occurs [P1.6], the citizen is forced to prove an impossible negative—that they will not commit future violence.
[C1.5] Finally, this mechanism endangers the very people it claims to protect. Sending police to execute surprise confiscation raids against uncharged, distressed citizens invites volatile confrontations, while deterring struggling gun owners from seeking therapy or confiding in family lest honesty trigger a police raid. Courts should address genuine emergencies by securing the *person* through criminal arrest or clinical custody, not through pre-crime property seizures.
