[P1.1] In-person clubs and recurring events build belonging through repeated physical presence that digital platforms cannot replicate. Students who meet face-to-face develop accountability and weak ties that turn into study groups, rides to appointments, and late-night check-ins when someone stops showing up. These mechanisms require shared space and scheduled time; an app notification never exerts the same pressure to appear.
[P1.2] Campus common spaces perform the same function at larger scale. A well-used lounge or courtyard becomes the default setting where marginal students cross paths with others without needing to opt in to a program. Digital wellness tools, by design, demand deliberate access from the very students least likely to seek them out. The isolated user must open the platform alone, often while already avoiding other people, so engagement numbers mostly reflect those already somewhat connected.
[P1.3] Prioritizing physical infrastructure therefore raises the floor for every subsequent digital effort. When clubs and rooms already exist and function, an app can advertise real gatherings and coordinate follow-ups rather than substitute for them. Universities that fund the digital layer first spend resources maintaining parallel systems that compete for attention while the underlying shortage of places to gather remains unaddressed. The result is higher total cost and weaker measured gains in student connection.
