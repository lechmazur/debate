When schools and workplaces ban headscarves, turbans, niqabs, or modest dress and call that “neutrality,” they disguise a majoritarian standard as universal. The burden falls predictably on Muslim women, Sikhs, Orthodox Jews, and others whose conscience or identity requires covering.
[P1.2] That is not equal treatment; it is forced assimilation.
[P1.3] Safety cannot rescue the broad bans. The proposition already preserves safety. Security checks can be done privately or by same-sex officials, hazardous areas can require fitted protective coverings or clear face shields, and identity can be verified at controlled checkpoints. These narrow measures protect everyone without excluding otherwise qualified students and workers.
[P1.4] A blanket ban is overbroad: it removes a person for what they wear, not for any demonstrated risk.
[P1.5] The human cost is concrete, not symbolic. A student told to remove her hijab or niqab is not making a fashion choice; she must choose between education and faith. [P1.6] Opponents may say face coverings block communication or undermine shared identity. But discomfort and unfamiliarity are not safety. [P1.7] Most codes already tolerate wigs, beards, heavy makeup, or elaborate hairstyles, showing the rule is rarely about a consistent standard.
