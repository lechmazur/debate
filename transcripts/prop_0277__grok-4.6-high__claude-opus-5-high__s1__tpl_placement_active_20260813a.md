[P1.1] Tourism is not supposed to wreck the places people come to see. Yet popular cities keep pouring visitors into the same few districts at the same few times of year. That funnel is a policy choice. It should end.
[P1.2] Concentration looks efficient only if you ignore who pays. Historic cores become warehouses for cameras. Rents climb, local shops vanish, and residents treat their own streets as hostile territory. The money concentrates too: a few landlords and tour operators capture the gain, while sanitation, transit, and housing absorb the loss.
[P1.3] Spreading visitors across more neighborhoods turns tourism from extraction into a broader local economy.
[P1.4] The visitor experience collapses the same way. Send everyone to the same square in July and the destination becomes a queue. Authenticity is not a slogan. It is the ability to walk, eat, and look without being packed against a tour group.
[P1.5] Opening other streets and seasons restores that.
[P1.6] Seasonal dumping is equally reckless. Peak months overload water, waste, and housing. Off-peak months leave workers idle and rooms empty. Flattening demand does not shrink tourism; it makes the same volume livable.
[P1.7] Hotels, museums, and transit can plan. Neighborhoods can breathe.
[P1.8] Opponents will say iconic sites are the product, and markets already send people where they want to go. That misses the point. Cities already shape demand with advertising, cruise berths, hotel licensing, and transit design. They are funneling on purpose.
[P1.9] They can funnel less. A livable neighborhood two tram stops away is not a second-rate substitute; it is how a city stays a city. Guarding a few overused blocks while starving the rest is not preservation. It is managed decline of the places that made the city worth visiting.
