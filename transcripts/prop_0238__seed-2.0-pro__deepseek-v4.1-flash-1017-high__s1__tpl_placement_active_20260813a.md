[P1.1] A free society is not defined by how few crimes occur. It is defined by who holds power over whom. This is the core truth this debate turns on.
[P1.2] Security without privacy is not security for the people. It is security for the state, against the people.
[P1.3] Every trade of privacy for security grants permanent, unaccountable power to the authorities that run security systems. That power never goes away when the threat passes. We have all watched this play out: after public crises, states gain warrantless phone monitoring, location tracking, bulk data collection. Not one of these powers, anywhere in the modern world, has ever been fully rolled back once granted.
[P1.4] Instead they get repurposed: to break up strikes, spy on political candidates, intimidate protestors, and police minor administrative offences that had nothing to do with the original threat.
[P1.5] This is not an accident. It is the actual nature of the trade. You are not exchanging a little personal secrecy for a little more safety. You are exchanging the ability of the public to hold power to account, for a temporary, revocable promise of safety.
[P1.6] And here is the decisive point: once those in power can see every call, every private message, every quiet meeting of everyone who might oppose them, there is no longer a free society to protect. Security only matters if it is guarding something worth keeping. When you sacrifice privacy first, you have already burned the thing you claimed you were defending.

Privacy-first has its own ratchet: it protects the landlord who hides discriminatory screening, the platform that hides how it manipulates users, the abuser who hides his location.
[C2.4] When privacy outranks security, the strong get secrecy and the vulnerable get exposure.
[C2.5] The weighing is backwards. A lost privacy right can be repealed, data purged, compensation paid, practices reformed. A person killed, trafficked, or driven from public life by intimidation cannot be restored. PRO’s “temporary security failure” is permanent for its victims.
[C2.6] And political accountability does not require absolute privacy; it requires protection for journalism, assembly, and opposition—protections compatible with security under law.
[C2.7] So the real choice is not surveillance or freedom. It is whether security is accountable and distributed, or absent and privatised. Ranking privacy first hands the vulnerable to private power and leaves public power unable to protect them. Security first is what makes privacy usable for everyone, not just the already safe.
