[P1.1] Hyperscale data centers are not ordinary commercial development; they are concentrated industrial loads with a municipal-scale appetite for electricity and water. The tax incentive is the part of the bill residents never see. A county waives property tax for a decade, the utility builds new substations and peaking capacity, the water system upgrades mains and treatment, and the costs land on households and small businesses. The motion does not ban incentives.
[P1.2] It says: cap them unless the operator pays the grid and water costs its project creates. That is the minimum condition for a subsidy to be a bargain rather than a transfer.
[P1.3] The central test is net local benefit, not ribbon-cutting totals. Hyperscale facilities can bring huge capital investment while creating relatively few permanent jobs and, under abatement, little near-term revenue. When the new load requires capacity that would not otherwise be needed, and when cooling demand competes with drought-stressed supplies, the public balance can be negative even if the building is expensive. A tax cap forces the project to internalize those costs or forgo the special break.
[P1.4] If the project is genuinely valuable, it can still proceed—just without asking everyone else to underwrite it.
[P1.5] Opponents will say incentives attract investment. But incentives are most dangerous where competition becomes a race to the bottom: every jurisdiction is pushed to bid more, and the firm captures the difference. Capping the subsidy unless costs are internalized changes the negotiation from “who will give away the most revenue” to “what is the real net deal?” A dedicated grid connection charge, a water-impact fee, or a binding infrastructure payment is not hostility to data centers. It is pricing.
[P1.6] The proposition is deliberately conditional. Communities may still offer capped incentives, and operators that fully internalize grid and water costs can access more. That protects local fiscal capacity and scarce resources while preserving genuine growth. The alternative—uncapped abatements plus unpriced infrastructure—is how ordinary ratepayers subsidize the cloud.
