[P1.1] Treatment-resistant conditions are a medical dead end. For severe PTSD, treatment-resistant depression, or end-of-life distress, the existing toolkit runs out, and the consequences are not abstract: suicide, permanent disability, families gutted by a person who is still alive but unable to live. Psychedelic-assisted therapy operates through fundamentally different mechanisms than conventional treatments—promoting neural plasticity, disrupting rigid patterns of thought, and enabling emotional processing that standard therapy alone often cannot reach.
[P1.2] That is precisely why it offers promise for the people everything else has failed.
[P1.3] But clinical trials alone cannot meet this need. Trials have narrow inclusion criteria, limited slots, and geographic constraints that leave most sufferers without access. A veteran with severe PTSD in rural America cannot simply enroll in a study. Even where trials exist, the process takes years—years these patients do not have.
[P1.4] The suicide risk in treatment-resistant depression is not a waiting-room problem. Restricting access to trial settings tells the most desperate patients that their best hope is a lottery they cannot enter.
[P1.5] The proposition does not ask for a free market in psychedelics. It asks for careful regulation: trained therapists, medical screening, controlled dosing, supervised sessions, follow-up care. This resembles how we already handle other high-risk but valuable treatments—from ketamine clinics to off-label oncology protocols. The risks are real but manageable under structured oversight, and they must be weighed against the certainty of harm when no effective treatment exists.
[P1.6] The choice is not between safety and access. It is between regulated access with safeguards and a status quo where suffering patients have no options and some will not survive the wait.

[C1.1] The strongest reason to vote against this proposition is not that psychedelics are dangerous. It is that we do not actually know yet whether they work—and leaving clinical trials is precisely how we would stop finding out.
[C1.2] Look at the evidentiary foundations honestly. Psychedelic trials have a well-known blind-breaking problem: participants overwhelmingly know whether they received the drug or a placebo, because a six-hour visionary experience is hard to fake. That means the impressive effect sizes are heavily contaminated by expectancy effects—the same mechanism that makes any novel, dramatic, expensive, labor-intensive treatment look brilliant in early trials. History is unkind to therapies that looked transformative under those conditions.
[C1.3] And the trials are small, short, and run by investigators with financial stakes in the outcome, at a moment when sponsors have already taken the compounds through commercialization pipelines worth billions. This is not a hostile reading; it is the ordinary baseline skepticism we apply to every drug before approval.
[C1.4] The proposition's response is "careful regulation." But regulation cannot manufacture evidence, and the closest real-world analogue—ketamine clinics—shows what happens instead. clinics proliferated ahead of the science, dosing drifted, screening loosened, follow-up evaporated, and we now have a sprawling, uneven industry with a growing abuse problem and no good data on what it actually does to patients long-term. That is the realistic future here: not the protocol in the proposal, but the protocol as it degrades under commercial pressure, applied to patients selected for desperation.
[C1.5] And desperation is the point. Treatment-resistant patients are the population least able to give meaningful informed consent and most vulnerable to hope inflating reported outcomes. Every quack remedy in medical history—from lobotomy to opioid pills for chronic pain—was sold on exactly this logic: these people have nothing else, so how can we refuse them?
[C1.6] The answer: we refuse them the shortcut, not the treatment. Expanded-access programs and larger trials can deliver compassionate access while keeping the data flowing. The proposition offers compassion as a substitute for knowledge. That trade has harmed desperate patients before, and it will again.
