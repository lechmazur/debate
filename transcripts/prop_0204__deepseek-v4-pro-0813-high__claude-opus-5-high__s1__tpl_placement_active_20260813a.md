[P1.1] Critical minerals are not ordinary commodities. Lithium, cobalt, rare earths, gallium, and graphite feed precision munitions, fighter aircraft, radar, submarine batteries, and grid storage. Export restrictions on rare earths have already been used as diplomatic leverage. The state that controls those inputs can slow production, impose export coercion, or collect technical and geological intelligence.
[P1.2] When the buyer is closely tied to a rival state, that is exactly the risk a security review must preempt, not discover after the fact.
[P1.3] That is why the presumption should be denial. Rival-tied firms often hide ultimate control behind funds, nominee shareholders, state banks, and supply contracts. It is unrealistic to expect reviewers to prove harm in time. If an approval is wrong, the rival gains a chokepoint: reserves, processing capacity, customer data, and export routes.
[P1.4] If the block is wrong, capital can seek other projects, but security is not lost. The asymmetry is decisive. A mistaken approval can be irreversible; a mistaken block can be revisited.
[P1.5] The presumption is also targeted. It does not ban all foreign ownership, all commodity trade, or even all acquisitions by citizens of rival states. It applies only when a firm is closely tied to a rival state and seeks critical-mineral assets. It does not end review; it reverses the burden.
[P1.6] The acquirer, which knows its own ownership and direction, must show that the deal creates no control or denial risk. That is a smaller burden than asking the public to accept a chokepoint first and hope to unwind it later.
[P1.7] Opponents will say case-by-case review already works. But without a presumption, that means default permission under opacity. Critical minerals are too close to the core of defense and energy security to treat foreign control as the baseline. The safer default is no, with a real chance for the buyer to prove yes.

[C1.1] Presumptions are not free. Every presumption is a decision to accept a known cost in exchange for an uncertain benefit, and PRO has priced only one side of that ledger.
[C1.2] Start with the claim the whole case rests on: "If the block is wrong, capital can seek other projects" [P1.4]. That is not how mining works. Junior explorers sitting on lithium or rare earth deposits are chronically capital-starved. Mine development runs a decade or more from discovery to production.
[C1.3] When you strike the deepest pool of mining capital and offtake financing off the table, the marginal project does not find a new buyer — it sits undeveloped, or the deposit is sold in a jurisdiction where no one reviews anything. A mine not built for fifteen years is every bit as irreversible as a bad approval, and it leaves us more dependent on the rival's existing refineries, not less. PRO's asymmetry runs backwards.
[C1.4] Second, equity is not control. Ore does not walk. The host state keeps licensing, export permits, environmental approval, taxation, expropriation power, and the ability to impose golden shares, domestic offtake mandates, and board restrictions. A rival-tied minority stake in a Canadian deposit does not give anyone a chokepoint over Canadian rock.
[C1.5] Conditional approval with binding mitigation captures the security benefit while keeping the capital — and it keeps the acquirer inside a supervised structure where we can watch them, rather than pushing the same money into opaque third-country vehicles we cannot see at all.
[C1.6] Third, PRO's own logic defeats its escape hatch. If ultimate control is genuinely hidden behind funds and nominees [P1.3], reviewers cannot verify a rebuttal any more than they can verify a threat. "Show no denial risk" is a demand to prove a negative under acknowledged opacity. That is not a reversed burden; it is a prohibition wearing a procedural costume.
[C1.7] And prohibitions invite reciprocity — against our processing access, our joint ventures, our supply, in a market we do not dominate.
