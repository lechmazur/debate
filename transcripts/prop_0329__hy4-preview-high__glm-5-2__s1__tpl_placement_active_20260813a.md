[P1.1] The regular season is a league's core promise: fair competition and a live bond with the local community. A midseason international trip is not that promise; it is a branding excursion. The motion's test is therefore decisive: when athlete fatigue and local fan disruption outweigh global brand gains, scaling back is not retreat. It defends the product that makes the brand worth anything.
[P1.2] Athlete fatigue from long-haul travel is not a soft complaint. Flying a team across several time zones, often eight or more for a West Coast club, for one regular-season game compresses recovery, scrambles circadian rhythms, and raises injury risk. Recovery is uneven: one club may return to a soft stretch of schedule while another faces a back-to-back, so rest becomes a hidden competitive variable. Fans are sold a genuine contest, not a schedule artifact tilted by jet lag.
[P1.3] If the league's showcase game is decided partly by travel fatigue, the brand is not being expanded; it is being cheapened.
[P1.4] Local supporters are also being asked to pay for the privilege. A London or Mexico City "home" game is usually a home date removed from the building that actually sustains the team. Season-ticket holders lose real value, local gate revenue is replaced by neutral-site tickets, and the team vanishes from its market during the season. This is not abstract disruption: it is a tax on the most loyal stakeholders so a league can sell a one-off spectacle elsewhere.
[P1.5] Global brand expansion, meanwhile, is routinely overrated. A neutral-site game produces a short ratings spike, but durable fandom comes from streaming, local-language content, academies, and offseason tours, none of which require distorting the regular season. Brand growth is a means; competitive fairness and local trust are the foundation. When fatigue and disruption exceed the incremental global gain, leagues should scale back until they can grow internationally without making players and home fans subsidize the trip.
