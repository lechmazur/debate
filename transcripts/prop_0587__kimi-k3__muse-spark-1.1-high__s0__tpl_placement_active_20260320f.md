[P1.1] Start with the metric: global emissions, five years. That framing decides this debate, because the overwhelming majority of emissions over the next five years will occur outside the United States — and domestic subsidies and regulations cannot touch them. A border carbon adjustment can.
[P1.2] First, leakage. Domestic-only policy doesn't eliminate emissions-intensive production; it relocates it. Raise costs on American steel or cement and imports from coal-heavy producers fill the gap. U.S. emissions fall on paper while global emissions barely move — the atmosphere sees no difference between a ton avoided and a ton outsourced.
[P1.3] A border adjustment is the one tool that closes this channel, pricing carbon no matter where it was emitted.
[P1.4] Second, reach. Access to the U.S. consumer market is among the most valuable prizes in world trade. Price the carbon embedded in imports and every major exporter — Chinese steel, Indian aluminum, Turkish cement — faces a choice: decarbonize or pay. That produces abatement precisely where emissions are growing fastest and cuts are cheapest.
[P1.5] This isn't hypothetical: the EU's border mechanism, still only in its reporting phase, has already pushed trading partners to build carbon pricing and emissions-measurement systems to protect market access. A U.S. mechanism covering the world's largest import market multiplies that pressure several-fold.
[P1.6] Third, the five-year clock favors us. Subsidies take years to build factories and clear permitting; regulations spend half the window in court. A border price transmits through supply chains the moment the schedule is announced — firms renegotiate contracts and shift sourcing before the first dollar is collected. And by shielding domestic industry from dirtier competition, it makes domestic climate policy politically durable enough to survive election cycles, rather than being repealed by the next administration.
[P1.7] The alternative being defended here is climate policy that stops at the water's edge while the problem is global. Only a border adjustment follows the carbon wherever it's emitted — and that is why, on the motion's own terms, it wins.
