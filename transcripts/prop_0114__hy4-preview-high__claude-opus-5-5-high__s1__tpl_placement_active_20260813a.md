[P1.1] A daughter quits work for eight years to care for a mother with dementia. She is not "economically inactive." She prevents a nursing-home placement and keeps a citizen alive. Yet each caring year is a year of zeros in her pension record.
[P1.2] At retirement, the state thanks her with poverty and diminished independence. That is not neutral accounting; it is a penalty for work the market and state depend on.
[P1.3] Unpaid care is not worthless. It is invisible because social-insurance ledgers count only market earnings. But care raises future contributors and absorbs health and long-term-care costs that would otherwise fall on public budgets. A pension system that rewards only paid contributions does not discover value; it rigs the metric.
[P1.4] It treats a nurse's taxable shift as work and a daughter's unpaid night shift as nothing, even when both keep a person alive and out of an institution.
[P1.5] Credits fix that distortion without inventing an entitlement. They are not wages for housework or a welfare handout; they are deferred recognition of a shared risk. Dependency is predictable, not a private misfortune. We already credit contributions for unemployment, sickness, and disability, and some public pensions already credit caring years.
[P1.6] Care can be verified through the same routine records—benefit receipts, medical eligibility, dependency status—that governments already use.
[P1.7] Opponents will say cost. But invisibility is the expensive option. An impoverished caregiver does not disappear; she becomes a claimant for means-tested support or institutional care. Credits shift a small, predictable cost forward instead of a large, chaotic cost backward.
[P1.8] The state can pay a little now through social insurance, or pay more later in crisis.
[P1.9] Keep a ledger that penalizes care, or adopt one that counts it. A pension system that ignores care does not save money; it privatizes public goods onto those least able to pay. Credits are how governments stop treating that labor as invisible.
