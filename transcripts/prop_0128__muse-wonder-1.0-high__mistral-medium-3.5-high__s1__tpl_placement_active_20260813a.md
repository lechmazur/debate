[P1.1] Requiring a bachelor's degree to answer support tickets, manage a sales pipeline, coordinate schedules, or supervise experienced staff is not quality control. It is credential inflation, and it makes hiring worse.
[P1.2] A degree is a weak proxy for those jobs. It shows someone could afford tuition, navigate admissions, and persist in classrooms — not that she can fix a technical problem, calm a client, build a spreadsheet, or learn a system quickly. Those skills also come from certificates, military service, self-teaching, freelancing, and prior work. When a posting demands a BA for work a candidate already does well, the employer is not raising standards.
[P1.3] It is shrinking its pool, lengthening vacancies, and calling the predictable shortage a skills gap.
[P1.4] That filter is also expensive. Workers borrow heavily and delay earnings to buy a signal employers could test directly and cheaply through work samples, structured interviews, trial projects, apprenticeships, and training. The burden falls hardest on low-income, first-generation, and older workers who have competence but not pedigree. It rewards access to college over demonstrated ability, leaves good jobs unfilled, and traps capable adults in lower-wage work beneath their skills.
[P1.5] Dropping an unnecessary requirement does not lower the bar; it moves the bar to the job itself. Employers can still demand literacy, numeracy, technical mastery, portfolios, and licenses where safety truly requires them. They simply verify those things directly instead of outsourcing judgment to admissions offices. That widens competition and improves fit, because people are selected for what they can do.
[P1.6] Where a degree genuinely teaches necessary knowledge — medicine, licensed engineering, and similar fields — keep it. Where it does not, requiring it is costly, exclusionary, and anti-meritocratic. Hire for ability to perform, not for having attended.

[C1.1] College degrees signal more than technical competence. They demonstrate a candidate’s ability to commit to long-term goals, meet rigorous standards, and engage with complex systems—traits critical for collaboration, problem-solving, and adaptability in most professional roles. The degree requirement is a low-cost, socially validated filter for these qualities, which are difficult to assess reliably through short interviews, trial projects, or self-reported experience. Without it, employers are overwhelmed by applications from under-qualified candidates, forcing them to develop costly, inconsistent in-house assessments that are easily gamed and hard to scale.
[C1.2] Some major employers that dropped degree requirements for certain roles later reintroduced them after finding that alternative assessments did not yield better hires. Moreover, the degree standard provides a common, objective benchmark that reduces bias in subjective evaluations and ensures a baseline of general education that supports long-term growth and promotion. The burden on workers is often overstated: many employers offer tuition reimbursement, and affordable pathways like community colleges and online programs exist. The core issue is not the requirement itself but unequal access to education; the answer is to expand access, not to lower standards.
[C1.3] Removing the filter would not widen the talent pool; it would flood it with unqualified applicants, increasing hiring costs and reducing the average quality of hires. This is not just about day-one tasks, but about the foundation for long-term contribution and leadership. In jobs where a degree is not strictly necessary for day-one performance, it remains a practical and necessary proxy for the durable skills that drive sustainable organizational success.
