[C1.2] The analogies don't hold. Airfare total-price disclosure requires airlines to be honest about their own price. Unit pricing tells you the unit price of the can in your hand. Neither obligates a supermarket to post a sign saying "the bodega two blocks east is cheaper."
[C1.3] That is what this motion demands, and no amount of "it's just information" rhetoric changes the fact that it's compelled promotion of rivals.
[C1.4] Nor is the information actually hidden. Every fee appears before you tap "order." The customer sees the full twenty-nine dollars and chooses to pay it. That is informed consent, not deception.
[C1.5] My opponent's real complaint is that customers don't comparison-shop — but a market where consumers decline to walk four blocks is not a market failure; it's consumers pricing their own time.
[C1.6] The burden is anything but narrow. To trigger accurately, the app must continuously monitor every independent restaurant's direct-order price, pickup availability, and current status. [C1.7] And "materially higher" is doing enormous work here. Five dollars? Ten percent? Whatever line we draw, lawyers will litigate it for a decade.
[C1.8] Finally, consider who actually gets hurt. Platforms built the demand, the logistics, the payment rails. [C1.9] If it can't, this motion isn't restoring competition; it's confiscating a platform's checkout screen and handing it to competitors who didn't earn it.
