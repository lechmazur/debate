[P1.1] High-frequency trading has fundamentally altered market structure, and not for the better. When a single algorithm can trigger a cascade that evaporates a trillion dollars in market value in minutes—as happened during the 2010 Flash Crash—countries have not just the right but the responsibility to intervene. A financial transaction tax on high-frequency trades is a targeted, proportionate response.
[P1.2] Here is the core problem: HFT firms profit by exploiting speed advantages measured in microseconds. This creates perverse incentives. Algorithms are optimized not for price discovery or genuine liquidity provision, but for detecting and front-running other participants' orders, or for executing thousands of fleeting trades that cancel before completion. Much of the "liquidity" HFT provides is phantom—it exists during calm markets when it's least needed, then vanishes in volatile moments when investors most require it.
[P1.3] A tax that discourages this activity doesn't destroy real liquidity; it sheds illusory liquidity that was never serving the market's core function.
[P1.4] The instability HFT causes is well-documented. Beyond the Flash Crash, we've seen repeated "mini-flash crashes" in individual stocks and ETFs, events where algorithms interact in ways no human intended, creating price dislocations that harm ordinary investors. The speed arms race itself is destabilizing: when multiple firms race to shave microseconds, small technical glitches or latency differences can trigger cascading feedback loops across interconnected markets.
[P1.5] A modest financial transaction tax—fractions of a basis point per trade—would raise the cost of these high-volume, low-margin strategies just enough to discourage the most parasitic trading patterns while leaving genuine market-making and long-term investment unaffected. The reduction in overall trading volume would be real but marginal; what would decrease is precisely the kind of volume that adds risk without adding value.
[P1.6] Countries should impose this tax because market stability is not a luxury good to be traded away for marginal liquidity gains. When the cost of stability is slight and the risk of inaction is catastrophic, the policy choice is clear.

[C1.1] The proposition's fatal flaw is that it taxes the wrong thing. Instability events like flash crashes are failures of market structure—fragmented venues, stale circuit breakers, order-type complexity—not of trading volume. A financial transaction tax doesn't touch any of those causes. It's a sledgehammer aimed at a ghost.
[C1.2] Start with the "phantom liquidity" claim. If HFT liquidity were illusory, removing it would cost nothing. But the evidence of the last two decades points the other way: bid-ask spreads in heavily traded equities have compressed dramatically since algorithmic market-making replaced human specialists, and quoted depth in normal conditions is far deeper than in the pit-trading era. Yes, market-makers withdraw in chaos—so did human specialists; they simply withdrew more slowly and less visibly.
[C1.3] The relevant question is whether spreads and execution costs for ordinary investors are better with HFT than without, and they plainly are. "Slightly reduces liquidity" is doing enormous work in this proposition: taxes on trading are per-trade costs, and market-makers operating on razor-thin margins are the first to widen spreads or exit entirely. "Slightly" becomes a permanently wider cost of capital for every pension fund and retiree in the market.
[C1.4] Second, the tax won't even hit its target. High-frequency strategies are the most mobile activity in finance. Sweden's transaction tax in the 1980s saw the bulk of equity trading migrate to London within years, costing the treasury revenue and gutting the domestic market without improving anything. An HFT-specific tax either gets arbitraged around—trades restructured into derivatives or offshore venues—or becomes so broad it taxes everyone's retirement savings.
[C1.5] Third, if the goal is stability, targeted tools exist that actually address mechanism: batch auctions to neutralize speed races, well-designed circuit breakers, message-level controls that penalize order cancellations directly. These confront the pathology at its source. A tax confronts it at the wallet, hoping the right firms quit.
[C1.6] The proposition asks us to accept real, permanent costs for all investors in exchange for speculative stability gains. That trade fails on its own terms.
