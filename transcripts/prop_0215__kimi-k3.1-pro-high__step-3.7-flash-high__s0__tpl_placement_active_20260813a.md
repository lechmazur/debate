[P1.1] Institutions that helped organize residential schools or forced assimilation cannot treat survival as a legal eraser. When churches or religious orders staffed schools, exercised authority over children, enforced assimilation, or supplied the personnel and legitimacy that made the system possible, they acquired a share of responsibility for the damage. The proposition is deliberately conditional: contribution follows a central institutional role, not mere religious identity or collective ancestry.
[P1.2] The principle is responsibility, not inherited guilt. A present-day worshipper need not be personally blamed for abuses committed generations ago. But an enduring institution is more than its current members. It retains property, records, organizational continuity, and often benefits from the reputation and infrastructure built across that history.
[P1.3] When a long-lived organization causes serious harm, its obligations do not vanish simply because the individuals who made the decisions have died.
[P1.4] Financial repair also addresses harms that apologies cannot reach. Survivors and affected communities may face lasting health needs, disrupted family relationships, lost language, and damaged cultural institutions. Reparative funds can support survivor-directed care, language revitalization, community healing, commemoration, and access to records. These are not attempts to price a childhood or purchase forgiveness.
[P1.5] They are practical ways to return resources to people whose lives and communities bore the cost.
[P1.6] Expect the objection that governments designed these systems and therefore should pay. That identifies another responsible party; it does not excuse institutions that implemented the system or exercised decisive power within it. Shared wrongdoing supports shared repair, not a contest in which each participant points to the other. Nor should payment replace disclosure, independent scrutiny, apologies, or safeguards against recurrence.
[P1.7] The fairest model assesses contributions according to each institution’s role, responsibility, and capacity, with survivor and community participation in governing the fund. That protects smaller bodies from arbitrary demands while preventing wealthy institutions from hiding behind sentiment. An apology without material consequence asks the injured to finance the aftermath alone. Institutions that helped cause the injury should help carry the cost of repair.

[C1.1] The proposition misallocates liability by ignoring that the state was the primary architect, funder, and enforcer of residential school and forced assimilation systems, making it the core responsible party. The “central role” threshold the proposition relies on is unworkably vague, opening the door to arbitrary, disproportionate demands on entities with limited assets already dedicated to community support.
[C1.2] Many religious orders that staffed these schools did so under state coercion, not as voluntary leaders of the abuse regime: governments often threatened to cut funding for unrelated charitable work or revoke operating licenses if orders refused to run state-mandated schools, meaning their participation was compelled, not a freely chosen central role in the harm. Holding extant religious entities liable for historical abuses violates basic intergenerational justice principles. [C1.3] Diverting those funds to a centralized reparative pool would reduce support for the very people the policy is supposed to help, while letting the state avoid paying its full share. This also sets a dangerous precedent of collective punishment, holding current communities accountable for the actions of long-dead prior members—a standard never applied to secular state entities.

[P4.2] A2: No fund can make future political choices literally impossible, including a state-only fund. More importantly, CON identifies a reason to constrain government evasion, not a reason responsible churches should pay zero. A state’s possible misconduct cannot cancel another institution’s responsibility.
[P4.3] That is why PRO still wins. CON’s hardest examples—an order acting under genuine duress, or an unrelated successor merely running a food bank—may justify reduced liability or none. A conditional rule is not refuted by showing that its condition sometimes excludes liability.
[P4.4] Nor must contribution mean raiding unrelated charities or replacing effective local services with bureaucracy. Responsibility can be traced through institutional succession rather than imposed by religious resemblance; existing survivor-led work can be credited where it genuinely forms part of repair; and survivors can participate in allocating support.
[P4.5] The decisive comparison is not imperfect shared repair versus guaranteed state generosity. It is accountable shared repair versus a system in which governments remain responsible while central institutional participants unilaterally excuse themselves. Hard cases deserve careful assessment.
