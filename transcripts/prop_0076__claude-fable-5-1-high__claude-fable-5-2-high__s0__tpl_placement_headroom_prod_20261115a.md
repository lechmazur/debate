[P1.1] Nobody chooses whether they will spend their last years with dementia. Roughly half of people reaching retirement will need meaningful care, and a minority will need it for years, at costs that can swallow a lifetime of savings. That is not a planning problem; it is a lottery. And when a risk is universal, unpredictable, and catastrophic for the unlucky, the civilized answer is pooling it.
[P1.2] We already accept this for hospitals and pensions. Long-term care is the last great uninsured risk of ordinary life.
[P1.3] The alternatives the motion names have each been tried, and each fails on its own terms.
[P1.4] Private savings fail because almost no one can save for a one-in-five chance of needing several hundred thousand dollars, and private insurance has proved nearly unsellable: adverse selection, decades-long horizons, and cost inflation drove most insurers out of the market and left survivors raising premiums on retirees who could no longer afford them.
[P1.5] Means testing fails because it pays only after ruin. It tells a widow to spend down to poverty before help arrives, punishes the thrifty, rewards those who shift assets to children, and creates a cliff where earning or saving a little more costs everything. It is insurance that triggers only once the house is gone.
[P1.6] Families fail not from lack of love but from arithmetic. "Leaving it to families" means, overwhelmingly, a daughter in her fifties cutting her hours or leaving work, forfeiting wages and pension rights, often at the cost of her own health. That cost is real; it simply never appears on a government ledger.
[P1.7] Germany, Japan, and the Netherlands built broad social long-term care systems decades ago. None has collapsed; each spreads a predictable burden across a working population rather than dumping it randomly on the unlucky. The question is not whether society pays for elder care. It already does, chaotically.
[P1.8] The question is whether we do it deliberately.

[C1.1] Pooling a risk is one thing. Building a universal entitlement on a shrinking workforce is another, and the motion asks for the second.
[C1.2] Start with who pays. A universal long-term care system is financed mainly by payroll taxes or premiums on working-age people, and in an aging society each cohort of workers is smaller than the cohort it supports. The old are also the wealthiest age group, with most of that wealth in housing. So a universal system taxes a thirty-year-old renter to protect the house a seventy-five-year-old will leave to heirs.
[C1.3] The "spend-down" in [P1.5] is described as cruelty. It is, bluntly, people using their own assets for their own care before asking strangers to pay. Means testing has rough edges that can be fixed with higher asset floors, smoother tapers and spousal protections. Abolishing the principle fixes none of them.
[C1.4] Second, look harder at the models in [P1.7]. Germany's insurance was designed as partial cover: families still pay substantial out-of-pocket costs, and most care is still delivered at home by relatives. Japan has repeatedly raised premiums and co-payments and tightened eligibility. The Netherlands, the most generous, restructured its system and pushed responsibility back toward municipalities and families because the costs were climbing unsustainably.
[C1.5] These systems have not "collapsed"; they have retreated toward exactly the mix the motion condemns.
[C1.6] Third, the binding constraint is caregivers, not money. A universal entitlement creates demand overnight but does not create a single nurse or aide. A promise that outruns the workforce is rationed by waiting lists, thin hours and low quality, and the daughter in [P1.6] still ends up filling the gap, now while paying higher taxes.
[C1.7] The better path is targeted: a strong safety net for those who truly cannot pay, direct support for family caregivers, and incentives to save and insure. That means help where need is real, not a universal promise the next generation must fund and may not receive.
