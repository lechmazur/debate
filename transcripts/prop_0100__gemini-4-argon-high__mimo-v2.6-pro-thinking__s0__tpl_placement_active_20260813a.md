[P1.3] First, speed and capital efficiency. Wind, solar, and batteries are modular technologies riding steep manufacturing learning curves. They deploy in months or a few years, immediately displacing fossil fuels. Conversely, modern nuclear construction suffers from chronic delays and runaway costs.
[P1.4] Recent flagship projects in the US, France, and the UK have taken well over a decade to build while multiplying initial budgets. Climate capital is finite. Locking up billions of dollars in a reactor that will not generate a single watt until the late 2030s starves rapid renewable deployment that cuts emissions today.
[P1.5] Second, institutional reality in most countries. The vast majority of nations have no domestic nuclear industry. Starting one from scratch requires massive sovereign debt, complex fuel supply chains, non-proliferation oversight, and years just to build a regulatory apparatus before pouring concrete. For most developing and middle-income nations driving future energy demand, nuclear is a bureaucratic and financial impossibility on a climate-relevant timeline.
[P1.6] Solar panels and wind turbines require no nuclear watchdog and scale incrementally to fit any grid.
[P1.7] Finally, the old "baseload" justification has collapsed. Plunging battery costs, regional grid interconnections, and smart demand management now turn variable renewables into reliable systems. In fact, inflexible nuclear reactors are poor partners for cheap renewables: when solar floods the grid at midday, nuclear plants cannot ramp down economically. Renewables are no longer the supplement; they are the backbone.
