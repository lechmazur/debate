[P1.1] Large supermarkets discard mountains of edible food every day—bread still soft, produce still sound, dairy still within date—while nearby food banks ration what they have and landfills vent methane from meals that never happened. That waste is treated as a routine operating cost. It should not be. Requiring large retailers to donate unsold edible food or discount it sharply before disposal replaces a socially destructive default with a workable duty.
[P1.2] These firms already run sophisticated inventory systems. Redirecting near-expiry stock to donation partners, or marking it down hard enough that customers clear it, costs far less than the water, energy, labor, and landfill burden already sunk into that food. Donation converts a total loss into public benefit and, where tax rules allow, a deduction. Sharp discounts recapture revenue that the bin destroys.
[P1.3] Practices like late-day markdowns already move product without erasing ordinary full-price demand. The rule does not confiscate profit; it forbids treating edible food as trash until every reasonable alternative has been used.
[P1.4] Liability and logistics objections do not defeat the case. Existing food-safety standards and donor-protection rules already separate responsible donation from reckless sale. Large chains possess the cold chain, transport, and scale that make compliance feasible; the motion rightly limits the duty to them. Oversight can rest on waste audits and donation logs rather than minute-by-minute policing.
[P1.5] Leaving waste as a normal cost simply socializes the harm. Society pays in hunger, emissions, and squandered inputs while retailers optimize for convenience. A clear requirement—donate or discount sharply, then dispose—corrects the incentive without price controls or public ownership. Edible food should feed people first.
[P1.6] Anything less is a choice we no longer need to indulge.

[C1.1] Supermarkets do not throw food away because they enjoy it. Every discarded loaf is inventory they already paid for. Margins in grocery run thin—low single digits—which means shrink is one of the things managers are most ruthlessly measured on. The waste you see at closing time is not the easy waste; it is the residual tail left after the easy savings have already been taken.
[C1.2] A mandate does not hand retailers a free idea they somehow missed. It forces them to eat the expensive part of the curve, and that changes behavior in ways the motion never budgets for.
[C1.3] Here is the core problem. The motion regulates disposal, not surplus. If "donate or discount" becomes the legal exit, over-ordering stops being a failure and becomes a compliance event. Full shelves sell better; a store that stocks generously and then ships the remainder to a charity has satisfied the law while generating more waste upstream than a store that orders lean and occasionally runs out of bananas.
[C1.4] You end up subsidizing abundance and calling it virtue.
[C1.5] Second, mandated sharp markdowns train customers. Shoppers who learn that the 7pm price is always half off simply shift their trips—cannibalizing full-price sales rather than rescuing food. Retailers respond by buffering more stock to protect the discount shelf, or by shrinking the fresh assortment that generates waste in the first place. Fewer prepared meals, fewer bakery items, thinner produce sections.
[C1.6] That is a real cost, and it lands hardest on smaller-format stores in poorer neighborhoods.
[C1.7] Third, food banks are not an infinite sink. Their binding constraint is refrigeration, transport, volunteers, and sorting time—not donor generosity. Push legally compelled volumes of day-old perishables at them and you transfer the disposal cost to underfunded charities, who then bin it out of public view. Waste laundered is not waste prevented.
[C1.8] The honest question is not whether waste is bad. It is whether a disposal mandate reduces it. I say it relocates it, and charges the public for the illusion.
