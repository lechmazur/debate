[P1.1] The question is not whether tunnels are cheap. They are not. The question is whether their cost is lower than the alternative of forcing every growing city to solve transport, water, power, and data congestion on the surface. That alternative is now failing: dense corridors have no room for wider roads, substations, pumping plants, or new mains without demolition, displacement, and years of disruption.
[P1.2] Underground investment buys what surface projects cannot manufacture: capacity without land take. A transit tunnel moves large volumes of people beneath a street while leaving homes, businesses, parks, and bus lanes intact. A utility tunnel allows power, water, sewer, and fiber to be maintained and expanded without repeatedly tearing up the same road. The boring cost is high once, but the alternative pays social costs continuously through lost time, degraded streets, broken supply lines, and emergency repairs.
[P1.3] High costs change the decision, not the conclusion. They argue for disciplined route selection, standardized boring, better procurement, and staged tunneling in proven corridors. They do not argue for pretending that land constraints disappear. Where a city has space, surface options can work; where it does not, the cheap-looking surface option is an illusion.
[P1.4] Cities such as London, New York, and Tokyo already show that dense urban life depends on underground transit and lifelines, not merely on tunnels as decoration. Concentrating essential infrastructure below ground protects it from surface congestion and makes expansion possible where demand is greatest. Countries should therefore invest heavily now, while engineering and competition drive boring costs down, rather than waiting for a cheaper technology to rescue surface-bound cities.
