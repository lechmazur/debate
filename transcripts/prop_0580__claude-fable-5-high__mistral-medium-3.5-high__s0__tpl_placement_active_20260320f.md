[P1.1] Child poverty is, by definition, a shortage of income. So the most cost-effective cure is the one that converts the highest share of every appropriated dollar into income in poor families' pockets. Monthly child tax credits do exactly that; childcare subsidies do it only indirectly, partially, and for a fraction of poor children.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. A monthly credit is nearly frictionless: it rides existing tax infrastructure, with administrative overhead of a few percent at most. We saw this in 2021, when the temporarily expanded U.S. credit was delivered monthly and child poverty fell dramatically within months—and snapped back when payments ended. That is about as clean a natural experiment as policy ever gets.
[P1.3] Childcare subsidies, by contrast, must pass through a leaky pipeline: eligibility bureaucracies, provider reimbursement systems, and a supply-constrained market where new money predictably bids up prices. A dollar in, far less than a dollar of poverty reduction out.
[P1.4] Second, coverage. Childcare subsidies only help families who use formal, licensed care. That excludes infants whose parents stay home, children cared for by grandparents, rural families with no licensed providers within reach, and parents working night shifts when centers are closed. These are disproportionately the poorest families.
[P1.5] A subsidy you cannot use has zero anti-poverty value. Cash reaches every poor child, every month, regardless of family arrangement.
[P1.6] Third, flexibility. Poor families' binding constraint varies—rent one month, a car repair the next, and yes, sometimes childcare. Cash lets each family attack its own constraint; a subsidy forces everyone to consume one earmarked service whether or not it's their bottleneck. Parents can spend the credit on childcare; they cannot spend a childcare voucher on heat.
[P1.7] Childcare subsidies may serve other goals—maternal employment, labor supply. But the motion asks which reduces child poverty per dollar. On that metric—direct, universal-among-the-poor, low-overhead income transfer versus a leaky, exclusionary, supply-limited service subsidy—cash wins decisively.

[C1.1] Child poverty isn’t just low income; it’s the inability to earn more. Childcare subsidies address this directly by removing a primary barrier to work, enabling parents to increase earnings—each subsidy dollar triggers additional income that multiplies its anti-poverty impact. The 2021 child tax credit experiment, often cited as proof of cash’s power, actually demonstrates its limitation: poverty rates snapped back when payments ended because the credits didn’t change families’ underlying capacity to earn. Subsidies, by contrast, create sustainable pathways out of poverty.
[C1.2] Targeting matters. Childcare subsidies reach low-income working families—the group most likely to see poverty reductions from employment supports. Universal cash, however, flows to all eligible families, including those without childcare constraints or with higher earnings potential, diluting its impact per dollar spent on the poorest children.
[C1.3] The ‘leaky pipeline’ critique of subsidies ignores their economic multiplier. Quality childcare assistance can boost maternal employment by 10–20%, with each new working mother gaining thousands in annual earnings. That’s not leakage; it’s leverage that cash cannot match. Meanwhile, administrative costs for modern subsidy programs are often on par with tax credit delivery when properly designed.
[C1.4] Finally, flexibility isn’t always an advantage. Cash can be diverted to immediate needs or non-essentials, whereas childcare subsidies ensure funds are invested in a proven poverty-fighting strategy: stable parental employment. For cost-effective, lasting poverty reduction, targeted childcare support outperforms universal monthly payments.
