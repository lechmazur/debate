Every swipe adds seconds, and across hundreds of stops those seconds become late buses, missed transfers, and extra buses needed to hold a schedule. Charging a fare on a local bus is like putting a tollbooth at the end of every driveway: the cost of collecting the toll can rival the toll.
[P1.2] Major cities should scrap that toll permanently and fund transit through broad-based taxation, just as they already fund streets, parks, schools, and police.
[P1.3] The case does not need a ridership explosion. The motion grants modest initial gains, and that is enough when the real gains are elsewhere. Fare-free transit removes fareboxes, revenue handling, enforcement, and fare-evasion policing. [P1.4] It ends a regressive fee that hits low-wage riders, seniors, students, and people with disabilities hardest, replacing tangled discount programs with simple access. A broad-based tax asks everyone who benefits—drivers with less congestion, employers with wider labor pools, businesses with more customers—to pay, and it can be progressive.
[P1.5] This is administratively real, not fantasy. Tallinn and other adopters show that replacing the fare line with budget funding keeps service running. Kansas City’s zero-fare buses show the operational logic: remove payment and boarding gets faster, not chaotic.
[P1.6] Judge transit like other public goods. We do not make libraries break even at the door or fire departments charge per call. Even modest ridership gains are worthwhile when the fare is a tax on movement, and broad-based taxation is the honest, stable way to pay for a service we need.
