[P1.1] Peer review is the quality-control system of modern science, and it runs on two assumptions that would be laughed out of any other industry: that skilled experts will do demanding work for nothing, and that nobody needs to see whether they did it well. Both assumptions are failing, and this motion fixes both.
[P1.2] Start with payment. Commercial publishers run some of the most profitable businesses in media, and their core input, expert evaluation, is donated. The result is predictable. Editors routinely describe inviting reviewer after reviewer before anyone accepts.
[P1.3] Reviews arrive late, or arrive as three vague paragraphs written between grant deadlines. The work falls hardest on a small pool of conscientious academics while everyone else free-rides. Payment changes that. It turns reviewing from a favour squeezed into evenings into a professional task with a deadline, a standard, and a price.
[P1.4] It widens the pool to capable people who cannot afford unpaid labour: postdocs, researchers in poorer countries, industry scientists. And it gives journals leverage: if you pay for a review, you can reasonably refuse a lazy one.
[P1.5] Now publication. Today a report vanishes into an editor's inbox. A careless reviewer who waves through a flawed study, or a hostile one who sinks a rival, faces no consequence because no one ever reads what they wrote. Publish the reports alongside the paper and that changes overnight.
[P1.6] Readers see which objections were raised and how the authors answered them. Other scientists can judge whether the paper earned its approval. Reviewers know their reasoning will be read by peers, so they write reasoning worth reading. Journals that already publish review histories show this is workable, not utopian.
[P1.7] The two reforms need each other. Payment without transparency invites box-ticking for a fee; transparency without payment asks unpaid volunteers to take on more scrutiny. Together, you get reviewers who are compensated for effort and accountable for quality.
[P1.8] The opposition must defend a system whose flaws are invisible by design and whose labour is extracted for free. That is not a system worth protecting.

[C1.2] Paying reviewers sounds fair until you ask who pays. Publishers will not absorb the cost of millions of reviews; they will bill authors through higher fees. [C1.3] No one clears a teaching week for a small honorarium. What payment does buy is the wrong motive: rushing through reports to collect the fee and stay in editors' good graces for the next gig.
[C1.4] Publication of reports sounds like accountability until you ask who dares to be accountable. Science is hierarchical. A junior researcher asked to judge a famous lab's flagship paper can be brutally honest behind anonymity; put her name beside a rejection and she is risking grants, jobs, references. So she softens, hedges, praises.
[C1.5] Candor dies and politeness thrives. At the same time published reports invite retaliation, harassment, and grandstanding for an audience rather than quiet error-catching.
[C1.6] Together the vices multiply. A paid, named reviewer is not an independent judge but a contractor performing for rehiring. Like financial raters paid by the banks they graded, the incentive is to approve, to avoid enemies, to write lengthy but toothless commentary that looks rigorous while waving work through.
[C1.7] Better review means faster, tougher, and fairer. Paid public review means slower, softer, and more expensive.
