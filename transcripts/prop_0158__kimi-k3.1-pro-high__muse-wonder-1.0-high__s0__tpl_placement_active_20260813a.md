[P1.1] The choice is not between a spotless transplant system and compensation. It is between a regulated system that rewards a life-saving act and a prohibition that leaves patients waiting while driving desperate transactions underground. Governments should allow compensated donation because strict regulation can save lives without turning access to organs into an auction.
[P1.2] First, prohibition has a human cost. A patient on dialysis may spend years tethered to a machine, hoping a compatible kidney becomes available. Altruistic and deceased donation are invaluable, but where they cannot meet demand, refusing even to permit a carefully governed additional source consigns some patients to avoidable suffering. Compensation can also remove practical barriers to donation—lost wages, travel, and recovery costs—rather than asking donors to bear the entire burden of benefiting someone else.
[P1.3] Second, regulation targets the dangers CON rightly fears. Payment should be fixed and publicly set, not negotiated by the wealthy; organs should still be allocated by medical need, not ability to pay. Independent medical and psychological screening, donor advocates, a reflection period, the right to withdraw before surgery, and guaranteed follow-up care can protect donors. Transparent legal channels also give authorities something prohibition cannot: records, oversight, and a safer alternative to coercive underground brokers.
[P1.4] Third, payment does not automatically destroy consent. Adults often accept risk for compensation; the ethical response is to limit risk, test understanding, and prevent abuse, not declare every paid volunteer incapable of choosing. A donor can sincerely want to save a life and also value financial security. Calling the transaction morally impure does nothing for the patient whose treatment is postponed.
[P1.5] The decisive comparison is not paid donation versus a world with no poverty and no organ shortage. It is regulated compensation versus continued scarcity and illicit markets. CON preserves a comforting label—“donation”—while outsourcing the risks to patients and the desperate. PRO makes sacrifice visible, protects the donor, keeps allocation just, and creates another route to survival.
[P1.6] A government serious about both dignity and lives should permit it.

[C1.1] Compensated donation does not solve scarcity without exploitation; it makes exploitation the mechanism that creates supply. Strict regulation cannot save that bargain.
[C1.2] Consider who will actually sell. A healthy kidney donor faces major surgery, anesthesia, and a lifetime with reduced reserve if illness or injury strikes the remaining kidney. For a financially secure person, a fixed government payment will rarely justify that risk. For someone in debt, facing eviction, or without health security, the same payment becomes pressure disguised as choice.
[C1.3] Regulation can test whether a seller understands the risk; it cannot test whether poverty left a reasonable alternative. If payment is low, it will not produce many organs. If it is high enough to work, it will work mainly by making desperation decisive.
[C1.4] That imbalance also corrodes the transplant system. Transplantation depends on trust: that doctors act only for patients, organs are allocated by need, and a vulnerable person will never be viewed as a source of spare parts. Put a state price on kidneys and that trust frays. Families may be less willing to authorize deceased donation when the body is understood as an asset, and relatives may step back expecting a paid stranger instead.
[C1.5] Clinicians must cut into a healthy person not because medicine requires it, but because a transaction does. Fair allocation for recipients does not clean exploitation of donors.
[C1.6] Legalization also will not reliably displace illicit trade. It creates hospitals, paperwork, and cross-border demand that brokers can hide behind. Sellers who fail screening, need faster cash, or are offered more than the official cap retain an underground option, now beside a legitimized market.
[C1.7] The better response to shortage is stronger deceased-donor systems, paired exchange, prevention, and reimbursing donors’ expenses without paying for the organ itself. Paying for bodies does not overcome scarcity and inequality. It recruits inequality to supply organs, then asks regulation to call the result free choice.
