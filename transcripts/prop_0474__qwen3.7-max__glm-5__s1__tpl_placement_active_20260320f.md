[C1.1] The proposition commits a fundamental category error: it conflates inaction with aggression. A country that "tolerates" botnet infrastructure may simply lack the technical capacity, legal framework, or institutional capability to detect and dismantle it. This is fundamentally different from a state that actively directs cyberattacks against foreign targets. Collapsing this distinction treats the under-resourced the same as the malicious—a mistake with devastating consequences.
[C1.2] The standard of "knowing" harboring is practically unworkable. Attribution in cyberspace is already fraught with uncertainty; proving what a foreign government "knows" about servers within its borders adds another layer of unverifiability. Major powers will inevitably abuse this standard, sanctioning weaker nations under the pretext of "knowing toleration" when the real evidence ranges from thin to nonexistent. The proposition hands powerful states a ready-made pretext for economic coercion.
[C1.3] Consider what "comparable sanctions" means in practice: asset freezes, technology embargoes, financial isolation. These are tools reserved for the most serious offenses—direct aggression, human rights atrocities, declared cyber warfare. Applying them to toleration rather than action is wildly disproportionate. A country with weak cybersecurity infrastructure but no hostile intent becomes economically devastated for a sin of omission rather than commission.
[C1.4] The proposition claims it targets "deliberate, calculated choice," but there's no reliable mechanism to distinguish deliberate shielding from institutional incapacity. Romania and Estonia have robust cyber defense capabilities; many developing nations have virtually none. The same botnet might be "tolerated" in one country simply because no one has the expertise to find it. Treating these situations identically punishes poverty, not malice.
[C1.5] Finally, sanctions have a poor track record of changing cyber behavior. Russia, Iran, and North Korea face heavy sanctions yet continue cyber operations. The proposition's core assumption—that severe penalties will make states evict botnet infrastructure—ignores the evidence: sanctioned regimes typically dig in, not clean up. We should not weaponize sanctions against nations for problems they may be powerless to solve.
