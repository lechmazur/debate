[P1.1] The issue is not whether premium formats are good; they can be. The issue is whether a blockbuster’s schedule may be allowed to become mostly premium when the result is that ordinary viewers cannot reasonably find a standard-price option. That should not be allowed.
[P1.2] Movie theaters are not just selling luxury experiences; they are the gateway to a mass-cultural event. For the first weeks of a major release, most people cannot wait for streaming, and local theaters often have limited competition. If the only convenient evening screenings are IMAX, 4DX, or other high-priced formats, the choice is no longer “pay more for luxury” but “pay more or do not go.” A family of four that could afford standard tickets may face double that cost.
[P1.3] Students, seniors, and casual viewers are effectively excluded.
[P1.4] This matters because blockbuster scheduling is zero-sum in the short run. Screens, showtimes, and staffing are finite. Every extra premium showing displaces a potential standard showing. When premium screens dominate, the theater is not serving a niche; it is restructuring the release around a high-spending minority.
[P1.5] That may boost per-ticket revenue, but it harms ordinary access and treats price-sensitive customers as disposable demand.
[P1.6] The CON may say consumers choose premium formats voluntarily. But voluntary choice requires a real alternative. If standard showings are pushed to inconvenient hours or eliminated, the upcharge becomes a toll, not an option. And if premium formats are genuinely attractive, theaters can program them alongside adequate standard screenings rather than crowding them out.
[P1.7] The proposition does not ban premium formats; it says they should not dominate when the cost is too few affordable screenings.
[P1.8] Finally, accessibility is good for cinema itself. Blockbusters rely on broad cultural participation, word-of-mouth, and repeat viewing. Squeezing ordinary moviegoers out may maximize one weekend’s revenue while weakening the habit of going to movies. The right scheduling rule is simple: premium can enhance a release, but it must not replace ordinary access.

[C1.1] Standard moviegoing is no longer competing merely with other theaters; it is competing with the living room sofa. In an era of massive home displays and rapid streaming releases, standard projection alone is rarely enough to draw audiences out of the house. Premium large-format screens—IMAX, Dolby, and specialized auditoriums—are not an exclusionary luxury. They are the single strongest reason the theatrical blockbuster survives as an event.
[C1.2] When a major spectacle debuts, moviegoers overwhelmingly seek the most immersive experience possible; premium auditoriums routinely sell out days in advance while standard screenings sit half-empty. Exhibitors invest millions in proprietary projection, giant screens, and spatial audio precisely to satisfy that demand. Treating this scheduling as artificial scarcity ignores how modern exhibition works. Blockbuster runs are intensely front-loaded.
[C1.3] Allocating screen capacity to premium formats during opening weeks allows theaters to capture peak revenue during the narrow window when audiences are most eager to participate—after which schedules naturally rebalance toward standard options.
[C1.4] Crucially, those premium revenues subsidize broader access. The capital generated by packed premium screenings keeps theaters solvent, allowing them to offer weekday discounts, preserve standard screens across diverse releases, and maintain operations throughout the year. If we force theaters to suppress their highest-demand screens in favor of lower-margin standard seating that price-conscious viewers often wait to watch on streaming anyway, we erode the financial foundation of exhibition.
[C1.5] PRO warns that premium dominance excludes price-sensitive patrons, but the real threat to affordability is the shuttering of theaters entirely. When exhibitors cannot monetize the few tentpole weekends that underwrite their annual overhead, marginal cinemas close down. A shuttered theater offers zero standard screenings and zero community access. Prioritizing premium formats during peak blockbuster rollouts is not an exclusionary toll; it is the economic engine that keeps the cinema doors open for everyone.
