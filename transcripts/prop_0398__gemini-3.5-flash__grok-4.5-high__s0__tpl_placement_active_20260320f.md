[P1.1] The transition to remote work does not exempt employees or employers from basic economic realities. When a remote worker chooses to relocate from a high-cost hub like San Francisco or New York to a significantly lower-cost region, expecting to retain a headquarters-level salary is both economically unsustainable and fundamentally unfair.
[P1.2] First, maintaining headquarters pay bands for relocated workers violates the core principle of internal equity: equal pay for equal work. Imagine two remote software engineers living in the same low-cost town, performing the exact same role with the same level of responsibility. If one is paid a local market rate while the other receives a massive, unadjusted Silicon Valley salary simply because of where they used to live, it creates an unjust, two-tiered compensation system. This arbitrary disparity destroys team morale and penalizes local hires.
[P1.3] Second, compensation is tied to the cost of labor, not just the cost of living. Employers operate on finite talent budgets. By adjusting salaries to match local market rates, companies can reinvest those savings to hire more workers, expand operations, and spread high-quality job opportunities to historically underserved regions. Forcing companies to pay inflated headquarters wages globally restricts their hiring capacity and ultimately stifles job creation.
[P1.4] Finally, location-based adjustments preserve purchasing power parity. A salary adjusted for a lower cost of living still allows a relocated worker to enjoy an equivalent, if not superior, standard of living compared to their time in a high-cost metro area. Expecting to arbitrage a high-cost salary in a low-cost economy is a windfall, not a right.
[P1.5] To build a sustainable, equitable, and geographically diverse remote workforce, we must accept that compensation must align with the economic reality of where the work is actually performed.
