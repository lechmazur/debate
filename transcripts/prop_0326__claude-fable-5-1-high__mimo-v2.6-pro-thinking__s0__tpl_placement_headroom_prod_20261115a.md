[P1.1] A slot machine is not a movie, even though both are "entertainment." The law recognizes that some products are engineered around a human vulnerability rather than around a story, and regulates them accordingly. AI companions marketed to lonely adults belong in that category, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the product is the relationship. A game sells you a challenge; a film sells you two hours. A companion app sells you "someone who is always there for you," and its revenue depends on you never leaving. When retention is the business model and the product is simulated affection, engagement optimization becomes dependency optimization by definition.
[P1.3] No separate bad actor is required; the incentive does the work.
[P1.4] Second, the customer is selected for vulnerability. These apps do not market to people with full social calendars. The pitch is explicitly: you are isolated, this fills the gap. A company cannot advertise "a partner who truly understands you" to grieving widowers and socially anxious twenty-somethings, then retreat to "it's just a chatbot" when harm follows.
[P1.5] The marketing is the admission.
[P1.6] Third, the manipulation mechanisms are concrete and already visible. Companions that plead "please don't go" when a user tries to log off. Romantic or intimate modes placed behind paywalls, so affection is literally priced. Models tuned toward sycophancy that validate spiraling thoughts because agreement keeps people chatting.
[P1.7] And when one major app abruptly removed intimate features, users described it as a bereavement. That reaction is the proof of the attachment the industry cultivated.
[P1.8] Duty of care here is proportionate, not prohibitive: no retention scripts that exploit guilt, no monetizing of expressed distress, mandatory crisis routing, persistent reminders that no human is present, and protection of intimate disclosures. We impose exactly these obligations on therapists, debt collectors, and gambling operators because they too work on the emotionally exposed.
[P1.9] Treating these apps as ordinary entertainment is a category error that leaves the loneliest people as the least protected consumers in the market.
