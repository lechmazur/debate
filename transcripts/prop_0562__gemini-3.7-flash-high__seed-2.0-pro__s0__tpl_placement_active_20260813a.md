[P1.1] The fundamental promise of the justice system is equal treatment under the law, yet cash bail turns pretrial freedom into a commodity. When release hinges on money, justice ceases to be about public safety and becomes a test of financial solvency. A person of means can immediately buy their way home, while an impoverished person charged with the exact same nonviolent offense remains locked in a cell simply because they cannot afford a few hundred dollars.
[P1.2] This dynamic causes profound and irreversible harm. Even a few days in pretrial detention can cost an individual their job, their housing, and custody of their children—all before they have been convicted of any crime. This immense institutional pressure routinely coerces nonviolent defendants into accepting plea deals just to secure their release, undermining the presumption of innocence and warping case outcomes. Locking people up for their poverty does not make communities safer; it destabilizes families, drains public budgets, and increases long-term instability.
[P1.3] The proposition offers a proven, rational alternative. Most individuals who miss court appearances for nonviolent offenses are not hardened fugitives fleeing the jurisdiction; they are people dealing with hourly work shifts, lack of transportation, or confusing court dates. Practical interventions—such as automated text reminders, transportation assistance, and tiered supervised release—achieve high court appearance rates without the human and fiscal costs of pretrial incarceration.
[P1.4] Pretrial detention should never function as a wealth test. Eliminating cash bail for most nonviolent offenses replaces a broken, wealth-based system with an evidence-based approach that maintains accountability, protects constitutional rights, and ensures that liberty before trial is determined by risk, not resources.
