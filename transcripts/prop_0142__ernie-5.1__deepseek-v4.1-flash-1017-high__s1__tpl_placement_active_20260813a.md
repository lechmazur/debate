[P1.1] Every day, a teenager scrolls through feeds saturated with impossibly smooth skin, impossible waists, and AI‑generated bodies that never existed. These images are not neutral illustrations; they are engineered to sell products by exploiting a developmental window when self‑image is still forming. That is a minimal, proportionate safeguard, and it is necessary.
[P1.2] First, the psychological evidence is clear: exposure to digitally altered bodies is linked to body dissatisfaction, eating‑disorder risk, and lowered self‑esteem among adolescents. Traditional photo‑editing already pushes an unattainable norm; AI tools now can fabricate entire physiques that no human can achieve, amplifying the distortion. When a brand uses such imagery to market cosmetics or fashion to a 13‑year‑old, it is not merely advertising—it is shaping a distorted self‑concept during a critical period.

[P1.4] Third, industry self‑regulation has proven insufficient. Voluntary codes on influencer marketing have been repeatedly breached, and platforms lack consistent enforcement. A prohibition with a clear‑disclosure requirement creates a legal floor that complements existing consumer‑protection frameworks, just as bans on tobacco ads to youth did decades ago.
[P1.5] The opposition will likely argue that such a ban stifles creativity or free speech. But the right to market does not include the right to deceive a vulnerable audience with fabricated bodies.
