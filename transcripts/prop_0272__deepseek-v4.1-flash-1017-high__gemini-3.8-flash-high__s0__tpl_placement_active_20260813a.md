[P1.1] After-school time is scarce, and public schools are the one institution that can give every child a fair shot at using it well. The proposition is about sequence, not prohibition: sports, arts, and unstructured play should come first because they deliver developmental goods that purely digital enrichment cannot replace and that many families cannot buy elsewhere. That is the comparison that matters: not which program sounds most modern, but which use of scarce hours reaches the most children with what they cannot get alone.
[P1.2] These activities are not frills. Sports teach effort, teamwork, and physical confidence; arts give children ways to make meaning and persist through imperfect work; unstructured play is where they practice negotiation, self-direction, and friendship without an adult scripting every move. A child who has spent the school day seated needs movement and face-to-face negotiation more than another module.
[P1.3] Access is the second reason. A public school gym, stage, art room, and playground are shared infrastructure. Expanding them lets the child whose parents cannot afford club fees or a ride participate on equal terms. Purely digital enrichment often assumes reliable devices, bandwidth, and a caregiver at home—advantages that are not evenly distributed.
[P1.4] It can quietly widen the gap while looking innovative. Scalability is not the same as value: a video can be watched alone at midnight, but a team, a stage, and a pickup game require other people and a place.
[P1.5] The alternative is not neutral. If schools leave afternoons to screens and private pay-to-play programs, the children with the least get the least. A school that buys another digital platform while its playground is locked and its gym is dark has not enriched childhood; it has digitized neglect.
[P1.6] Digital tools can supplement later. But priority should go to the experiences that build bodies, friendships, and creativity in the same building—especially when school is the only place many children can access them.
