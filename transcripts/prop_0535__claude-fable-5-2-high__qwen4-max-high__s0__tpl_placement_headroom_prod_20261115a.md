[P1.1] A person diagnosed with early Alzheimer's sits down, fully competent, and writes: "When I no longer recognize my children, do not treat my pneumonia." She watched her mother decline. The question is whether that sentence means anything when the moment arrives, or whether it becomes a suggestion that anyone at the bedside can overrule.
[P1.2] First, a directive is the only instrument that lets a person author the end of her own life. Its entire purpose is to speak when she cannot. A directive that holds only while she can still confirm it is useless exactly when needed. We do not void a will because the testator is no longer around to reaffirm it.
[P1.3] Second, the alternative is never neutral. If the directive does not govern, someone else does: a guilt-stricken son, a liability-conscious hospital, a physician with different convictions. Weakening directives does not hand power to the patient with dementia; it hands power to whoever stands nearest the bed. And this cuts both ways: the person who wrote "do everything" deserves protection from relatives who think she is better off gone.
[P1.4] Third, the proposition already contains its safety valve. Clear evidence of materially changed values releases the directive. What it refuses to accept is counterfeit evidence: a pleasant afternoon, a smile at ice cream, a flinch at a needle. Those are moods and reflexes, not a revised judgment about what makes a life one's own.
[P1.5] A person who spent decades saying "never let me live like that" has not changed her values because she can no longer remember holding them.
[P1.6] So the burden belongs with those who would override. Make directives unreliable, and you take from every newly diagnosed patient the one assurance that makes this disease bearable: that the person they are now still gets a say.

[C1.1] Notice what the exception requires. To escape her own directive, the woman with dementia must produce clear evidence that her values materially changed — while the disease has taken her memory of those values, her ability to compare states, and her capacity to explain herself. PRO has built a safety valve that only the competent can turn. That is not a safety valve; it is a lock.
[C1.2] Then the remaining evidence is ruled out in advance. Contentment, appetite, delight in a grandchild's visit, protest when a needle comes — PRO calls these moods and reflexes, "counterfeit evidence." But those are the only vocabulary dementia leaves. If calm, sustained, observable enjoyment of her days cannot count as evidence of what she now values, the standard is not demanding; it is unsatisfiable by construction.
[C1.3] The will analogy hides the problem. A testator is dead; nothing of him remains to be harmed. The woman who no longer recognizes her children is here, eating, warm, capable of pleasure and of terror. Her earlier sentence was a forecast about a stranger she imagined, written in the frightened weeks after diagnosis, when people routinely overestimate how unbearable later stages will be.
[C1.4] Forecasts deserve weight. They do not deserve to override the person they were about.
[C1.5] And the errors are not symmetric. Ignore a directive and you may prolong a life its author judged undignified — a serious harm, but revisable through palliation and further refusal. Enforce one against a contented patient and you end a life that its present occupant is not asking to end. There is no correcting that.
[C1.6] Nobody needs "whoever stands nearest the bed." The alternative is structured best-interests judgment: the directive as powerful evidence of her settled commitments, tested against her actual condition, with review. That is not making directives meaningless. It is refusing to make them unanswerable.
