[P1.1] The comparison is not the average graduate’s wage premium against a caricature of vocational training. It is what each additional subsidy dollar does for the marginal student. Expanding enrollment pays more people to attempt a long route whose rewards depend heavily on completion. Paid apprenticeships turn spending into employment, supervised skill formation, and an employer-verified record.
[P1.2] A young person otherwise liable to stop out with debt gains years of earnings and experience instead.
[P1.3] That timing compounds. Wages grow with demonstrated competence, responsibility, and professional networks. An apprentice in industrial maintenance, healthcare, or software operations can earn while learning, then progress into senior technical, supervisory, or further-credentialed roles. College enrollees often sacrifice years of earnings before acquiring comparable workplace evidence.
[P1.4] CON must count foregone pay, noncompletion, debt, and delayed progression among those newly induced to enroll, not just graduate salaries.
[P1.5] Employer linkage also answers a question colleges often leave to students: which skills lead to a job? Firms participate because they need productive workers; apprentices receive current equipment, mentoring, and a credible hiring pathway. Portable occupational standards and classroom instruction prevent capture by one employer or obsolete technology. Workers can add degrees later, rather than treating a degree as the only respectable starting point.
[P1.6] The college premium does not rescue CON. Much of it reflects who attends and completes college, not the causal gain from persuading additional students to start. Expanding seats does not guarantee advising, teaching quality, or completion. Subsidizing attempts is not producing capability.
[P1.7] This is not opposition to college for those who benefit; it is about the stronger marginal return. Paid pathways combine income, human capital, employer demand, and options for further education. Broader enrollment subsidies stake everything on completion and a later payoff. On long-run earnings, apprenticeships reach people whom expanded enrollment leaves exposed to failure.
