[P1.1] Large event organizers should reserve a meaningful share of tickets for local residents or members before opening dynamic general-sale markets because those markets optimize for price, not for the social base that makes large events possible. A stadium concert, city marathon, or playoff game does not happen in a vacuum. It uses public streets, transit, policing, and the patience of neighbors; it is sustained by fans who attend regularly, buy season tickets, and treat the event as part of their civic identity.
[P1.2] If the first and only rule is “highest dynamic bidder wins,” locals and members are often crowded out by bots, resellers, tourists, and wealthier latecomers. The result is not merely unfair; it hollows out the event. Empty local seats, hostile neighborhoods, and fan backlash become rational responses.
[P1.3] A reservation is not central planning and it is not a ban on dynamic pricing. It simply says: before surge pricing begins, set aside a meaningful block—enough to be real, not symbolic—for people with a stable connection to the place or institution. The remaining tickets can still be sold dynamically, capturing revenue and allocating scarcity to those willing to pay more. That balance matters because willingness to pay is a poor proxy for legitimate interest.
[P1.4] A visiting speculator may pay more for a cup final ticket than a lifelong supporter, but the supporter’s claim is deeper: the club depends on that loyalty, and the city’s cultural life depends on residents being able to participate in major events held in their own streets.
[P1.5] The decisive point is legitimacy. Large events need public permission, political goodwill, and community tolerance. Reserving local or member tickets before dynamic sales reduces exploitation, protects access, and gives organizers a stable social license. The proposition is modest, pro-market, and necessary.

[C1.2] Cousins' flats, forwarded utility bills, and quiet WhatsApp markets do the rest. If the ticket stays cheap and the demand stays high, the gap gets captured by someone. PRO's mechanism decides *who* gets the windfall, not whether the windfall exists.
[C1.3] And look at who that someone is. "Local resident" near a downtown arena or a Premier League ground is often a comparatively affluent person who won the property lottery. "Member" is frequently the incumbent insider who already holds the season ticket, or who could afford the membership fee years ago.
[C1.4] Meanwhile the person actually priced out is the teenager three hours away, or the immigrant family in the next city who saves for a single show. PRO's policy takes tickets away from precisely those people and gives them to the entrenched. That is not redistribution; it is a moat with a civic vocabulary.
[C1.5] Worse, the carve-out is paid for by the outsider. Shrink the yield-managed pool and the remaining seats clear higher. The distant, poorer fan subsidises the well-placed local's discount.
[C1.6] If the real goals are affordability and anti-exploitation, there are instruments that hit them directly: verified-fan registration and identity-linked entry to break bots, hard resale price caps, face-value lotteries, and a genuine block of low-priced seats sold cheaply to anyone. Those help poor fans regardless of postcode. Locality is simply the wrong axis.
[C1.7] As for social license — neighbours are compensated by noise controls, transit money, tax revenue, and jobs, not by seats a tiny fraction of them will ever claim. And the travelling fan filling the hotels is the one generating the local benefit PRO says the event owes the city.
