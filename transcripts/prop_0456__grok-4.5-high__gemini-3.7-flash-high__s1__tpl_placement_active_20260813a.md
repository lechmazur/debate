[P1.1] Compulsory licensing is how copyright has always solved the clash between exclusive rights and large-scale cultural reuse. Generative AI training is the next instance of that clash. Publishers, labels, and studios hold the catalogs that make industrial-scale training feasible. They should be allowed to train on those works without chasing every individual permission—but only if they pay into a compulsory scheme that returns real money to rightsholders.
[P1.2] Individual opt-in licensing collapses under the volume. A single model may absorb millions of texts, tracks, or frames. One-by-one negotiation freezes development or concentrates power in the few firms that already own clean data or can buy silence. Blanket prohibition treats every training pass as infringement, fuels endless litigation, and still leaves models training overseas on the same material.
[P1.3] Neither extreme works.
[P1.4] A compulsory license does three things at once. It legalizes the training act for these intermediaries so creative industries can compete rather than litigate themselves into irrelevance. It imposes a payable duty—rates set by statute or tribunal, collected on a predictable base such as commercial model revenue or catalog usage. And it routes compensation to authors, performers, and smaller rightsholders who otherwise capture nothing when their work dissolves into statistical weights.
[P1.5] Music already lives with mechanical licenses and collecting societies. Radio never required a signature from every songwriter before a song aired. The principle is identical: efficient access paired with guaranteed payment. Training is not marketplace substitution; it is non-consumptive analysis that still depends entirely on protected expression.
[P1.6] That dependence is why a fee is justified and why pure free-use claims overreach.
[P1.7] Without the payment condition, permissionless training becomes a wealth transfer from creators to model owners. Without the permissionless side, the fee never materializes because training stops or flees. The proposition ties those ends together. That is the only stable equilibrium.

Generative AI does the exact opposite. PRO labels training “non-consumptive analysis” [P1.5], but its commercial purpose is consumptive market substitution—ingesting protected human expression to generate synthetic outputs that compete directly against the artists who built the dataset.
[C1.2] Forcing creators into a compulsory scheme strips them of copyright’s defining pillar: the right of consent. Under this motion, an author, illustrator, or voice actor cannot refuse to have their life’s work harvested. When creators lose the power to withhold their work, they lose all bargaining leverage; they cannot protect their unique artistic style, nor can they opt out of training systems that undercut their livelihood. A statutory fee in this context is not fair remuneration; it is an involuntary, state-mandated severance package.
[C1.3] Furthermore, the motion uniquely empowers corporate gatekeepers at the expense of individual talent. Decades of experience with collective licensing and streaming royalties demonstrate that statutory pools are overwhelmingly captured by major publishers, studios, and record labels. Individual freelancers, novelists, and session musicians receive negligible micropayments, while large studios use permissionless access to build proprietary models that eliminate human hiring in future productions.
[C1.4] Finally, PRO presents a false choice between total litigation paralysis and compulsory surrender. Dynamic voluntary licensing markets are already emerging, allowing rightsholders to negotiate genuine market value, establish ethical standards, or withhold bespoke works. Imposing a compulsory regime short-circuits this nascent market, caps creator compensation at bureaucratic rates, and permanently institutionalizes non-consensual harvesting.
[C1.5] Copyright must remain a shield for human agency, not an open pipeline that forces creators to train their own competition.
