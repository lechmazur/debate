[P1.1] The central question is not whether climate-vulnerable cities will eventually retreat, but whether that retreat is governed or forced. The proposition asks cities to begin mandatory long-term relocation planning before repeated disasters make the decision for them. That is the safer, fairer, and more democratic path.
[P1.2] Long-term planning turns retreat from an emergency scramble into a managed transition. It lets a city identify which neighborhoods face existential flood, storm-surge, wildfire, or subsidence risks; designate safer receiving areas; phase infrastructure, schools, and services; and create buyout, compensation, and job-transfer rules before lives are lost. A city that plans can say: these parcels will become wetlands or buffers, these communities will move over fifteen years, and residents will not be abandoned to repeated loss. Without that plan, the same city after each disaster faces the worst possible moment to decide: exhausted households, shattered tax base, emergency rules, and pressure either to rebuild in harm’s way or to displace people with no coherent destination.
[P1.3] Mandatory planning is essential because the hardest choices will otherwise be avoided. Local officials face short election cycles, shrinking property-tax revenues, and intense pressure to promise normalcy. Voluntary, piecemeal action tends to arrive late, favor wealthier residents who can leave, and leave renters, informal settlers, and low-income homeowners with the least protection. A mandatory framework forces disclosure, budgeting, and public participation while there is still time to shape outcomes.
[P1.4] It also protects community ties by moving neighborhoods together rather than scattering them after catastrophe.
[P1.5] The opposition may say planning is premature or too costly. But the costliest option is repeated disaster-driven retreat: emergency evacuations, temporary shelter, duplicated rebuilding, and permanent trauma. If retreat becomes unavoidable anyway, beginning now is not alarmism; it is responsibility. Cities should plan while they still have choices.

You don't get an orderly fifteen-year transition—you get a run on the neighborhood, capital flight, and a hollowed-out tax base that guts the very services PRO says will support residents through the move.
[C1.3] Mandatory planning can manufacture the emergency it's meant to avoid.
[C1.4] Second, "mandatory" assumes governments know enough, right now, to lock in the correct answer. Climate risk models for flooding, storm surge, and wildfire shift substantially as data improves; a mandated plan drafted today may misidentify which neighborhoods are actually doomed, wasting political capital and community trust on relocations that turn out unnecessary, while under-protecting areas the models missed. Once a mandatory framework is on the books, reversing course is politically brutal—no official wants to be the one who "cancels the evacuation."
[C1.5] Flexibility, not premature commitment, is what real uncertainty demands.
[C1.6] Third, this falls hardest on the very communities PRO claims to protect. Low-income and minority neighborhoods are already stigmatized and under-invested; a state-mandated relocation designation becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy of disinvestment, echoing historic redlining, without the compensation ever actually arriving on time.
[C1.7] The better path is targeted, voluntary, trigger-based adaptation: seawalls, buyout programs activated by actual repeated loss, and flexible zoning—responsive to real conditions, not administrative guesses locked in years early.
