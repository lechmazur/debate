[P1.1] The core question is simple: when a driver is moving through traffic, should basic safety and visibility functions be operable by feel, or should they require looking at a glass panel? The answer is physical buttons.
[P1.2] Cars are operated under conditions hostile to touchscreens: vibration, gloves, rain, bright glare, cold fingers, child seats, stress, and split-second hazards. A physical switch for hazards, defrost, or climate gives a fixed location, tactile confirmation, and often a single action. The driver can keep eyes on the road and hands near the wheel. A touchscreen usually requires searching an icon, pressing accurately, maybe confirming a submenu, and it gives no reliable feedback without a glance.
[P1.3] That extra glance is exactly what safe-driving design should minimize.
[P1.4] These functions are not trivial conveniences. Defrost and climate control govern windshield visibility. Hazard lights are a warning device used during breakdowns, debris, sudden stops, or emergency scenes. If a driver must navigate a menu while traffic approaches from behind, the interface has made a safety response slower and more error-prone.
[P1.5] Muscle memory matters precisely because emergencies degrade attention; a button can be found instinctively.
[P1.6] Touchscreens can be useful for navigation, media, and settings. But routing core driving functions mainly through screens privileges styling, dashboard minimalism, and software flexibility over basic human factors. It also creates common-mode failures: a frozen display can strand controls that used to be mechanical and independent. Physical controls remain legible, operable, and dependable when software lags, reboots, or becomes cluttered.
[P1.7] Even a well-designed screen cannot supply the same zero-glance certainty as a dedicated switch.
[P1.8] The practical standard is whether an ordinary driver—including older drivers, new drivers, or someone wearing winter gloves—can operate a critical function quickly, confidently, and without diverting vision. Physical buttons meet that standard better for these functions. For that reason, automakers should keep them, not migrate them mainly into touchscreens.
