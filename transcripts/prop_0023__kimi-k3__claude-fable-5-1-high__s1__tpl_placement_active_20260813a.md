[P1.1] A crypto exchange holds your money, matches your orders, quotes prices, lends against your positions, and sometimes trades against you. Strip away the branding and that is a broker-dealer running an exchange. The question is whether we regulate institutions by what they do or by what they call themselves. Function must govern, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the failure modes are identical, so the cures should be. FTX collapsed because customer assets were commingled and quietly funneled to an affiliated trading firm. Celsius and Voyager failed the same way: no custody segregation, no capital cushion, no audited books. These are not novel blockchain pathologies; they are the oldest abuses in finance, and securities law already built the instruments against them — segregation of customer property, net-capital rules, disclosure, record-keeping, conflict-of-interest limits.
[P1.3] The lightly supervised category offers none of these, and the losses from that gap fell overwhelmingly on retail savers.
[P1.4] Second, the "distinct light category" is not a regulatory theory; it is regulatory arbitrage. Firms performing broker-dealer functions migrated to the weakest available label precisely because it was weak. The result was a race to the bottom, with billions in customer assets overseen less strictly than a small-town stockbroker's order book. Whatever label one pins on any individual token, the platform's own conduct — custody, matching, dealing — is what should trigger these obligations.
[P1.5] Third, sound regulation grows markets rather than killing them. The deepest capital markets in the world are also the most heavily regulated, because participation requires trust. Legitimate crypto firms already advertise licenses and audits as trust signals. Nothing about broker-dealer status prevents innovation in settlement or custody technology.
[P1.6] What it prevents is innovation in hiding liabilities.
[P1.7] The other side must explain why a platform custodying billions in retail savings deserves less oversight than the broker across the street. Until they can: same business, same risks, same rules.

[C1.6] Firms that genuinely tried to register found no workable path, only litigation. That is regulation by lawsuit, and its predictable effect is to push trading to offshore venues, the exact places where retail savers were hurt.
[C1.7] The alternative is not nothing. Japan after Mt.
