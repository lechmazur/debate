[P1.1] The core question is what should happen by default when political speech is contested: erase it, or annotate it. The answer should be annotation, for three reasons that hold even when notes are imperfect.
[P1.2] First, error costs are asymmetric. Political claims are exactly the category where truth is murkiest, stakes are highest, and platforms are least trusted to referee. When a takedown is wrong — and on contested political claims, many will be — the harm is severe and self-inflicting: the speaker becomes a martyr, the claim migrates to platforms with no moderation at all, and the platform hands its critics proof of bias. The lab-leak episode is the canonical case: a hypothesis suppressed as misinformation later became a mainstream position, and the suppression itself became a lasting argument against all moderation.
[P1.3] When a community note is wrong, by contrast, the underlying speech still exists, the note can be corrected, and the error is visible and contestable. A governance system should fail safe, and context fails safer than deletion.
[P1.4] Second, notes actually persuade where takedowns cannot. Deleting a post does nothing for the millions who already saw it and screenshot it; a note travels with the claim and reaches the same audience. And because bridging-style algorithms surface notes only when raters who usually disagree both endorse them, the correction arrives with cross-partisan credibility no trust-and-safety memo can match. Takedowns say "trust us"; notes show their work.
[P1.5] Third, this is a question of emphasis, not abolition. Illegal content, coordinated inauthentic behavior, and direct incitement should still be removed. But for the vast middle — misleading statistics, exaggerated claims, decontextualized clips — context scales, deletion doesn't. No platform can adjudicate millions of political posts daily; it can host a system where informed users add context in hours.
[P1.6] So the comparison is simple: a system that corrects in public, at scale, with legitimacy — versus one that censors in secret, slowly, and radicalizes its own errors. Context should lead.

[C1.2] Notes also depend on voluntary participation that is uneven and gameable. Coordinated groups can delay or dilute notes on favored claims while amplifying them on opponents. Platforms cannot reliably predict or correct these imbalances before damage occurs. Takedowns, by contrast, let platforms apply consistent rules to the worst cases without waiting for crowd consensus.
[C1.3] The persuasion claim for notes collapses under the same test. Most political posts that require intervention are viewed by narrow audiences who already share the claim’s premise. [C1.4] Finally, treating notes as the default shifts responsibility onto users who lack both incentives and tools to police millions of posts. Platforms already struggle to recruit and retain enough high-quality raters. Expanding that system while shrinking takedowns simply widens the period during which false or incendiary material operates without friction. For contested political speech that carries immediate real-world risk, speed and certainty still favor removal over annotation.
