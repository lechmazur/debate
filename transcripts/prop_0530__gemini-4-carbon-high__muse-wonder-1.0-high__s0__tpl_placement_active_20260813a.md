[P1.1] Atrocity and forced labor strip people of bodily autonomy in life; unilateral outsider control over their graves and camps strips their communities of agency in death. In ordinary law and ethics, next of kin decide how human remains are handled and mourned. Mass murder or enslavement should never extinguish that right and transfer custody of the dead to state bureaucracies, private landowners, or museum curators.
[P1.2] Substantial control does not mean banishing scientists or inventing history; it means binding authority rather than toothless consultation. Advisory panels let developers, ministries, and museums listen politely before doing whatever is cheapest or most marketable. Substantial control gives descendant communities real governance—setting ethical limits on excavation, vetoing degrading displays, and shaping public explanation alongside forensic and historical specialists.
[P1.3] First, this authority prevents a second violation of the victims. For generations, institutions excavated Indigenous graves and enslaved peoples’ burial grounds, storing ancestral bones in museum drawers or displaying remains behind glass against living communities’ deepest beliefs. At Holocaust killing sites, Jewish religious law broadly forbids disturbing graves, requiring non-invasive mapping where outsiders might otherwise dig. Scientific curiosity does not override obligations to the dead; when descendant communities direct how sites are investigated, archaeology adapts—using respectful methods rather than treating victims as specimens.
[P1.4] Second, descendant governance protects historical truth better than institutional custody. The real-world alternative is rarely neutral objectivity; it is control by states, tourism boards, or landowners with strong incentives to sanitize the past. Governments routinely mute their own complicity in forced-labor camps and state terror, while commercial operators have marketed plantations as picturesque wedding venues that erase slavery. Descendant communities have the fiercest stake in defeating both state amnesia and voyeuristic dark tourism, contributing oral histories and lived context that perpetrator archives deliberately omitted.
[P1.5] Without substantial control, victims’ remains and suffering stay someone else’s property. Empowering descendant communities restores dignity to the dead and honesty to public memory.

[C1.1] Next-of-kin rights do not scale into group ownership of history. A spouse deciding a burial is an identifiable person exercising a personal duty. A descendant community centuries after enslavement, or scattered across a diaspora after genocide, is a broad political constituency. Giving it substantial control transfers public evidence and public memory to whoever claims to speak for the dead.
[C1.2] That claimant is rarely clear or united. Descendants disagree over religion, excavation, DNA testing, reburial, and display. Communities also overlap: the same camp, grave, or plantation may hold victims from several ethnic, religious, and national groups, plus survivors, local residents, and families still seeking identification. Substantial control forces officials to anoint one leadership, freeze internal dissent, and privilege the best-organized faction over quieter relatives with equal loss.
[C1.3] Mass graves are also crime scenes, not only places of mourning. Independent excavation preserves evidence, identifies remains, and can support prosecutions or refute denial. If a community council can veto digging, delay testing, or limit what investigators may publish, evidence decays and other families lose answers. Respect for one tradition, such as a preference against disturbing graves, cannot automatically override another family’s demand to recover and name its missing.
[C1.4] Public explanation raises the same problem. Oral history and community memory are vital evidence, but evidence must be tested, not enthroned. Descendant organizations, like states, can have incentives to simplify: emphasizing heroism, omitting collaborators from within the group, advancing reparations or tourism claims, or suppressing findings that complicate a founding narrative. Scholarship accountable to documents, forensics, and open criticism protects truth better than history subject to identity-based approval.
[C1.5] The defensible standard is enforceable consultation, legal protection against desecration and commercial exploitation, guaranteed representation, and special deference on treatment of identified relatives’ remains. Final authority, however, should rest with independent, multidisciplinary bodies bound by law, evidence, and duties to all affected groups and the wider public. Dignity requires a voice; it does not require a veto over excavation, display, and explanation.
