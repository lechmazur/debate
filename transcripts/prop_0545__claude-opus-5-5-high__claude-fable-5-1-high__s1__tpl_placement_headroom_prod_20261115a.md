[P1.1] Every day, people on transplant waiting lists die while usable organs are buried with donors who would have said yes. The core problem with opt-in is not that people oppose donation. Surveys in country after country find large majorities support it, yet far fewer ever complete a registration. That gap is not a choice; it is inertia.
[P1.2] Presumed consent closes it.
[P1.3] First, defaults change outcomes. Behavioral research on defaults is among the most robust in social science: whatever requires no action is what most people end up with. Cross-national comparisons consistently show opt-out countries with far higher registered-donor rates than opt-in neighbors with similar cultures. Spain, a long-time world leader in deceased donation, operates under presumed consent.
[P1.4] CON will say Spain succeeds because of its coordinators and hospital infrastructure. Partly true, but presumed consent and good infrastructure are complements, not rivals. The coordinator approaching a grieving family works far better when the starting point is "your relative is presumed willing" than "your relative never signed anything."
[P1.5] Second, no new ethical violation is created. Modern systems use soft opt-out: anyone can register refusal in minutes, and families are still consulted. The real ethical failure is the status quo, where a person who wanted to donate but never filled out a form has their wishes silently overridden. Opt-in does not avoid presumptions; it presumes refusal, which is the wrong guess for most people.
[P1.6] Third, family distress goes down, not up. Under opt-in, families are asked to make an agonizing decision cold, at the worst moment of their lives, often with no idea what their loved one wanted. A default gives them a frame: they are honoring a presumed wish rather than inventing one.
[P1.7] So the case is simple. More organs, more respect for majority wishes, and lighter burdens on grieving families.

[C1.2] First, the transplant claim confuses registration with organs. A default changes who is on a register; it does nothing to the bottleneck that actually determines transplant numbers: identifying brain-dead patients in intensive care, maintaining organs, and having trained coordinators approach families. Spain is the clearest case against PRO, not for it. Spain passed presumed consent in the late 1970s, and donation stayed flat for roughly a decade.
[C1.3] The surge came only after it built a national network of hospital coordinators around 1990. Same law, different infrastructure, different results. Meanwhile Wales switched to deemed consent in 2015 and England followed in 2020, and in both places donation rates showed no clean, measurable jump attributable to the law. Brazil adopted presumed consent in the late 1990s and reversed it within about a year after public backlash and falling trust.
[C1.4] Second, the ethical cost is real. "Soft" opt-out means the state treats silence as agreement from people who never heard of the change, including recent migrants, people with limited literacy, and those who simply never engaged. PRO calls opt-in a presumption of refusal; but refusing to take someone's organs without permission is not a presumption, it is the baseline of bodily consent in every other area of medicine.
[C1.5] Third, family distress rises when the state arrives having already decided. Picture a coordinator telling a widow her husband "was presumed willing" when she knows he never discussed it. That is not a frame; it is a confrontation. Families in opt-out countries still veto at substantial rates, which is why Spain still asks them.
[C1.6] The default only adds a fight.
