[P1.1] Countries that have already woven migrant workers into their labor markets should reward that integration, not punish it with harsher deportation drives. The core advantage is simple: these workers pay taxes, fill critical shortages, and sustain industries that domestic workers no longer want. When a country grants them long‑term status, it locks in those fiscal contributions, reduces turnover costs, and stabilises supply chains.
[P1.2] By contrast, stricter return enforcement diverts resources to policing, fuels a shadow economy, and erodes the very productivity it claims to protect.
[P1.3] Consider seasonal agriculture in Southern Europe. Farms depend on laborers who have been picking fruit for years; replacing them each season costs recruiters, training, and lost yield. Countries that have introduced multi‑year permits report higher harvests, lower food prices, and fewer exploitative contracts. The same logic applies to construction in Gulf states and caregiving in Northern Europe: integration yields measurable GDP gains, while enforcement yields only administrative expense.
[P1.4] Moral authority also matters. A policy that throws people who have built homes, schools, and community ties back into limbo signals that a nation values borders over people. That erodes public trust and fuels the very irregular migration that stricter enforcement aims to stop. Prioritising status does not open the floodgates; it simply recognises a reality that already exists and turns it into a regulated, taxable, and socially cohesive segment of the economy.
[P1.5] In the United States, the DACA program showed that work permits for long‑settled undocumented youth raised tax revenues and cut public‑assistance use, while mass removal would have cost billions and disrupted key sectors. Canada’s Provincial Nominee Program shows similar gains from status extensions.
[P1.6] Thus the winning comparison is clear: the long‑term status path delivers economic growth, fiscal stability, and social cohesion, whereas return enforcement delivers cost, disruption, and moral damage. The proposition asks us to choose the side that builds, not the side that tears down.
