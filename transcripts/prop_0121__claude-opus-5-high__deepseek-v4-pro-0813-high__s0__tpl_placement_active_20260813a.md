[P1.1] Start with the choice actually in front of us. A 19-year-old sophomore is having panic attacks and intrusive thoughts. She is on her father's insurance until 26. If she books a therapist, an explanation of benefits lands in his mailbox with a provider name and a date.
[P1.2] She knows this. So she does not book. The realistic alternative to confidential care is not "care plus a parental conversation." It is no care at all — the symptoms compound, and the family finds out later, in a worse way, in an emergency room.
[P1.3] That is the whole case. Confidentiality is not a perk attached to treatment; it is a precondition of treatment. A young adult who believes the visit will be disclosed simply does not become a patient, and a person who never walks through the door cannot be helped by anyone's good intentions.
[P1.4] We already accept this logic everywhere else in medicine. Minors can obtain STI testing and contraception confidentially in most of the country, precisely because legislatures concluded that a disclosure rule functions as a deterrence rule. Mental health is the clearest case for that principle, not the exception to it: the very conditions we most want treated — depression, self-harm, eating disorders, substance use — are the ones carrying the heaviest shame and the highest risk of a hostile reaction at home.
[P1.5] And for some young adults, the reaction is not merely hostile. The gay student in an unaccepting household, the young woman whose parent is the source of the anxiety, the person whose family treats therapy as betrayal — for them, automatic disclosure converts a clinic into a surveillance device.
[P1.6] Note what is not being claimed. Emergency exceptions stay: imminent danger to self or others still permits breaking confidentiality, as it always has. Nothing here silences a clinician facing a suicidal patient. What ends is automatic, bureaucratic disclosure — the billing statement that outs a patient before the therapist has even met them.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes secrecy for safety. It makes mental-health treatment the one medical service a dependent young adult can use on a family policy without the policyholder ever knowing care is happening. That is not clinical confidentiality—it is financial and logistical invisibility, and it has dangerous consequences.
[C1.2] Take a 19-year-old whose panic attacks are the leading edge of a depression sliding toward suicide. The therapist sees her once a week and hears a filtered account; the therapist cannot see that she has stopped eating, stopped leaving her room, or begun stockpiling medication. An explanation of benefits would tell her mother only that treatment started, not what was said. That one fact would let a parent watch for deterioration and act before a crisis.
[C1.3] The motion’s emergency exception does not cover this slow decline; it covers the final moments. That trades early intervention for emergency response.
[C1.4] The claim that disclosure means no care is a false binary. Many young adults seek treatment despite a billing notice, and those who avoid it need targeted options—school services, sliding-scale clinics, safety planning for abusive homes—not a system concealing every mental-health visit from every family. The motion protects the frightened student at the expense of the one who needs a parent to notice she is getting worse.
[C1.5] The STI and contraception analogy also fails. Those services are usually episodic and private because daily home involvement is not required. Mental illness is different: it impairs insight, persists between sessions, and depends on home environment for sleep, meals, medication, and relapse signs. A patient in the middle of that illness may be the last person to sound the alarm.
[C1.6] Therapy content deserves protection. But the fact that a young adult on her parents’ insurance is in treatment is not itself the threat; losing the family’s ability to respond is. The motion optimizes for avoiding an awkward conversation and pays for it with a collapsing safety net.
