[P1.1] Cash bail does not sort defendants by danger. It sorts them by bank balance. A man charged with a serious assault who can post ten thousand dollars walks out that afternoon; a woman charged with shoplifting who cannot post five hundred sits in jail for weeks, losing her job, her apartment, and often custody of her kids. That is the system the motion proposes to replace, and the replacement is not "release everyone."
[P1.2] It is a two-part substitute: a validated risk assessment to identify the genuinely dangerous few, who can be detained outright, and supervised release for everyone else—court reminders, check-ins, and conditions tailored to actual risk.
[P1.3] Both halves of the motion are now tested in the real world. New Jersey effectively abolished cash bail in 2017. Its pretrial jail population dropped by something like forty percent within two years, and violent crime in the state continued to decline over the same period—no spike, no wave. Washington, D.C. has run almost entirely without money bail for decades, releasing the large majority of defendants, and the overwhelming share of them are not rearrested for a violent offense while awaiting trial.
[P1.4] Harris County's misdemeanor reform, Kentucky's statewide system, and Illinois's recent abolition all point the same direction: fewer people in jail, appearance rates stable or improved, and no measurable rise in violent crime.
[P1.5] The mechanism explains why. Pretrial detention is itself criminogenic. Hold a low-risk person for even a few days and research consistently finds they become more likely to reoffend later, because detention destroys exactly the stabilizers—employment, housing, family—that keep people out of trouble. Cash bail manufactures future crime from today's poor defendants while letting wealthy high-risk defendants buy their way past any assessment at all.
[P1.6] So the burden is clear. Jail populations fall; that is barely disputed. The real question is violent crime rates, and the evidence shows them flat or falling. Expect anecdotes of individual releases gone wrong.
[P1.7] Every system, including cash bail, releases people who later offend. The motion is about rates, and on rates, reform wins.
