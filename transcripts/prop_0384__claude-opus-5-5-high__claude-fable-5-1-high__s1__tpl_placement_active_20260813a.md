[P1.1] Start with a nine-year-old whose classmates walk down the hall for an optional faith class on Wednesday afternoon. If she stays behind, what happens to her? In too many schools the answer is that she sits in the library doing nothing, or gets sent to supervise-free "study hall," while everyone notices she's the one who didn't go. That is not choice.
[P1.2] That is pressure dressed up as choice. This motion exists to end it.
[P1.3] The proposition sets two conditions, and each one does real work.
[P1.4] First, comparable secular alternatives. When opting out means getting something of equal value, like an ethics seminar, a philosophy-for-kids session, or a world-cultures project, declining religious instruction stops being a punishment. Germany's long practice of pairing confessional religion classes with an ethics option in many states shows this is ordinary, workable policy, not a utopian demand. The alternative removes the hidden cost that turns "optional" into "expected."
[P1.5] Second, clearly voluntary participation. That means opt-in rather than opt-out, parental consent forms that aren't buried, no teacher recruiting, no rewards for attendance. Voluntariness that only exists on paper is how minority families end up quietly outnumbered.
[P1.6] Now consider the two ways CON can attack this, because both fail.
[P1.7] If CON says schools should allow religious instruction without these conditions, they are defending the version where the atheist kid, the Muslim kid in a mostly Christian district, or the Catholic kid in a mostly evangelical one pays a social price for their family's convictions. They have to explain why a public institution should permit that.
[P1.8] If CON says public schools should ban on-campus religious instruction entirely, they are telling religious families that even a strictly voluntary, balanced, non-coercive option is too dangerous, while the school hosts chess club, debate club, and every other elective. That is not neutrality. It is singling out faith for exclusion.
[P1.9] Our standard is simple: the state neither pushes religion nor shuts it out. Only the conditioned approach does both. Unconditional permission fails the first test; blanket prohibition fails the second.
