[P1.1] The core of this debate is who should bear the friction in a food-ordering interface: the user, or the platform’s revenue model. Today, most delivery apps are built to make the most expensive fulfillment path feel inevitable. Delivery is often the first screen, the fastest button, and the option padded with “free” trial memberships or priority upsells, while pickup and cheaper alternatives sit smaller, later, or buried in menus. That is not neutral design; it is steering, and it profits from inattention.
[P1.2] The proposition asks only for a better starting point. Default users toward pickup or lower-fee options more often, while still letting them choose delivery with a tap. That distinction matters. This is not a ban on delivery.
[P1.3] It is a requirement that the cheaper option get the advantage that defaults naturally confer.
[P1.4] Why does that matter? Because the costs are not trivial. For a $12 meal, delivery fees, service fees, small-order surcharges, and tips can double the price. Many consumers do not mentally reconstruct that total when the interface says “arrive in 25 minutes” and offers one-click checkout.
[P1.5] A lower-fee default makes the price comparison unavoidable. It turns a hidden upsell into a visible tradeoff.
[P1.6] This also helps restaurants. Delivery platforms often take substantial commissions, and restaurants frequently respond by raising app prices or shrinking margins. Pickup and cheaper fulfillment can let more of the payment stay with the food seller, especially small operators. It can also reduce low-value courier trips, a modest but real benefit in dense cities.
[P1.7] The strongest objection is that some people need delivery. True—but the motion says “more often,” not “always.” Apps can preserve one-tap delivery, remember preferences, and exempt contexts where delivery is clearly necessary. The question is whether the starting point should be the most expensive option or a cheaper one. It should be the cheaper one.

[C1.1] Food-delivery apps exist because someone wants food brought to a door. The proposition treats that request as a design error. It is not. Defaulting users to pickup or the cheapest path more often does not protect consumers; it withholds the service they opened the app to buy and then celebrates the extra tap.
[C1.2] A default is not a moral ranking of prices. A good default matches the action a user most likely intended. Anyone who wants a burrito walked across town already has cheaper paths: the restaurant counter, a maps pin, a phone call. They opened a delivery app because delivery is the product.
[C1.3] Making it smaller, later, or opt-in is not unavoidable price comparison. It is friction aimed at the modal customer.
[C1.4] The fee story is already visible. Service charges, delivery charges, and tip prompts appear before payment. Adults who still tap deliver are buying time, weather, childcare, disability access, or a night they do not want to drive. “More often” is not a surgical exception. It makes the ordinary case harder, then offers memory settings and exemptions as if those cancel the bias.
[C1.5] Restaurants join these platforms for incremental orders they would not otherwise receive. A pickup-first funnel shrinks that volume—the same volume that pays kitchens, couriers, and the marketplace. Cheaper defaults do not transfer surplus to small operators; they shrink the transaction that justified the commission.
[C1.6] Interfaces always steer. Steering toward cheapest is still steering, only now conscripted into price paternalism. The right starting point is the path people came for, with prices shown and choice intact—not a cheaper option dressed up as neutrality.
