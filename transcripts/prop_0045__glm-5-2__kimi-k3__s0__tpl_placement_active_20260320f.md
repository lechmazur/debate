[P1.1] Personality tests like MBTI and DISC persist in hiring and team-building not because they work, but because they feel useful. That gap between feeling and evidence is the entire problem.
[P1.2] The central question is predictive validity: do these instruments tell us something about how a person will actually perform or collaborate? The honest answer is no—not at a level that survives scrutiny. MBTI sorts people into tidy four-letter codes, but its test-retest reliability is notoriously poor; people routinely get different types on retakes. Its binary structure—introvert versus extravert, thinking versus feeling—collapses continuous distributions into false either-ors.
[P1.3] DISC fares no better. It offers an appealing four-quadrant map of behavioral styles, but its constructs lack the rigorous empirical grounding needed to predict workplace outcomes.
[P1.4] The deeper problem is opportunity cost. Every hour spent administering and interpreting these instruments is an hour not spent on methods that actually predict performance: structured interviews, work samples, job-knowledge tests, and trial periods. When companies build team-building exercises around MBTI types or DISC colors, they embed categories with weak scientific footing into their organizational culture, lending false confidence to decisions about who fits where.
[P1.5] MBTI and DISC are not marginally useful tools that just need refinement. They occupy a distinct category: instruments whose popularity outpaces their evidence base. Employers using them are not making data-driven decisions—they are making intuitively satisfying ones dressed in the language of data. That distinction matters because hiring and team-building carry real consequences for careers, team dynamics, and organizational effectiveness.
[P1.6] If the predictive validity is too thin to justify those stakes, the responsible course is to stop using them—not to hedge or qualify, but to stop.
[P1.7] This debate comes down to one standard: do these tests predict what they are being used to predict? They do not.
