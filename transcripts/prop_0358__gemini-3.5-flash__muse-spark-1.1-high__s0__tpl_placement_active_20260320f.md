[P1.1] To eliminate traffic deaths, we must design for human fallibility. Lowering default speed limits and redesigning streets is a far more effective tool for reducing traffic deaths than ramping up post-crash penalties. This is true because of a simple reality: proactive prevention saves lives, while reactive punishment only documents them.
[P1.2] First, physical infrastructure is self-enforcing. Relying on post-crash penalties assumes that the threat of future punishment alters immediate driver behavior. But drivers do not calculate long-term legal risks in the fraction of a second before a mistake; they react to the road environment directly in front of them. A wide, straight, multi-lane road practically begs a driver to speed, regardless of the legal limit or the penalties for crashing.
[P1.3] Conversely, street redesigns—such as narrower lanes, raised crosswalks, curb extensions, and roundabouts—physically compel drivers to slow down. Redesign forces compliance through geometry rather than fear.
[P1.4] Second, the physics of speed dictate survival. The severity of a collision is determined by kinetic energy, which scales quadratically with speed. A pedestrian struck at 40 mph has an incredibly high probability of death, while one struck at 20 mph almost always survives. By lowering default urban speed limits and designing streets to naturally enforce those limits, we systematically keep impact forces below the threshold of human lethality.
[P1.5] Finally, post-crash penalties are fundamentally reactive. They only trigger after a tragedy has already occurred. A driver facing a heavy prison sentence or a massive fine does not bring a deceased pedestrian back to life. If our goal is to reduce traffic deaths, we must build a system where a single mistake or moment of distraction does not carry a death sentence.
[P1.6] Proactive street design and lower speed limits forgive human error; harsher penalties merely punish it.
