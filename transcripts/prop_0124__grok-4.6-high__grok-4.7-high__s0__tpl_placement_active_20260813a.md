[P1.1] A competent adult is not the state's property. When terminal cancer, ALS, or another degenerative disease has already stolen a livable future, death is coming either way. The only live question is whether the last stretch must be unbearable. A ban on medical assistance in dying does not preserve life.
[P1.2] It preserves only a slower, more degrading manner of dying. Nature will finish the dying. The law should not require cruelty in the meantime.
[P1.3] Autonomy is already recognized at the end of life. Patients may refuse ventilation, dialysis, chemotherapy, and even food and water. Those refusals cause death. The gap between letting someone starve and offering a supervised, peaceful death is technical, not moral.
[P1.4] If a person of sound mind, after counseling and independent review, judges their suffering unbearable, the state needs more than unease to overrule them.
[P1.5] Palliative care should be a right, not a shield. Even excellent hospice cannot always restore control. Some neurological and terminal illnesses leave patients fully aware as they lose speech, breath, and movement. Telling them to wait is not compassion.
[P1.6] The proposition is not a general license to die. It is limited to competent adults with unbearable terminal or degenerative suffering, under strict safeguards: confirmed diagnosis, verified competence, independent opinions, waiting periods, screening for coercion and treatable psychiatric illness, and a persistent, documented request. Those limits answer the real risks.
[P1.7] Prohibition does not prevent death. It drives people to starve themselves, travel while they still can, or attempt suicide that fails and leaves them worse. Families are left to watch a legal starvation because refusing food is one of the few permitted exits. A regulated medical path is safer and more honest.
[P1.8] Fear of abuse is a reason to design constraints, not a reason to compel competent adults to endure what they have already judged unbearable.

[C1.1] The law should not authorize physicians to end life. Once the state accepts that some competent adults may be killed because their suffering is judged unbearable, the bright line against killing has already been crossed. Promised safeguards do not restore it. They become the paperwork through which the exception expands.
[C1.2] Competence is the weakest link in the proposal, not its protection. Terminal and degenerative illness commonly bring exhaustion, heavy medication, fluctuating despair, and total dependence. A second opinion and a waiting period cannot reliably distinguish a settled request from treatable depression, transient agony, or the quiet conclusion that one has become a burden. The patients most likely to ask are those least positioned to resist family fatigue, institutional cost, or a culture that has already agreed their condition justifies death.
[C1.3] Refusing a ventilator is not the moral equivalent of a lethal prescription. One declines an intervention and accepts that medicine cannot conquer every death. The other makes the doctor the agent who causes death. That is not a technical gap.
[C1.4] It rewrites medicine’s duty from relieving suffering while protecting life to eliminating the person who suffers. Patients who still want treatment, and disabled people who reject the prevailing view of a livable life, then live inside a system that has officially ranked some conditions as reasons to die.
[C1.5] A ban is not a command to be cruel. It is a refusal to solve suffering by killing, and a demand for palliation, presence, and care that does not abandon people. Jurisdictions that started with narrow terminal cases have widened eligibility beyond imminent death. Abuse is not a hypothetical to be designed away.
[C1.6] It is what follows when the law licenses death under standards it cannot police.

[C2.1] PRO treats the physician’s hand as morally identical in both cases. It is not. When a patient refuses dialysis or food, medicine withdraws or withholds an intervention. The lethal prescription does the opposite: it adds the cause that ends life that day.
[C2.2] The person might still have had months, or a change of mind. Calling that gap “only speed and dignity” [P2.2] concedes the point. Dignity, here, is a euphemism for intending death and bringing it about.
[C2.3] That is why the competence reply fails. [P2.4] claims an unreliable distinction would also invalidate stopping a ventilator. A doubtful refusal can be paused; treatment can resume; another morning can be waited for. The error is often correctable.
[C2.4] Assisted death is not. The heavier the medication and the deeper the dependence, the worse a one-way consent becomes. Tools built to honor bodily refusal were not built to license killing.
[C2.5] “Later statutes” are a distraction [P2.5]. Coercion screening cannot hear the sentence never written on the form: that staying is a burden. Audited paperwork proves only that the paperwork existed.
[C2.6] Desperate exits do not justify authorization. We do not repeal a prohibition because some people attempt the forbidden act. Nor do those who want treatment simply keep it [P2.7].
[C2.7] Once death is a clinical pathway, the consult includes the question, the family hears it, and the person who would have continued must defend the decision to live.
[C2.8] A ban is not nostalgia for a cleaner story. It is the judgment that medicine may fail to conquer suffering and still must not end the patient.
