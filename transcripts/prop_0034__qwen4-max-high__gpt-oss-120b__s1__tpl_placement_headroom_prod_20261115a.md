[P1.1] A voice is not a style. It is the sound of a specific person's body, and for singers, actors, broadcasters and teachers it is the asset they earn with. The question is narrow: may a company synthesize an unlimited supply of that person's voice and sell it without ever asking? Consent is the only rule that scales.
[P1.2] Consider the asymmetry. Asking costs an email, a contract, a fee. Not asking costs the person control over every sentence they will ever appear to have said: endorsement pitches, political audio, pornography, a scam call to their own mother. A clone is infinitely reproducible and effectively unretractable; the harm arrives faster than any takedown process.
[P1.3] When one side's burden is paperwork and the other's is irreversible, you put the burden on the paperwork.
[P1.4] This is not a novel imposition. Commercial creative work already runs on releases: recognizable faces in stock imagery, sampled recordings, a novelist's name on a franchise. Consent regimes are administrable; estates and agencies license voices today.
[P1.5] And the motion is bounded. It reaches commercial distribution of clones of identifiable people, not parody, not criticism, not a human impressionist on a comedy stage. [P1.6] The default should be that a person owns the commercial use of their own identity, and whoever wants to monetize it must ask. If a business only works when the person whose voice it sells never gets a say, the business model is the defect.
