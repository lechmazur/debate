[P1.1] Public universities exist to serve the public that funds and hosts them. When local housing shortages turn severe, continuing to recruit international students without limit is not an act of openness. It is a refusal to match institutional ambition to the physical capacity of the community that makes the campus possible.
[P1.2] Housing is not an infinite backdrop. Severe shortages mean students and residents already compete for scarce rooms, face rent spikes, overcrowding, or displacement. Cities that host large public campuses have watched this sequence repeat: enrollment rises faster than purpose-built beds, and the private market absorbs the surplus. Each additional student recruited beyond what that market can absorb adds demand universities themselves often do not house.
[P1.3] Price then does the rationing. Those who lose are local families, lower-income domestic students, and frequently the international students themselves, pushed into unsafe or exploitative arrangements far from campus.
[P1.4] A cap triggered only by severe shortage is a proportionate response, not a closed door. It preserves the benefits of international enrollment—tuition revenue, classroom diversity, research talent—while stopping the institution from compounding a crisis it helps create. Unconstrained recruitment treats housing as someone else's problem. Public universities cannot outsource the consequences of their growth to neighborhoods that have no vote over admissions targets.
[P1.5] The alternative assumes markets or future construction will always catch up in time. They do not. Construction lags, zoning constrains supply, and short-term enrollment surges produce immediate harm that no later building boom undoes for the people already priced out. A conditional cap creates breathing room for housing supply to recover and forces universities to internalize the costs of expansion rather than socialize them.
[P1.6] Domestic students and local residents are not obstacles to a global campus. They are the constituency a public university is built to serve. When beds run out, restraint is not hostility to international students. It is the minimum condition for a university that remains livable, legitimate, and public.

[C1.1] Public universities should not answer severe housing shortages by capping international recruitment. The proposition’s forced choice is false: capacity can be constrained through housing guarantees, enrollment tied to verified beds, and phased growth, without a nationality quota. A cap converts a public planning failure into an admissions penalty on the cohort with the least local political power.
[C1.2] The housing logic does not survive contact with the shortage. A cap creates no bed. If construction lags and zoning constrains supply, those facts remain after admission offers are cut; domestic enrollment growth, staff hiring, short-term rentals, investors, and ordinary households still add demand. It also hits students who may live in purpose-built housing; fees from them can help finance dorms, and their work can keep labs running.
[C1.3] It is under-inclusive about causes and over-inclusive about people.
[C1.4] The promised “breathing room” [P1.5] is backwards. Sudden caps make enrollment and revenue less predictable just when universities need funds for leases, services, and new beds; housing projects depend on steady cohorts. A vague severe-shortage trigger tells city governments that the university will absorb scarcity whenever zoning blocks supply. And price rationing continues: deleting international applicants does not guarantee a low-income local family gets the room.
[C1.5] Affluent renters still outbid them. Need-based guarantees and university master leases target that mechanism; a passport quota does not.
[C1.6] There is also a size dilemma. A cap small enough to be symbolic will not move rents; a cap large enough to move rents is an academic and fiscal shock, cutting tuition that often supports course sections, domestic aid, labs, and dorm finance. That is a poor way to serve the public that funds the university. Public accountability means housing-linked rules applied to all students: tie enrollment growth to beds the institution can verify, prioritize low-income students for those beds, and phase total enrollment with construction.
[C1.7] The cap is not restraint; it is a visible substitute for governing scarcity.

If the cap remains an international headcount, it is arbitrary: a campus could add hundreds of unhoused domestic places and cut international research students living in a master-leased block.
[C2.2] If it spares housed internationals, it collapses into a universal bed rule.

[C2.4] The power argument reverses itself. International students should be protected by guarantees, not by deleting the cohort with the least local leverage. Calling them a “discretionary cohort” [P2.7] ignores that course sections, labs, domestic aid, and housing finance can depend on them, especially when housed. A nationality trigger gives city hall a release valve: if zoning blocks supply, sacrifice foreign applicants while avoiding harder constraints on domestic growth.
[C2.5] A universal bed rule forces the real decision-makers to confront scarcity.
[C2.6] The motion’s comparison is misleading. Capacity is not “unconstrained” when every offer is tied to a verified bed and growth is phased until units exist. The cap adds only a passport line, fiscal risk, and under-inclusion. [P2.5] assumes the marginal international student is the marginal unhoused bidder; that is the disputed point.
[C2.7] Severe shortage calls for housing conditions on every offer, not a nationality quota.
