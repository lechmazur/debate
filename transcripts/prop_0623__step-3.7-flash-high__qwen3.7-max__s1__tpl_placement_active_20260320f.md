[P1.1] Enterprise-level collective bargaining is failing low-wage workers in fragmented, high-turnover sectors like retail, hospitality, and in-home care, where individual workers have no meaningful leverage to negotiate fair pay. Sectoral bargaining frameworks—which set industry-wide minimum pay, safety, and scheduling standards through negotiated agreements between employer associations and unions—fix this structural failure, and deliver better outcomes for workers, employers, and the broader economy on balance.
[P1.2] First, sectoral bargaining eliminates the destructive race to the bottom that plagues enterprise-level systems. When pay is set on a firm-by-firm basis, employers competing on cost are forced to cut wages and benefits to stay afloat, dragging down standards across entire sectors and suppressing wage growth for all but the highest-skilled workers. Sectoral bargaining creates a level playing field, so employers compete on service quality and productivity, not who can pay their workforce the least. This is exactly why Germany and Austria, where sectoral bargaining covers the vast majority of private sector workers, have far lower wage inequality than peer economies reliant on enterprise-level bargaining alone.
[P1.3] Second, sectoral bargaining closes structural pay gaps that enterprise systems perpetuate. Women, immigrant workers, part-time staff, and young people are overrepresented in low-wage sectors, and disproportionately penalized by individual negotiation systems that reward workers with more social capital or formal bargaining power. Industry-wide baseline standards guarantee the same minimum for every worker in the sector, regardless of their background, seniority, or willingness to push back against a manager.
[P1.4] Critics will frame sectoral bargaining as rigid and incompatible with dynamic industries, but modern frameworks are easily tailored to address these concerns: they can include cost-of-living adjustment clauses, targeted carve-outs for small businesses, and fast-track procedures to update standards as sectors evolve, including for emerging gig and platform work. The core question is not whether sectoral bargaining is flawless, but whether it outperforms the status quo of fragmented enterprise bargaining that has left tens of millions of full-time workers living in poverty across the developed world.
