[P1.1] The era of solving urban water scarcity by plundering someone else’s watershed is over. Prioritizing direct potable reuse—purifying municipal wastewater and routing it straight back into the drinking water system—turns a city’s existing water into a local, drought-proof supply. Importing more water from distant basins, by contrast, locks cities into vanishing rivers, ruinous infrastructure costs, and ecological destruction.
[P1.2] First, direct potable reuse provides true climate resilience where imports fail. Megadroughts are regional. When cities in arid regions face shortages, the distant rivers they tap are usually shrinking at the exact same time. Chasing new imports means gambling urban survival on distant snowpack and waging decades of legal warfare against rural and agricultural communities fighting over the same dwindling drops.
[P1.3] Direct potable reuse breaks this dependency. Because indoor domestic water use stays steady regardless of rainfall, purifying municipal wastewater creates a reliable, locally controlled loop that lets a city use the same water multiple times.
[P1.4] Second, importing more water is economically and environmentally destructive. Pumping heavy water across hundreds of miles and over mountain ranges requires massive amounts of energy, while long canals lose vast volumes to evaporation and leaks. Building new inter-basin pipelines takes decades of litigation and devastates source ecosystems, turning wetlands and farming valleys into dust bowls, just as historic transfers drained California’s Owens Valley. Direct potable reuse keeps investment inside the city, avoiding massive conveyance losses and leaving distant rivers intact.
[P1.5] Finally, direct potable reuse delivers superior water quality. Imported river water is not pristine; it already carries agricultural runoff and treated sewage discharged by upstream towns. Direct potable reuse replaces that uncontrolled, de facto reuse with strict multi-barrier purification—using membranes, reverse osmosis, and advanced oxidation to produce water cleaner than conventional surface supplies, as proven in places from Namibia to Texas. Water-stressed cities must recycle the water they already have instead of draining distant basins dry.

[C1.1] Start with arithmetic, because the motion hides it. Direct potable reuse cannot create water; it can only re-run the water a city already has. A city's recoverable sewage flow is a fraction of its demand—outdoor use, leaks, and industrial consumption never return to the sewer—and every pass through reverse osmosis throws roughly a fifth or more away as concentrated brine. The loop shrinks each cycle.
[C1.2] For a city whose problem is a widening gap between people and water, a technology that can only recirculate a shrinking fraction of yesterday's supply cannot be the priority. It is a supplement. Prioritizing it means sending the bulk of your capital and political effort at the smaller lever.
[C1.3] Second, "drought-proof" is the wrong word for a loop that depends on everyone else's water first. Wastewater exists only because water came in from somewhere—usually an import. Cut the import and the sewage flow drops with it; conservation mandates in drought shrink it further. Reuse rides on top of imported supply; it does not replace it.
[C1.4] Third, direct reuse carries a failure mode imports don't. Indirect reuse routes purified water through an aquifer or reservoir, buying months of dilution and detection time. Direct reuse removes that buffer by design: a membrane breach or a missed pathogen reaches taps within hours. That is why nearly every large, successful recycling program—Orange County, Singapore's NEWater—is indirect or non-potable, while direct schemes remain small, and why public referendums have repeatedly killed "toilet-to-tap" proposals outright.
[C1.5] Prioritizing the most fragile and least accepted version of reuse is a strange bet.
[C1.6] Fourth, PRO's import is a strawman. "Importing more water from distant basins" today means negotiated agricultural transfers, compensated fallowing, canal lining, and water markets—not Owens Valley. Those deliver volumes measured in hundreds of thousands of acre-feet, from a different hydrology than the city's own, which is what genuine portfolio resilience looks like.
[C1.7] The right order is imports and conservation for volume, reuse to stretch them. Not the reverse.
