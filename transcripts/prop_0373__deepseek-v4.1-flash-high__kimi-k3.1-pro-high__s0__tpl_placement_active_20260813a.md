[P1.1] Some books are not right for every age. A parent can reasonably object to an explicit memoir in an elementary school. But the proposition does not ask us to defend every title for every child; it asks whether the challenge-and-removal regime has become disproportionate. It has.
[P1.2] The core mistake is treating a parent’s objection as a community veto. A library serves voluntary readers across ages; it is not a single classroom syllabus. A parent who does not want their teenager reading a book can opt them out. That does not justify pulling it from every other teenager, and it certainly does not justify a process where one complaint, vague labels like “offensive,” and pressure on librarians can remove a title before any real review.
[P1.3] Age-appropriateness calls for placement—elementary versus high school, optional versus assigned—not erasure.
[P1.4] Second, the machinery chills access even when books formally survive. Librarians facing complaints and threats buy fewer risky titles; teachers drop units rather than become targets; students lose the quiet ability to find a book without asking an adult for permission. That is a cost to everyone, and it falls hardest on isolated students who need the library precisely because home and school do not offer those perspectives.
[P1.5] Third, broad challenges are a blunt instrument. If a book contains one passage a parent finds unsuitable, the proportional response is an age band or an alternative assignment—not removing a work that speaks to other students. And when the same objections cluster around LGBTQ and racial histories while comparable content in established classics draws no fire, the claim that this is neutral hygiene becomes hard to credit.
[P1.6] The test is not whether any challenged book contains a passage someone dislikes. It is whether that dislike justifies removing the book from everyone else with flimsy process and no proportionality. It does not.

[C2.5] Nor does relocation make removal inherently excessive. Relocation answers “right book, wrong shelf”; it does not answer “wrong book for this collection.” If explicit material is offered in an elementary library with no older-grade section, moving it within that institution may accomplish nothing. PRO’s proportional tools are often best, but their availability does not prove that the stronger remedy is never warranted.
[C2.6] Pattern evidence likewise warrants investigation, not a verdict. A concentration of objections around particular subjects can expose viewpoint discrimination, and discriminatory decisions should be overturned. Treating the pattern as conclusive would replace individual review with the very guilt-by-category PRO condemns.
[C2.7] Finally, professional caution is not self-validating evidence of excess. Reviews consume time and controversy can distort purchasing; unaccountable selection can also leave mistakes uncorrected. The answer is specific reasons, continued access during review where feasible, documented decisions, and viewpoint-neutral criteria. PRO’s strongest arguments justify repairing defective procedures.
