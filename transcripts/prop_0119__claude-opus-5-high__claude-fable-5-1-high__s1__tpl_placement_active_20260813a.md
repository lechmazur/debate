[P1.1] A restaurant menu that says $18 for a burger and then expects $22 is not a price. It's a riddle. And the answer to the riddle is: someone else's rent.
[P1.2] That's the heart of this. Tipping is a system where the employer sets a price that hides a chunk of its own labor costs, then outsources collection of that chunk to the customer's conscience at the end of the meal. The worker's income becomes a function of the weather, a slow Tuesday, a party that stiffs, a table that thinks 12% is generous. That volatility is not a market signal.
[P1.3] It's risk transfer — from the business, which can absorb and forecast it, onto the person least able to absorb it.
[P1.4] Three reasons the swap wins.
[P1.5] First, honesty in pricing. A service charge that is stated up front and included is simply the true price of the meal. Customers who claim they'd revolt at a higher menu number are revolting against arithmetic, not against cost. You already pay it.
[P1.6] You just pay it in a second, awkward transaction where you're guessing.
[P1.7] Second, fairness in who gets paid. Tips do not cleanly track service quality. They track familiarity, flirtation, appearance, and — as has been documented repeatedly — the race of the server. A server who must court a customer's affection for the last third of her income has a structural reason to tolerate behavior she should be able to shut down.
[P1.8] Higher base pay converts that from a survival calculation into a workplace rule.
[P1.9] Third, accountability. Under a wage, if the labor cost is too high, that is the restaurant's problem to solve — through pricing, staffing, or menu design. Under tipping, the same problem is solved silently by underpaying a worker on a bad night. Guilt is a terrible payroll department.
[P1.10] The question is not whether servers should be paid well. It's whether their pay should be a bill the customer is quietly handed at the end, or a cost the business owns.
