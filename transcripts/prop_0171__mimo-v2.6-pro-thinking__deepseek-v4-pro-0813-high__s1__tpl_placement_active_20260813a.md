[P1.1] When animal models are inadequate, the choice is not between risk and safety. It is between a small, monitored, informed risk undertaken by volunteers and a large, unmonitored, uninformed risk imposed on patients, trial participants, and the public. That is the comparison the motion forces, and it is the one this debate should be decided on.
[P1.2] Start with what "inadequate animal models" actually means in practice. It means we dose animals with a pathogen that behaves differently in them, watch a signal that may not translate, and then push into large field trials where the first real test of efficacy and safety happens in thousands of people, in uncontrolled conditions, with slow endpoints and ambiguous results. That path has failed repeatedly across respiratory viruses, enteric infections, and parasitic disease, wasting years and exposing people to products whose promise rested on a mismatched model.
[P1.3] Where the model is bad, more animal work is not caution; it is delay dressed as caution.
[P1.4] Controlled human infection studies replace that guesswork with a defined dose, a known time of exposure, a measurable endpoint, and a small number of closely watched volunteers. This is not experimental in the reckless sense. Decades of such work in cholera, typhoid, malaria, influenza, RSV, and norovirus has produced vaccines and treatments, refined dosing, and answered questions that animal work could not. Norovirus is the emblematic case: with no good animal model, challenge studies became the only serious way to test vaccines and antivirals at all.
[P1.5] The ethical objections are serious, and the motion concedes that. But seriousness is not the same as decisiveness. The safeguards are known and enforceable through exactly the regulators the motion names: competent volunteers giving genuinely informed consent, low-risk healthy adults, proven rescue and treatment available, predefined stopping rules, independent ethics review, and limits that tighten as pathogen severity rises. Note too that "permit more broadly" means expanding a supervised pathway, not deregulating one.
[P1.6] The objection also proves too much. We already accept healthy volunteers in first-in-human drug studies and in kidney donation to strangers. [clipped]

[C1.1] The central trade-off is too stark. Rejecting broader controlled human infection studies does not force regulators into large, unmonitored field trials or endless animal work. It leaves intermediate tools: human-relevant tissue and organoid models, outbreak cohorts, adaptive field trials, and staged clinical testing. Calling these “delay dressed as caution” assumes deliberate infection is the only way to learn; that is the very claim the motion must prove.
[C1.2] The epistemic case is weaker than the clean comparison suggests. Challenge volunteers are typically a narrow group—screened, healthy adults—given a defined high dose in an artificial setting. That removes age, prior immunity, pregnancy, malnutrition, co-infections, and comorbidities: the factors that determine whether a vaccine or treatment actually works in the population. A clean endpoint is not a generalizable answer.
[C1.3] Norovirus is the cautionary example. Despite poor animal models and years of human challenge work, no licensed norovirus vaccine has emerged. The hard gap is translation from healthy adults to children, the elderly, and the immunocompromised.
[C1.4] The ethical objections do not merely remain serious; they worsen as permission broadens. More pathogens means more volunteers, larger payment inducements, confinement, and pressure on students, lower-income participants, and research staff. Consent cannot disclose unknown long-term sequelae. Stopping rules and rescue therapy manage acute risk, not delayed or subclinical harm.
[C1.5] The comparison to first-in-human drug trials or kidney donation misses the point: those do not deliberately make a healthy person ill. Broadening also changes the social meaning of deliberate infection, moving it from a tightly justified exception to a routine early-development tool and eroding public trust.
[C1.6] Animal models are imperfect nearly everywhere. That is not a sufficient trigger for broader human infection. The proposal would weaken a genuine ethical boundary in exchange for evidence that is often too clean to translate. Regulators should keep human challenge narrow and exceptional.

[P2.3] Generalizability argues for challenge work *followed by* field trials, not instead of them.

[P2.6] Finally, C1.5's distinction is semantic. Deliberately dosing a healthy volunteer with a biologically active substance carrying unknown delayed harm is the same moral act.
