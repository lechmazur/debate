[P1.1] Elite universities do not mainly discover talent wherever it lives; they certify advantage purchased long before the application opened. Legacy preferences, donor interest, early decision favoring families who can commit without comparing aid, recruitment from private and affluent suburban schools, travel sports, tutoring and polished extracurriculars are not incidental. They convert parental money, schooling and know-how into a credential that then looks like pure merit.
[P1.2] That is why student bodies remain skewed. At many highly selective universities, students from the richest fifth heavily outnumber those from the poorest fifth, students from the very top are present many times their population share, and the typical parent is already professional-class. Generous aid helps the few low-income students who survive this pipeline, but aid after selection cannot undo selection itself.
[P1.3] Reproduction continues after admission. The highest-return extras — unpaid internships in finance, media and politics, alumni introductions, research sponsorship, freedom to choose a prestigious but risky path over immediate earnings — flow easiest to students with a safety net who already speak the culture. Their peers graduate toward debt pressure and family obligations while wealthier classmates convert the same degree into networks that compound.
[P1.4] Yes, the low-income graduate who makes it often does very well. That is the legitimizing trick: a few visible climbers make the race look fair, while elite cohorts are far too small to drive mobility at national scale. Broad-access public universities move vastly more students upward in absolute terms; elite schools mostly allocate scarce status among those already near the top.
[P1.5] Policy should stop treating these schools as charitable engines of fairness entitled to deference, tax-advantaged endowments and subsidy. Treat them as institutions of inheritance: condition tax benefits and federal funds on real access, end legacy and donor preferences, tax endowment wealth more seriously, and shift marginal investment toward colleges that move large numbers upward.

She graduates at very high rates, with manageable debt, and enters professions that shape who holds power.
[C1.2] That is not a “legitimizing trick”; it is mobility where mobility actually happens—at the individual level.
[C1.3] The skewed student bodies mostly prove selection before college, not reproduction by the college. By enrollment, K-12 inequality and neighborhood schooling have already done their work. Legacy and donor preferences are real but narrow; they do not explain the dominant effect of these institutions. If policy treats the universities as estates and taxes their endowments heavily, it will cut the same aid that admits and supports low-income students.
[C1.4] The scale objection fails. Elite colleges are small by design, so their raw number of low-income graduates will always be smaller than a giant public system’s. The fair test is counterfactual: the same low-income applicant usually completes at a higher rate, with less default and better professional access at a selective campus than at an underfunded broad-access college. If support shifts away from these colleges, elite networks do not open; they become more family-based.
[C1.5] Wealth reproduces itself without elite universities. Low-income students need those universities to break in.
[C1.6] So do not treat elite universities as mere inheritance. Expand their low-income pipelines and fix K-12 and broad-access funding. Punishing the few institutions that do the most per admitted low-income student would reduce mobility, not increase it.

[P2.2] A ladder raised for a handful inside a walled estate does not make the estate a mobility program.

They choose to weight legacy status, early decision, expensive extracurriculars, counselor-polished essays, and recruitment from familiar affluent schools. They could weight first-generation status, work experience, and achievement relative to opportunity far more heavily.
[P2.4] When an institution selects criteria readable by wealth, then calls the result merit, selection is reproduction — performed by the university, not just inherited from childhood.
[P2.5] Their per-student counterfactual [C1.4] changes the motion. Comparing one admitted low-income student at an elite campus versus an underfunded college proves elite resources help — resources hoarded behind exclusion. It does not prove the system promotes mobility more than it reproduces hierarchy. Judge the cohort: for each low-income entrant lifted, many more wealthy entrants receive the same network, stamp, and intergenerational insurance.
[P2.6] Scale matters because policy must ask where subsidy and tax privilege produce mobility across society, not maximum polish for the already-selected few.
[P2.7] Finally, conditioning privilege is not punishing success. If endowments exist to fund access, require access: tie tax benefits and federal support to enrolling and graduating far more low-income and first-generation students, eliminate legacy and donor edges, and spend wealth rather than merely grow it. Institutions that meet a mobility mission deserve support; those that hoard prestige should not receive charitable deference on trust.

Recovered public value can fund broad-access colleges that lift students at scale. Beyond admissions, the tools are mandatory payout for access, public reporting of legacy and donor admits, and loss of charitable deference for hoarding.
[P4.4] The penalty falls on accumulated wealth, not on the student who needs aid.
