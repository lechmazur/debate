[P1.1] A point-of-sale background check asks whether a buyer is legally disqualified. It does not establish whether that person knows how to handle or store a firearm safely, and screening requirements are not uniform across every purchase. For an object whose misuse can be irreversible in seconds, that is an inadequate safety floor.
[P1.2] The United States should require an objective federal purchaser license: verified identity, a background check, and completion of practical safety training before buying any firearm. Every seller, including private sellers, should verify that authorization. Licensing should supplement, not eliminate, checks for newly disqualifying information.
[P1.3] This improves on screening alone in three ways.
[P1.4] First, it makes eligibility a consistent requirement across the market. Federal law generally requires licensed dealers to conduct background checks, while private-sale requirements vary. A nationwide license means the buyer’s obligation does not disappear because the transaction changes venues or crosses a state line. Sellers would have a clear, common means of checking authorization.
[P1.5] Second, training addresses risks that criminal records cannot reveal. A legally eligible first-time buyer may not understand safe unloading, secure storage, or how to prevent unauthorized access. A background check cannot teach those skills. [P1.6] It cannot guarantee responsible behavior; it can replace avoidable ignorance with demonstrated knowledge.
[P1.7] Third, this need not become discretionary permission reserved for the wealthy. [P1.8] Criminals may evade licensing, just as they evade screening. That does not make preventable access failures and unsafe handling irrelevant. The choice is not perfect prevention versus freedom.
