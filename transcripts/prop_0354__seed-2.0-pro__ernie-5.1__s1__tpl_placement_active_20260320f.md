[C1.1] Intelligent speed assistance should stay optional because imposing it by default trades one set of accidents for another, undermines driver skill, and ignores the messy reality of roads.
[C1.2] First, defaults create a false sense of security. When drivers know the car will automatically brake, they tend to disengage attention from speed. Studies of adaptive cruise control show reduced lane‑keeping vigilance, and similar over‑reliance would appear with speed limiters. The result is not fewer crashes but more rear‑end collisions when the system mis‑reads a temporary sign or a construction zone and slams the brakes.
[C1.3] Second, speed limits are context‑dependent, not static numbers. A 50 km/h zone near a school may need to be ignored for an emergency vehicle, while a 100 km/h motorway segment may safely allow higher flow in heavy traffic. An always‑on limiter cannot discriminate, so it either forces unsafe deceleration or is overridden so often that drivers learn to ignore it, reproducing the very inertia the pro side laments.
[C1.4] Third, mandatory defaults erode the skill base that makes driving safe. When seatbelts were made compulsory, compliance rose, but the principle was different: seatbelts protect the occupant regardless of driver judgment. Speed management is a judgment call; removing that judgment dulls the habit of scanning the environment, and the long‑term safety cost is hard to measure but real.
[C1.5] Finally, the pro side’s “one‑third reduction” figure comes from controlled trials where participants knew they were being monitored. In the wild, compliance with defaults is lower, and the net benefit shrinks. We already have effective voluntary tools—speed‑camera alerts, navigation‑based warnings—that respect driver autonomy while still curbing excess speed.
[C1.6] For these reasons, keeping intelligent speed assistance optional preserves driver agency, avoids new crash modes, and respects the nuanced demands of real‑world driving.
