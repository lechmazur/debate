[P1.1] Life tenure was designed for a world where a justice might serve fifteen, twenty years. Today, thanks to longer lifespans and the incentive to appoint younger nominees, American justices now often serve thirty years or more, casting a single president's judicial preferences over five or six subsequent presidential terms and a dozen congressional elections. That is not independence from politics — it is a single political moment frozen in amber and imposed on generations who never voted for it. Fixed, staggered terms of, say, eighteen years fix this without sacrificing what independence actually protects.
[P1.2] Start with the legitimacy problem, because it is the whole case. Democratic legitimacy means that power exercised over citizens today can be traced, at reasonable intervals, back to their consent. A justice appointed in 1991 ruling on cases in 2024 answers to no electorate that exists anymore. Worse, life tenure turns death and strategic retirement timing into the primary mechanism of constitutional change — a lottery of mortality and health, not a process any democratic theory can defend.
[P1.3] When a justice's replacement depends on whether they can hang on until a friendly president takes office, we have replaced principled succession with actuarial politics.
[P1.4] Fixed terms fix the appointment stakes too. Because vacancies become rare and unpredictable, every Supreme Court nomination turns into a civilizational battle — because the winner might lock in influence for forty years. Regularize turnover with staggered fixed terms, and each individual seat matters less, lowering the temperature of confirmation fights and reducing the incentive to pick the youngest, most ideologically reliable nominee available rather than the most qualified one.
[P1.5] Independence, meanwhile, does not require immortality in office — it requires insulation from that term. An eighteen-year non-renewable term, with no reappointment possible, protects a justice from currying favor just as well as life tenure does, since there's no future benefit to chase. What fixed terms remove is not independence from pressure, but insulation from generational accountability — and that insulation is exactly what damages democratic legitimacy today.

[C1.1] Fixed terms would trade genuine judicial independence for a thinner, majoritarian version of “legitimacy” that hollows out the court’s constitutional role. Democratic legitimacy for a high court does not mean its membership must track successive electorates; it means the court can apply the constitution against those electorates when the text and precedent require it. Life tenure supplies that insulation. Fixed, staggered terms systematically erode it.
[C1.2] Under any predictable vacancy schedule, every election cycle becomes a contest to rewrite the court’s long-term direction. Justices then decide cases knowing that a politically timed replacement is already on the calendar. That knowledge is pressure—subtler than a reappointment vote, yet real. It invites strategic moderation late in a term, public campaigning around impending vacancies, and the permanent institutionalization of confirmation warfare.
[C1.3] Far from cooling stakes, regular openings turn court control into a standing campaign promise rather than an occasional contingency. The result is a bench whose median continuously tracks recent political victories rather than the slower, more insulated evolution life tenure produces.
[C1.4] Independence is not merely the absence of reappointment incentives for the individual justice. It is the institutional capacity of the court to remain counter-majoritarian across decades. Eighteen-year terms still allow a determined political coalition to reshape constitutional doctrine within one generation—faster, more reliably, and more openly than mortality ever has. That accelerates turnover at the expense of the stability and detachment that make unpopular but legally correct rulings durable.
[C1.5] A court whose composition is deliberately refreshed by each political era loses the authority to tell that era it has exceeded constitutional bounds.
[C1.6] What PRO labels a legitimacy deficit is actually the price of genuine independence. Generational accountability belongs to the elected branches and to the amendment process, not to the tribunal charged with checking them. Convert that tribunal into a rotating prize and you damage the very independence that gives judicial power any legitimate claim on citizens’ obedience.
