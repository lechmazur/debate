[P1.1] When a storefront puts a 'Buy' button beside a book, game, film, or album, charges a purchase price comparable to the physical version, and files it in 'your library,' the buyer is not renting. Yet under revocable licensing, that library can shrink years later because a publisher lost distribution rights, a platform closed, an account was flagged, or a company edited or withdrew the work. The money is gone, the copy is gone, and the consumer never owned the thing they paid to own.
[P1.2] Meaningful ownership restores the basic bargain. It does not mean owning the copyright, copying without limit, or demanding free updates and servers forever. It means owning your copy: a durable right to access, keep, reread, replay, and watch it; to move it across your devices; to lend, resell, or give it away once, with your access ending on transfer; and to pass a collection to family rather than see a lifetime's purchases vanish with an account. Those were normal incidents of owning a book or disc, and digitization should not quietly erase them.
[P1.3] That protection matters because digital libraries are long-term investments built in reliance on permanence. Consumers pay title by title, curate collections for children, annotate books, build game backlogs, and preserve music and films that may later leave streaming catalogs. If platforms can revoke at will, they hold all post-sale power: they can raise the cost of leaving, discourage rival stores, eliminate secondhand competition, and make culture dependent on whichever licensing deals survive this quarter.
[P1.4] Ownership is also practical. A transferable copy can be tied to one active user, watermarked, or deactivated on resale, just as a used disc changes hands without multiplying copies. Explicit subscriptions and rentals can remain licenses; no one confuses them with purchases. The line consumers need is simple: if it is sold as bought, it should behave as owned.

[C1.1] The "Buy" button is a labeling complaint dressed up as a property theory. If the harm is that consumers misunderstand what they're getting, the remedy is honest wording and refund rules, not rewriting the legal nature of a copy of bits. Ownership is the wrong tool because digital goods are not scarce objects, and every attempt to make them behave like scarce objects makes consumers worse off.
[C1.2] Consider what "meaningful ownership" actually requires. A used disc can be resold because there is one disc. A digital file is a perfect, costless copy. To let it be "sold once, with access ending on transfer," someone must verify that your copy died when the buyer's was born.
[C1.3] That means mandatory, permanent, centralized DRM: account tracking, device fingerprinting, watermarking, revocation servers that must run forever. PRO's own plan says so: "tied to one active user, watermarked, or deactivated on resale." That is not freedom from the platform; that is the platform as perpetual sheriff over every file you hold. The least-locked digital goods today are DRM-free downloads that come with licenses, not ownership.
[C1.4] Second, resale of identical copies destroys the market that funds the work. A used paperback is worn and competes weakly with new. A "used" ebook is indistinguishable from new and sells at a discount forever. The predictable response is higher launch prices, smaller catalogs, and a shift to subscription-only, where no one owns anything.
[C1.5] Ownership rights would accelerate the very world PRO fears.
[C1.6] Third, licensing delivers what physical ownership never did: re-download after a lost phone, access across devices, patches, lower prices, instant availability. Disc rot, dead consoles, and orphaned formats erased far more "owned" media than any account suspension has.
[C1.7] Revocations are rare, newsworthy because rare, and fixable with narrow rules: clear labels, refunds when a title is withdrawn, export rights when a store closes. Those protect consumers without conscripting them into an ownership regime that costs more, locks more, and delivers less.
