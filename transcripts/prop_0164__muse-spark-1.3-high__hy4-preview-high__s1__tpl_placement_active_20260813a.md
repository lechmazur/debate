[P1.1] Publicly funded science has a reliability problem, not a novelty shortage. Thousands of eye-catching findings in psychology, biomedicine, and social science shape medical practice, policy, and further research, yet when independent teams try to reproduce them, a disturbing share fail. Every unreplicated false lead then multiplies waste: other labs build on it, graduate students chase ghosts, companies and agencies design interventions on sand. Funding more novel projects without verifying the old ones is pouring faster into a leaky bucket.
[P1.2] The reason is incentives, not bad intentions. Journals, hiring committees, and grant panels reward breakthrough claims. Replication is slower, less glamorous, and professionally risky, so it is chronically under-supplied. Telling researchers replication is “allowed” changes nothing.
[P1.3] Without earmarked money, early-career scientists cannot stake careers on it and labs cannot plan for it.
[P1.4] That is why the share must be fixed and meaningful. A fixed set-aside is a commitment device: it guarantees a standing pipeline, creates journals’ and departments’ expectation that replications will come, and lets funders triage strategically — prioritizing high-stakes, highly cited, or policy-relevant results, using preregistration and shared protocols to keep costs down. Discretionary leftovers will always lose to the next shiny proposal; a protected budget will not.
[P1.5] Yes, this means fewer novel grants. That trade is worth it. Ten solid bricks build more than a hundred crumbling ones. A single flawed cancer finding or educational intervention can burn far more public money downstream than a replication costs upfront, while a confirmed result becomes safe ground for dozens of genuine innovations.
[P1.6] Novelty without credibility has negative value.
[P1.7] Public funders owe taxpayers more than excitement. They owe knowledge that can be trusted and acted on. Reserving a fixed slice for replication turns verification from an afterthought into infrastructure — and makes the rest of the budget actually worth spending.

[C1.1] Everyone agrees publicly funded science must be trustworthy. But a fixed grant share for replication is the wrong tool. It is a rigid tax on discovery that misfires precisely because it is fixed.
[C1.2] A set-aside treats all replications as equally valuable. They are not. A high-stakes clinical or policy finding deserves independent verification; a minor priming effect that nobody builds on does not. Yet a guaranteed pipeline does not triage—it fills a quota.
[C1.3] Panels will fund replications that are cheap, safe, and easy, not necessarily the ones that matter. It also ignores that replications often fail for benign reasons like different settings, mislabeling context-sensitive findings as false. That is how public money re-tests trivia while a novel battery material or pathogen surveillance method goes unfunded.
[C1.4] Nor is novelty the villain. Most science self-corrects through follow-up studies, meta-analysis, and failure to generalize; dead ends are abandoned long before a formal replication is commissioned. The "solid bricks" metaphor in [P1.5] assumes we can spot crumbling work before funding it, but novelty is the very bet we cannot pre-judge. Slashing the novel budget to pre-fund verification of old claims reduces the new findings that future work depends on.
[C1.5] We would trade a shot at the next CRISPR or mRNA platform to re-check someone else’s mouse maze.
[C1.6] The fixed share also distorts incentives. It creates suppliers dependent on the earmark and signals that exploration is secondary. It removes discretion to reject weak replications and fund bold ideas. Worse, it excuses funders from real fixes: enforce data and code sharing, reward rigorous design in every grant, and launch targeted replication consortia only when a field shows genuine crisis.
[C1.7] Those improve reliability where it counts without a permanent cut to the frontier.
[C1.8] A leaky bucket is fixed by patching holes, not by pouring a fixed percentage of water on the ground. Fund replication where risk is real and high; do not mortgage discovery to a quota.

[P2.1] Fixed does not mean indiscriminate. A fixed share decides how much is protected for verification, not which studies get checked. Within that earmark panels can — and under this proposal must — triage toward high-stakes, highly cited, policy-relevant claims. The claim in [C1.2] that a guaranteed pipeline "fills a quota" confuses a floor with a lottery.
[P2.2] Novel grants already fund plenty of cheap, safe, incremental work; nothing about earmarking makes replication uniquely vulnerable to gaming if funders set preregistration and importance criteria.
[P2.3] Second, self-correction is a comforting myth. Follow-up studies that extend a finding are not replications: they assume the original is true and add new twists, so failures get buried as "boundary conditions" rather than exposed. Meta-analysis then pools biased literature. Dead ends are not quietly abandoned; graduate theses, systematic reviews, and startup pitches keep citing the flashy original for years.
[P2.4] That is precisely why we need funded, adversarial, direct re-tests rather than hoping the literature tidies itself.
[P2.5] Nor would this mortgage discovery to re-check someone's mouse maze. A meaningful share — think single-digit insurance premium, not half the budget — leaves the vast majority for novelty while protecting its value. CRISPR and mRNA did not emerge from unchecked speculation; they depended on reliable molecular biology that others could build on. Confirming foundations accelerates breakthroughs, it does not steal them.
[P2.6] Finally, data sharing and better design are necessary but cannot substitute for money to redo the work. Open data without funded time, staff, and reagents is an unfunded mandate. Discretion to fund replication when convenient has existed for decades and produced almost none. That is what a commitment device fixes.
[P2.7] Patching holes is exactly what replication funding does: it finds where the leaks are instead of assuming better pouring technique will seal them.
