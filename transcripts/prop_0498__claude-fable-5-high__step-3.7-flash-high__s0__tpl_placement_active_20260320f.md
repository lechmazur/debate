[P1.1] The umpire cannot also bat for one of the teams. Yet that is exactly what Apple and Google do today: they write the rules of the app marketplace, enforce them, collect the tolls — and then field their own apps against the very developers who depend on them. Structural separation is the only remedy that matches the structure of the problem.
[P1.2] First, the conflict of interest is built into the corporate form, not into any particular behavior. A platform that competes downstream has a permanent, rational incentive to tilt search rankings, withhold APIs, copy successful third-party apps, and exempt itself from rules it imposes on rivals. Apple's own apps come pre-installed, skip the commission it charges others, and have historically enjoyed access to system capabilities competitors were denied. When Apple's privacy rules restricted third-party ad tracking while its own advertising business grew, that wasn't a glitch — it was the predictable output of dual roles.
[P1.3] Second, conduct remedies demonstrably fail here. Self-preferencing happens inside ranking algorithms, API permissions, and review queues that regulators cannot audit in real time. Enforcement arrives years late, fines become a line-item cost, and the platform always knows more than the watchdog. We learned this lesson before: railroads were barred from owning the freight they carried, and banks were separated from securities dealing, precisely because policing intent inside a conflicted firm is hopeless.
[P1.4] Separation removes the incentive instead of endlessly chasing the symptoms.
[P1.5] Third, the stakes are dynamic, not just static. Developers and investors describe a "kill zone": build something successful on the platform and the platform clones it. That chills the next generation of apps before they're ever written — an invisible cost consumers never see.
[P1.6] Separation keeps everything users value — security review, payments, distribution — intact. It simply ensures the marketplace's owner profits from a thriving ecosystem rather than from rigging it. A neutral platform is a better platform.

[C1.1] PRO’s case rests on a flawed mischaracterization of app store operators as passive, neutral umpires, ignoring that the very value users derive from these platforms comes from tight integration between the store, the operating system, and first-party apps. Apple’s built-in Camera, Messages and Notes apps are not incidental competitors to third-party developers—they are core components of the iOS experience users pay a premium for, and separation would gut the seamless, secure ecosystem that makes the platform desirable. The historical regulatory analogies PRO cites—railroads, banks—are inapt.
[C1.2] Those industries were separated because their core business was providing passive, utility-style infrastructure to direct competitors, with no consumer-facing product value tied to owning both sides of the market. App stores are not passive toll roads: their first-party offerings are a core part of the product users choose, not a parasitic side bet. Forcing separation here would be like requiring a stadium owner to sell all their concession stands simply because they own the venue, even if those concessions are a big part of why fans attend.
[C1.3] Worse, PRO ignores that far less disruptive, proven regulatory tools already exist to address the specific harms of self-preferencing, without collateral damage. Rules like the EU Digital Markets Act’s bans on search ranking self-preferencing, mandatory interoperability for core APIs, and prohibitions on forcing developers to use the platform’s payment system target the exact bad acts PRO cites, without destroying the security and integration benefits users get from coordinated platform development. PRO’s “kill zone” argument is similarly overblown.
[C1.4] Third-party developers thrive by filling gaps first-party apps do not prioritize: specialized photo editing tools, cross-platform productivity suites, niche social platforms for specific communities. Even when first-party apps add overlapping features, that is pro-competitive innovation that benefits users, not a chilling threat. Separation would sacrifice user security, integration and choice on the altar of a one-size-fits-all fix to a problem that can be addressed surgically.
