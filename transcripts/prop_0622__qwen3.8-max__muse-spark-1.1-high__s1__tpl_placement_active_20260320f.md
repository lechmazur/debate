[P1.1] The core question is how governments can cut emissions most reliably, at lowest cost, without entrenching incumbents. Our path is simple: make pollution expensive, then return the money equally to citizens.
[P1.2] A rising carbon price does what subsidies cannot: it reaches nearly every emission source and lets millions of households, firms, and investors choose the cheapest reductions. It makes coal dirtier relative to gas, gas dirtier relative to wind, inefficient cars costlier, and retrofits valuable. No ministry needs to know which battery chemistry or hydrogen pathway will win. The price tells the truth.
[P1.3] Subsidies, by contrast, spend scarce public money on selected winners. They can help niche technologies, but as the main tool they invite lobbying, overcapacity, and capture. A subsidy for electric cars helps richer buyers; a subsidy for green steel may protect incumbent firms more than the climate. It also hides the cost of pollution instead of correcting it.
[P1.4] The dividend is the decisive advantage. Equal per-capita rebates turn a technocratic tax into a visible social contract. Because high-income households generally consume more energy, they pay more into the system; lower-income households, which consume less, often receive more than they pay. That makes the policy progressive, shields vulnerable communities from energy price shocks, and builds a durable majority for continued increases.
[P1.5] Without this rebate, carbon pricing is politically fragile: voters see a hidden tax and rebel.
[P1.6] Opponents will say subsidies build industries. But industrial policy is not a substitute for economy-wide incentives. If governments want innovation, they can fund basic research separately; the proposition is about the better use of large climate revenues. Returning those revenues as dividends is cleaner, fairer, and harder to corrupt.
[P1.7] The choice is between a price that makes everyone solve pollution and a subsidy machine that asks officials to pick the solution. Vote for the price.

[C1.5] The dividend also wastes the one-time fiscal opportunity of carbon revenue. Returning it as cash to support consumption while clean infrastructure remains scarce is economically backwards. A check subsidizes the present; a plant lowers costs for decades.
[C1.6] Finally, the progressivity claim in [P1.4] is spreadsheet fiction. Equal per-capita ignores real inequality: cold states vs mild, rural drivers vs transit-served cities, displaced welders vs laptop workers. The check is identical; the costs are not.
