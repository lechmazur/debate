[P1.1] Every year, thousands of couples who cannot carry a pregnancy find someone who will. Prohibition does not change that number. It changes only where the pregnancy happens and who gets to protect the woman carrying it.
[P1.2] That is the real question here: not whether surrogacy carries risk, but which legal regime handles the risk best. There are three options. Ban compensation and watch the practice go underground or abroad. Permit it but refuse to regulate.
[P1.3] Or legalize it openly with enforceable protections. Only the third option puts a surrogate in a room with her own lawyer.
[P1.4] Consider what a ban actually produces. Countries that permit only unpaid "altruistic" surrogacy see chronic shortages, so intended parents fly to jurisdictions with weaker protections and hire women there. The wealthy still get their children; the woman just gets fewer rights. And where domestic surrogacy continues under altruistic rules, payment doesn't vanish; it becomes "expenses," paid in cash, unenforceable in court.
[P1.5] The surrogate carries all the risk and cannot sue if the money never arrives.
[P1.6] Regulation reverses that. A licensed framework can require independent legal counsel paid for by the intended parents, comprehensive medical and life insurance, psychological screening, compensation that does not depend on delivering a live birth, an absolute right for the surrogate to make her own medical decisions, and pre-birth parentage orders so no child is left in legal limbo. None of this exists in a black market. All of it is possible only when the transaction is visible.
[P1.7] Then there is the woman herself. We pay clinical trial participants, plasma donors, deep-sea welders, and wet nurses throughout history. Nobody says a firefighter is "exploited" merely because she is paid for a dangerous job; we say she deserves fair pay and safety standards. Insisting that pregnancy alone must be done for free is not protection; it is telling women the one form of labor they may not be compensated for is the one only they can perform.
[P1.8] Coercion is real. The answer to coercion is enforceable rights, and rights require legality.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to build a legal market in which one class of people—overwhelmingly wealthier—pays another class—overwhelmingly poorer—to gestate and hand over a baby. No layer of paperwork changes what is being sold.
[C1.2] Start with the fatal flaw in "strong protections against coercion." In compensated surrogacy, the payment *is* the pressure. A woman with a mortgage in arrears who signs a $50,000 gestation contract has not been coerced by any actor a regulator can prosecute; she has been coerced by her circumstances, which the market is designed to exploit. That is why surrogates everywhere cluster among low-income women, and why almost no wealthy women do it.
[C1.3] You cannot screen out economic desperation when economic desperation is the supply chain. Regulation can police fraud; it cannot make a sale of one's body by the poor to the rich into an equal bargain.
[C1.4] Second, the experiment has been run. India, Thailand, and Cambodia each legalized or tolerated commercial surrogacy, watched exploitation industrialize—dormitories of contracted women, brokers skimming fees, abandoned disabled infants like the well-known Baby Gammy case—and then banned it. Legal status didn't tame the market; it scaled it. Legalization doesn't merely absorb existing demand, it manufactures new demand, and supply must be recruited from ever-poorer women.
[C1.5] Third, contracts over pregnancy are monstrous to enforce and hollow if unenforced. What happens when the contract requires selective reduction and the surrogate refuses? Either the state compels a medical procedure on her body, or the "enforceable rights" the other side promises are fiction. There is no third option.
[C1.6] Finally, the child. Compensation contingent on relinquishment means a human being changes hands pursuant to payment. We prohibit that everywhere else in law for a reason that doesn't dissolve because the transfer is prenatal.
[C1.7] The humane alternative is genuinely altruistic surrogacy with documented expense reimbursement—generosity without a price tag on a child. Legal markets in gestation fail the women and the children they claim to protect.

[P2.5] India, Thailand, Cambodia [C1.4] were the second regime from my opening—tolerated, unlicensed, broker-run.
