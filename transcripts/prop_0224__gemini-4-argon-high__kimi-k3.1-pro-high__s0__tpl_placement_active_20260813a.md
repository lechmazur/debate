[P1.2] First, nuclear life-extension is the fastest, cheapest carbon-abatement tool available in this decade. Extending an existing reactor’s operating license for another twenty to forty years requires modest refurbishment of turbines and instrumentation while reusing the most expensive assets: the containment structure, land, workforce, and high-voltage grid connection. Whenever a reactor closes prematurely, fossil gas or coal immediately rushes in to fill the round-the-clock void, wiping out years of renewable additions overnight. Public dollars spent keeping existing reactors online deliver massive, immediate emissions avoidance starting in 2026 at a fraction of the cost of building any replacement generation from scratch.
[P1.3] Second, scaling wind, solar, and batteries over the next decade faces severe diminishing returns per public dollar. Cheap solar panels do not equal cheap system decarbonization. Worse, lithium-ion batteries remain prohibitively expensive for anything beyond a few hours of storage.
[P1.4] Because batteries cannot bridge multi-day winter wind lulls, renewable-heavy grids must keep natural gas plants firing in the background. Public dollars get burned on curtailment, grid congestion, and redundant backup, shrinking the actual carbon reduced per dollar.

Life-extensions lock in immediate reductions while new reactors, especially standardized units built at existing nuclear or retiring coal sites that bypass transmission bottlenecks, come online in the early-to-mid 2030s. Operating above ninety percent capacity, every nuclear gigawatt replaces fossil baseload directly, delivering faster, deeper decarbonization for the public purse.
