[P1.1] Farmland is not another office tower. It is irreplaceable ecological infrastructure and the economic bedrock of rural life, and when Wall Street treats it as an inflation hedge, both food resilience and communities pay the price.
[P1.2] Institutional ownership raises land prices far beyond what farm income can support. Pension funds and farmland REITs can tolerate lower yields and longer paybacks than a 30-year-old farmer bidding for her first 200 acres. In the US Midwest, that bidding war has pushed prices to levels where ownership is impossible without inheriting land. Result: fewer owner-operators, more tenant farmers, fewer families spending in town, coaching little league, supporting the school levy.
[P1.3] Rural communities don't collapse because farming is unproductive; they hollow out because the people who farm no longer own the place they live.
[P1.4] That tenure shift also destroys resilience. An institutional owner doesn't farm. It leases, usually on short cash-rent terms, to the largest operator who can maximize row-crop returns. That operator has no incentive for long rotations, cover crops, or rebuilding organic matter; the incentive is corn-on-corn, mined soil, drained wetlands.
[P1.5] The owner wants a stable dividend. For resilience we need the opposite: owners who internalize soil health, keep livestock and grain together, experiment with diversity because their children will inherit the consequences. Absentee ownership breaks that feedback.
[P1.6] A cap solves concentration without killing capital. No one proposes banning investment. Farmers still need loans, not landlords. A 10 or 15 percent county-level cap, or a statewide acreage ceiling, keeps markets functional but stops a handful of funds from controlling the food supply.
[P1.7] Governments already limit foreign ownership and corporate farming for exactly this reason.
[P1.8] If we want food systems that survive droughts and towns that survive generations, we must keep farmland in farming hands.

[C1.1] Price ran ahead of farm income long before pension funds discovered farmland, and blaming them mistakes a symptom for the disease.
[C1.2] Institutional owners hold only a small fraction of U.S. and European farmland, low single digits. A 10 or 15 percent county cap as in [P1.6] would therefore bind almost nowhere, doing nothing for affordability while adding bureaucracy and legal games over who counts as “farming.” If concentration ever arrived, the cap would be far too high to stop it.
[C1.3] What the cap would stop is needed capital. American farmers are aging, land is enormously expensive, and most beginners cannot buy 200 acres outright. They need sellers able to retire and landlords offering stable leases. Institutions provide both: liquidity to exiting families, and long leases that let a tenant farm without a million-dollar mortgage.
[C1.4] Cut that channel and sellers get lower prices, tenants get fewer options, and investment in drainage, irrigation, storage and drought-proofing shrinks — the opposite of resilience.
[C1.5] The stewardship story in [P1.4] and [P1.5] also gets tenure backwards. An indebted owner-operator facing a loan payment has every incentive to plant corn-on-corn and skip cover crops. [C1.6] Finally, towns do not hollow out because the deed sits in a pension portfolio that belongs, ironically, to teachers and nurses. They hollow out because machines replaced labor. A tenant family still buys in town and sends kids to school. Starving the countryside of outside money will not bring sons and daughters home.
[C1.7] If governments want resilience, fund conservation leases and beginner loans rather than capping the investors willing to finance them.

[C2.4] The debt-not-landlord contrast [P2.3] is cruellest to beginners. A mortgage on $2 million of Illinois ground at today's rates extracts far more cash from town each year than a lease, and puts all the weather and price risk on the young farmer. One failed harvest and the bank forecloses. A lease puts that risk on the deep-pocket owner while the tenant builds scale and credit.
[C2.5] The retiring seller still gets liquidity and spends it locally. The distant owner, ironically, is often California teachers or Midwestern union pensions.
[C2.6] Tenure insecurity is real, but it is not an institutional invention. One- or two-year cash leases [P2.5] are the norm in farmer-to-farmer rentals too. The fix is to reward longer, conservation-linked leases for all landlords, which patient capital can afford more easily than a leveraged neighbor. An indebted owner facing foreclosure has no freedom to plant for 2035.
[C2.7] Finally, fifty 200-acre owners [P2.7] would use the same GPS tractors and still fail, because 200 acres of grain no longer supports a family. Scale came from technology, not the deed's zip code.
