[C1.1] The motion leaps from a real premise—octopuses are cognitively complex—to a sweeping remedy: a worldwide ban. That leap is the weak point. Cognition establishes moral standing and raises the welfare bar; it does not by itself prove that every form of industrial confinement is indefensible. The relevant question is what conditions, densities, handling, enrichment, transport, and slaughter methods are compatible with octopus welfare.
[C1.2] Those are design and regulation questions, not proof that the entire production model must be prohibited.
[C1.3] The opposing case depends on treating industrial farming as one fixed blueprint: bare tanks, crowding, neglect. But scalable production can be held to enforceable standards—minimum space, shelter, environmental complexity, low stocking, individual monitoring, escape-proof design, and humane endpoints. If those requirements make octopus farming expensive, that is an argument for strict standards and higher costs, not for prohibition. Societies regulate other cognitively rich farmed animals rather than banning their farming outright because the ethical issue is how they are kept, not merely that humans manage them.
[C1.4] A global ban is also the least proportionate remedy. It would foreclose even well-designed, low-density facilities while pushing production toward weak enforcement or informal markets. Prohibition does not erase demand; it makes the supply chain less visible. Licensing, inspection, labeling, liability, and a cautious moratorium where evidence is thin target actual cruelty while preserving the possibility of improvement.
[C1.5] The motion’s logic proves too much: if complex cognition plus confinement equals indefensible cruelty, many existing animal industries fail the same test, yet the rational response is not a symbolic worldwide ban but demanding better conditions. Octopus farming may be risky and should face a demanding standard. That supports caution and strict regulation, not the categorical ban proposed.
