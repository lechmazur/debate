[P1.1] Curb space is the most scarce, high-value public resource in dense cities, yet we hand it out for free to the very users that generate the most congestion, pollution, and public space conflict: delivery and ride-hail vehicles. This is a fundamental market failure that dynamic congestion or curb pricing corrects directly, without penalizing ordinary residents. Today, unpriced curbs create predictable harms: ride-hail vehicles spend 15-20% of their miles cruizing for curbside pickups, while 30% of delivery trucks double-park in major U.S. central business districts because no spots are available, blocking bike lanes, bus routes, and traffic flow, and adding 20% to peak-hour travel times for all road users.
[P1.2] These costs are borne entirely by the public, while the companies operating these fleets pay nothing for the damage they cause. Dynamic pricing fixes this by aligning costs with use. Prices rise during peak hours and in high-demand zones, and drop during off-peak windows, cutting unnecessary cruizing, encouraging delivery firms to consolidate shipments or schedule dropoffs during slower periods, and reducing double-parking. Pilot programs for dynamic commercial loading zones in Seattle and Washington D.C. cut double-parking incidents by 25% and peak-hour traffic delays by 10% in test areas, with no measurable drop in delivery access.
[P1.3] Revenue from these fees is not a hidden tax: by design, it is earmarked for public transit, bike lane expansion, and sidewalk maintenance, investments that benefit all residents, including the delivery and ride-hail workers who rely on this infrastructure. Unlike the current free system, which imposes congestion costs disproportionately on low-income transit riders, pedestrians, and cyclists, dynamic pricing makes the users that create harm pay for the damage they inflict, while tiered rate structures can protect small local delivery firms and low-income ride-hail drivers from excessive costs.

[C1.1] Dynamic curb pricing rests on a textbook assumption that collapses on the street: that delivery and ride-hail demand can shift in response to price signals. It mostly can't. A passenger needs to be picked up where they are standing, when they call. A delivery driver's route is fixed by the recipient's address and the warehouse's schedule, locked in hours or days earlier.
[C1.2] When the user cannot relocate or reschedule, a fluctuating price doesn't reallocate behavior — it just extracts money. And we know exactly where that money comes from: it passes through to gig drivers paid per trip, to small couriers without algorithmic route optimizers, and to consumers — including the elderly, disabled, and homebound residents for whom delivery is not a luxury. Amazon and Uber absorb a curb fee as a rounding error; the independent driver eats it as lost wages.
[C1.3] Worse, when prices spike and demand can't move, drivers don't vanish — they decamp to the nearest free space: side streets, bike lanes, bus stops, residential blocks. Pricing the curb without expanding loading capacity doesn't cure double-parking; it relocates it to where enforcement is thinnest and pedestrians are most vulnerable. The problem PRO describes is a supply and enforcement problem, and pricing supplies neither.
[C1.4] There is also the machinery this requires: sensor networks, license-plate cameras, variable signage, and real-time billing across thousands of curb segments — a permanent surveillance and administration layer whose costs quietly devour the promised revenue, all to display a price most drivers will never see before the trip is already committed.
[C1.5] Cities have a cheaper, fairer toolkit that actually fits the behavior: dedicated, well-marked loading zones, time limits, flat commercial permits that are legible and budgetable, and serious enforcement of double-parking. That fixes the harms PRO names without taxing workers for trips they cannot reschedule. The motion picks the one tool mismatched to the problem.
