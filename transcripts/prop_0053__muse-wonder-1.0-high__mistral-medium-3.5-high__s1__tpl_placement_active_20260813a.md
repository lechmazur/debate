[P1.1] A fifteen-year-old does not encounter a lip filler promotion the way an adult encounters a billboard. She encounters it inside a trusted routine: a favorite creator whispering between skincare tips, showing a swollen-to-perfect transformation in fifteen seconds, linking a discount code. That is not ordinary advertising. It is medical marketing delivered through parasocial trust, precisely when identity, peer approval, and body image are most unstable.
[P1.2] Cosmetic procedures are not makeup. Fillers can migrate, block blood vessels, and require dissolving. Rhinoplasty permanently alters breathing and bone. Brazilian butt lifts and liposuction carry anesthesia and infection risks, plus revision surgery and scarring.
[P1.3] Yet influencer content reverses the medical order: the reveal comes first, the risks — if mentioned at all — come as a joke about bruising, and dissatisfaction is framed as needing a top-up. Minors are primed to overweight the reward and underweight that tail risk.
[P1.4] Existing labels fail because they were designed for lipstick, not injections. A small #ad or paid partnership tag tells a teenager a post is commercial; it tells her nothing about permanence, contraindications, age limits, psychological screening, or the fact that the glowing result is filtered, angled, and selected. Stronger warnings would correct that asymmetry at the start of the content: this is a medical procedure, results may be irreversible, complications can require surgery, and a consultation does not guarantee suitability.
[P1.5] Age-gating is the matching safeguard. When a creator or clinic markets to an audience they know skews young — through youth-oriented platforms, trends, language, or analytics — that content should sit behind meaningful age checks and not be algorithmically pushed to under-18 accounts. That is narrow, not a ban. Adults keep access, creators keep speaking, and clinics keep advertising to adults.
[P1.6] What ends is the profitable loophole of claiming to target 25-year-olds while harvesting attention from 14-year-olds.
[P1.7] Children receive extra protection for alcohol, gambling, and tanning precisely because persuasion works and harms endure. Elective surgery marketed as self-care deserves no weaker standard.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes age-gating and stronger warnings can be cleanly applied, but the standard “audiences likely to include minors” is unworkably vague. A creator with 15% under-18 viewers would face the same restrictions as one targeting teens, because any popular content attracts minors. Age-gating then becomes a blunt instrument, blocking adult access to information about legal, regulated procedures. Minors will still bypass gates via screenshots, reposts, or shared devices, rendering the measure futile.
[C1.2] Stronger warnings are equally flawed. Experience shows that warnings on social media often lead to desensitization rather than deterrence. Graphic disclaimers risk exaggerating harms, making cosmetic procedures seem more dangerous than everyday activities like driving or contact sports, while doing little to alter minor behavior. Existing safeguards already address the core concern: TikTok and Instagram already restrict cosmetic procedure ads to users over 18, and clinics require parental consent for minors.
[C1.3] The “loophole” is a strawman.
[C1.4] The collateral damage is severe. Invasive age verification alienates users, imposes heavy compliance costs on creators and platforms, and chills speech through fear of misclassification. Small creators, lacking legal resources, would self-censor to avoid risk. It also treats young adults as lacking agency, despite their legal rights.
[C1.5] Moreover, this sets a dangerous precedent. If we accept special restrictions for cosmetic procedures, the next step will be age-gating skincare, fitness, or fashion content. This is not a slippery slope fallacy; it is the inevitable expansion of any censorship regime. The cure—censorship, inefficacy, and overreach—is far worse than the disease.

Minors can borrow accounts or find screenshots of alcohol and gambling marketing too, but friction still reduces casual, repeated, algorithmic exposure — the drip that normalizes a procedure. Nor must adults lose access: using a platform’s existing logged-in age is not universal identity verification.
[P2.3] Existing safeguards miss the channel at issue. Restricting paid ad buys does not govern gifted treatments, discount codes, affiliate links, and ‘educational’ transformations functioning as sales. [P2.4] Starting protection at the clinic door starts too late.
[P2.5] Warnings need not exaggerate or compare surgery with driving. They must correct the impression the content creates: that fillers are reversible beauty tweaks, the filtered result is typical, and unhappiness means a top-up. Naming irreversibility, blocked vessels, revision surgery, contraindications, and possible refusal after screening is simply the missing half of the pitch. If vague labels breed desensitization, that argues for specific warnings before the reveal, not silence.

Commercial marketing for elective medical intervention can carry higher duties without censoring adult discussion.
