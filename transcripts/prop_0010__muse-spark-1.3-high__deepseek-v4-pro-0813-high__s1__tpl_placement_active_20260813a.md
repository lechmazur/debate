[P1.1] A nondisclosure agreement about a defective toaster protects trade secrets. A nondisclosure agreement about a boss who gropes assistants protects the groping.
[P1.2] That is the core distinction. Workplace harassment, discrimination, and abuse are not private embarrassments to be priced and buried. They are ongoing harms that repeat precisely because they stay secret. When one victim is paid and gagged, the next victim walks blind into the same office, the same shift, the same predator.
[P1.3] Enforceable NDAs do not resolve misconduct; they license its repetition. That is how serial abusers survive for years, moving from victim to victim while HR files stay sealed.
[P1.4] Talk of freedom of contract ignores how these clauses actually work. They are rarely negotiated between equals. They are buried in onboarding paperwork as a condition of getting hired, or sprung in a severance meeting when a traumatized worker needs rent money and a reference. A young warehouse worker, hotel housekeeper, or junior assistant facing a corporate legal department does not bargain; she signs.
[P1.5] Courts already refuse to enforce contracts signed under duress, for illegal purposes, or against public policy. Shielding civil rights violations fits squarely in that tradition.
[P1.6] Crucially, making these NDAs unenforceable does not ban settlements, does not force anyone to speak, and does not strip victims of privacy. It simply returns the choice to the victim. She can still accept money and choose silence for her own healing. What her abuser cannot do is send lawyers to punish her if she later warns a coworker, talks to a reporter, or testifies.
[P1.7] Privacy becomes a right, not a gag.
[P1.8] Harassment law only works if patterns are visible. Discrimination only ends if workplaces learn what they did wrong. No private contract should have the power to veto that public accountability.

[C1.1] The proposition starts from a real harm but lands on a remedy that would punish many of the people it claims to protect. It treats every workplace harassment, discrimination, or abuse allegation as an established fact and every confidentiality clause as a predator’s shield. Law cannot responsibly run on that assumption.
[C1.2] The biggest flaw is the settlement structure. Most harassment and discrimination claims are hotly disputed, expensive to prove, and deeply personal. An enforceable confidentiality clause is often the reason an employer will pay a worker before trial. Without it, the employer has little reason to settle: any payout can be followed next week by a press interview or a social media accusation, with no legal consequence.
[C1.3] The realistic result is not PRO’s image of victims who get paid and later speak. It is fewer off-ramps, lower settlement offers, and more workers forced into public litigation they never wanted.
[C1.4] PRO says unenforceability merely returns the choice to the victim. That ignores how bargaining power works. A worker can only trade confidentiality for money if the promise is enforceable. Take away enforceability and you do not liberate the worker; you strip away the one piece of leverage that often produces a quick, private, livable resolution.
[C1.5] A hotel housekeeper who wants three months’ pay and to rebuild her life without becoming a local news story loses that option.
[C1.6] The motion is also overbroad because it sweeps in unproven allegations. Before any finding of liability, it would make settlement agreements unstable and expose employers and accused individuals to reputational attack on claims that may be false or legally insufficient. That is why the better path is a surgical one: ban coercive pre-dispute gag clauses, preserve access to agencies and testimony, and refuse to enforce agreements that conceal ongoing crimes. Existing public-policy limits already address the worst cases.
[C1.7] A blanket rule does not end abuse; it ends the confidential settlement route that many victims deliberately choose. That is a poor trade.

If a worker can take a settlement and then tell a reporter or post that she was harassed and paid off, the employer has not purchased finality—it has purchased a reputational risk it cannot manage. That is not a technical point; it is the core of why employers settle quietly.
[C2.2] Strip out the enforceable gag and the rational response is lower offers, stronger defense, or litigation to clear the accused’s name, exactly the public process many workers want to avoid.
[C2.3] Nor does the housekeeper lose nothing [P2.2]. She loses the one thing she can trade. PRO says she can still remain quiet, but “can” is not a promise. The employer knows she cannot sell silence, so it will not pay a silence premium.
[C2.4] The three months’ pay is partly compensation for closing the matter. Void the promise and the price drops or the settlement disappears.
[C2.5] On unproven claims, defamation law cannot substitute for contract [P2.4]. Defamation requires proving falsity, often after months of publicity, and a disputed story can destroy a small business long before a verdict. An unenforceable NDA also creates a settle-then-accuse incentive: collect compensation, then republish the accusation without violating any gag. That is not protecting victims; it is redesigning settlements as a subsidy for later public accusations.
[C2.6] Finally, the motion sweeps broader than the coercive severance scenario. It would void confidentiality even in claims negotiated with counsel, explicit consideration, and a genuine desire for privacy on both sides. The better rule polices coercion without erasing the only asset a worker can exchange for an early, quiet resolution. The motion leaves victims with no more legal speech than they already have, but with less bargaining power and fewer private exits.
