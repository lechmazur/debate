[P1.1] Universities should teach students to think with powerful tools, not pretend those tools do not exist. Allowing AI-assisted drafting — disclosed and owned by the student — does exactly that.
[P1.2] In professional life, value no longer lies in producing a first draft from a blank page. A junior analyst, engineer, or journalist is expected to prompt, draft quickly, then check, revise, and defend the result. A history student who uses AI to outline competing explanations for the Cold War, then verifies claims against readings and writes the final judgment herself, is practicing that exact skill. For a dyslexic student or non-native speaker, the same aid clears surface barriers so seminars judge ideas, not grammar.
[P1.3] Banning the draft step does not protect rigor; it freezes pedagogy in a pre-AI workplace that graduates will never enter.
[P1.4] Disclosure plus accountability preserves integrity better than prohibition. The rule is simple: say what the tool did, and you own every fact and inference that remains. That mirrors how scholarship already works — research assistants, editors, statistical software are permitted because the author takes responsibility. Errors are not excused by “the AI wrote it,” they count against the student.
[P1.5] What is assessed shifts upward, from recall and fluency to verification, sourcing, and argument.
[P1.6] Prohibition, by contrast, is unenforceable and corrosive. Detection tools are notoriously unreliable, flagging non-native English and missing polished AI text. The result is not honesty but a lottery that punishes the candid and rewards the stealthy, while wasting faculty time on policing rather than teaching.
[P1.7] The motion says most coursework, not all. Foundational skills — introductory language, basic proofs, in-class exams — can still require unassisted performance. For the rest, let students draft with AI in the open and be judged on the accuracy and reasoning they submit.

[C1.1] The motion asks universities to trust a signature rather than a mind. [P1.4] says the rule is simple: disclose the tool, and you own every fact and inference. But owning the final text is not owning the reasoning behind it. If AI produces the outline, inferential steps, and objection handling, the student contributes selection and polish.
[C1.2] Accountability becomes a ritual: we grade errors in a text whose intellectual architecture the student never built.
[C1.3] The analogy to research assistants and software fails ([P1.4]). Those instruments do what the author directs. Generative drafting supplies the direction. A student can fact-check a Kant paper, correct a hallucinated citation, and smooth the prose, yet the framing—what counts as a relevant objection, which example carries weight—came from the machine.
[C1.4] Even verified facts sit inside an argument the student did not construct. The student's "judgment" is post-hoc approval of a system that knows nothing about the course. A degree should not certify that.
[C1.5] Nor does disclosure solve enforcement. PRO notes detection is unreliable ([P1.6]), but that cuts against them. If software cannot reliably flag AI text, it cannot audit a disclosure either; a student can disclose lightly while the model quietly supplies most of the reasoning, and no one can tell. The result is a new lottery: candid disclosers invite suspicion; stealthy users ignore the rule and hide behind undetectable text.
[C1.6] Worse, permitting assistance for "most coursework" changes norms: students will use it because it is allowed, and the hard practice of composing—where understanding forms—atrophies across disciplines, not just intro courses.
[C1.7] Accessibility is real ([P1.2]), but the answer is targeted support, not waiving the core requirement for all.
[C1.8] The alternative is not ignoring AI. It is protecting where thinking happens: in-class writing, scaffolded drafts, oral defenses. Keep unassisted drafting the default for most coursework. A degree should certify the demonstrated ability to think, not merely manage a machine.
