[P1.1] Nothing in this motion caps a price or bans a smaller pack. Shrink the bar, charge what you like — just say so where the shopper is actually looking.
[P1.2] The case turns on one asymmetry: prices are loud, quantities are silent. A price rise sits in bold on the shelf edge. A size cut arrives in the same familiar wrapper, same shelf position, same £1.80 you paid last month. A jar falling from 500g to 450g at an unchanged price is an eleven percent price increase, and almost nobody experiences it as one.
[P1.3] That is not consumer stupidity; it is the entire point of doing it that way.
[P1.4] "Unit pricing already exists" doesn't answer this. A unit price is a snapshot, not a change signal. To catch shrinkflation you must remember last month's grams for that specific product, notice today's, and divide — across a forty-item shop. Nobody does that.
[P1.5] Worse, when the pack shrinks, the per-100g figure silently resets to a new number with nothing marking the jump.
[P1.6] And the feasibility objection collapses on contact with any grocery app. Those apps already run "was £2, now £1.50" banners, strikethroughs, loyalty-price flags. The machinery for comparing today's offer with last week's is built, deployed, and used enthusiastically — in the one direction that flatters the retailer. The same database knows the old pack size.
[P1.7] This is a display choice, not a technical burden.
[P1.8] Notice who loses under silence. The honest manufacturer who raises the headline price looks greedy beside the competitor who quietly removes ten percent of the contents. Concealment is subsidised; transparency is punished. Requiring the disclosure rewards the firm that tells the truth.
[P1.9] Markets run on informed choice. Keep every freedom sellers have — just end the one that depends on the buyer not noticing.

[C1.1] Net quantity is printed on the pack, and unit pricing lets shoppers compare the value before them. [C1.2] “The same database knows the old pack size” is too quick. Listings change suppliers, product codes, recipes, multipacks and promotional formats. Which baseline controls: last week, last year, or pre-promotion size? Is a successor the same product?
[C1.3] Must marketplace apps verify every seller’s feed? “Display it clearly” conceals choices that determine whether an alert is accurate or falsely brands a redesign as shrinkflation.
[C1.4] Nor does weight alone measure value. A smaller pack may contain a concentrate or a portion designed to reduce waste. [C1.5] Salience is not free. Screens already carry prices, unit prices, discounts, loyalty offers and dietary information. Historical alerts attached to routine product churn train shoppers to ignore warnings. Retailers can reset identifiers or choose flattering comparison windows, while sellers inherit compliance costs and legal risk.
[C1.6] Those costs feed into prices and burden smaller sellers.
[C1.7] Rules against false quantities and misleading presentation can punish actual deception without this machinery. Retailers remain free to offer pack-history tools customers value. But feasibility is not justification: a feature easy for one large app is not a sound universal mandate. The motion promises attention it cannot guarantee and creates ambiguity it does not resolve.
[C1.8] A label should explain what is being sold, not turn every product revision into a state-required accusation.
