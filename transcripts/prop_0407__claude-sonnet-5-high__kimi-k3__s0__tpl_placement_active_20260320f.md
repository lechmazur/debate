[P1.1] The core question isn't whether employers have interests worth protecting—it's whether an employer's leverage over your paycheck should extend into what you do on your own time, with your own money, causing your employer no actual harm. It shouldn't, and the law should say so clearly.
[P1.2] Start with the power asymmetry. For most workers, a job is not one option among many equals—it's the thing that pays rent, buys insulin, keeps a family fed. When an employer can terminate someone for a lawful weekend activity, a political opinion posted from a personal phone, attending a protest, dating someone the boss dislikes, or smoking a legal product at home, that employer isn't protecting the business—it's exercising raw power over a person's private life because it can. At-will employment already gives employers enormous discretion over hiring and firing; without protection for off-duty conduct, that discretion becomes a tool for policing thought and lifestyle, not performance.
[P1.3] Consider the chilling effect. Workers who fear termination for lawful political speech, off-duty association, or personal choices will simply stop engaging in civic life—no protests, no yard signs, no controversial opinions online, no unpopular religious or lifestyle choices visible to anyone who might report back to HR. That's a quiet erosion of the civic and personal freedoms we claim to value, achieved not through government censorship but through economic coercion, which is arguably more effective and harder to challenge.
[P1.4] Note what the proposition does not do. It doesn't protect conduct that materially affects job performance—so conflicts of interest, safety-sensitive impairment, or genuine reputational harm tied to actual duties remain fair game for discipline. This is a narrow, targeted fix: it draws the line exactly where legitimate employer interest ends and private life begins. The current patchwork—a handful of state laws protecting some categories, nothing federal, wide gaps everywhere else—leaves millions of workers exposed to termination for conduct that has nothing to do with their competence or conduct at work.
[P1.5] That gap is the injustice this proposition closes.
