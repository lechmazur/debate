[P1.1] The modern workplace operates on a fundamental power imbalance: employers know exactly what every role is worth, while employees are kept in the dark. Mandating that large employers disclose pay bands and promotion rates by role is the only way to dismantle this information asymmetry and guarantee a fair labor market.
[P1.2] Without transparency, salary negotiations are rigged. When large companies hide pay bands, they leverage their systemic knowledge against individual workers. This disproportionately harms women and minorities, who are historically penalized for negotiating aggressively. Disclosing pay ranges ensures workers are compensated for the actual value of their labor, not their ability to guess a secret budget or their desperation to accept a lowball offer.
[P1.3] The mandate to disclose promotion rates by role is equally critical. Companies frequently boast about meritocracy while relying on opaque internal pipelines that trap certain employees in lower-level roles. By requiring hard promotion data, we force companies to align their practices with their rhetoric. It exposes dead-end roles and systemic biases, empowering prospective and current employees to make informed career decisions before investing years of their lives into an organization.
[P1.4] The resistance to this policy inevitably centers on the loss of "managerial flexibility." We must be honest about what that flexibility means in practice. Far too often, managerial flexibility is merely a euphemism for the unchecked power to allocate rewards based on favoritism, arbitrary subjective judgments, or who complains the loudest. It allows managers to quietly underpay loyal, high-performing employees while overpaying risky outside hires.
[P1.5] If standardizing pay and promotion criteria restricts a manager's ability to arbitrarily underpay a qualified worker, that is a vital feature of this policy, not a bug.
[P1.6] True meritocracy cannot exist in secret. When large employers are forced to operate in the light, fairness, market efficiency, and trust replace exploitation and bias.

When you strip away managerial discretion in favor of rigid, publicly audited grids, you force managers to rely strictly on safe, traditional credentials.
[C1.6] This mandate trades dynamic opportunity and individualized reward for a flattened, box-checking bureaucracy.
