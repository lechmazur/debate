[P1.1] Broad assertions about "culture" are the perfect rhetorical shield for arbitrary power. When a CEO mandates five days in the office while invoking vague notions of "synergy" or "belonging," they are not making a business case—they are issuing a decree and dressing it in language no employee can falsify. This is precisely why the burden must shift: employers who disrupt millions of workers' lives with return-to-office mandates should have to articulate concrete, role-specific business reasons, not hide behind the nebulous comfort of "culture."
[P1.2] Consider the asymmetry of evidence. During the pandemic, organizations proved that most knowledge work can be performed remotely without collapse. Productivity in many sectors held steady or rose. Yet mandates now return not because the work demands it, but because management misses corner offices, seeks to justify expensive real estate leases, or simply prefers visual supervision.
[P1.3] If a software engineer can ship code effectively from home, what specific collaboration breaks down on Tuesday at 2 PM in a conference room? If the answer is merely "we feel it" or "it builds culture," the mandate is a managerial preference, not a business necessity—and preferences should not override employees' time, commute costs, and caregiving responsibilities without scrutiny.
[P1.4] Role-specific justification forces honesty. It reveals whether a mandate is genuinely about client-facing duties, hands-on laboratory equipment, or data-security requirements—or simply about control and optics. It protects workers from discriminatory impacts, since blanket policies hit caregivers, disabled employees, and those in rural areas hardest. Transparency is not bureaucratic overhead; it is the minimum respect owed to people whose daily lives are being reorganized by fiat.
[P1.5] The standard should be simple: if you want to change where someone works, tell them why in terms that apply to their actual job. Anything less is management by assertion, and it deserves to be treated as such.

[P2.2] If culture truly drives measurable outcomes, the employer should be able to name the mechanism.
[P2.3] The interdependence argument backfires. Yes, an analyst benefits from proximity to senior colleagues. But that means the mandate should apply to that analyst—and perhaps to the seniors—with reasons specific to those roles. It does not justify forcing a payroll specialist or a data engineer into the office when their collaboration happens entirely through shared drives and video calls.
[P2.4] Blanket policies ignore the very interdependence they claim to serve. Role-specific justification is not a denial that work is connected; it is the tool that matches the policy to the actual connections.
[P2.5] The claim that this creates an unworkable adjudication regime misunderstands the burden. We are not proposing a new tribunal to grade business judgments. We are asking employers to state, in writing, why this role requires presence. Existing law already demands justifications when policies discriminate against caregivers or disabled workers.
[P2.6] The same standard should apply before disruption occurs, not after a lawsuit. A manager who cannot articulate why a coder must commute three hours a week has not made a business decision; they have made a convenience decision.
[P2.7] Finally, market discipline is a slow and brutal filter that punishes workers before it punishes employers. Attrition hits employees who cannot afford to quit. Reputational damage takes years to accumulate. By then, caregivers have left the workforce and disabled workers have been pushed out.
