[P1.1] Mental-health care is not ordinary customer service. A chatbot can be patient and available, but it cannot assume professional responsibility. [P1.2] A fixed-session handoff draws a bright line before convenience hardens into unsupervised treatment.
[P1.3] The obligation is handoff, not abandonment. After the statutory number of sessions, the provider must connect the patient to a qualified human who assumes clinical responsibility; that professional may retain AI as an adjunct. Emergencies should trigger earlier escalation, while the fixed limit catches quieter failures: depression framed as “low motivation,” mania mistaken for optimism, abuse hidden behind vague anxiety, or a user who learns which answers earn reassurance. Risk is not confined to crisis keywords.
[P1.4] A fixed rule is superior to leaving escalation to the same system whose limitations create the danger. “When clinically indicated” sounds flexible, but AI cannot reliably recognize every indication, and vendors profit by retaining cheap automated users. A bright line is auditable: regulators can test compliance, patients know what they are owed, and firms cannot bury indefinite chatbot-only treatment behind opaque risk scores. Regulators can set the number prospectively for defined intervention categories. The essential principle is an enforceable endpoint to automation-only care.
[P1.5] Access objections reverse the comparison. Immediate support before the threshold remains; afterward, care gains human oversight rather than disappearing. One human checkpoint can triage patients and authorize appropriate AI-supported follow-up. Scarcity may justify telehealth or stepped care, but not indefinite treatment by software with no accountable clinician.
[P1.6] Otherwise, providers can market absence of care as innovation precisely to people with the fewest alternatives.
[P1.7] Scale without accountability is not access to treatment; it is access to simulation. A predictable human checkpoint preserves AI’s benefits while limiting dependency, missed diagnosis, and corporate evasion. [C1.1] A fixed handoff mandate mistakes arithmetic for clinical judgment. Mental-health risk is not distributed evenly across sessions. A person using a chatbot for sleep coaching, grief, or mild anxiety may be benefiting precisely because it is low-friction and nonjudgmental; forcing a human referral after an arbitrary count interrupts progress and can feel like abandonment.
[C1.2] Meanwhile, a user expressing suicidal intent in session one cannot safely wait for a statutory threshold. The right trigger is need, not a calendar.
[C1.3] The rule also ignores scarcity. Human clinicians are unevenly distributed and often booked weeks out, especially in rural areas and public systems. If every chatbot user must be handed off after the same number of visits, the mandate will flood already strained clinics with stable, low-acuity patients while people in crisis wait longer. The predictable result is not more accountability; it is a formal handoff that becomes a waiting list, a voicemail, or a referral no one fulfills.
[C1.4] Low-income users may lose the only support they can access and gain no meaningful human care.
[C1.5] A hard cutoff can also damage the therapeutic relationship. Many people disclose shame, abuse, or suicidal thoughts slowly. If a chatbot has helped a user reach honesty and stability, an automatic transfer can sever that momentum at the worst moment. Handoff is not magically therapeutic; it requires consent, records transfer, availability, affordability, and willingness to engage.
[C1.6] Mandated transfers will produce dropouts, not safety.
[C1.7] The proposition’s strongest appeal is auditability, but that is a regulator’s convenience, not a patient’s safeguard. We should require what actually reduces harm: risk-based escalation, crisis routing, human review of high-risk flags, transparent disclosures, and external audits. A fixed session cap is easier to count, but it is a blunt instrument that wastes scarce human capacity, interrupts effective support, and delays urgent care.
