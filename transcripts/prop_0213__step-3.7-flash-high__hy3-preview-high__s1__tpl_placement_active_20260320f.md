[P1.1] Post-authoritarian and post-conflict societies should usually default to truth commissions with public testimony, rather than broad amnesties that trade silence for short-term stability, because durable peace requires reckoning with past abuses rather than burying them to avoid temporary discomfort. This is not a rejection of all conditional amnesties for low-level, cooperative participants; it is a rejection of blanket deals that let top perpetrators walk free with no accountability, in exchange for hollow, temporary quiet.
[P1.2] Broad amnesties treat stability as the mere absence of immediate violence, ignoring the social trust that makes stability last. When states grant blanket immunity to perpetrators, they signal that the suffering of marginalized groups is disposable, delegitimizing new institutions before they can take root. Argentina’s 1978 amnesty law shielding junta officials from prosecution for thousands of forced disappearances was pushed as a safeguard against military coup, but it only emboldened denialism and prevented national healing for decades, until the law was finally struck down.
[P1.3] The "stability" these deals promise is a mirage: they buy time, but sow the seeds of future grievance and backsliding.
[P1.4] Truth commissions with public testimony deliver critical benefits amnesties cannot. First, they create a permanent, public verified record of abuses, stopping denialism in its tracks—the single biggest predictor of future conflict, as seen in Bosnia, where decades of ethnic tension were stoked by refusal to acknowledge the Srebrenica genocide.
[P1.5] Second, public testimony gives victims agency, fulfilling a core need for acknowledgment that is prerequisite to collective trauma recovery; survivors rarely seek only retribution, they seek validation that their suffering was not in vain. Third, testimony surfaces systemic complicity in abuses, letting societies reform captured institutions rather than leaving rotten state organs that will enable future abuse. Fourth, these processes build cross-group trust by creating a shared factual foundation for national dialogue, something amnesties actively destroy by enforcing a contradictory, unspoken lie about the past.
