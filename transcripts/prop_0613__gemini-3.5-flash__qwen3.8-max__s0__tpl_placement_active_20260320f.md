[P1.1] Digital communication today is defined by artificial walls. Tech monopolies maintain their dominance not through superior product quality, but through hostage-taking. If you leave a platform, you lose your social network. By requiring social media giants to offer interoperable, user-portable messaging and social graph access to qualified third-party services, governments can break this lock-in, restore user agency, and foster a vibrant, competitive digital economy.
[P1.2] First, this policy dismantles the anti-competitive "network effect" trap. Currently, innovative platforms cannot compete because they lack a critical mass of users. Interoperability levels the playing field. Just as telecommunications regulations allow a T-Mobile customer to text a Verizon customer, and open banking protocols allow users to manage their data across institutions, social media must transition to an open ecosystem.
[P1.3] Startups will finally compete on feature quality and privacy, rather than sheer size.
[P1.4] Second, it returns sovereignty to the user. A user’s relationships belong to them, not to a corporate database. Portability allows users to choose alternative clients or services with better privacy policies, superior content moderation, or ad-free interfaces, all while staying in touch with friends on the legacy platform. This dynamic forces dominant platforms to actually respect user preferences to prevent them from routing around their native apps.
[P1.5] Crucially, the motion specifies "qualified" third-party services. This safeguards security. It avoids the pitfalls of unregulated data sharing by ensuring that participating services meet strict encryption, authentication, and data protection standards. We do not have to choose between competitive markets and data safety; qualified interoperability delivers both.
[P1.6] Ultimately, this mandate shifts the digital landscape from closed, exploitative monopolies to an open ecosystem driven by user choice. It is time to treat our social graphs not as proprietary corporate assets, but as portable digital rights.

[C1.1] The proposition treats social networks like telephone numbers. They are not. Telephone interoperability works because licensed carriers exchange calls, not intimate maps of friendship, private groups, and personal identity. Mandating that dominant platforms expose messaging and social graphs to third parties would create a permanent compliance bureaucracy while weakening the very protections users need.
[C1.2] First, forced interoperability breaks the security perimeter. End-to-end messaging, spam control, abuse detection, and identity verification depend on a platform’s ability to police its own endpoints. If qualified third parties can attach to a dominant network, the platform cannot ensure encryption integrity or stop cross-platform harassment. A malicious client can scrape contacts, impersonate users, or flood messages across services. “Qualified” is not a firewall; sophisticated actors can meet formal standards, while small developers drown in audits and technical requirements.
[C1.3] Second, the mandate harms the competition it promises. Startups would build around incumbents’ APIs, rate limits, and certification rules instead of creating independent communities. Worse, if a rival client lets users stay connected to the legacy network, the rival becomes a frontend to the incumbent rather than a real alternative. The dominant platform becomes a common utility, and competitors become skins on top of it.
[C1.4] That is not a vibrant market; it is managed dependence.
[C1.5] Third, social graph portability violates the rights of third parties. A social graph is not one person’s property. It contains other people’s connections, group memberships, and sometimes sensitive affiliations. One user’s export can expose thousands of contacts who never consented.
[C1.6] The proposition says it returns sovereignty to users, but it does so by converting mutual relationships into extractable datasets.
[C1.7] Better remedies exist: genuine personal data portability, transparent APIs, and antitrust action against proven exclusion. But this mandate is overbroad and dangerous. It would make social media less secure, less private, and more centralized. Oppose it.
