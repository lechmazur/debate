[P1.1] The proposition asks whether the recovery of severed family histories should be treated as a public duty or a subscription product. It should be a public duty.
[P1.2] When states enforced slavery, forced adoptions, indigenous removal, or wartime displacement, they created the records and the injury. Leaving reconstruction to private genealogy markets turns that injury into a luxury good. A descendant who can pay may find a ship register, adoption file, mission ledger, or wartime alien registration; one who cannot pay hits a paywall, a missing index, or a collection that was never digitized because it was not commercially attractive. That is not neutral.
[P1.3] It makes truth dependent on wealth. Markets digitize what sells; public systems preserve what matters.
[P1.4] Public digitization and funded access solve three problems markets cannot. First, preservation: fragile ledgers, church registers, agency files, and local court records decay or disappear unless there is a mandated, well-resourced effort. Second, equity: free portals, standardized search, and community assistance mean poor descendants, rural communities, and displaced peoples are not locked out.
[P1.5] Third, accountability: public projects can attach provenance, correction rights, cultural protocols, and privacy safeguards instead of treating family trauma as raw data for upselling.
[P1.6] Objections about cost or privacy are real, but the status quo already costs: families remain separated, claims for restitution are harder, and histories disappear. Privacy can be handled through access tiers, redaction, consent for living persons, and oversight boards. What cannot be handled by a paywall is the moral claim of people whose ancestors were recorded as property, wards, enemies, or cases.
[P1.7] If the government broke the chain of memory through public power, it owes the public means to restore it. This debate should turn on whether identity and historical repair are basic public goods. They are, and private markets fail that test.

[C1.3] Adoption records in many jurisdictions are sealed *by statute*, and governments defend those seals in court against the very adoptees this motion claims to serve. Indigenous communities have spent decades fighting state agencies for access to files those agencies classified, lost, or "reorganized." [C1.4] Second, "publicly funded access" is not a neutral phrase. Public funding arrives with public conditions: budgets that shift with elections, privacy regimes that close records one administration and dump them into searchable databases the next, and political incentives to smooth the narrative. A single state portal is a single point of failure and a single point of leverage. Dispersed, competitive archives—churches, historical societies, descendant-led projects, commercial platforms—cannot all be captured at once.
[C1.5] Redundancy is resilience.
[C1.6] Third, the equity framing is backwards. Markets charge $30 a month; the alternative on offer is not free universal access but a bureaucracy with intake queues, eligibility rules, and decades-long backlogs—the lived experience of anyone who has requested records from a national archive. Competition has already pushed prices down and indexes open; a state monopoly removes the pressure that made access improve.
[C1.7] The real moral work here—restitution, repatriation, reconnection—has always been done by communities and civil society, with governments dragged along behind. Keep it that way.

Private firms cannot subpoena a sealed court file, compel an agency to disclose a removal register, or force an archive to index what officials want hidden.
[P2.2] If the danger is state control, the remedy is a public right of access with independent oversight, community governance, appeal rights, and mandatory disclosure—not a market that only touches records already easy to monetize.
[P2.3] Their claim that “redundancy is resilience” [C1.5] sounds prudent, but commercial archives are not neutral backups. They digitize what is profitable, change prices, alter terms, withdraw collections, and close accounts when strategies shift. For slavery ledgers in neglected courthouses or displacement records in minority languages, redundancy often means no one collects them at all. A $30 subscription is no answer when the record you need was never digitized, or sits behind multiple proprietary tiers.
[P2.4] Public funding can instead be federated—libraries, tribal archives, universities, descendant organizations—without making access depend on a customer.
[P2.5] The adoption example proves our side. When records are sealed by statute [C1.3], a subscription site cannot solve the injustice. The answer is to replace secrecy with a rights-based system: consent and mediation for living persons, redaction where needed, but no paywall between a person and the state’s record of their own family.
[P2.6] Fear of bureaucracy is not a reason to accept inequality. If archives are slow, fund and mandate them. If indexes are poor, require open standards. The real choice is whether severed family history is a public good or a premium product.
[P2.7] Public duty, properly constrained, is the safer and fairer route.
