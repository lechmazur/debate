[P1.1] Public dollars should buy public goods, and a youth-sports budget is a test of that principle. A municipality has a fixed number of fields, gym hours, and coaching stipends. Every reserved field and every subsidized travel roster is a choice about who gets to play. The question is not whether elite competition should exist—private clubs will supply it regardless—but whether taxpayers should fund the narrow tier while the broad base shrinks.
[P1.2] Start with who actually benefits. Travel teams are, by design, exclusionary: tryouts cut most kids, fees and travel costs filter families by income and by whether a parent can drive to a tournament three hours away on a weekday. When a school district or parks department pours resources into that tier, it is subsidizing the families who already have the most options.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, the kid whose parents work weekends, who isn't the best eight-year-old on the block, or who simply wants to try three sports instead of one, finds the rec league understaffed, the fields booked, and the season cancelled.
[P1.4] Second, the health case points the same direction. The public benefit of youth sports is overwhelmingly about getting the median child moving: fitness, sleep, mood, friendships, lower screen time. Those gains come from participation, not podiums. And the intensive year-round model carries documented costs—overuse injuries, burnout, and kids quitting sports entirely by early adolescence because it stopped being fun.
[P1.5] Third, the talent-pipeline argument collapses on inspection. Elite athletes emerge from large, multi-sport bases; broad participation enlarges the pool from which talent surfaces. Narrowing the funnel at age nine does not create more champions—it just decides earlier, and by family income, who is allowed to try.
[P1.6] Picture two uses of the same gym on a Tuesday night: twelve pre-selected kids drilling for a weekend tournament, or sixty kids from the neighborhood playing. One of those is a public purpose. Schools and cities should prioritize the sixty.
