[P1.1] Core developmental priority. Most children already spend excessive time on screens, far beyond what's healthy for their cognitive and physical development. Expanding purely digital enrichment first would pile more sedentary, screen-based activity onto a generation already struggling with rising rates of myopia, obesity, and screen-related attention issues. Foundational skills like conflict resolution, physical literacy, and creative risk-taking are nurtured almost exclusively through in-person sports, arts, and unstructured play—none of which can be replicated by even the highest-quality digital program.
[P1.2] Order matters for efficacy. Digital enrichment works best when layered on top of these core, embodied skills, not the other way around. A student who has learned to collaborate on a mural, resolve disputes during unstructured play, or push through a tough sports practice will get far more out of a digital coding or design program than a peer who has never developed those baseline social and executive function skills. Prioritizing non-digital programs first ensures digital expansion has a stronger, more equitable foundation.
[P1.3] Unstructured play specifically is a non-negotiable gap. Schools have cut back on free play time during the academic day, leaving after-school hours as the only window for many kids to engage in self-directed, screen-free exploration. Expanding digital programs first would crowd out this critical, rare downtime, exacerbating the mental health toll of constant structured, screen-based activity. The motion does not oppose digital enrichment—it simply insists we build the most impactful, gap-filling programs first.
[P1.4] Equity demands this order too. Low-income students are far less likely to have access to non-digital after-school enrichment outside of school, while digital tools are increasingly ubiquitous even in under-resourced homes. Expanding sports, arts, and play programs first targets the most acute access gap, rather than doubling down on a type of enrichment that many students already have partial access to via personal devices.
