[P1.1] Protecting at least 30% of every fishing nation's EEZ in no-take reserves is not a luxury for rich conservationists; it is the cheapest insurance coastal fishing communities can buy. Where communities depend heavily on fish income, dependence is not safety—it is exposure. Too many coastal fleets are mining down breeding stocks, targeting ever-smaller fish, and converting natural capital into today's cash. A no-take reserve reverses that logic: it leaves fish alive long enough to grow, spawn, and export larvae and adults into adjacent waters.
[P1.2] Fishers still work the remaining 70%, but they work it next to a hatchery instead of a graveyard. Today, genuinely no-take areas are only a sliver of most EEZs, so fleets are fishing down the very nursery grounds that replenish them. Thirty percent is not a radical shutdown; it is the scale at which protection starts to outrun extraction. That is why community-managed no-take zones, from island reefs in the Philippines to recovery sites in Baja California, have repeatedly seen biomass rebound inside the closure and catches improve just outside it.
[P1.3] Defending fishing income by defending current fishing practices is a bait-and-switch. The real question is whose income survives twenty years from now. No-take reserves rebuild spawning biomass, protect nursery habitat like mangroves and seagrass, and make ecosystems more resilient to warming and acidification. Protecting a third of the EEZ does not end fishing; it caps the liquidation of the asset and restores the yield the other two-thirds can sustainably produce.
[P1.4] Coastal communities deserve a just transition, not a delayed collapse. Reserves must be phased, co-managed with fishers, and paired with alternative livelihoods—ecotourism, restoration work, monitoring—so the people who bear the short-term cost capture the long-term dividend. The proposition is not "close the ocean." It is "stop eating the seed corn, protect 30%, and let the other 70% feed people for generations."
