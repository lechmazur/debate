[P1.1] The decisive question is who controls the waste and who pays for it. A beverage company chooses the bottle, the cap, the label, the shrink-wrap, and the material mix. A packaging producer chooses whether a container is mono-material or a laminated puzzle. Taxpayers choose none of that—yet they fund the bins, trucks, sorting lines, litter crews, and contamination costs when those choices become waste.
[P1.2] That is a cost-shift, not a market outcome. The proposition simply says: make the party with design control carry much more of the collection and recycling bill.
[P1.3] This is not symbolic. When producers pay fees based on weight, material, and recyclability, packaging design changes: less unnecessary material, more mono-material formats, caps that stay attached, containers that fit existing recycling streams. Producer-funded deposit systems for beverage containers are the clearest proof of concept: when the producer chain finances collection, return rates rise and litter falls. Taxpayers then stop subsidizing the disposal of products they did not design or sell.
[P1.4] The fairness point is equally direct. Households already pay for waste collection through taxes and fees; they should not also absorb the cleanup cost of corporate packaging choices. Major producers have the scale, data, and bargaining power to finance and improve collection systems. A targeted obligation on them is not a burden on the little guy; it is a level playing field for producers who already design responsibly, and it stops free-riding by those who do not.
[P1.5] Yes, some cost may show up in prices. But hidden municipal costs are still costs—just less visible and less controllable. Moving them to producers makes the true cost of packaging visible at the point of design, where it can actually be reduced. The burden should follow control.

[P4.3] A2: Producers should pay much more of net collection cost, not every dollar of local wage or route decisions. An independent regulator can set rates from audited municipal costs; cities keep service authority and incentives to control costs; producers get design standards and data. [P4.4] Those answers expose CON’s false dilemma: either producer fees are a blank cheque, or they are a disguised grocery tax, so taxpayers must pay. But taxpayer-only finance is not neutral. A regulated cost-share puts the price signal where control exists—material, format, volume—while keeping public authority where service delivery belongs.
[P4.5] If fees pass through, they replace hidden municipal costs rather than simply adding to them; CON has not shown the total bill rises. Their regressivity objection therefore loses force: the relevant comparison is total household cost, not the visibility of the charge.
