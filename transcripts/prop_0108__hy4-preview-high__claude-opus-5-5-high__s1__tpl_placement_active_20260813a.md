[P1.1] Start with the engine. Most American legislative races are single-member plurality: one seat, most votes wins, runner-up gets nothing. That winner-take-all rule is not neutral; it is the machine manufacturing two-party polarization. Under plurality, a candidate can win a safe seat after a base-mobilization primary, often without majority support.
[P1.2] Because general elections in gerrymandered or sorted districts are frequently predetermined, the only real contest is the party primary—low-turnout, dominated by intense activists. Fear of a primary challenge, not persuasion of the median voter, disciplines legislators. That is the incentive trap: winner-take-all rewards candidates who please the angriest slice, then calls the rigidity “polarization.”
[P1.3] Ranked-choice or proportional voting cuts the wiring. In a single-member district, ranked-choice voting forces a winner to earn a majority, not just a factional plurality. Candidates must compete for second and third choices beyond their base; the spoiler dynamic that punishes independent or moderate candidates disappears. Picture a three-way race—Left, Right, Center-Right.
[P1.4] Under plurality, the Center-Right voter may choke back her true first choice to stop the Left, handing the seat to a narrow, unrepresentative winner. Under ranked-choice, she ranks Center-Right first and Right second; the winner must now appeal across factions for second-choice support. Races stop being binary fear contests and become coalition searches.
[P1.5] Proportional voting attacks the same problem structurally. Winner-take-all lets a party with a statewide minority translate geography into zero seats, and lets a 55% district winner sweep all representation; every seat becomes total war. In multi-member proportional or multi-winner ranked systems, seat shares track vote shares more closely, parties must campaign beyond safe enclaves, and governance becomes coalition-building rather than annihilation. The choice is not “moderation” versus “choice.” It is whether the rules pay a politician for narrow base rage or for building a coalition.
[P1.6] Winner-take-all pays for rage; ranked-choice and proportional voting pay for coalition. That is why the United States should adopt ranked-choice or proportional voting instead of winner-take-all rules.
