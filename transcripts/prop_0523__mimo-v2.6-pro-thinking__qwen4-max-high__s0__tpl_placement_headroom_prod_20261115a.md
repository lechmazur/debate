[P1.1] The debate should not be about whether states control borders. They should. It should be about what states do with workers their own labor markets have already absorbed, hired, trained, and come to depend on. Once a worker is integrated—employed, paying into payroll and consumption taxes, renting homes, raising children in local schools—the case for spending scarce enforcement money to remove them collapses.
[P1.2] Start with economics. Stricter return enforcement means raids, detention, appeals, flights, and surveillance. It is expensive, slow, and imperfect. Meanwhile, the employer who hired the worker still needs the job done.
[P1.3] Remove the workforce and the work does not disappear; it either goes underground, gets offshored, or gets filled by another irregular worker. Enforcement against settled workers is the most expensive way to fail to solve the problem.
[P1.4] Long-term status does the opposite. It converts a shadow worker into a taxable, complaint-ready, upwardly mobile one. A worker with papers can report wage theft, unsafe conditions, or a landlord's abuse. A worker without them cannot, which is exactly why irregular labor undercuts local wages: employers exploit the undocumented precisely because they can't object.
[P1.5] Regularization is therefore not soft on labor markets; it is the enforcement tool that actually protects them.
[P1.6] Second, legality. A state that tolerates a worker for a decade and then removes them is not enforcing a coherent rule. It is enforcing arbitrariness. Long-term status restores the rule of law by making the rules match the reality the state itself permitted through its hiring systems, its tax collection, and its inaction.
[P1.7] Children who grew up speaking the national language are the sharpest case: "return" means exile from the only country they know, for a decision they never made.
[P1.8] Finally, deterrence is not lost. Prioritizing settled workers over removals does not mean open borders; it means spending enforcement resources where they change outcomes—at the border and on new inflows—not on destroying a labor force already woven into domestic life.
[P1.9] That is the comparison: integration already done, versus enforcement that cannot be done well. [clipped]
