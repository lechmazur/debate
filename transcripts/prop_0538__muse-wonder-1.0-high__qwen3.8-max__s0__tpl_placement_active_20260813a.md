[P1.1] West Africa grows most of the world's cocoa, yet most profit is made where it is branded and sold as chocolate in Europe. The same logic runs through Congolese cobalt shipped out for batteries made elsewhere, Nigerian crude exported only for refined fuel to be imported back, and Zambian copper that returns as finished wire. Raw exports surrender the valuable steps — processing, jobs, technology, tax revenue — abroad.
[P1.2] Prioritising intra-African trade and manufacturing reverses that leak. When Ethiopia processes leather at home, Rwanda assembles phones for regional buyers, or Senegal refines oil for West African customers, value stays on the continent. Manufacturing is also where mass employment lives. A mine can generate billions with few workers; factories, food processing, textiles, pharmaceuticals and building materials employ many more, including women and young people in fast-growing cities.
[P1.3] Regional trade makes industrialisation possible. Most African economies are too small alone to sustain efficient plants, but a continental market of well over a billion people changes the maths. A pharmaceutical plant in Kenya, a fertiliser plant in Morocco or a battery-component plant in Zambia becomes viable when it can sell across borders without punitive tariffs and broken transport links. Scale lets firms learn regionally before facing the world.
[P1.4] It also buys resilience and bargaining power. Dependence on distant buyers means a slump in Europe or China, a spike in shipping costs, or a fall in one commodity price can wreck a budget and force austerity overnight. Selling food, fuel and manufactured goods to neighbours diversifies demand, shortens supply lines in a crisis, and keeps foreign exchange circulating within Africa. To prioritise does not mean sealing borders.
[P1.5] It means ending the colonial habit of building ports, railways and budgets around extraction first — and building production for Africans first.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a legitimate aspiration for a dangerous priority. Africa’s immediate problem is not that it exports commodities; it is that too many governments lack fiscal space, foreign exchange, and investment to build reliable power, ports, roads, finance, and skills. Raw-material exports are one of the few proven ways to earn those resources now. Copper, cobalt, cocoa, oil, and minerals are not merely symbols of exploitation; they pay teachers, import medicines, service debt, and fund the infrastructure that later industry requires.
[C1.2] If a state downgrades its export engine in favour of ambitious regional manufacturing, it risks cutting the branch it hopes to climb.
[C1.3] The second flaw is demand. Intra-African trade sounds attractive, but neighbouring markets are often poor, fragmented, and unable to provide the scale and hard currency that richer global markets provide. A factory selling to low-income regional buyers may struggle to reach efficient scale, while a mine or plantation selling globally can earn dollars immediately. Continental free-trade deals can reduce tariffs, but tariffs are rarely the binding obstacle.
[C1.4] Customs delays, weak rail links, divergent standards, poor ports, and limited trade finance matter more. Prioritising manufacturing for a market that cannot yet pay for it invites protected, high-cost industries sustained by subsidies and tariffs.
[C1.5] This would hurt the people the proposition claims to help. Protected manufacturing raises prices for clothing, construction materials, and farm inputs. It can reward connected elites with licences while consumers pay more. Meanwhile, millions of smallholders, miners, transport workers, and traders depend on export commodities today.
[C1.6] Botswana’s diamonds show that raw exports can fund development when revenues are managed, not when they are abandoned.
[C1.7] The motion treats raw exports as dependence; in capital-scarce economies they can be sovereign leverage. The right priority is upgrading within export sectors—local processing where economic, better contracts, stable taxes—while keeping the foreign-exchange engine running.

[P2.1] Raw exports supposedly fund the state, but they fund it erratically and on terms set elsewhere. When copper, oil or cocoa prices fall, exports that paid teachers one year force borrowing and austerity the next. That is not fiscal space; it is fiscal exposure. Much value never reaches the treasury either: enclave mines import machinery, export unprocessed output, employ few people, while profits and processing taxes leak abroad.
[P2.2] Prioritising regional manufacturing does not mean switching that engine off overnight. It means directing the next dollar of investment, procurement and infrastructure differently: not only roads from mine to port, but power and transport linking African cities; not incentives only for extraction, but for refining, food processing, fertiliser, pharmaceuticals and components sold across borders. The choice is whether commodity income keeps subsidising finished imports, or builds capacity to replace and export them regionally.
[P2.3] Their demand argument is circular. Neighbours are said to be too poor to buy African manufactures, while Africa stays poor by importing manufactures and exporting low-value commodities. In aggregate the continental market is enormous, and its needs — affordable food, housing materials, medicines, tools, phones and fuel — match what African firms can make now. Selling processed food, textiles or building inputs regionally is more realistic than competing first in distant luxury markets.
[P2.4] Botswana proves how narrow the exception is: a small population, unusually stable management, and still dependence on one gem. Most exporters get price shocks, elite capture of mining licences, and already pay high prices for imported goods in scarce dollars. Regional competition answers the protection fear: firms selling across many African markets must win customers against African rivals, not hide behind one national border. Even the opposition endorses “local processing where economic.” That is manufacturing.
[P2.5] We refuse to leave it rare and subordinate to the raw-export pipeline.
