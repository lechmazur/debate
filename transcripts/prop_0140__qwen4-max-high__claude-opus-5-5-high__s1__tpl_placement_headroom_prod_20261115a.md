[P1.1] A ban on carrying a drink down the street doesn't remove the drink. It moves it — into a stairwell, a parked car, a stranger's apartment, an unlicensed warehouse party. That is the choice on the table: not drinking versus no drinking, but managed visibility versus unmanaged invisibility.
[P1.2] Consider what a tightly managed zone actually buys a city. Drinks come from licensed bars, poured by staff trained to stop serving the visibly drunk, sold to people whose ID was checked at the door. The street is lit, camera-covered, patrolled, and full of witnesses. Cups are plastic, not glass.
[P1.3] Boundaries and hours are posted; violations cost an owner their license. Those levers exist only where the city can see the drinking.
[P1.4] Now the alternative. Pre-gaming in a private flat: nobody measures the pour, nobody checks the age, nobody cuts anyone off, and the guest arrives downtown already more intoxicated than any bartender would have served him. The house party spills into an unlit lot where the first responder is a friend, not a medic. Prohibition doesn't prevent that night; it just guarantees no one official is present for it.
[P1.5] Places that do this — the French Quarter, Savannah's historic district, Beale Street in Memphis — are not anarchies. They run on posted boundaries, plastic-cup rules, closing hours, and dense police and camera presence. The permission comes bundled with the management; that is the entire design.
[P1.6] So the opposition's burden is narrow and heavy: show that pushing drinking out of sight reduces harm, not merely reports of harm. Fewer citations for open containers is not fewer assaults; it is fewer witnesses. A city that cannot see its nightlife cannot manage it — and the people most exposed in the dark are the youngest, the drunkest, and the ones nobody was watching.

[C1.2] When alcohol becomes easier to get and to carry, people drink more. That relationship between availability and harm is about as well established as anything in alcohol policy. PRO must show that a permissive zone lowers total consumption, not merely moves it, and nothing in [P1.1] does that.
[C1.3] Second, look at what "managed" means once the drink leaves the bar. PRO praises trained staff and ID checks [P1.2], but a to-go cup ends the bartender's control at the door. Inside a licensed venue, there is a bouncer, a capacity limit and a single owner who loses a license for overserving. On the street, a patron finishes his third bar's drink in nobody's premises.
[C1.4] When he collapses or swings a fist, which license is on the line? The status quo is not the stairwell. It is the licensed room, the most regulated drinking space a city has. Open containers pull drinking out of that room and onto pavement where accountability belongs to no one.
[C1.5] Third, these zones concentrate intoxicated strangers in one place, and concentrated drunkenness is what produces fights, assaults, injuries and impossible policing. PRO's own model admits this: the zones "run on dense police and camera presence" [P1.5]. That is not management. That is a city paying, every weekend, to contain a crowd it invited.
[C1.6] Finally, pre-gaming survives the zone. People still drink cheaply at home first, then add street drinking on top. PRO keeps the dark stairwell and adds a crowded, poorly accountable street.
