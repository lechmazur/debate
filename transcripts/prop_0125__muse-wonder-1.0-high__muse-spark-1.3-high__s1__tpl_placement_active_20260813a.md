[P1.1] When a carrier says a rural county is "not commercially viable," it is not refuting the case for public investment — it is making it. Laying fiber costs roughly the same per mile whether that mile passes fifty homes or five. Private profit therefore fails exactly where need is hardest, not where need is smallest. Waiting for the market to solve a problem its own math guarantees it will ignore is not fiscal prudence; it is permanent exclusion by spreadsheet.
[P1.2] Broadband is no longer an entertainment upgrade. It is the platform for school assignments, job applications, telehealth visits, banking, crop and weather data for farmers, and dispatch for emergency services. A child who must do homework from a fast-food parking lot, a diabetic patient who drives hours for a check that could be a video visit, a farmer who cannot use precision tools his competitors use — none of them are lacking demand. They are lacking infrastructure that modern citizenship now assumes.
[P1.3] We have made this decision before. Rural electrification, interstate highways, and universal mail were not built because every mile or farmhouse paid for itself in direct revenue. They were built because a country cannot function when whole regions are cut off from the networks everyone else uses to participate. Broadband is that network today.
[P1.4] The returns also extend far beyond a monthly bill. Connected towns keep clinics, schools, and small businesses viable; they let residents work remotely, sell beyond the county line, and stay rather than leave. Those gains — a broader tax base, less forced migration, stronger local economies — never appear in a carrier's spreadsheet, which is precisely why public value and private profit diverge.
[P1.5] That does not require a blank check. Competitive awards, deadlines, speed requirements, clawbacks for failure, and open-access conditions can target only the upfront gap the market cannot cross.

[C1.1] Carriers saying an area is not commercially viable is not a confession that the state must step in. It is information. It means the cost of reaching those last homes vastly exceeds the value even users place on the service at prices they will pay. A responsible government heeds that signal instead of bulldozing through it with taxpayer money.
[C1.2] A mile of fiber past five homes costs nearly as much as one past fifty, but helps one-tenth as many people. That matters when budgets are finite. Paying tens of thousands in subsidy for one remote household passed means leaving many more families unconnected over unaffordable bills, outdated schools, and strained clinics. Fairness is most people online per dollar, not most dollars per person.
[C1.3] Even after the line is built, connection is not access. The larger barrier is adoption: monthly prices, devices, skills. A subsidized trunk past a farmhouse does nothing for the renter who cannot afford service, the older patient who cannot use video care, or the student with a broken laptop. If the goal is homework, health, and jobs, demand-side help puts far more people online than cable to the remotest addresses.
[C1.4] The electrification analogy hides that difference. Electricity had no wireless substitute; broadband does. Fixed wireless, improving mobile, and low-orbit satellite can reach scattered homes without trenching every mile, and keep improving. Locking subsidies into fiber to the remotest farm buys the priciest, slowest option just as cheaper alternatives mature.
[C1.5] Finally, these programs rarely stay disciplined. Incumbents help shape coverage maps, win awards, overbuild where service exists, miss deadlines, then demand ongoing support because a network unviable to build remains unviable to run. Taxpayers buy the asset, then rent it forever, while carriers learn to wait for subsidies instead of investing.

[P2.1] Treating "not commercially viable" as a stop sign mistakes a price signal for a measure of value. A farmer, clinic, or school may depend heavily on connectivity yet be unable to finance the last ten miles alone. Willingness to pay tracks wealth and density, not need, and omits wider gains: a clinic kept open, a business able to hire, tax revenue retained.
[P2.2] That is why "most people per dollar" is the wrong fairness test. Applied consistently, it would have blocked rural roads, water, and electricity too, because cities always win a headcount contest. Fairness for essential infrastructure means a floor below which no community falls, not a contest sparsely populated places are designed to lose. A one-time capital subsidy to reach that floor, once built, serves successive families and students for decades.
[P2.3] The adoption objection reverses cause and effect. A voucher, laptop, or digital-skills class is worthless where no reliable service reaches the home to adopt. Demand-side help matters, but it cannot conjure a signal. Build the network and targeted affordability support can do its work; skip the network and that support strands exactly the student and patient it claims to prioritize.
[P2.4] Wireless and satellite do not rescue the hands-off position either. If they can meet speed, reliability, and affordability standards more cheaply in a given valley, technology-neutral bidding lets them win the subsidy. But shared wireless capacity strains as use grows, satellite can mean high monthly costs, weather disruption, and capacity limits, and clinics and schools need more than a best-effort link. "Something may improve later" is not access now.
[P2.5] Finally, the risk of capture argues for discipline, not abandonment. Competitive awards, verified maps, milestones, clawbacks, and penalties answer the failure story; surrendering the field answers nothing and leaves the same incumbents with unchecked local monopolies or no service at all. Every major network build faced waste risks. We managed them because the cost of exclusion was higher.
