[P1.1] Personalized algorithmic dynamic pricing is not just pricing—it is profiling. When a retailer sets a price based on a customer's perceived willingness or ability to pay, the retailer has already built a model of that person's life: their income, their urgency, their alternatives, their weaknesses. The price is not the product of supply and demand; it is the output of a surveillance apparatus trained on that individual's vulnerability.
[P1.2] Consider what "willingness to pay" actually means in practice. A customer searching for a last-minute flight because a parent is dying has high willingness to pay. A customer in a low-income neighborhood with fewer competing stores nearby has fewer alternatives and thus higher willingness to pay. An elderly user who doesn't know to comparison-shop across devices presents as high willingness to pay.
[P1.3] The algorithm does not see these distinctions as moral issues; it sees them as revenue opportunities. It charges more to the grieving, the stranded, and the uninformed.
[P1.4] This is not market efficiency—it is exploitation dressed in technical language. Traditional dynamic pricing, like surge pricing or seasonal sales, applies the same price to everyone at a given moment. Everyone sees the same surge multiplier. That is transparent and contestable.
[P1.5] Personalized pricing hides the number behind a profile the customer cannot see, cannot audit, and cannot challenge. Two people looking at the same product at the same time see different prices—and neither knows why.
[P1.6] The defense that algorithms don't use protected characteristics is naive. Willingness to pay is reconstructed from behavioral proxies—zip code, device type, browsing speed, purchase history—that correlate closely with race, age, and class. Discrimination doesn't need a label to be real; it just needs a proxy.
[P1.7] Banning this practice restores a simple principle: the price should be the price. Transparent, uniform, and open to competition. The alternative is a market where the most vulnerable always pay the most, and never even know it.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a worst-case abuse for the whole practice. The decisive question is not whether personalized pricing can be intrusive or unfair; it is whether a blanket ban makes consumers better off. It would not. It would outlaw a broad category of price competition, including many practices that lower prices and help marginal buyers.
[C1.2] Personalized pricing is often personalized discounting. If a retailer infers from browsing history, cart abandonment, loyalty data, or location that a shopper is price-sensitive, the rational competitive move is to offer a lower price, not a higher one. A targeted coupon for a budget-constrained household, a verified student discount, a win-back offer to a lapsing customer, or a lower fare for a flexible leisure traveler all turn on perceived willingness or ability to pay. Under the proposition’s logic, each becomes suspect.
[C1.3] The result is not fairness; it is a forced uniform price that usually settles near the retailer’s estimate of the average buyer, squeezing out discounts that make goods affordable for people at the margin.
[C1.4] The ban also harms competition. Smaller retailers and entrants use targeted price cuts to lure customers away from incumbents. A large chain can survive on brand recognition and uniform list prices; a challenger needs flexibility to bid for individual shoppers. Freezing price personalization therefore protects entrenched players more than consumers.
[C1.5] PRO’s real concerns—deception, emergency exploitation, and proxy discrimination—should be regulated directly. Ban price gouging, forbid protected-class proxies, require clear disclosure and opt-outs, punish manipulative design. That targeted regime captures the wrongs without destroying beneficial price variation.
[C1.6] Uniformity is not the same as justice. A market that can offer a lower price to the shopper who needs one is not automatically less fair than one that charges everyone the same. The proposition is overbroad, anti-competitive, and likely to raise average prices. Vote CON.
