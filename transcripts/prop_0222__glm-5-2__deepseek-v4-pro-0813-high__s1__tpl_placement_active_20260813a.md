[P1.1] Punitive drug enforcement does not suppress the drug market. It suppresses honesty about drug use. People who fear arrest do not call emergency services when a friend overdoses. They do not seek treatment when addiction takes hold.
[P1.2] They do not carry naloxone or test their substances. Every one of those behaviors—behaviors that prevent death—is chilled by the threat of a criminal record. Decriminalization removes that chill. It does not legalize the market or endorse drug use.
[P1.3] It stops treating the person using drugs as the enemy of public safety.
[P1.4] The mechanism is straightforward. When possession of small amounts stops triggering criminal penalties, people who use drugs are more willing to identify themselves to services. They can be offered harm reduction—naloxone distribution, substance testing, syringe access programs, and pathways to voluntary treatment—without forfeiting their freedom or future employment. The evidence for this is not abstract.
[P1.5] Harm reduction programs that already operate in spaces where users are not targeted consistently show lower overdose fatalities in surrounding areas. Criminalization creates the opposite incentive: use alone, use quickly, use in the dark. Those are the conditions under which overdoses become deaths rather than near-misses.
[P1.6] Downstream crime follows the same logic. A criminal record for simple possession does not cure addiction. It forecloses employment prospects, destabilizes housing, and fractures social support networks. Those are the same risk factors that drive property crime.
[P1.7] Imposing a criminal record on someone already vulnerable does not prevent crime—it manufactures the conditions for more of it. Enforcement of a possession charge creates the very instability that predicts future offending.
[P1.8] The choice is not between decriminalization and intervention. It is between an intervention that reaches people and one that drives them away from any help. Punitive enforcement asks us to accept the same failed mechanism one more time and call it "deterrence." Decriminalization lets us treat the actual person, in the actual crisis, before the crisis becomes an irreversible loss—for the individual and for the community left to absorb the fallout.

[C1.1] The proposition sells half a mechanism and ignores the other half. Decriminalization may reduce some fear of arrest, but it also lowers the legal and social cost of beginning and continuing drug use. Overdose deaths are not driven only by whether a witness calls 911. They are driven by how many people use, how often, and what substances they encounter.
[C1.2] Decriminalization can expand the number of users, especially among younger people who are most sensitive to legal penalties. A larger user pool can easily overwhelm any improvement in per-use fatality rates.
[C1.3] The downstream crime argument cuts the other way. Decriminalizing possession expands demand while leaving the supply chain illegal. That means more money flowing to traffickers, more theft and fraud to fund addiction, and more street-level violence over a larger customer base. The response to a criminal record’s harms is not to erase the legal line.
[C1.4] It is to convert enforcement into an off-ramp. Drug courts and diversion programs use the leverage of a possession charge to compel treatment. Remove that leverage and you leave voluntary services—which addiction itself makes unreliable. A person in withdrawal rarely walks into treatment simply because the law asks nicely.
[C1.5] The comparison also attacks a strawman. “Continuing punitive enforcement” can still include Good Samaritan protections, naloxone distribution, substance testing, and expanded diversion. Those measures address the fear of calling for help without removing the deterrent against use. The overdose crisis is driven largely by a toxic illegal drug supply, especially potent synthetic opioids. Decriminalizing possession does not make that supply safer.
[C1.6] It just enlarges the customer base.
[C1.7] The burden is on decriminalization to show it alone lowers deaths and crime. Often the favorable cases come with mandatory referral and heavy treatment investment, not mere removal of penalties. Strip out those investments and decriminalization becomes de facto permission. The better policy keeps the legal line but reorients it: less jail, more treatment, and no official signal that small-scale drug use is safe.
