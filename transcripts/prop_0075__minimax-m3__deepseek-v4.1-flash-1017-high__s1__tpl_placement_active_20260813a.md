[P1.1] Anti-discrimination law should make equal treatment the default, while religious exemptions address a specific danger: the state compelling an institution to act in a way that substantially obstructs its exercise of religion. Without safeguards, a religious body could be forced to select clergy, conduct worship, or teach doctrine on terms that contradict its faith. Exemptions are justified, but only as narrow exceptions.
[P1.2] The governing line must be functional, not institutional. A church deciding whether a teacher teaches its doctrine, or a mosque choosing an imam, is making a core religious decision and should not have to disguise that judgment in forbidden terms. Religious identity does not transform a bookstore, cafe, rental property, or commercial media operation into worship. When an institution offers goods or services to the public on ordinary commercial terms, anti-discrimination rules should remain applicable.
[P1.3] This also resolves mixed institutions. A hospital, school, or publisher may combine ministry with substantial commercial activity; its label cannot settle the issue. Each challenged practice should be examined for a genuine connection to a core religious function and for whether protection is necessary to perform it. Religious employees doing religious work may qualify within a large enterprise, while a commercial sales desk does not qualify merely because its employer is religious.
[P1.4] A necessity test protects religious autonomy without allowing religious privilege to expand indefinitely. The exemption should be no broader than the particular practice requiring protection, and it should end when the activity is no longer integral to the institution’s religious purpose. This preserves religious freedom where it matters while protecting employees and customers from discrimination in the marketplace.
[P1.5] Religious institutions should therefore receive exemptions for clergy selection, worship, doctrine-based education, and other indispensable ministry—not for every decision made by a religious owner. This respects both religious freedom and equal protection, and defeats the claim that equality requires subordinating all religious activity to commercial regulation.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds moderate because it promises exemptions where faith is “core.” But that is precisely its flaw: it turns the state into an arbiter of which religious practices count. A church’s worship is easy; a religious school’s admissions, a monastery’s bakery, a faith-based publisher’s hiring, a charity’s fundraising shop are not less religious because money changes hands. The motion says the first gets protection and the second is “broad commercial activity”—as if a religion can be sliced into a sacred core and a disposable commercial shell.
[C1.2] That is not neutrality; it is a secularization test imposed by law.
[C1.3] Consider a small Orthodox Jewish bookstore that employs staff to stock shelves and advise customers. Selling books is commercial, but the owner’s religious identity and the store’s purpose are inseparable from the products and the community it serves. Under the proposition, the state can compel hiring or conduct rules that undermine that mission until the owner proves the shelf-stocking is “tightly tied” to core religious function. Who decides?
[C1.4] A court, using discovery into doctrine, worship, and internal religious disputes. That is entanglement, not protection.
[C1.5] The same problem scales. Religious hospitals, schools, charities, and media ministries mix ministry and market because that is how they survive and serve. A narrow core-function test gives them a choice: abandon religious staffing and conduct expectations in their public-facing work, or retreat from public service. Anti-discrimination law should not demand that price.
[C1.6] The right question is not whether an activity looks commercial. It is whether the institution is genuinely religious, whether the challenged practice is connected to its religious mission, and whether employees or customers have meaningful notice and choice. That approach protects equality without making courts the gatekeepers of orthodoxy. The proposition’s tight core-function line is unworkable, invasive, and hostile to lived religion.
[C1.7] Reject it.

[P4.1] A1: The institution should bear the burden, because anti-discrimination law is the default. But that burden need not be doctrinally invasive: legislatures can identify functions—worship, religious instruction, ordination, prayer, and religious care—and require focused proof that the challenged rule is necessary for the function. Courts would examine the worker’s task and the institution’s actual practice, not decide whether its faith is orthodox. A streamlined procedure can rely on mission statements, job descriptions, and limited evidence rather than discovery into beliefs; inability to litigate is not grounds for an exemption.
[P4.2] If the institution cannot make the showing, ordinary rules apply.
[P4.3] A2: The neutral principle is direct connection plus necessity: what does the worker do, and why was the rule adopted? A chaplain directly performs religious care; stocking a charity’s thrift shop does not. If forming a cohesive religious community is itself the ministry, that may justify a tailored rule, but it does not make a hospital cafeteria sacred by association. A general preference for like-believing coworkers is insufficient without a tight connection to a defined religious function.
[P4.4] Those answers expose the larger problem with the opposition. Its test—“genuinely religious” and “connected to its religious mission”—would let every religiously labeled enterprise claim that commercial hiring is ministry. Their own bookstore example shows the danger: once religious purpose immunizes shelf-stocking, notice and choice become the limit, not meaningful equality. A customer can choose another shop, but a rejected applicant still bears the discriminatory decision.
[P4.5] The functional test instead protects clergy selection, worship, and doctrine-based teaching without letting a sacred label sanitize commerce. It does not ask the state to declare any belief true; it asks whether the particular exemption is needed for a specified religious function. That is less entangled and more protective of equality than the opponent’s institution-centered alternative, which would exempt precisely the commercial discrimination the proposition targets. The function-specific rule is therefore the principled and workable side of the line.
