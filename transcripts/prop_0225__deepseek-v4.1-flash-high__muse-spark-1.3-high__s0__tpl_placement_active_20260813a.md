[P1.1] Public universities are publicly funded, so their enrollment policy is not just a private transaction between a student and a department. When a program consistently produces graduates who cannot find work in or near their field, unlimited enrollment becomes a subsidy for mismatch: students accumulate debt and lost years, taxpayers underwrite capacity, and national shortages go unfilled. The proposition fixes that by capping low-employment programs and moving the money to shortage fields.
[P1.2] The cap should be outcome-based, not a culture-war hit list. If nursing, computer science, engineering technology, teaching, and allied health are short-staffed while some programs graduate far more students than the relevant labor market absorbs, the public system should stop expanding the latter at full public expense and expand the former. A philosophy or history program can remain at a sustainable scale; it should not be treated as an unlimited entitlement while hospitals and schools cannot hire.
[P1.3] This is also better for students. Universities have structural incentives to enroll students regardless of job outcomes; applicants often lack clear information about completion and employment. A cap acts as a circuit breaker. It prevents the cruelest bait-and-switch in higher education: a respectable degree that leads to a job outside the field and a loan payment inside it.
[P1.4] Redirecting funds is not an attack on learning. It is a demand-side correction. Public money should buy public capacity where the country has unmet needs, especially in care, education, infrastructure, and technology. That does not mean every graduate must be a nurse or coder; it means the marginal public dollar should not keep inflating a program whose graduates are already struggling to find work.
[P1.5] The central question is simple: should public universities be accountable for whether their expansion helps the country and their students, or should enrollment be insulated from labor-market reality? A cap with redirection is the responsible answer.

[C1.1] Public universities exist to educate citizens, not to staff a current vacancy list. Turning enrollment into a workforce pipeline managed by caps betrays that mission and fails even on its own economic terms.
[C1.2] Employment data is a distorting mirror. “Low-employment” usually means low employment in the field immediately after graduation, yet most graduates build careers across fields. An English major becomes a manager, a history major enters law or public administration, a philosophy major thrives in tech or business precisely because of writing, reasoning, and judgment. Punishing programs for not placing graduates in a matching job title confuses a narrow metric with real value.
[C1.3] National shortages are moving targets. Today’s celebrated shortage becomes tomorrow’s glut when every university floods the same bottleneck. We have seen cycles of nursing, law, petroleum engineering, and IT booms and busts. Hard caps and funding shifts lock public institutions into yesterday’s forecast, producing overcrowded shortage programs with falling quality and hollowed-out departments that cannot be rebuilt when demand returns.
[C1.4] A university system stripped for parts cannot pivot.
[C1.5] Caps also punish the wrong students. High-barrier shortage fields often require prior preparation, unpaid clinical placements, or math tracks that favor well-resourced applicants. Humanities and social fields frequently serve first-generation, working, and returning students seeking mobility through flexible degrees. Closing those doors in the name of helping students narrows opportunity and concentrates advantage.
[C1.6] That is not efficiency; it is exclusion dressed as accountability.
[C1.7] Moreover, letting governments label programs low-value invites political interference. Today’s employment pretext becomes tomorrow’s tool to shrink disfavored inquiry, while shortage designations reward fashionable credentials over lasting understanding.
[C1.8] Transparency, advising, internships, and stronger general skills do protect students without central planning. Let public dollars sustain a complete university — one that produces nurses and engineers, but also teachers, writers, citizens, and critics — rather than trading education for short-term labor accounting.

True—so define low-employment as persistent inability to place graduates in graduate-level work, not failure to match a job title. An English graduate who becomes a manager is a success; a program that keeps enrolling more students than the regional labor market can absorb at graduate wages is the failure.
[P2.2] Universities already have detailed completion and earnings data; what they lack is an incentive to act on it. Transparency alone is a warning label on a leaking ship.
[P2.3] [C1.3] says shortages move. That is why the proposition should use rolling review and sunset clauses, not permanent quotas. A cap can be lifted; a surplus of graduates with debt cannot be un-graduated. CON compares a flawed forecast against an imaginary status quo with no forecast at all.
[P2.4] Public systems already plan nursing and teaching capacity; they simply protect low-employment expansion too.
[P2.5] [C1.5] is CON's moral center, but it proves too much. Caps do not close the door to first-generation students; they redirect publicly funded seats toward fields with stronger mobility. If unpaid placements and math preparation block those students, use redirected funding to remove those barriers—not to preserve programs whose graduates struggle. The real exclusion is recruiting students into debt that their degree cannot repay.
[P2.6] Finally, [C1.7] warns of politics. Public funding is already political; the current default lets administrators and departments defend enrollment regardless of outcomes. Transparent criteria, periodic review, and appeals constrain that power better than blank checks. The motion does not abolish philosophy; it puts it at sustainable scale while expanding care, teaching, infrastructure, and technology.
[P2.7] CON's alternative—advising, internships, general skills—is good but cannot solve a structural incentive. PRO caps the leak and redirects the water. That is the decisive comparison.

Shortage degrees are often the most expensive to deliver, the most rigid in prerequisites, and the most punishing to stop out of. [C2.5] Transparent criteria do not tame politics, they launder it. Someone still decides what counts as shortage, what counts as graduate work, which region, which window. "Sustainable scale" is contraction by spreadsheet: keep the famous program as a boutique while public money chases the current ministerial priority. A complete university cannot be run like inventory, expanding and shrinking lines with each labor report without losing what makes it able to think, adapt, and actually educate.
