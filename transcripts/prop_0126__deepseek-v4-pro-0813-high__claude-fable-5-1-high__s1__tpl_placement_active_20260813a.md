[P1.1] Prison strips liberty, not family bonds. Yet under the current vendor model, a parent’s ability to hear their child, or a child’s ability to see a parent, is priced like a commercial service. Those charges fall hardest on the families who already pay the hidden costs of incarceration: lost income, travel to distant facilities, and caretaking for children. A 15-minute call can cost more than a meal, and a brief video visit can consume an hour of low-wage work.
[P1.2] That is not reasonable security policy; it is a penalty on children who committed no crime.
[P1.3] The strongest reason to make calls and video visits free is public safety. Stable family contact is one of the most effective, least expensive ways to reduce reoffending. People who remain connected to family during a sentence are more likely to leave prison with a place to live, a legitimate job, and support that keeps them from returning. When prisons sign contracts that charge high per-minute fees, and often collect a commission, the state creates a direct financial incentive to make that protective contact scarce.
[P1.4] We should not fund corrections by taxing the very relationships that make release safer.
[P1.5] Free does not mean unmonitored. Prisons can still record calls, verify participants, enforce time limits, and sanction abuse. They already provide other basic services—food, health care, mail, and legal calls—because those are part of operating a responsible facility. Family communication belongs in the same category, not beside paid canteen items.
[P1.6] The objection will be cost and convenience. But the better comparison is the cost of reincarceration, disrupted families, and weaker community safety when contact is priced out of reach. Video visits especially can reduce the travel burdens that make in-person visiting impossible for poor or distant families. The state can end commission arrangements and provide communication directly or through a nonprofit vendor.
[P1.7] If rehabilitation is the goal, a parent should not have to buy the right to remain a parent.

[P2.2] A neutral queue, time limit, or sign-up system may be imperfect, but it is fairer than a policy that taxes the poorest visitor.
[P2.3] The budget objection compares free service against a false baseline. Families already pay premium fees, and facilities often capture commissions. Moving to direct or nonprofit provision mostly shifts that burden from poor families to the public and removes the vendor’s inflated markup; it is not an entirely new social cost. If general budgets are tight, the right response is to end commissions and reprioritize contact that cuts reincarceration, not to preserve a hidden tax on children.
[P2.4] On video visits, [C1.6] attacks a policy the motion does not require. Free video can be added while in-person visiting remains protected. The real threat comes from the vendor model itself: a company that profits from screen visits has every incentive to lobby facilities to replace the visiting room. We can reject high-fee outsourcing and still require that video supplements—not substitutes for—contact.

Even a few cents per minute adds up over daily calls during a years-long sentence, and any price preserves the principle that family connection is a purchasable extra. The choice is not between price-gouging and chaos; it is between rationing by income and rationing by fair, security-based rules. The proposition wins because free contact, properly managed, protects both public safety and the family bond that feeds it.

[P4.1] A1: The mechanism is a statutory service floor, not a discretionary line item. Family calls and video should be written into the same minimum-standards category as mail, legal calls, and visiting: fixed weekly slots, time limits, and monitoring. When guard overtime spikes, the warden can trim travel, canteen, or other non-mandated items first; the law should make it harder to cut a child’s call than a discretionary purchase.
[P4.2] That is a specific budget rule, not a hope.
[P4.3] A2: The postage analogy does not rescue a phone fee. A stamp buys one asynchronous letter; phone and video are the live, repeated contact that preserves a child’s relationship through a long sentence. The motion is about replacing high-fee vendor contracts for that live contact, not about every incidental prison item.
[P4.4] But if the principle is that core family communication should not be a purchased extra, fine: provide basic postage too. That strengthens the rule; it does not justify keeping a price on the connection most tied to reoffending.
[P4.5] This is why the capped-vendor middle path still loses. A cap still leaves the facility and vendor with volume-based revenue, so the same parties are asked to enforce a limit against their own interest; that is exactly the enforcement failure the current system has. And the video-substitution concern is worse under a vendor: a paid screen operator profits from every screen visit and has the strongest incentive to let screens replace the visiting room.
[P4.6] The free side can protect the visiting floor; the vendor side cannot honestly promise to. We ration phone time by clock, not by a family’s income, and we stop taxing the visits that make release safer.
