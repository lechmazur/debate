[C1.1] Not every partial remedy deserves permission; some license the failure they claim to mitigate. In high-harassment systems, women-only cars turn segregation into the official answer and move the duty to avoid assault from the transit agency to the women riding it.
[C1.2] The central problem is not that the car helps some women today. It is what permission does to the system’s obligation tomorrow. When a transit authority can point to a reserved car, it gains a visible, low-cost defense against charges of failure. The message becomes: we gave women a safe space, so why are they in the other cars?
[C1.3] Harassment stops being a system-wide emergency and becomes a sorting problem—women must sort themselves into the protected car, while the rest of the network remains male space.
[C1.4] This burden shift is not abstract. A woman who cannot reach the women-only car, who misses it, who travels with male relatives, who does not fit a binary gender test, or who refuses segregation is left with weaker protection and greater blame. Enforcement requires staff to inspect bodies and identities at the door, inviting humiliation instead of preventing abuse. Because the car is limited, it can become overcrowded and less useful, while the unprotected cars are tacitly written off.
[C1.5] PRO’s seatbelt analogy fails because seatbelts protect everyone without conditioning access on sex. Women-only cars do not make the train safer; they relocate risk and rename it choice. Choice is a euphemism when the alternative is known danger. Quiet cars are not comparable: noise is not violence, and no one’s right to ride is made conditional on accepting separation.
[C1.6] The better partial remedies are universal: lighting, visible staff, alarms, crowd control, faster investigation, and design changes that reduce anonymous crush. These are imperfect too, but they expand safety without surrendering equal access. High-harassment systems should be forced to make every car safer, not permitted to make women disappear into one.
