[P1.1] Picture a courier who works fifty hours a week: twenty on one rideshare app, fifteen delivering groceries, fifteen on a freelance site. She earns a full-time living. Yet when she breaks her wrist, no one is responsible, because each platform looks at its slice and sees a part-timer. That is the gap this motion closes, and it is a gap created by design, not accident.
[P1.2] A portable benefits floor means protections attach to the worker, not the firm. Every platform or contracting client pays a proportional contribution, scaled to the earnings it generates, into an account that follows the worker. The floor covers basics: injury and sickness coverage, paid leave accrual, retirement contributions. It is the same logic as multiemployer plans that have worked for decades in construction and entertainment, where nobody has a single boss either.
[P1.3] Several jurisdictions have already begun applying it to drivers. The mechanism is proven; the only question is whether it should be required.
[P1.4] It must be required, because this is a textbook collective action problem. Any platform that funds benefits alone raises its costs and gets undercut by the rival that doesn't. Voluntary schemes therefore stay small and vanish in downturns. A mandate makes decency competitively neutral: every firm pays, so none is punished for it.
[P1.5] Notice what the motion does not do. It does not reclassify anyone as an employee, does not impose schedules, and does not touch casual earners, since the floor applies only to substantial income. The flexibility that gig workers genuinely value survives intact. What changes is who absorbs the downside when things go wrong.
[P1.6] Today that downside lands on emergency rooms, public assistance, and families. Platforms keep the margin while taxpayers cover the injuries. That is not a free market; it is a subsidy. The floor simply asks the firms profiting from this labour to carry a proportional share of its risk.
