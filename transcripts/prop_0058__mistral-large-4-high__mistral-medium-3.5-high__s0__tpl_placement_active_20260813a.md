[P1.1] Government efficiency drives of the DOGE variety mistake motion for progress. They slash headcount and budgets with ideological fervor, but what they dismantle is state capacity—the accumulated expertise, institutional memory, and coordination mechanisms that make public services actually function. This capacity is radically asymmetric: it takes decades to build and days to destroy.
[P1.2] When thousands of probationary employees are fired indiscriminately, the government doesn't just shed "waste"—it loses the people who understand how the systems work. IRS agents who audit complex returns disappear; the immediate payroll savings are swamped by a ballooning tax gap. Inspectors who prevent bridge collapses or foodborne illness look like "duplicated overhead" on a spreadsheet, until the infrastructure fails or the outbreak hits. Grant managers who shepherd infrastructure projects are labeled redundant, so roads and broadband sit unbuilt for years while communities wait.
[P1.3] The claimed savings are largely illusory accounting. Deferred maintenance on federal facilities, delayed benefit processing, and lost revenue from collapsed enforcement all shift costs downstream. A government that cannot deliver becomes more expensive, not less—in emergency disaster response, litigation, and private contracting to replace lost competence. These cuts compound: losing safety inspectors or claims processors creates backlogs and errors that cascade for years.
[P1.4] Genuine efficiency requires surgical precision and domain expertise. DOGE-style drives substitute ideological certainty for competence, treating the state like a tech startup to be "disrupted." The damage to service delivery is not an unfortunate side effect; it is the predictable outcome of confusing destruction with reform.
