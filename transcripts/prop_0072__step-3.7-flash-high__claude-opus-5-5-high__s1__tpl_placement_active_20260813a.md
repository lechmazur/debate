[P1.1] The purpose of public civic education is not to produce uncritical nationalists, but to equip young people with the factual grounding to hold their government accountable and participate meaningfully in democracy. Requiring curricula that include a country’s own atrocities and abuses, rather than only its achievements and narratives of victimhood, is a non-negotiable part of that mission.
[P1.2] When schools erase or downplay state-perpetrated harm, they produce citizens who cannot recognize when their government repeats those same wrongs. A student who only learns of their country’s WWII resistance, with no instruction on its systematic internment of ethnic minorities or colonial atrocities in occupied territories, will lack the context to push back when modern policy targets marginalized groups or justifies foreign intervention on false heroic premises. This is not a hypothetical: in nations where curricula center only national triumph and foreign victimhood, public support for abusive state policies and extremist nationalist movements is consistently higher, because citizens have no frame of reference for their country’s capacity for harm.
[P1.3] Critics will claim this approach is divisive or unpatriotic, but the opposite is true. A national identity built on honest accounting of both achievement and failure is far more resilient than one built on myth. When students learn that their country has both done great good and committed grave wrongs, they are more likely to work to expand the good and correct the wrongs, rather than blindly defending harmful policies in the name of patriotism. Teaching these hard truths does not undermine national pride; it aligns it with ethical responsibility, creating a citizenry that is both informed and committed to justice.
[P1.4] Requiring this curriculum is not an attack on the country, but an investment in its better future.

[C1.2] First, the pen-holder problem. A mandate means the state decides which of its own acts count as atrocities. Was the firebombing of German cities an atrocity or a grim necessity? Was a colonial war a crime or a liberation struggle gone wrong?
[C1.3] These are live historical disputes, and PRO hands the verdict to whichever party holds the education ministry this year. Governments are the worst possible judges of their own guilt. They will confess the sins of rivals and predecessors while guarding their own. Soviet leaders denounced Stalin's crimes partly to consolidate power, not to teach honesty.
[C1.4] A mandated "atrocities unit" becomes a political weapon, rewritten with each election.
[C1.5] Second, mandates provoke the backlash PRO claims to cure. When one side legislates what moral lessons children must absorb, the other side legislates the opposite. We have watched this happen in American curriculum fights: required lessons met by bans, teachers frightened into reading scripts, parents pulling children out. History stops being a discipline and becomes a loyalty test in both directions.
[C1.6] Students learn not that their country can do wrong, but that history is whatever the winning faction says.
[C1.7] Third, there is a better path that doesn't require this motion. Set standards for accuracy, evidence, and source analysis. Train teachers to examine primary documents. Let students who read an internment order, or a massacre survivor's testimony, reach conclusions themselves.
[C1.8] Honest inquiry teaches citizens to question power; a state-assigned list of national sins teaches them to obey a new script.
[C1.9] Finally, PRO's key empirical claim, that mythologizing curricula "consistently" raise support for abuses, arrives with no evidence. Even if the correlation is real, the causation likely runs backward: nationalist governments write nationalist curricula. Handing those same governments a mandate to define atrocities won't fix that. It will arm them.
