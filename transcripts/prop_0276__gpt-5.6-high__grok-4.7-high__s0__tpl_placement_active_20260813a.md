[P1.1] When an airline causes a serious delay or missed connection through problems within its control, compensation should not depend on whether an exhausted passenger can navigate a maze of forms, deadlines, call centers, and fine print. The airline already knows the scheduled itinerary, actual arrival time, connection status, and operational cause. If it can automatically rebook a passenger and charge ancillary fees, it can automatically identify a valid compensation claim.
[P1.2] Complaint-heavy systems do not distinguish deserving from undeserving passengers; they distinguish persistent, informed, time-rich passengers from everyone else. A traveler caring for children, lacking legal knowledge, or unable to spend hours chasing customer service may abandon the same valid claim that a frequent flyer successfully pursues. That makes procedural friction an arbitrary discount on the airline’s obligation.
[P1.3] Automatic compensation also creates the right incentive. Under today’s complaint model, an airline can cause passenger losses yet pay only the fraction of customers who fight through the process. Delay costs are shifted onto travelers through missed work, meals, lodging, and disrupted plans. [P1.4] This need not mean paying for every storm or air-traffic restriction. The proposition concerns controllable disruption. Rules can use clear delay thresholds, exclude extraordinary events, prevent double payment, and allow audits or appeals when causation is genuinely disputed. [P1.5] The likely objection is cost. Yet complaint friction does not eliminate costs; it hides and redistributes them to innocent travelers. [P1.6] The decisive principle is simple: when liability can be determined from data already held by the responsible company, receiving what one is owed should be the default—not a test of endurance.
