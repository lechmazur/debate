[P1.1] The core bargain of employment is work for pay, not attention for pay. What ultimately matters is the value a person creates—finished reports, resolved customer issues, closed sales, functional code—not how many hours their cursor blinked. Evaluating remote and hybrid workers mainly on outputs aligns the evaluation with the actual reason they are hired, while constant monitoring of activity or presence signals is a costly, inaccurate proxy that damages performance and trust.
[P1.2] Output-based evaluation sharpens incentives exactly where the business needs them. When people are judged on completed deliverables, they focus on eliminating bottlenecks, communicating clearly, and solving problems efficiently. A software engineer measured on merged pull requests that pass review and meet quality standards will write cleaner code and collaborate earlier, rather than leaving a chat status green while spinning wheels. This orientation rewards effectiveness, not just endurance.
[P1.3] By contrast, activity monitoring fosters performative busyness: camera always on, rapid responses to trivial messages, mouse-movement simulators—none of which puts a product in customers’ hands.
[P1.4] Trust and autonomy are not soft values; they are hard drivers of retention and discretionary effort. Knowledge workers who know they are trusted to manage their own time experience less burnout and stay longer, which directly lowers turnover and hiring costs. Constant surveillance signals the opposite: “We don’t believe you’re working unless we can see you.” That posture sucks the intrinsic motivation out of complex, creative tasks and replaces it with compliance theater. The result is often a resentful workforce that does exactly what is watched—visible activity—while neglecting the deeper, less observable thinking that produces breakthroughs.
[P1.5] Remote and hybrid settings expose just how unreliable presence signals are. A colleague in a different time zone may do her best work at hours when monitors show her offline; a parent may front-load the day and finish tasks early, then appear idle during the childcare window, yet still deliver on time. Tethering evaluation to activity punishes these patterns, even though output is identical. Conversely, someone can be perpetually “active” in chat and meetings while contributing nothing of substance.
