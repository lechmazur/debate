[P1.1] When a publisher shuts down an online game, the casualty is not merely a discontinued service. It is a town square, a decade of player-created history, and a design experiment that may never be repeated. Because the server was the artifact, shutdown can make the work unplayable and unstudied—unlike a book on a shelf. That is why the default response must change: publishers should allow community preservation through private servers, archival modes, or mod support rather than erase access.
[P1.2] This is not a demand to seize intellectual property. Ownership is not the issue; destruction is. After a title stops being commercially supported, the justifications for control weaken: there is no active market to protect, no revenue at risk, and no official player base harmed by a non-commercial revival. Copyright and trademark still guard against copying or a competing official product; a licensed, non-commercial, attribution-only emulator or offline archive threatens no legitimate interest.
[P1.3] The real question is whether the mere desire to keep control justifies making a cultural work disappear forever.
[P1.4] History shows preservation works. When Disney closed Toontown Online, fans rebuilt it as Toontown Rewritten; without that effort, the game would be little more than screenshots and memory. Similar fan projects have kept early online worlds alive long after companies moved on. These efforts already happen in legal shadows, vulnerable to takedowns and unstable hosting.
[P1.5] Bringing them into the light through clear end-of-life terms—no monetization, preserved credits, reasonable security—reduces risk rather than increasing it, and converts antagonism into goodwill.
[P1.6] The burden should fall on erasure, not survival. Total deletion is irreversible, cheap to order, and catastrophic for players and future historians; preservation is reversible, governable, and already feasible. A publisher that will no longer support a world should have to justify demolishing it, not merely claim the right to do so. A shutdown should be a sunset, not a demolition.

[C1.2] Calling that “erasure” already assumes players have a moral claim to the game’s infrastructure.

A publisher may remaster, license, or reopen a title later; a blanket expectation that shutdown means community private servers or mod support would pre-commit those assets to unofficial forks.
[C1.4] The claim that there is “no revenue at risk” after shutdown is wrong because dormant IP still has potential commercial value, and trademark owners must police unauthorized uses.

[C1.6] That is not preservation; it is liability without governance.
[C1.7] Preservation can happen without forcing publishers to open private servers or mod tools. Video archives, documentation, client-side preservation, and licensed rereleases can keep history alive.
