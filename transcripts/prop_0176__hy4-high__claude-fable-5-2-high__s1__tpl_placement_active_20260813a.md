[P1.1] The choice is not citizens consuming now versus a distant elite hoarding money. In a developing country, a commodity windfall is an exhaustible public asset delivered through violent price spikes. It must do three things: smooth boom-bust cycles, avoid overheating the economy, and turn a barrel or ton of ore into assets that remain after depletion. A sovereign wealth fund is built for those jobs.
[P1.2] Direct cash is built for one job: immediate consumption.
[P1.3] That is cash's central weakness. Pay oil or copper revenue to millions during a boom and much becomes food, fuel, imported goods, and higher local prices. The exchange rate rises; farmers and manufacturers lose competitiveness; the budget still faces teachers, clinics, roads, and debt when prices fall. A dividend has no memory.
[P1.4] Once spent, it cannot cushion next year's crash, finance a port, or compensate the next generation. A fund can hold foreign assets, sterilize inflows, follow a withdrawal rule, and release revenue when the cycle turns down.
[P1.5] Cash also looks cleaner than it is. It needs a state able to identify citizens, prevent duplicate claims, reach rural households, avoid capture, and sustain a formula as prices collapse. Those are the capacities a fund needs. But the fund concentrates them in one auditable balance sheet, published rules, and visible assets.
[P1.6] A badly run fund is a scandal with a number; a badly run universal dividend is an excluded family, an inflated market, or an unfunded election promise.
[P1.7] None of this requires claiming every fund is wise, or that poor households never need support. It means the primary windfall manager must have a long horizon and macro tools. A fund can later finance targeted transfers, public infrastructure, debt reduction, or stabilization; cash can only be paid out. The decisive comparison: only a fund can turn a volatile finite resource into durable development, and make the boom serve the bust.

[C1.1] A sovereign wealth fund is a promise that politicians will leave a growing pile of money alone for decades. In Norway, that promise holds. The motion is about developing countries, and there the record is grim. Nigeria's oil savings account was drained by the very governments meant to guard it.
[C1.2] Angola's fund ended up managed by the president's son, and Libya's became a regime piggy bank. PRO calls the fund "one auditable balance sheet" [P1.5]. For anyone in power it is one target, and it is audited only if the powerful allow it.
[C1.3] That is the central clash. The resource curse is a political disease, not a spreadsheet problem. When revenue flows from the ground to the treasury without passing through citizens, rulers do not need taxpayers and so do not answer to them. A fund keeps the money exactly where the curse breeds.
[C1.4] Cash does the reverse: it puts the windfall in millions of hands that cannot be captured, and a government that wants revenue must tax it back and justify itself. A stolen fund is discovered years later. A missing dividend is noticed by every household that month.
[C1.5] Second, PRO's sneer that cash is "immediate consumption" [P1.2] misreads poverty. Cash transfer programs across Africa and Latin America repeatedly show poor households buying livestock, tools, roofing, school fees and better nutrition. A fed and schooled child is an asset that outlasts the mine. Compare parking the money in rich-country bonds at modest returns while stunting persists at home.
[C1.6] Poor countries are capital-starved, so the highest-return investments are in their own people, and households make them without a ministry taking a cut.
[C1.7] Third, the macro tools PRO lists work only if the withdrawal rule is obeyed. Rules get rewritten at the first crash or election. And PRO's admission that the fund "can later finance" transfers or infrastructure [P1.7] is exactly the discretion that invites raiding.
[C1.8] The question is not which instrument looks best on paper, but which survives contact with weak institutions. Money already in citizens' pockets cannot be looted.

[P2.1] The cash case rests on a sleight of hand. “Money already in citizens’ pockets cannot be looted” [C1.8] is true of a banknote, false of a windfall. Before pockets, revenue is collected, converted, budgeted, registered, and paid through contractors and banks: many targets, not zero. After pockets, the public value vanishes through inflation, import surges, exchange-rate appreciation, or clinic closures. A stolen fund leaves a hole; a dividend boom leaves diffuse losses no household can sue over.
[P2.2] The tax-accountability claim is backwards. Resource dividends are not taxes; rulers buy consent with rents and avoid scrutiny. A missing check produces “pay more,” not “show the depletion path.” To “tax it back” [C1.4] from millions is costly; easier to tax consumption, borrow, print, or cut services. Cash can become clientelism with clean wording.
[P2.3] Drained funds [C1.1] prove weak institutions can raid any public asset; they do not prove cash is safer. If rulers can rewrite a fund law, they can rewrite a dividend formula, pick contractors, exclude regions, delay payments, or borrow to sustain handouts. A withdrawal rule can be broken [C1.7], but cash needs rules too: who counts, how much, what happens when prices fall. Those rules are rewritten at elections and crashes.
[P2.4] Household investment [C1.5] changes the metric. Roofs, livestock, and school fees are good anti-poverty spending, not a way to manage volatile, exhaustible revenue. The dividend has no throttle: it peaks when the economy is overheating. Everyone spends together; prices and imports rise, tradable producers suffer, and the budget has no reserve when prices fall.
[P2.5] A schooled child still needs teachers, clinics, stable prices, and a payroll in the bust.
[P2.6] On the weak-institution test, the fund remains the better manager because it makes failure legible and keeps options under a rule: stabilize, transfer, repay debt, build. Cash has one option at the peak: spend; it leaves an entitlement behind. The better manager can say not now.
