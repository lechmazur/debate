[P1.1] India is uniquely positioned to benefit from a national right-to-repair law for consumer electronics. For millions of Indian consumers, extending the lifespan of a smartphone or laptop is not just a convenience—it is an economic necessity. By requiring manufacturers to provide access to parts, tools, and diagnostic documentation, India can dismantle artificial repair monopolies, empower local economies, and directly combat an escalating environmental crisis.
[P1.2] First, a right-to-repair law champions consumer affordability and economic empowerment. India relies heavily on a sprawling, highly skilled network of independent repair technicians. However, manufacturers increasingly use software locks, proprietary hardware, and restricted parts distribution to freeze these small businesses out of the market. When original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) monopolize repair, they inflate prices and arbitrarily declare devices "obsolete" to force expensive new purchases.
[P1.3] Mandating access to parts and manuals democratizes repair, driving down costs for price-sensitive consumers while securing the livelihoods of independent technicians across the nation.
[P1.4] Second, a national mandate is essential to addressing India’s severe e-waste crisis. India ranks among the world's top generators of electronic waste. Much of this waste is currently processed in informal, hazardous recycling hubs, exposing workers and local environments to toxic materials. The most effective way to reduce e-waste is to stop creating it at the source.
[P1.5] When a perfectly functional device is discarded simply because a battery is glued in or a specialized diagnostic tool is withheld, the environmental cost is catastrophic. Empowering consumers to repair their electronics keeps devices in use and out of toxic landfills.
[P1.6] Opponents often shield OEM monopolies behind vague claims of intellectual property or safety. But providing a replacement screen, a schematic, or an error code does not steal trade secrets; it simply allows a purchased device to be maintained. A national right-to-repair law ensures that when Indians buy electronics, they actually own them—granting them the fundamental right to fix and sustain their technology.

The PRO’s claim that repair monopolies inflate prices ignores the reality that the “monopoly” is often the result of economies of scale that keep devices cheap in the first place.
[C1.3] Second, mandatory disclosure of diagnostic tools and firmware creates serious safety and security risks. [C1.4] Third, enforcement would be a bureaucratic nightmare. [C1.5] Finally, market‑driven solutions—voluntary repair programs, extended warranties, and a thriving informal sector—already deliver most of the benefits the PRO cites, without the collateral damage of a one‑size‑fits‑all law.
