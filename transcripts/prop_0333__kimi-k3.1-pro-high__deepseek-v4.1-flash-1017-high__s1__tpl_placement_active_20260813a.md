[P1.1] Public broadcasters cannot remain relevant by simply producing more places for audiences not to visit. The choice is where to put scarce attention, talent, and investment: expanding traditional channels, or meeting young people where they already discover culture and news. Prioritizing youth-oriented short-form video and creator partnerships is the stronger public-service strategy—not because every broadcast must become a dance clip, but because distribution must follow the audience.
[P1.2] First, relevance begins with discovery. A documentary, investigation, or drama cannot shape public life if younger viewers never encounter it. Short-form video provides an entry point: a clip can explain one idea, showcase one character, or correct one false claim, then lead viewers toward reporting, full programmes, and broadcaster platforms. Traditional channel expansion reverses that logic.
[P1.3] It spends money building destinations and then hopes audiences arrive. Youth-oriented short-form starts inside existing routines and earns attention before asking for commitment.
[P1.4] Second, creator partnerships offer something a new channel logo cannot: credibility within communities. Young audiences often interpret institutions through people they already follow. Partnering with creators lets public broadcasters communicate in native formats without pretending to be something they are not. Editorial standards, transparent funding, fact-checking, and clear public-service briefs can preserve integrity while creators supply fluency and reach.
[P1.5] Rejecting partnerships because some creators are unreliable merely leaves those audiences to creators with fewer obligations and weaker standards.
[P1.6] Third, this is the better use of constrained resources. Additional channels bring scheduling, distribution, commissioning, and promotional demands, while risking duplication and cannibalization. A short-form strategy can test ideas quickly, adapt formats, and identify what deserves longer investment. It is not automatically cheap, but it is more experimental and less structurally committed.
[P1.7] This does not require abandoning older audiences, linear broadcasting, or long-form excellence. Prioritization concerns the next marginal investment, not the destruction of existing services. The opposition must defend expansion as a relevance strategy, not merely defend tradition. Public broadcasting survives by carrying public value into new attention spaces; building more channels while youth culture forms elsewhere is institutional self-preservation without public connection.

[C1.1] Public broadcasters do not stay relevant by renting relevance from platforms whose business model is distraction. The proposition mistakes visibility for public value. A public broadcaster’s distinctive asset is not short-form agility; it is trusted, universal, accountable distribution. That is what traditional channels and their extensions—free-to-air, local and regional services, live coverage, connected-TV apps, long-form—protect.
[C1.2] Short-form video is a crowded, algorithmically mediated supplement. It can introduce a clip, but it cannot carry the core functions: election coverage, emergency information, investigations, children’s programming, minority-language service, accessible news. Those require durable scheduling, editorial control, and reach beyond people already served by a feed. If a wildfire alert or local election debate depends on a creator’s upload and platform ranking, the public has not gained a channel; it has gained a lottery.
[C1.3] The claim that a clip leads viewers toward full programmes [P1.2] is the weakest link: platform incentives are designed to keep people in the feed, not deliver them to a broadcaster’s accountability.
[C1.4] Creator partnerships carry a second cost: credibility is not borrowed cleanly. Audiences may trust a familiar face, but public broadcasters are trusted because responsibility is visible—who commissioned, who corrected, who is accountable. Blending an institutional brand with commercial creators risks importing creator incentives into public service: engagement bait, sponsorship ambiguity, simplification, outrage. The answer is not to ban creators; it is to refuse to make them the priority.
[C1.5] And traditional channel expansion is not nostalgia. It can mean extending free, universal, local service to places platforms underserve: rural and older audiences, disabled viewers, communities without reliable broadband. Public broadcasting remains relevant by being the institution people turn to when information must be reliable, not by competing for the same thirty seconds as every brand and influencer.
[C1.6] Youth-oriented short-form should be a tactical doorway, not the house. Prioritizing it makes public broadcasters more dependent on private platforms and less distinct from commercial media chasing the same metrics. Relevance is not being seen everywhere; it is being worth finding when it matters.
