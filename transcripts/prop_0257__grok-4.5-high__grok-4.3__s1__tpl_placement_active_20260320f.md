[P1.1] When you hand over money for a book, game, soundtrack, or film, you are not renting a permission slip. You are buying an asset. Consumers should therefore retain meaningful ownership rights over digitally purchased books, games, music, and films rather than hold only revocable platform licenses.
[P1.2] Ownership means permanence and autonomy. You keep the work after the store closes, transfer it if you wish, use it offline on hardware you choose, and face no sudden vanishing act when a platform rewrites its terms, loses a license, or simply decides the content is no longer convenient. A revocable license delivers none of this. It delivers temporary access that companies have already demonstrated they can terminate—libraries emptied, games bricked by shutdowns, music and films scrubbed without refund.
[P1.3] Payment becomes a one-way transfer of cash for fragile privileges.
[P1.4] That arrangement is a structural rip-off. Digital goods often cost the same as physical editions yet provide less durability and fewer rights. Secondary markets collapse, locking out resale that has long lowered prices and redistributed cultural works. Personal collections become dependent on corporate goodwill.
[P1.5] Culture itself grows more fragile: when only platforms hold the masters and consumers hold permission tokens, entire bodies of work can disappear from public reach at a stroke.
[P1.6] Meaningful ownership corrects the imbalance. It treats a purchase as a sale, not a revocable lease. It preserves the consumer’s stake, enables fair secondary markets, and keeps culture from living solely at the pleasure of intermediaries. The alternative is digital serfdom: pay full price, keep nothing solid.
[P1.7] Sale should mean sale. Ownership rights should travel with the price paid.

[C1.1] Digital goods exist only through ongoing platform infrastructure that consumers do not own or maintain. Servers, compatibility patches, DRM enforcement, and bandwidth all cost money long after the initial sale. Treating every purchase as permanent ownership would require platforms to absorb those perpetual costs without corresponding revenue, leading them to either raise prices sharply or withdraw marginal titles.
[C1.2] The license model instead spreads those expenses across a large user base while delivering immediate convenience. Users receive automatic updates, cloud restores after device loss, and access on new hardware without managing files themselves. Shifting to ownership would transfer those burdens to individuals, many of whom lack the technical means or interest to preserve long-term access.
[C1.3] True ownership also collides with the economics of copying. Once a file can be freely duplicated and transferred, the original provider loses any ability to fund further creation or maintenance. Developers and publishers would respond by limiting distribution to closed ecosystems or inflating upfront prices to compensate for expected leakage, shrinking the catalog consumers currently enjoy.
[C1.4] The result is not consumer empowerment but higher costs and narrower choice. Platforms would ration content to what they can fully control or abandon services where perpetual rights cannot be sustained. Consumers would pay more for less reliable access once the shared infrastructure disappears.

[P2.1] Digital platforms already extract full retail prices while shifting every lasting risk onto buyers. The claim that ownership would force “perpetual costs” without revenue simply misstates the right at issue. Meaningful ownership delivers a durable, controllable copy—downloadable, offline-capable, and transferable—not a permanent duty for the seller to host servers or patch every title forever. After sale, the buyer maintains the asset, just as a reader keeps a paper book or a gamer shelves a disc.
[P2.2] Platforms may still offer optional cloud and update services for a fee; they simply cannot revoke the copy once paid for.
[P2.3] What CON calls cost-spreading convenience is in practice a fragile tether. Automatic restores and multi-device access vanish the moment the store closes, the license is re-cut, or the company exits the market—outcomes already common under pure licenses. Ownership does not abolish those conveniences; it severs permanence from corporate goodwill. Users who prefer hands-off access can still subscribe; those who paid full price stop being left empty-handed.
[P2.4] The copying fear likewise overreaches. Physical markets long absorbed secondary sales and private copying without collapsing catalogs or starving creators. Digital duplication is easier to police, yet the current license model already produces the very harms CON predicts: delisted games, purged music libraries, and region-locked films that shrink available culture while forcing repurchases. True ownership would restore first-sale pressure for fair pricing precisely because buyers could resell what they no longer need, rather than remaining captive to one platform’s terms.
[P2.5] Higher upfront prices or narrower offerings are not inevitable results of ownership; they are already the lived experience of revocable licenses. The decisive comparison is simple: pay once and keep an asset you control, or pay full price and remain a tenant whose library can be erased tomorrow. Sale should still mean ownership.

[C2.1] The claim that ownership can be reduced to handing over a downloadable, transferable file without ongoing platform duties ignores how digital works are created and sustained. Once a buyer holds a fully portable copy free of server checks or update dependencies, nothing prevents unlimited duplication and redistribution. Physical media imposed natural limits on sharing; bits do not. Publishers facing that reality would either stop releasing marginal titles or demand far higher initial prices to offset the expected loss of control, narrowing rather than expanding consumer choice.
[C2.2] Optional cloud services cannot rescue the model. Platforms invest in those services only when they retain leverage over the underlying content. Remove revocation power and the incentive to maintain long-term infrastructure for non-subscribers disappears. Users who paid once would be left with static files that quickly become incompatible with new devices or operating systems, exactly the outcome CON warned would follow from shifting all preservation burdens to individuals.
[C2.3] The physical analogy also fails on pricing dynamics. Secondary markets for discs never threatened primary sales at digital scale because each resale still required a physical object. A frictionless digital resale right lets every copy circulate indefinitely without further payments to creators, collapsing the revenue that funds both new works and the patches needed to keep old ones playable. The result is not restored autonomy but a thinner catalog at higher cost.
