[P1.1] Conservation can stretch a water supply; it cannot manufacture one. In regions facing structural scarcity, the arithmetic eventually defeats demand reduction: population grows, aquifers decline, reservoirs become less dependable, and basic consumption cannot be cut indefinitely. Rationing asks communities to shrink their lives around a permanent deficit. Desalination changes the denominator by adding a substantial, controllable source of fresh water.
[P1.2] That distinction matters most during shocks. A drought can simultaneously reduce rainfall, river flow, and hydropower while increasing demand. Conservation then offers only deeper restrictions. Large-scale desalination provides planned output largely independent of rainfall, allowing reservoirs and aquifers to function as reserves rather than everyday crutches.
[P1.3] It also reduces the zero-sum conflict between households, farms, industry, and ecosystems.
[P1.4] Rationing carries costs that are often hidden. The first efficiency gains—repairing leaks, installing efficient fixtures, changing wasteful landscaping—are sensible. But each additional cut becomes more disruptive. Intermittent supply undermines sanitation and hospitals; unpredictable allocations deter businesses; agricultural restrictions threaten livelihoods; and households with the least money or storage suffer most.
[P1.5] A desalination bill is visible, but so are the costs of tanker deliveries, depleted groundwater, crop losses, and economic flight.
[P1.6] Desalination does require energy, capital, and responsible brine management. Those are engineering challenges, not arguments for accepting shortage. Modern plants can be paired with renewable electricity, efficient recovery systems, careful intake design, and monitored discharge. Building at scale also supports professional operation, shared pipelines, storage, and lower unit costs than fragmented emergency responses.
[P1.7] “Primarily” does not mean “exclusively.” Regions should still price water sensibly, fix leaks, reuse wastewater, and discourage waste. But those measures should complement a secure supply backbone, not substitute for it. The decisive question is what remains when every reasonable efficiency measure has been exhausted and the drought continues. Conservation can promise only that less water will be needed.
