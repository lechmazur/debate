[P1.1] The key distinction is between decarbonizing electricity and sustaining a nuclear-centered strategy. Renewable power is no longer merely a supplement: rapidly deployed solar, wind, storage, and flexible demand can supply most electricity and remove the need for new nuclear as the central decarbonization lever. Nuclear’s advantages—steady output and low operational carbon—do not erase its weakness: years-long planning, construction, and regulation. The practical choice is which clean portfolio can be built at the required speed.
[P1.2] Rapid renewable growth changes economics. In many markets, solar and wind are now the cheapest new electricity. Committing to nuclear instead ties up capital years before the first electron. Modular renewables can be bought in tranches, matched to demand, and expanded as grids improve.
[P1.3] Batteries smooth peaks; diverse wind and solar reduce dependence on one resource; demand response, transmission, and storage absorb variability. These tools scale more readily than a multi-decade reactor program.
[P1.4] The strongest nuclear argument is that some nations need firm low-carbon generation for industrial heat, seasonal supply, or grid reliability. That concedes the motion: nuclear may have value without being central in most countries. Countries with existing fleets or specialized needs may retain it. But making it central elsewhere diverts capital and attention from deploying renewable capacity.
[P1.5] Consider a government trying to meet a near-term emissions target. A reactor may not contribute in time, whereas wind farms linked to storage and transmission can. Electrification raises electricity demand, which renewables can meet. Retaining nuclear can be sensible as a complement; insisting on centrality is not.
[P1.6] The benchmark should be delivered low-carbon electricity, not the existence of another low-carbon technology. On that benchmark, rapid renewable growth makes nuclear unnecessary as a central strategy in most countries.

[C1.1] Growth is not sufficiency. PRO's case rests on a quiet substitution: because wind and solar are cheap and fast for the first half of decarbonization, they must be adequate for all of it. The hard part is the last third: the windless winter week, the industrial load that runs all night. That is exactly where nuclear earns the word "central."
[C1.2] First, the evidence of finished work. The large economies that actually run deeply decarbonized grids, France, Sweden, Ontario, did it with nuclear and hydro, and did it decades ago. Germany spent enormous sums on renewables while closing reactors, and its electricity remains several times dirtier than France's because coal and gas fill the gaps. No major industrial economy without abundant hydro has yet reached a clean grid on wind, solar, and batteries alone.
[C1.3] PRO is asking most countries to bet their climate targets on something undemonstrated.
[C1.4] Second, the mechanism. Batteries shift power across hours, not across weeks or seasons. As renewable share climbs, each additional point demands more overbuild, more transmission, more storage, costs that the "cheapest new electricity" claim in [P1.2] leaves off the invoice. A plan whose reliability quietly depends on gas backup is not a decarbonization strategy; it is a partial one.
[C1.5] Third, [P1.4] concedes more than it realizes. If countries need firm low-carbon power for reliability, seasonal supply, and industry, then that firm source is the keystone holding the arch up. Central does not mean biggest; it means the plan fails without it. Most countries lack the hydro or geothermal to play that role.
[C1.6] And speed? Electrification will roughly double demand. France decarbonized its grid in about fifteen years with reactors. Slow is a choice about how we build, not physics.
[C1.7] So when the wind drops in January, what keeps the lights on carbon-free?

[P2.1] France, Sweden, and Ontario show that nuclear can deeply decarbonize grids; they do not show that nuclear remains the best central strategy now that solar, wind, storage, and modern grids are scalable. Those cases came from another technological era, before alternatives matured. Using twentieth-century achievements to set the 2030s template confuses historical possibility with present necessity. Germany’s higher emissions show the cost of replacing nuclear while retaining coal and gas; they do not prove a properly sequenced renewable build could not have supplied reliability.
[P2.2] The “last third” argument assumes the only firm low-carbon option is a new nuclear plant. It is not. A windless winter week can be met through a coordinated portfolio: multi-hour and multi-day storage, reservoirs, demand flexibility, stronger interconnection, geographically diverse wind and solar, and existing firm low-carbon sources.
[P2.3] Crucially, the system can also reduce demand by supplying industrial heat directly. If new firm capacity is needed, nuclear must compete with geothermal, long-duration storage, and other technologies. Its presence would not make it central.
[P2.4] Nuclear also carries costs left off that integration invoice: long development periods, large upfront commitments, construction and regulatory risk, inflexible output, and eventual decommissioning and waste obligations. Batteries and transmission have system costs too, but they can be deployed modularly and tested before committing the whole system. A new reactor is not a quick response to electrification; the opposite may be true.
[P2.5] “What keeps the lights on carbon-free?” therefore has a portfolio answer: procure firm low-carbon reserves before high renewable penetration. My opponent identifies services that must be planned for, not proof that nuclear must provide them. The proposition does not claim renewables alone suffice everywhere; it says rapid growth makes nuclear unnecessary as the central strategy in most countries. A minority benefiting from nuclear cannot make it central for the many whose fastest feasible decarbonization path is renewable expansion.
