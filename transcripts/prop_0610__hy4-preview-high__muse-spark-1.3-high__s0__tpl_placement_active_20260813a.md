[P1.1] The motion is not about avoiding a trade fight. It is about whether the threat of retaliation should veto the one policy that makes European climate leadership honest.
[P1.2] The EU already makes its own steel, cement, aluminium, and chemical plants pay for carbon through the emissions trading system. If foreign producers can sell into the single market without paying an equivalent price, European industry loses market share or moves abroad, and global emissions barely fall—they simply relocate to dirtier grids. A European steelmaker who pays a carbon price cannot compete with imports priced as if the atmosphere were free. That is carbon leakage.
[P1.3] A border adjustment fixes it by charging imports the same carbon cost domestic producers already bear.
[P1.4] Narrow coverage would not work. If you price imported steel but not the machinery and vehicles made from it, manufacturers move the dirty step offshore and ship back finished goods. The adjustment must cover most industrial goods, not just raw materials.
[P1.5] Retaliation is real, but it is a second-order cost. The European market is too valuable for major exporters to abandon. Faced with a border charge or setting up their own carbon price and keeping the revenue, governments will eventually choose the latter. That is the point: the policy exports the carbon price.
[P1.6] The EU has weathered trade disputes before; it can weather this one. Backing down at the first tariff threat would destroy that leverage forever.
[P1.7] If the EU surrenders this tool because Beijing or Washington objects, it makes climate ambition negotiable and lets foreign governments veto European law. We would get weaker industry, no real emissions cut, and a precedent that punishes climate leadership.
[P1.8] The EU should impose the carbon border adjustment on most imported industrial goods, absorb the retaliation, and force other economies to choose: clean up your industry, pay at the border, or lose access. That turns European climate policy into global rules.

[C1.2] Measuring carbon in raw steel is hard enough. Measuring it in pumps, tractors, refrigerators with hundreds of components from a dozen countries is fantasy. Customs officials cannot trace each bolt's furnace. So Brussels must use crude default values.
[C1.3] That punishes efficient foreign producers who cannot prove it, rewards lobbyists who game classifications, and turns climate policy into protectionism by spreadsheet.
[C1.4] And retaliation is not background noise. Europe is an export superpower. German cars, French aircraft, Italian machinery live on foreign markets. When Washington and Beijing answer a sweeping border charge, they will not boycott Europe; they will surgically hit those exporters, farmers, and luxury makers.
[C1.5] Factories in Stuttgart and Lyon pay for a tax designed in theory to protect factories in Duisburg.
[C1.6] The climate payoff for that pain is illusory. Exporters reroute clean goods to Europe and dirty goods elsewhere, a well-known reshuffling. Total emissions barely move while cooperation collapses. You do not build a global carbon price by starting a trade war; you give rivals every reason to walk away from joint climate deals and subsidize their own champions instead.
[C1.7] Finally, Europeans themselves pay at the checkout and on the building site. More expensive steel, cement, fertiliser mean more expensive housing, food, cars — and more expensive wind towers, grids, and rail. A sprawling border tax makes the green transition dearer inside Europe in order to look tougher outside it.
[C1.8] Europe should cut emissions by making clean production cheaper at home, not by making everything more expensive at the border while inviting retaliation that destroys jobs without cooling the planet.

[P2.1] CON says customs officials “cannot trace each bolt’s furnace” [C1.2]. That is a straw man. A border adjustment does not audit every component; it charges the carbon-intensive inputs and processes that dominate a product’s footprint—steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser, basic chemicals—using installation data, supplier declarations, or conservative default values. Defaults are not “protectionism by spreadsheet” [C1.3]; they are the backstop that gives large exporters a powerful incentive to install verified monitoring, because clean producers then pay less than the default.
[P2.2] The complexity objection, taken seriously, is an argument for careful design—not for exempting most industrial goods while leakage quietly continues.
[P2.3] Retaliation is real, but [C1.4] and [C1.5] merely restate the motion’s premise. The proposition asks whether the threat of retaliation should veto the policy. It should not. Europe is an export superpower, yes—but it is also a huge import market.
[P2.4] Big exporters will not abandon it; they will either clean up or pay at the border, and setting up their own carbon price lets them keep the revenue. If the EU backs down at the first tariff threat, it teaches every partner that a trade complaint can nullify European climate law. The cost to Stuttgart exporters is serious, but the alternative is worse: hollowed-out industry in Duisburg and everywhere else, with the same emissions simply relocated.
[P2.5] CON also says the climate payoff is illusory and that Europeans themselves pay at the checkout [C1.6][C1.7]. The rerouting they fear is exactly why broad coverage is needed [P1.4]. Narrow pricing lets dirty steel hide inside imported machinery; broad coverage follows the product. And “expensive” imports are only cheap because their carbon is unpriced.
[P2.6] If Europe refuses to charge at the border, it pays instead through lost factories, endless subsidies, and unchecked leakage. The border charge makes the polluter pay, revenues can ease the transition, and other economies face a real choice: clean up, pay, or lose market access.
