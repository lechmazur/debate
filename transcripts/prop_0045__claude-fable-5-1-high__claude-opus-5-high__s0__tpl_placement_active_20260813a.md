[P1.1] Every hiring tool earns its place by one test: does it tell you something about how the person will actually perform? MBTI and DISC fail that test, and everything else about them is decoration.
[P1.2] Start with the most basic requirement, consistency. A meaningful proportion of people who retake the MBTI a few weeks later come out as a different type. That happens because the instrument chops continuous traits into binary boxes; a person a hair to one side of the introvert–extrovert line becomes an "E" on Tuesday and an "I" on Thursday. A ruler that gives different lengths for the same board is not a measurement instrument.
[P1.3] DISC shares the same structural flaw with different labels.
[P1.4] Now the harder requirement, prediction. Decades of personnel research point in one direction: the best-validated personality model, the Big Five, shows only modest links to job performance, mostly through conscientiousness, and MBTI and DISC are cruder cousins that don't even measure conscientiousness well. Meanwhile, structured interviews, work-sample tasks, and general ability measures predict performance far better. Every hour and dollar spent on a type inventory is an hour not spent on tools that actually work.
[P1.5] Tellingly, even the MBTI's own publisher has long discouraged using it for selection.
[P1.6] The damage isn't abstract. Picture a candidate for a sales role screened out because her profile read "reserved," when a work sample would have shown she closes deals through preparation rather than charm. Or picture the team-building retreat where a manager learns his analyst is an "S" and stops offering her stretch assignments because "S types prefer stability." Labels that are supposedly for fun quietly harden into ceilings.
[P1.7] And because these instruments touch employment decisions while lacking validity, they invite exactly the kind of disparate-impact scrutiny employers should dread.
[P1.8] Expect the defense that these tests are "just a conversation starter." Fine: conversation starters shouldn't wear a lab coat. If the framework predicts nothing, the conversation it starts is astrology with a corporate license, and employers should stop.
