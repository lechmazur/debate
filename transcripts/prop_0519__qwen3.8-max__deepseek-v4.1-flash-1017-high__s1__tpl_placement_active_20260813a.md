[P1.1] Fragmented service industries—restaurants, retail, cleaning, home care—are hard to regulate because work is spread across thousands of small employers, turnover is high, and enforcement is thin. In that setting, repeated minimum-wage hikes are too blunt and too slow. They raise a single number after political delay, apply unevenly across business models, and leave most non-wage abuses untouched.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining is better because it sets enforceable minimum standards for an entire sector, not just a statutory price. A sectoral agreement can cover wages, scheduling notice, overtime, training, meal breaks, safety, and dispute resolution. It can distinguish between a large hotel chain and a small café while still lifting the floor for everyone. It also gives workers a collective voice without requiring every outlet to unionize individually.
[P1.3] That matters in services, where the worst problems are often unpredictable shifts, wage theft, and lack of benefits rather than the headline hourly rate alone.
[P1.4] The central advantage is stability. A legislated minimum wage changes only when politicians act, then jumps abruptly, creating uncertainty for employers and uneven compliance. Sectoral bargaining creates an ongoing institution: employers and worker representatives negotiate, monitor, and update standards. That continuity helps standards keep pace with inflation and labor-market conditions and avoid the boom-bust politics of wage floors.
[P1.5] This reduces the race to the bottom, because compliant firms are not undercut by competitors who cheat on pay and hours. It also makes standards more credible: industry-specific rules are easier to explain, inspect, and enforce than a patchwork of general wage mandates.
[P1.6] Finally, sectoral bargaining fits fragmented markets. Small firms often cannot absorb repeated uniform hikes, while large chains can. Sectoral standards can spread costs across the sector and prevent high-road employers from being punished. If the goal is durable minimum standards, not periodic political headlines, bargaining by sector is the more precise and sustainable tool.

[C1.1] Sectoral bargaining sounds precise, but in fragmented services precision is exactly what it cannot deliver. The decisive test is coverage and enforceability. Sectoral agreements, by contrast, depend on functioning unions and employer associations.
[C1.2] In these industries, union density is low, turnover is high, and small firms are dispersed. The likely result is not a comprehensive sector standard but a patchwork: strong coverage in the organized core, and the most vulnerable workers—domestic cleaners, small kitchens, immigrant-run outlets—left outside.
[C1.3] That creates a second problem: capture and exclusion. Sectoral bargaining invites incumbent firms and established worker representatives to set terms that raise barriers to entry or carve out exceptions for the smallest employers. A minimum wage can be undercut politically too, but at least the fight is public and the rule is simple. A multi-employer agreement is opaque, legally complex, and often enforced through the same thin inspectorate.
[C1.4] A worker who is owed wages under a sectoral schedule faces a harder claim than a worker who can point to one statutory number.
[C1.5] The stability argument is also backwards. “Repeated” hikes can be scheduled and indexed—predictable, transparent, and democratically accountable. Sectoral bargaining can stall for years, fragment by locality, or produce lower entry rates for supposedly disadvantaged firms. And non-wage standards such as scheduling notice or safety do not require bargaining to be set; they require legislation and enforcement. If the goal is minimum standards in fragmented services, the tool must reach the periphery, not just the organized core.
[C1.6] Minimum-wage hikes do that better.
