[P1.1] Dominant digital platforms don't compete like steel companies. They compete for the market, not in it. Network effects, data feedback loops, and zero-price products mean once a market tips, it stays tipped. Under current antitrust, authorities must prove future harm in that tipped world with limited data.
[P1.2] They lose. Every time.
[P1.3] Look at the record. Facebook bought Instagram for $1 billion when Instagram had 30 million users and no revenue. Under the old consumer-welfare price test, there was no harm to see. Today Instagram generates more than half of Meta's ad revenue and neutralized the only credible threat to Facebook's social graph.
[P1.4] Same with Google and DoubleClick, Waze, YouTube - buying the adjacent layer before it could become a rival platform. These are not efficiency mergers. They are moat-building.
[P1.5] The problem is asymmetric information and asymmetric costs. The platform knows exactly why it is paying a premium for a pre-revenue startup. Internal emails said Instagram could be "very disruptive." Regulators see only a small deal.
[P1.6] When regulators wrongly block a benign acquisition, the startup can find another buyer or grow. When they wrongly allow a killer acquisition, competition is permanently lost. You cannot unscramble a merged data set or resurrect a killed product path. In digital ecosystems, false negatives are fatal; false positives are temporary.
[P1.7] A rebuttable presumption fixes that. It does not ban deals. It says: if you control 40%+ of search, social, app distribution, e-commerce infrastructure, and you buy a firm in your ecosystem or adjacent to it, we presume risk and you must show substantial consumer benefit - lower prices, real quality or privacy improvements, interoperability - not just shareholder synergy. You have the documents, the data, the integration plan.
[P1.8] Prove it.
[P1.9] That is how we handle other high-risk, low-visibility conduct. It is time antitrust caught up with how dominance actually entrenches.

[C1.1] Presuming guilt to protect competition will strangle the competition it claims to protect.
[C1.2] Dominance is not an offense. Google, Apple, Amazon, Meta became dominant by building products billions chose for free or cheap. This proposal would punish that success by treating every expansion as suspect and forcing innovators to prove their innocence to keep innovating.
[C1.3] Take the prosecution's star witnesses. Instagram had a handful of employees, no revenue, and servers that crashed constantly when Facebook bought it. Facebook supplied capital, engineers, moderation, ad infrastructure and global distribution that scaled it to billions of users. YouTube was drowning in bandwidth costs until Google's infrastructure made mass video viable and free.
[C1.4] Waze improved under Google Maps resources. These were not murders of rivals; they were rescues that consumers benefited from daily.
[C1.5] That is the normal pattern. Most acquisitions of small firms are scaling deals, not killer deals. Founders build knowing acquisition is a viable exit, and investors fund them because it is. Make Big Tech a presumed criminal buyer and you do not liberate startups, you defund them.
[C1.6] Venture capital retreats, founders settle for worse partners or close up, integration and engineering talent scatter.
[C1.7] The claim that mistaken blocks are harmless is false. You cannot resurrect a startup denied scale, reassemble a departed team, or recover years of delayed distribution. Meanwhile mistaken approvals are not fatal because digital dominance is brittle: Myspace fell to Facebook, Yahoo to Google, BlackBerry to the iPhone, and TikTok exploded past Instagram despite Meta's supposed moat.
[C1.8] Finally, substantial consumer benefit is a blank check for regulators. With zero prices, quality and privacy tradeoffs, almost any deal can be framed as insufficiently beneficial. That replaces evidence with discretion and delay, chilling dozens of benign deals to speculatively stop one bad one. Antitrust should require proof of harm, not proof of virtue.
