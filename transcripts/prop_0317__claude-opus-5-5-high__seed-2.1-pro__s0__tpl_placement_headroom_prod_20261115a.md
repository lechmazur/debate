[P1.1] Start with who actually sets a ticket price. When an artist sells seats at a hundred dollars, that is a deliberate choice: fill the room with fans, keep teenagers and working families in the crowd, build loyalty for the next tour. Uncapped resale overrides that choice. Within minutes, bots and professional brokers buy in bulk and relist at three, five, ten times face value.
[P1.2] The artist did not get that money. The venue did not get it. The fan certainly did not. A middleman who added nothing but speed captured it.
[P1.3] Now ask why this persists. Major platforms earn fees on the original sale and again on every resale, so a ticket that flips twice is worth more to them than one that goes to a fan once. That is not a market failing by accident. It is a market whose gatekeeper profits from the failure.
[P1.4] The 2022 Eras Tour presale meltdown, where bot traffic overwhelmed the system, showed the result: fans locked out, then offered the same seats back at extortionate markups.
[P1.5] The motion asks for something modest. Platforms must either cap markups or offer a face-value resale channel for most tickets. Both already work. Several countries, including Ireland and parts of Australia, restrict resale above face value for major events.
[P1.6] Face-value exchanges like Twickets, and capped exchanges some artists have demanded from Ticketmaster, let a fan who gets sick sell the ticket to another fan at a fair price. Flexibility survives. Exploitation does not.
[P1.7] CON will say scalpers just go underground. But the major platforms are the chokepoint. They control ticket delivery, transfers, and increasingly identity-linked mobile tickets. [P1.8] The question is simple. Who should capture a ticket's value: the people who make and love the event, or the bots that beat them to checkout?
