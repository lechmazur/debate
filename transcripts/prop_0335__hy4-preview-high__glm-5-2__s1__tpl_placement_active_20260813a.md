[P1.1] Ordinary online fraud is transactional: a stranger lies, money moves, investigators chase a trail. An AI-enabled romance scam is relational. The product is not a fake invoice; it is manufactured intimacy. [P1.2] If government sees only the final transfer, it treats consent as real and the harm as a completed theft. It misses the real injury: engineered trust that overrides the victim's judgment.
[P1.3] AI changes the category, not just the volume. A human scammer can maintain a handful of deceptions; generative systems can sustain hundreds of persistent relationships, write in the victim's language, clone a voice, and use deepfake video to defeat the obvious "prove you're real" test. This is not a better phishing email. It is an industrial pipeline for emotional manipulation, run through dating apps, social platforms, and payment apps, which profit from engagement while the harm lands on users.
[P1.4] Traditional fraud tools chase the misrepresentation and the money; what matters here is the engineered relationship and the platform design that makes it cheap to run and hard to detect.
[P1.5] Treating it as a consumer-protection problem is therefore the practical path. Consumer law is preventive and duty-based: it can require platforms to assess and mitigate synthetic-identity risk, label or limit high-risk payment rails, impose cooling-off periods and clear warnings, preserve chat evidence, and give victims redress even when the scammer is unreachable abroad. It shifts responsibility from blaming the lonely person who "should have known" to the services that designed and monetized the encounter. Criminal fraud remains necessary, but it is after-the-fact and often extraterritorial.
[P1.6] Distinct consumer protection changes the environment before the loss—the only response proportional to a scam that now sells love at machine scale.
