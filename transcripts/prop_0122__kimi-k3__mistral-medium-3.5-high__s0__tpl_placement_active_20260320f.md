[P1.1] A ten-year-old sits in a courtroom built for adults, legs not reaching the floor, charged as a criminal. That image is not justice; it is a category error. The criminal law presupposes something a young child does not yet have: the developed capacity to understand consequences, regulate impulses, and grasp wrongfulness in the way punishment assumes. A system that punishes what a child cannot yet control is not holding anyone responsible — it is simply inflicting harm and calling it accountability.
[P1.2] Three claims carry this motion. First, culpability requires capacity. Developmental science is broadly consistent on this: the brain systems governing impulse control, foresight, and susceptibility to peer pressure mature through adolescence. We already act on this everywhere else — children under fourteen cannot vote, sign contracts, marry, or consent to medical treatment, precisely because we do not treat their judgment as fully formed.
[P1.3] Criminal punishment, the heaviest blame the state assigns, should demand the most capacity, not the least.
[P1.4] Second, treating young children as offenders produces worse safety outcomes. Early contact with courts and detention is one of the strongest predictors of persistent offending: it interrupts schooling, deepens trauma, attaches a criminal label a child then grows into, and mixes children with older offenders. Jurisdictions that set their age at fourteen or higher — Germany, Italy, Spain, Japan, the Nordic countries at fifteen — are not collapsing into lawlessness. They respond to harmful acts by young children through family intervention, welfare services, and restorative processes, and their youth crime rates do not suffer for it.
[P1.5] The UN's child-rights bodies recommend fourteen precisely because the evidence points that way.
[P1.6] Third, note what this motion does not say. It does not say do nothing. A child who seriously harms someone can be taken into care, supervised, treated, and made to repair what they broke. What changes is that we stop pretending a ten-year-old is a miniature adult deserving prison.

[C1.1] Protecting society demands that serious crimes by children remain criminal offenses. The proposition’s arbitrary fourteen-year cutoff ignores that moral and cognitive capacity develops along a spectrum, not a cliff edge. Many children under fourteen plainly understand that stealing, assault, or arson are wrong; the criminal law already accounts for reduced culpability through sentencing, not by erasing responsibility entirely.
[C1.2] When a twelve-year-old sets a fire that destroys a neighborhood or a thirteen-year-old participates in a gang murder, the state’s first duty is to protect the public, not to shield offenders from proportionate consequence. Welfare interventions are essential but insufficient for grave harms—they lack the coercive power, the moral clarity, and the proportional response that criminal sanctions provide. Deterrence also requires clear lines: if children believe serious acts carry no criminal penalty until fourteen, the margin for preventable harm widens.
[C1.3] Consider the alternative: a ten-year-old who kills must be answerable in a way that recognizes both the act’s gravity and the child’s age. Blanket immunity achieves neither. Jurisdictions with higher ages often pair them with robust social services; the correlation with low crime rates reflects those services, not the age threshold itself. Worse, raising the age creates a perverse gap where serious offenses by children just below fourteen face no criminal consequence at all.
[C1.4] The motion’s own logic undermines its cutoff—if development is continuous, any fixed age is arbitrary, and the only consistent position is to assess capacity case by case. Lower ages with strong safeguards balance protection, proportionality, and the reality that some children, however young, commit acts that demand a criminal justice response.
