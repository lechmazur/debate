[P1.1] Hand a mother an extra few hundred dollars a month and ask her to buy a childcare slot that does not exist, a bus route nobody runs, broadband no company will lay to her road, or cancer treatment at retail prices. That is the unconditional cash proposal: a voucher for markets that are missing, broken, or priced beyond her reach. The same money builds the thing itself.
[P1.2] First, cash cannot conjure supply. Transit and broadband are networks with huge fixed costs; no individual purchases a rail line. Healthcare is plagued by adverse selection and by patients who cannot judge what they are buying. Childcare runs on thin margins and chronic shortages.
[P1.3] Pour cash into those markets and much of it leaks into higher prices and rents rather than more service. Public provision adds capacity directly, and pooled purchasing bargains prices down in a way no household ever could.
[P1.4] Second, need is not equal, so equal cheques are poorly aimed. Divide any realistic budget across every adult and each receives a modest sum. That sum is a windfall for the healthy, childless commuter with a car, and a rounding error for the family with a disabled child or a diagnosis. Services are insurance: they deliver the most value exactly where need is greatest, without means tests, because use tracks need.
[P1.5] A universal cheque gives the cancer patient and the marathon runner the same amount; a health service gives the patient chemotherapy.
[P1.6] Third, services compound. Childcare lets parents work, transit connects workers to jobs, internet opens up education, healthcare keeps people productive. They are infrastructure for earning, not just consuming.
[P1.7] The reply will be "freedom of choice." But choice means nothing without options to choose. Cash hands people a price; services hand them the thing.
