[P1.1] Antimicrobial resistance is not a distant forecast; it is a present emergency. Drug-resistant infections already kill hundreds of thousands of people each year and, on conservative estimates, could claim ten million lives annually by 2050 while draining trillions from the global economy. Yet the pipeline for new antibiotics is nearly empty, not because the science is exhausted, but because the market is fundamentally broken. We deliberately conserve new antibiotics, reserving them for cases where older drugs fail.
[P1.2] This stewardship imperative destroys the traditional pharmaceutical sales model. A company that succeeds in developing a last-resort antibiotic is paradoxically punished with low sales volume. Fewer prescriptions mean less revenue, making it impossible to recoup the billion-dollar investment required to bring a novel drug to market. The result is predictable: bankruptcy, market withdrawal, and a barren pipeline.
[P1.3] Advance market commitments repair this failure by flipping the incentive structure from volume to value. Governments or international buyers guarantee purchase of a qualifying antibiotic at a pre-agreed price, ensuring predictable revenue regardless of how sparingly the drug is used. This "pull" mechanism de-risks development and creates a viable market where none currently exists.
[P1.4] The proposition concedes that unit prices may remain high, and this is precisely why it must pass. Because stewardship requires us to use these drugs sparingly, the price per treatment course must be high enough to amortize R&D costs across a deliberately small volume. High unit prices are not market gouging; they are the mathematical necessity of funding innovation in a conservation-driven market. Demanding cheap antibiotics reproduces the broken status quo: developers exit, and physicians are left with empty cabinets when pan-resistant infections arrive.
[P1.5] The alternative to high-priced AMCs is not affordable antibiotics. It is no antibiotics at all. We cannot conserve our way out of a pipeline collapse. Only guaranteed markets with adequate pricing can secure the steady supply of new drugs that stewardship demands.
[P1.6] The choice is stark: pay developers properly today, or pay in preventable deaths tomorrow.
