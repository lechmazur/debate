[P1.1] The question is not whether land-value taxation is perfect. It is which tool does more for two things at once: getting homes built, and funding the towns that need them. On both counts, land-value taxation wins, and the other two tools actively make things worse.
[P1.2] Start with supply. An ordinary property tax punishes building: add a floor, and your bill rises. A land-value tax taxes only the land, whose value comes from location, transit and the surrounding community, not the owner's effort. Build ten apartments on a lot and your bill stays the same.
[P1.3] Leave that lot as a surface car park waiting for prices to rise and you pay exactly as much as the neighbour who built. Speculative holding becomes expensive, development becomes relatively cheaper, and underused urban land is pushed into use. Because land cannot be moved or hidden, the owner bears the tax. It cannot be passed on to tenants the way a tax on scarce buildings can.
[P1.4] Pennsylvania cities that shifted weight from buildings to land, Pittsburgh most famously, are routinely cited as seeing stronger downtown construction than comparable cities.
[P1.5] Now compare the alternatives. Transaction taxes like stamp duty tax movement itself. Empty-nesters stay in large houses rather than pay to downsize, workers turn down jobs across town, and existing stock is used worse. Their revenue swings with the housing cycle and collapses just when councils need it most.
[P1.6] That is why Australian states have begun replacing stamp duty with annual land tax.
[P1.7] Rent caps are worse still for supply. They help sitting tenants while shrinking the rental pool. Landlords convert units, sell to owner-occupiers, defer maintenance and build elsewhere. Evidence from rent-control expansions in cities like San Francisco points to reduced rental supply.
[P1.8] And rent caps raise nothing for public finance.
[P1.9] Land-value taxation offers a stable base that cannot flee and recovers value the public created. When a council builds a tram line, surrounding land gains value; land tax returns part of that gain to fund the next line. Only land-value taxation both grows supply [clipped]

[C1.2] Start with the flagship. Pittsburgh's split-rate system was scrapped around 2001 after a countywide reassessment produced bills so erratic that residents revolted. That is not incidental. A land tax requires officials to value the ground beneath a building separately from the building, in dense cities where vacant lots almost never trade.
[C1.3] The number is an estimate defended in appeal hearings, and every dollar of revenue rests on it. Property taxes and stamp duties rest on observed sale prices. Rent caps rest on a lease. Only one of these tools has a base you cannot actually see.
[C1.4] Second, land tax does not build homes where zoning forbids them. Australian states have levied land tax for generations and sit near the top of global unaffordability rankings. Denmark has taxed land for decades; Copenhagen still faces a housing crunch. The binding constraint on supply is planning permission, and a tax cannot pour foundations the council will not approve.
[C1.5] Pressuring a car-park owner is pointless if the site is zoned for a car park.
[C1.6] Third, the finance claim inverts reality. Land value is the volatile part of property value. When markets fall, buildings hold their cost while land values crash first and hardest, so a land-only base amplifies the cycle rather than smoothing it. Meanwhile transaction taxes are proven earners: stamp duty raises very large sums in the UK, Hong Kong and Singapore, and Singapore's stamp-duty surcharges on foreign and multiple buyers are the sharpest working anti-speculation instrument any city has.
[C1.7] Finally, modern rent stabilisation is not the 1970s caricature. Germany, the Netherlands and Oregon cap increases while exempting new construction, so building continues and tenants are not displaced.
[C1.8] Working tools beat a beautiful theory that fails on contact with an assessor's office.

[P2.9] So the clash is this. Land tax does both jobs, adding supply and funding councils. Transaction taxes do one badly, and rent caps do neither.
