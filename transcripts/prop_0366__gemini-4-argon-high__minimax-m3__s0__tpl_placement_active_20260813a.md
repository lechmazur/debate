[P1.1] When a major movie hits theaters, millions of people are told to wait at the door. For viewers who are deaf, blind, mobility-impaired, neurodivergent, or immunocompromised—as well as caregivers and rural residents—theatrical exclusivity means cultural exclusion. Cinemas have had decades to fix accessibility, yet the reality remains dismal: clunky captioning glasses that frequently fail, dead audio-description headsets, sensory overload, and rare open-caption screenings exiled to midweek mornings. At home, accessibility can be seamless and dignified, offering customizable subtitles, reliable audio descriptions, and full environmental control.
[P1.2] Telling these audiences to wait months for a home release locks them out of the cultural moment. Big movies are communal events. By the time a delayed title finally reaches living rooms, the public conversation has moved on and every twist has been spoiled. Crucially, rejecting theatrical-first as the near-universal default does not mean abolishing cinemas.
[P1.3] It means recognizing that not every big-budget comedy, drama, or franchise release requires a mandatory theatrical quarantine before disabled or homebound viewers are allowed to participate.
[P1.4] Abandoning this rigid default also makes economic sense. Studios pour immense fortunes into marketing campaigns designed to peak on opening weekend, yet deliberately turn away paying customers who cannot attend a theater. By the time the home window opens months later, that marketing momentum has evaporated, forcing studios to spend again or lose viewers to piracy and apathy. Simultaneous release—especially through premium home pricing—monetizes peak hype across the entire population at once, yielding high-margin digital revenue that studios do not have to split down the middle with theater chains.
[P1.5] Theaters will not die if studios release more big titles simultaneously. A living room sofa does not replace a giant screen, booming sound, or a cheering crowd for people who want a night out. But if the theatrical default can only be justified by holding culture hostage from people who need accessible home viewing, that default does not deserve protection. Studios should meet the whole audience where they are.
