[P1.2] Housing obeys the oldest law in economics: when demand rises and supply cannot follow, prices rise. For decades, America's most productive cities made the obvious response—duplexes, fourplexes, small apartment buildings near jobs and transit—illegal on most residential land. Legalizing density two decades earlier wouldn't have caused one construction boom; it would have started twenty years of compounding. Housing is a durable stock.

[P1.4] We don't have to imagine this counterfactual; we can observe it. Houston, with famously light land-use rules, absorbed massive population growth while staying far cheaper than coastal metros. [P1.5] Same country, same interest rates, same lumber prices—different rules, different prices.
[P1.6] Density also helps renters beyond its own walls through moving chains: a new apartment lets one household vacate an older, cheaper unit, which another household takes, cascading down the price ladder. Blocking construction at the top raises rents at the bottom.
[P1.7] Note the motion's modest standard: more affordable, not perfectly affordable. We need only show two decades of legal supply would have bent the curve. CON will blame investors, mortgage rates, or construction costs—but those forces are national, while the crisis is concentrated where building was banned. The ban was the variable.
