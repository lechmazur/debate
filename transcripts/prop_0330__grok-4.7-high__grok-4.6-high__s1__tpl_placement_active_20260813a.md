[P1.1] Live sports broadcasts gather families, adolescents, and casual fans who came for the contest, not a wager. A sports-betting ad in that window does more than sell: it drops an immediate betting prompt into the emotional peak of the event—after a score, before the next play—and ties fandom to the next stake. Casino, poker, and lottery ads rarely enjoy that real-time lockstep with mixed-age mass viewing. Governments should therefore restrict sports-betting advertising during live sports more aggressively than other gambling ads.
[P1.2] Three differences justify the distinction. First, audience. Prime sports slots remain among the last shared broadcasts. Children watch with parents; teens follow clubs they idolize.
[P1.3] The ad reaches people who never sought gambling content and who are below or near legal betting age. Other gambling ads are easier to confine to late hours, adult channels, or self-selected sites.
[P1.4] Second, mechanism. In-play markets turn the break into a sales floor timed to adrenaline. The product invites rapid, repeated bets while the viewer is already aroused and socially primed to care about the result. Season-long repetition, plus odds in commentary and on-screen graphics, makes wagering feel like part of the sport rather than an optional extra.
[P1.5] A static casino spot cannot match that potency.
[P1.6] Third, harm. Where sports-betting ads have saturated broadcasts, youth and young-adult betting participation and harm have risen alongside them. Odds are framed as information, free bets as fan rewards, and knowledgeable support as a reason to stake money. Equal limits would treat the highest-potency channel like lower-potency ones and leave the most exposed viewers under-protected.
[P1.7] Tighter rules—fewer in-game spots, no live odds, watersheds that actually cover junior viewing—do not ban adult gambling or demand identical caps on every gambling ad. They match restriction to risk. The live broadcast is the distinctive danger; the rule should be distinctive too.
