[P1.1] Moviegoing has always derived its power from being a democratic cultural pastime. It is where a community gathers to share a story, accessible to teenagers, working families, and casual viewers alike. That social compact breaks down when theater chains allow premium large-format screens to aggressively crowd out standard-price screenings.
[P1.2] The scope of this debate is precise: this is not an indictment of premium technology. Enhanced projection and immersive sound are valuable options. The harm arises when multiplexes assign the overwhelming majority of peak-hour showtimes exclusively to premium auditoriums. In that scenario, consumers are not making a voluntary choice to upgrade; they are subjected to an artificial bottleneck and a forced upsell.
[P1.3] When the only standard-price showing on a Friday night is buried at 4:15 PM or 11:00 PM, everyday moviegoers are effectively excluded from the cultural conversation. For a family of four, an additional five-to-eight-dollar surcharge per ticket instantly transforms a routine weekend outing into a prohibitive luxury expense.
[P1.4] This practice does not just punish price-sensitive patrons; it endangers the long-term survival of cinema itself. The theatrical industry relies on volume, repeat attendance, and concession sales fueled by broad habits. Chasing short-term per-screen yield by prioritizing higher ticket prices risks driving casual and budget-conscious viewers away entirely. When regular screenings are squeezed out, ordinary patrons do not simply surrender and pay the premium indefinitely—they stay home and wait for streaming.
[P1.5] Conditioning the public to view movie theaters as luxury venues accelerates the erosion of the habitual audience base.
[P1.6] Blockbusters are meant to be widely shared cultural events, not gatekept commodities. Theaters can celebrate premium formats without cannibalizing basic accessibility. When high-surcharge auditoriums dominate the schedule to the point of displacing affordable options, cinema ceases to be popular entertainment and becomes an exclusionary tollbooth.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that the only meaningful access to a blockbuster is a cheap, peak-hour seat in a standard auditorium. That is too narrow. The real question is whether moviegoers are better served by a schedule that responds to demand or by a rule that preserves inexpensive seats even when audiences are choosing something else. Premium large formats dominate blockbuster scheduling when patrons are willing to pay for brighter lasers, larger screens, and better sound.
[C1.2] That is not an artificial bottleneck; it is a market signal. If chains ignored that signal and locked screens into standard showings, they would be scheduling empty rooms while lines formed downstairs.
[C1.3] PRO’s harm story also treats ordinary moviegoers as one fixed group with one fixed budget. In practice, ordinary viewers sort themselves. A family might choose a Saturday matinee, a subscription discount, a standard screening of a different film, or split the difference: one event movie in premium, others standard. The availability of premium formats does not erase standard access; it cross-subsidizes it.
[C1.4] Blockbuster economics are risky. Higher-margin premium tickets help theaters afford more screens, more staff, longer runs, and the occasional smaller film that needs standard auditoriums. If you cap premium scheduling, you do not protect the casual viewer in the long run. You reduce revenue, weaken the theatrical window, and make studios more eager to skip theaters and send films straight to streaming.
[C1.5] Most importantly, the proposition would punish success. If an IMAX or Dolby showing is full because audiences want that experience, replacing it with standard showtimes to satisfy an access formula gives people fewer of the screenings they actually chose. The right response to high demand is more showings, more locations, and flexible pricing—not a mandate that premium formats stay small so low-price seats look abundant. Cinemas remain popular when they offer choices, not when regulators or chains artificially freeze the schedule at a cheaper past.
