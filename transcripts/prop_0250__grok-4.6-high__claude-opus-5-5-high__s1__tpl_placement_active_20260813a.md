[P1.1] Low turnout is not neutrality. It is a filter. The people who skip elections are younger, poorer, more transient, and less attached to parties than those who show up. The government that follows is therefore not the public in miniature.
[P1.2] It is a biased sample with constitutional power. That bias is the quiet failure of voluntary voting, and stable democracies should close it.
[P1.3] Civic life already treats some participation as duty, not hobby. You can be fined for ignoring a jury summons or a census form. Voting is more foundational than either. A proportional civil penalty—scaled like a parking ticket, never jail, never loss of rights—simply says you may not stay off the roll while remaining inside the polity.
[P1.4] Australia has lived with this model for generations: participation near-universal, governments still change hands, the ballot still secret. Parties cannot ignore the unenthusiastic majority, because that majority still has to mark a paper. The result is a legislature closer to the actual adult population, not a harvest of the already mobilized.
[P1.5] The liberty objection treats abstention as a sacred negative right. It is not. Non-voters still obey the laws written by those who did turn up. A small civil penalty does not dictate the choice.
[P1.6] Informal and blank ballots preserve protest. What it forbids is free-riding on everyone else’s civic labor while the electorate shrinks to the loud and the leisured. In a stable democracy with secret ballots and competitive parties, that modest compulsion is cheaper than the distortion it removes. If “the people” only means those with time and intensity to appear, representation is already broken.
