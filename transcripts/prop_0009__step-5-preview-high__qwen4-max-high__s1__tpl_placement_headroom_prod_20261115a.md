[P1.1] The fifteen-day line is not arbitrary. It marks the point where isolation stops being a short-term management tool and becomes a deliberate campaign against the human mind. Solitary confinement beyond that threshold inflicts predictable psychological damage: disorientation, hallucinations, depression, suicidal ideation, and permanent cognitive impairment. These are not side effects; they are the mechanism.
[P1.2] International human-rights standards have long recognized this boundary. The Nelson Mandela Rules, the global benchmark for prisoner treatment, treat prolonged solitary confinement as a practice that can amount to torture or cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment. The fifteen-day threshold is the line where the practice becomes indiscriminate, not targeted, and destructive.
[P1.3] A prisoner held alone for twenty, forty, or two hundred days loses the ability to regulate emotion, perceive time, and trust reality. The state does not need to touch the prisoner to inflict this. It simply builds a box and keeps them there long enough. That is not discipline; that is psychological torture by design.
[P1.4] Opponents will argue that solitary is necessary for safety. But safety does not require months of total isolation. Short-term segregation for acute threats, with strict caps, regular human contact, and judicial oversight, can manage risk without crossing into torture. What the proposition forbids is not temporary separation; it is indefinite psychological destruction.
[P1.5] The burden is on those who defend this practice to show why more than fifteen days of solitary confinement is compatible with human dignity. They cannot. The state that locks a human being alone past that point is not protecting society; it is manufacturing madness. Abolishing solitary confinement beyond fifteen consecutive days is not a concession to prisoners; it is a defense of our shared humanity against the corrupting logic of total control.
