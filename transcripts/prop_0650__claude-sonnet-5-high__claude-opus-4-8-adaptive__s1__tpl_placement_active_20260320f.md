[P1.1] Type 2 diabetes and prediabetes together affect roughly a third of American adults, and most of them do not know it until organ damage has already begun. That single fact is the crack in the CON case that this debate will turn on: the harm of "not knowing" is not hypothetical anxiety, it is silent, cumulative, and expensive. Continuous glucose monitors give ordinary people a window into a process that is otherwise invisible until it's a diagnosis.
[P1.2] Here is the mechanism that wins this debate: glucose spikes are not evenly distributed across foods or people. The same bowl of rice that barely moves one person's blood sugar can spike another's by eighty points, and repeated spikes drive insulin resistance years before any lab test flags it. A CGM turns this from an abstract risk into a concrete, personal data point. Someone sees that their afternoon energy crash follows a sugary breakfast, adjusts, and the adjustment compounds daily.
[P1.3] That is public health working exactly as it should: cheap, individualized, preventive feedback replacing an expensive, reactive medical system that only intervenes once damage is done.
[P1.4] Compare the two failure modes at stake. Over-medicalization means someone gets mildly anxious about a normal post-meal blip and maybe overreacts for a while. That is a soft, self-correcting cost—people adapt, apps add context, clinicians calibrate expectations, exactly as happened with home blood pressure cuffs and step counters, both of which caused temporary hand-wringing and then became normalized tools of everyday prevention. Undiagnosed metabolic decline, by contrast, is a hard, compounding cost: it ends in insulin, dialysis, amputations, blindness.
[P1.5] These are not symmetrical risks. A behavior nudge that occasionally overshoots into worry is trivially cheaper to fix than a decade of undetected insulin resistance.
[P1.6] The proposition doesn't require CGMs to be perfect or anxiety-free. It only requires that the net public health effect be positive. Given the scale of undiagnosed prediabetes, the low cost of behavioral course-correction, and the historical pattern of self-monitoring technologies settling into normal life, that bar is easily cleared.
