[P1.1] When a platform receives credible warnings that users are coordinating threats, circulating someone’s home address, and encouraging strangers to confront them, doing nothing should not be a legally costless choice. The duty should be reasonable prevention—not a guarantee that nobody will ever be harmed.
[P1.2] The trigger must be evidence of coordinated harassment creating a foreseeable risk of offline harm, not merely controversy or an offended user. Platforms should take proportionate measures within their control: investigate credible reports, interrupt threatening coordination, restrict the spread of targeting information, and preserve evidence. Responsibility should depend on what they knew or reasonably should have known, their capacity to act, and whether their response was reasonable. That is a duty of care, not automatic liability for users’ misconduct.
[P1.3] Why impose it? Because platforms have capabilities victims and police lack. A targeted person sees incoming abuse; the platform can identify connected accounts, repeated targeting, and the distribution mechanisms accelerating a campaign. Police can pursue individual offenders, but that does not necessarily interrupt a swarm before someone arrives at the victim’s door.
[P1.4] Punishing attackers afterward and requiring reasonable platform precautions beforehand are complements, not alternatives.
[P1.5] Voluntary moderation leaves that prevention dependent on corporate priorities. Intervention costs money, while much of the cost of inaction falls on the target: security expenses, displacement, and fear of physical attack. A legal duty makes platforms account for risks they can meaningfully reduce rather than externalize.
[P1.6] The strongest objection is over-removal. That argues for careful legislation, not immunity from responsibility. Protect criticism, protest, and collective advocacy explicitly; require documented reasons and appeals; and assess reasonable precautions rather than reward blanket deletion. Coordination alone is not harassment, and harassment alone does not establish foreseeable offline harm.
[P1.7] The choice is not safety versus speech. A person driven offline by credible threats has lost practical freedom to speak. Requiring reasonable action against foreseeable danger protects participation without making platforms insurers of everything users do.
