[P1.1] When a streaming service tells a performer their residual check depends on "audience engagement" or "hours viewed," and then refuses to show them the underlying numbers, that is not a business arrangement — it is a one-way ledger where only one party can see the books. That asymmetry is the entire case for this resolution.
[P1.2] Start with the basic contract logic. If compensation is contingent on a metric, the metric must be auditable by the party being paid on it. This is not a radical idea; it is how royalties already work in music, publishing, and traditional syndicated television. A songwriter can request a royalty statement and, if suspicious, demand an audit.
[P1.3] A novelist sees sales figures. Streaming has broken this norm by inventing reach-based pay structures — "residuals" pegged to hours streamed, or bonus pools tied to top-ten placement — while keeping the measurement apparatus proprietary. Performers such as those affected by recent strikes have testified that they received bonus payments with no explanation of how the number was calculated, no comparison data, and no way to know if a colleague on a similar show got paid fairly. That is not compensation; that is a gift dressed up as a wage.
[P1.4] Second, standardization solves a real market failure. Right now every platform uses different, self-defined metrics — "views," "hours viewed," "completion rate" — chosen by the same company that profits from making them look flattering in public and murky in private negotiation. Without a shared standard, filmmakers cannot even compare offers across platforms, which suppresses their bargaining power industry-wide. A required, standardized data-sharing regime does not dictate creative decisions or cap profits — it simply ensures the number attached to your paycheck is the same number the company uses internally, and that it means the same thing from platform to platform.
[P1.5] Third, think about the alternative CON must defend: trust the payer to self-report the metric that determines your pay, with no verification. No functioning labor market operates that way, and none should have to.

[C1.1] The proposition conflates two separate claims: that parties whose pay depends on a metric deserve access to that metric, and that government should mandate a single standardized measurement regime across all platforms. The first is defensible. The second is where this policy breaks down — and the second is exactly what the mandate demands.
[C1.2] The real-world mechanism for the first claim already exists. Collective bargaining agreements, like those recently negotiated following major industry strikes, can and do include audit rights and data disclosure requirements. Individual contracts can require methodological transparency as a condition of compensation. These are the appropriate tools because they are targeted — they give the affected party access to their specific deal's underlying data without requiring every platform to adopt identical measurement frameworks designed by regulators who don't understand the underlying technology.
[C1.3] The standardization requirement is the hidden cost PRO isn't addressing. What counts as a "view" on a short-form mobile platform is structurally different from what it means for a prestige drama rewatched over months. Platforms compete partly by developing better, more meaningful metrics. Legislating a common standard locks in today's crude proxies — almost certainly "hours viewed" — and raises the compliance cost of adopting more accurate ones tomorrow.
[C1.4] You don't improve measurement by freezing it in regulatory amber.
[C1.5] There is also genuine competitive harm. Detailed viewing data is a trade secret that shapes content acquisition, pricing, and platform strategy. A mandatory disclosure regime does not stay neatly contained to one filmmaker's residual calculation; it creates systematic exposure that competitors and bad actors can exploit. That chills investment in original content — the precise opposite of what PRO intends.
[C1.6] PRO's strongest move would be to argue that existing mechanisms have failed in practice — that contract audit rights and collective bargaining simply haven't worked. They haven't explained that failure. Until they do, this mandate is asking regulators to redesign a watch because someone forgot to wind it.
