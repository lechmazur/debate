[P1.1] Reparations are the difference between confessing a debt and paying it. The countries in question did not merely commit moral offenses. They seized land, extracted enslaved labor, taxed colonized peoples, and converted those gains into durable infrastructure, financial institutions, and private wealth. The transfer did not stop with abolition.
[P1.2] Haiti, founded by the enslaved, was forced to pay France for generations for its own independence; when Britain abolished slavery, the state compensated enslavers, not the enslaved. The metropoles kept the original gains, while descendant communities inherited dispossession, debt, and underinvestment.
[P1.3] This is why symbolic apologies cannot be the main response. An apology may acknowledge the wound, but it leaves the balance sheet intact. It asks descendants to accept remorse from the same governments and institutions that still hold the benefit. Direct programs do what words cannot: they move resources back.
[P1.4] They need not be a single universal check. They can include dedicated health and education funds, housing and business capital, land restitution or community trusts, debt relief, and targeted payments where eligibility can be established through descendant status and community records.
[P1.5] The objection that reparations are complex is not an objection to the duty. It is a scheduling problem. The same states built tax systems, property registries, and bureaucracies to manage extraction; they can build systems to manage repair. Cost is likewise a weak defense.
[P1.6] If the obligation is genuinely large, that confirms the scale of the original enrichment, not the absence of a remedy. Where states cannot pay all at once, they can phase funded programs over time—but the commitment must be material, not ceremonial.
[P1.7] Apologies may accompany reparations. But a wrongdoer that returns only words while keeping the proceeds has not repaired the harm, has not discharged the debt, and leaves the original injustice compounding.
