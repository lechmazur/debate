[P1.1] A parking spot that sits empty most of the day serves two cars. That same space set with tables serves dozens of neighbors, supports a payroll, and keeps a block alive after dark. Cities learned that during the pandemic, and they should not unlearn it now.
[P1.2] Outdoor dining rescued thousands of small restaurants when indoor seating collapsed. Rents did not fall, labor did not get cheaper, margins did not get easier. Allowing sheds, patios, and sidewalk tables to continue is the simplest economic lifeline a city can offer: no subsidy, no new bureaucracy, just permission to use public land productively. Those tables mean servers, cooks, dishwashers with hours, landlords paid, sales taxes collected, and vacant storefronts avoided.
[P1.3] The alternative is nostalgia dressed up as order. Pre-pandemic sidewalk rules treated the street as a corridor for cars and a storage lot for parked vehicles, while pedestrians squeezed past. That was not neutral; it was a choice to prioritize metal boxes over people and small business. Flexible use corrects that misallocation.
[P1.4] A lively sidewalk with diners is safer, more walkable, and more welcoming than a row of bumpers and meters.
[P1.5] None of this requires chaos. Flexibility is not anarchy. Cities can charge reasonable fees, require wheelchair clearance, set closing times, enforce trash and noise rules, and remove bad actors. What they should not do is revert to a blanket no because some nearby residents prefer utter quiet.
[P1.6] Downtown and mixed-use living has always meant some bustle; no one has a veto over shared space.
[P1.7] Public sidewalks belong to the public — workers, families, elderly neighbors who enjoy eating outside, young people without backyards. Let them eat.

[C1.3] More customers per square foot is not a public good.

They kept a shared corridor from being captured by whoever could profit first.
[C1.7] Clear sidewalks belong to people who are not buying dinner. Keep that as the baseline. Allow dining only where access, safety, and livability are actually met.
