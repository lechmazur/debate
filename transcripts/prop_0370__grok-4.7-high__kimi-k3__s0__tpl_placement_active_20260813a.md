[P1.1] A personal phone is not a company asset. It holds family photos, private messages, medical appointments, and the ordinary record of a life outside work. When a large employer requires a work messaging or tracking app on that phone as a routine condition of employment, it converts private property into an extension of the workplace—without paying for the device, without bounding the intrusion, and without a genuine choice.
[P1.2] The expectation fails on three grounds.
[P1.3] Consent is illusory. A condition of getting or keeping a job is not a voluntary download. Large employers hold the leverage; workers who refuse risk lost income, shifts, or advancement. "Bring your own device" is not optional when refusal is costly.
[P1.4] The intrusion does not stay inside work hours. Messaging apps train people to expect replies at dinner, on weekends, and during leave. Tracking apps go further: location and movement can follow a worker off the clock, through errands and home. Permissions and culture expand the employer's reach even when the stated purpose is narrow.
[P1.5] A warehouse associate should not have an evening location exposed because a scheduling tool shares a phone with a child's school messages.
[P1.6] Large employers have alternatives. They can issue company phones, use web access that does not require a persistent install, or confine monitoring to employer-owned devices on the job. Convenience does not justify the privacy cost, the collapsed boundary, or the risk of mixing corporate access with a device the worker cannot segregate. Once installed, these apps often outlast the job—residual notifications, lingering permissions, and data the worker struggles to purge.
[P1.7] Employment already claims time and labor. It should not also claim the pocket. Large employers should not demand these apps on personal phones as a routine condition of employment.

[C1.1] Employment is a bundle of conditions—schedule, dress, location, tools—and workers accept or reject the bundle against the pay. A known requirement to install a scheduling or messaging app is one term in that bundle, not a trespass. Consent is not "illusory" [P1.3] merely because declining a job offer is costly; by that logic the commute is coerced too. And labor markets discipline employers: a firm whose app demands genuinely burden workers pays in turnover and wages.
[C1.2] Second, the phone stays the worker's. Modern operating systems sandbox every app: location can be denied, background activity killed, notifications silenced after hours, permissions revoked, the app deleted when the job ends. The warehouse associate in [P1.5] decides whether a scheduling tool ever sees location; an employer cannot reach through the glass uninvited. What PRO calls inevitable surveillance is mostly a settings menu.
[C1.3] Third, these apps serve workers, not just managers: shift-swap boards, safety alerts, two-factor logins, a schedule change seen at home instead of discovered after a wasted drive. That is why they spread. And the alternative scales badly—asking a large employer to issue a second device to thousands of workers multiplies cost and waste for privacy the worker could get from a toggle.
[C1.4] Fourth, watch what PRO's alternative actually does. A company-issued phone is fully owned and fully monitorable—every app, every byte. A personal phone running one sandboxed app is the least invasive architecture available. Banning the personal-phone condition pushes employers toward devices they control end to end.
[C1.5] PRO protects privacy into deeper surveillance.
[C1.6] Finally, proportionality. The motion condemns a lightweight messaging app with the same brush as constant GPS tracking [P1.6]. Real abuses—off-clock demands, creeping permissions—have remedies: right-to-disconnect norms, permission limits, pay for on-call time. The proposition declares a routine, revocable, mutually useful condition categorically illegitimate, and its cure surveils more than the disease.
