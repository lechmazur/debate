[P1.1] A corporation has no conscience and no vote; it has a treasury filled with other people's money. When a bank, a defence contractor or a pharmaceutical firm funds the politicians who write its rules, no shareholder, depositor or patient was consulted. That is not expression. [P1.2] Notice what the motion actually restricts: payments into politicians' campaign accounts. It does not silence anyone. [P1.3] Corruption here rarely looks like an envelope of cash. It looks like dependence. [C1.1] The proposition assumes that restricting donations will shrink corporate influence. Experience suggests it mainly relocates it. When direct gifts are banned, the money does not vanish; it moves to less visible channels: independent advertising, social-media campaigns, think tanks, lobbyists, and grassroots operations that parties cannot control and voters cannot easily trace. A regulated donation to a known campaign is at least public, attributable, and answerable.
[C1.2] An unregulated pressure campaign run by an unnamed network is not. Its own bank-account theory ignores that banned donors will spend upstream, where accounting is weaker and coordination is easier to disguise.
[C1.3] The same danger follows from sharply lower caps on large individual donors. Small donors matter, but they are rarely enough to launch challengers, fund rapid-response teams, or compete with an incumbent’s built-in advantages. If the law makes it harder for a candidate to raise substantial lawful support, the strongest beneficiaries are those who already have name recognition, state resources, or friendly outside megaphones. Campaign finance limits often become incumbency protection dressed as reform.
[C1.4] There is also a liberty problem. A donation is not just a transaction; it is a way of joining with others to amplify a shared political conviction. A union member, shareholder, or small-business owner may disagree with the recipient, but the association speaks with pooled resources. Banning that pooling treats citizens as too fragile to hear well-funded arguments.
[C1.5] The better answer is not prohibition but transparency: immediate disclosure, strict reporting, independent auditing, and clear penalties for covert coordination. Voters can judge who is funding a message only when the funding is forced into the open. The proposition chooses hidden influence, incumbent advantage, and weaker political competition over accountable participation. For that reason, the motion should fail.
