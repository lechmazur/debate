[P1.1] Let me be clear about what "weaker" means: when two moral claims genuinely conflict, the weaker one yields. My case is that national loyalty, whenever it collides with the welfare of human beings as such, is the claim that must give way. That is precisely what it means to be weaker.
[P1.2] Start with the foundation. National loyalty rests on a morally arbitrary fact: where you happened to be born. You did not choose it, you did not earn it, and it says nothing about the worth of the people inside or outside those lines. Duties to universal human welfare rest on something non-arbitrary: that other people can suffer, flourish, live, and die, exactly as you can.
[P1.3] When a moral claim is built on an accident of geography and another is built on the capacity to suffer, the second is the more fundamental.
[P1.4] Second, look at what we actually believe when the two collide. The Nuremberg principle—that "I was serving my nation" is no defense for atrocity—is not a fringe view. It is the settled moral verdict of the modern world. Loyalty to country cannot license torturing prisoners, bombing civilians, or turning away the drowning.
[P1.5] The moment national loyalty demands cruelty to human beings, we all recognize it must yield. That is the weaker claim losing to the stronger one.
[P1.6] Third, national loyalty is defensible only because, and only when, it serves human welfare. Solidarity, cooperation, mutual protection—these are good because they make human lives go better. Strip out that service and blind loyalty becomes mere tribalism. A value that borrows its entire legitimacy from a deeper value is, by definition, subordinate to it.
[P1.7] So the structure is simple. Universal welfare is the ground; national loyalty is an instrument. Instruments answer to purposes, not the reverse. My opponent must show a case where loyalty rightly overrides human welfare itself—not merely accompanies it.
[P1.8] I don't think that case exists.
