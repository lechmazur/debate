[P1.1] Companionship is not a staffing gap that machines can close. What eases isolation in later life is being known by another person who can be surprised, wrong, moved, and held accountable. Hospitals and care providers should refuse to replace most of that human presence with AI or robotic companions, even when shifts are thin.
[P1.2] A device can prompt a story, play a song, or score a mood. It cannot owe the resident anything. It cannot choose to stay. When most of the day’s social contact runs through a script or a sensor, the older adult is no longer among persons; they are inside a managed loop.
[P1.3] Loneliness is not low interaction volume. It is the felt absence of regard. Simulated regard can hide that absence without repairing it, and it gives institutions cover to thin out the very staff time residents still need for fear at night, meals, and the small recognitions that keep a life intact.
[P1.4] Tight staffing is a reason to protect human time for companionship, not to outsource it. Substitution does not merely fill a hole. It changes what care is allowed to be. Once most companionship is automated, remaining staff are pulled toward tasks the machine cannot do, and the relational core of the work is treated as optional.
[P1.5] Dignity is not preserved by a polite voice that never tires. It is preserved when another living person remembers a grandchild’s name, notices a change that no dashboard flagged, and answers for how the day went.
[P1.6] Narrow tools—reminders, mobility aids, monitoring that frees a nurse to sit and talk—do not cross this line. Replacing most companionship does. It trades persons for throughput and calls the trade care. Providers should not make that trade.
