[C1.1] The total ban is the wrong remedy. It replaces a flexible price signal with a blunt prohibition that forces everyone into the same rule regardless of how replaceable their car is. Congestion pricing targets the actual harm—excess driving and delay—while leaving people able to make urgent, off-peak, or caregiving trips. An outright ban removes that judgment from the person making the trip and hands it to a permit office.
[C1.2] The fairness objection cuts the other way. Pricing can be made fairer through lower off-peak charges, income-based discounts, and transit investment. A ban cannot be refunded. It tells the night-shift worker with no bus at 3 a.m. and the wealthy commuter alike that a private car is forbidden.
[C1.3] But the wealthy commuter can switch to a taxi, ride-hail, or a flat nearby; the worker loses the trip or risks a fine. PRO’s own concern about hired cars is worse under a ban: if private cars are excluded but taxis and ride-hail are not, the center still fills with paid motor traffic, just not owner-driven cars.
[C1.4] A city center also cannot function without some motorized access. Ambulances, waste collection, construction, paratransit, small businesses moving stock, and caregivers all require vehicles. A ban does not remove cars; it converts them into a permit system where officials decide what counts as essential. That invites arbitrary boundaries, costly enforcement, and loopholes.
[C1.5] Congestion pricing achieves the main goal—fewer low-value private-car trips—without requiring a waiver for every legitimate need.
[C1.6] The objective is less harmful driving, not criminalizing the private car. Pricing produces revenue for transit, walking, and cycling and can be paired with street redesign. The ban is symbolically cleaner but operationally worse. It would be less equitable, less adaptable, and no safer for buses than a pricing regime that actually manages demand.
