[P1.1] The right test is not whether more cars can briefly move faster. It is whether a city gives people better access to jobs, homes, and services per public dollar, without sacrificing the neighborhoods that make those destinations valuable. By that test, removal should usually beat widening in dense urban corridors.
[P1.2] Widening treats congestion as a shortage of concrete. But new urban capacity releases suppressed demand: drivers change routes and travel times, households and firms locate around the added capacity, and the road refills. Meanwhile, the costs are permanent—construction disruption, maintenance, noise, pollution, crash exposure, and often demolished homes or businesses. A wider freeway can therefore leave a city with the same peak-hour queue and less city around it.
[P1.3] That is not a mobility strategy; it is repeatedly buying temporary relief with lasting damage.
[P1.4] Removal attacks a different liability. Urban freeways consume scarce central land and divide street networks, making short trips longer on foot, by bicycle, and by bus. Replacing an obsolete or redundant segment with connected streets, housing, parks, and transit can turn infrastructure that mainly passes through a neighborhood into infrastructure that serves it. Portland’s removal of Harbor Drive and San Francisco’s removal of the damaged Embarcadero Freeway illustrate the basic opportunity: waterfront land became usable civic space rather than remaining underneath a traffic structure.
[P1.5] These are not universal templates, but they refute the assumption that removing an urban freeway must produce paralysis.
[P1.6] Traffic displacement is real, so removal should be phased and paired with transit, signal changes, demand management, and protection of essential freight routes. Yet those same tools are needed after widening when demand returns. The honest comparison is not removal with no preparation versus a frictionless new lane; it is managed network change versus another expensive expansion of the same bottleneck.
[P1.7] Some genuine bottlenecks justify widening, and some freeways remain essential. The motion requires no ban. It asks which intervention cities should choose more often. In land-constrained urban areas, removal can create access and durable value; widening usually consumes both to chase speed.
