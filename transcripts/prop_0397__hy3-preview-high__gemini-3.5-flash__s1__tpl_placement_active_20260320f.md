[P1.1] The current bankruptcy framework treats student debt as uniquely untouchable, requiring borrowers to clear an almost insurmountable "undue hardship" bar to discharge even when they face permanent, incurable inability to repay. A kitchen worker left quadriplegic after an accident, a parent forced to exit the workforce permanently to care for a severely disabled child, a factory worker whose industry evaporates with no transferable skills: all remain saddled with student debt decades after they lose any realistic path to repayment. This is a wholesale betrayal of bankruptcy’s core purpose: providing a fresh start for people crushed by unrepayable debt.
[P1.2] It forces borrowers to deplete retirement accounts, skip needed medical care, and live in poverty to service debt they can never escape.
[P1.3] When long-term inability to repay is proven—meaning no realistic prospect of repayment for five or more years, not temporary hardship—discharge should be automatic, not subject to years of costly litigation. Lenders already price in default risk for all consumer debt; a narrow carveout for permanent non-repayment does not destabilize the student loan market. Federal loans are set by statute, not market whims, so rate hikes are a fringe concern at best. Private lenders would face only marginal added risk, far outweighed by the societal benefit of freeing trapped borrowers to participate in the economy instead of diverting every spare dollar to debt they can never erase.
[P1.4] Trapping people in lifelong student debt for circumstances entirely outside their control is both unjust and economically inefficient. Bankruptcy law exists to clear unpayable obligations, not to create a permanent underclass of debtors. Easing discharge for those with long-term repayment inability aligns the law with its original mission, while causing negligible harm to lenders or future borrowers. Current discharge rules only punish unlucky borrowers for factors they could not anticipate, with no offsetting public benefit.

[C1.1] Student loans are fundamentally different from any other form of consumer credit. When a borrower defaults on a home or a car, the physical asset can be repossessed. But a university degree—the knowledge, credentials, and lifelong earning potential it unlocks—cannot be foreclosed upon or returned to a lender. Because this asset remains permanently with the borrower, bankruptcy law must maintain a high threshold for discharge.
[C1.2] Making it "much easier" to walk away from student debt creates a massive moral hazard, encouraging strategic defaults from borrowers who have already captured the lifelong benefits of their education.
[C1.3] Furthermore, this is not a victimless adjustment. Over ninety percent of student loan debt is issued or guaranteed by the federal government. When a borrower discharges their student loans in bankruptcy, the debt does not vanish; the burden is simply transferred directly to the American taxpayer. Relaxing discharge standards transforms a loan program designed for self-advancement into an unchecked, taxpayer-funded indemnity for private career choices.
[C1.4] This drastic step is also entirely unnecessary. The federal student loan system already possesses a highly compassionate, built-in safety net: Income-Driven Repayment (IDR) plans. Under these programs, borrowers with low or non-existent incomes see their monthly payments reduced to zero dollars. After a set period of years, the remaining balance is forgiven entirely.
[C1.5] IDR programs protect struggling borrowers without the severe, long-term credit damage that accompanies bankruptcy.
[C1.6] Finally, the unintended consequences of this policy would fall hardest on future students. If discharge becomes easy, private lenders will restrict credit, and the political consensus supporting the federal loan program will erode. The result will be higher interest rates and tighter borrowing standards, slamming the door of higher education in the faces of low-income students who rely on accessible financing for social mobility. We must protect the integrity of the system, not dismantle it.
