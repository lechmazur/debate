[P1.1] The motion sets a presumption, not a mandate: schools provide accessible materials by default, unless a *strong* pedagogical reason proves otherwise. That narrow carveout already defangs the most common CON objection—that mandatory recordings stifle in-person engagement. A seminar where live discussion is the core learning outcome qualifies for an exemption. A 300-person intro lecture, where most students can’t ask questions in real time, does not.
[P1.2] This is not a demand to retrofit every legacy course overnight, but a baseline for standard instructional practice.
[P1.3] Accessible materials are not a niche accommodation for a small subset of students. Captions help English language learners parse fast-paced speech. Lecture recordings let students with caregiving shifts, part-time jobs, or chronic illness review material they couldn’t attend live. Transcripts of recorded lectures help neurodivergent students who struggle to take notes while tracking a speaker.
[P1.4] These are not edge cases—they describe a third or more of a typical student body.
[P1.5] The default also cuts administrative bloat. Right now, schools field hundreds of individual accommodation requests per term, each requiring verification, back-and-forth with instructors, and ad-hoc material creation. A default system eliminates that redundant labor for the vast majority of cases, freeing staff to focus on students with truly unique needs that the default can’t meet. This is especially true for underfunded schools, where accessibility staff are already stretched thin, and ad-hoc accommodations drain resources that could go to direct student support.
[P1.6] The only valid counter is a proven, specific pedagogical harm that outweighs these gains. That is exactly what the motion’s carveout already allows. The presumption is a baseline of equity, not a straitjacket for instructors.
