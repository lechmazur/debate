[P1.1] Central banks have exactly one tool that works reliably and predictably over a three-year horizon: control of the money supply and interest rates to manage inflation expectations. They do not have a reliable tool for fine-tuning employment without inflating away the public's trust in the currency. That asymmetry is the whole case for PRO.
[P1.2] Start with the lesson of the 1970s. When the Fed and other central banks tried to run the economy "a little hot" to keep unemployment low, they did not get a permanent employment gain. They got stagflation: unemployment rose anyway, inflation became entrenched, and it took a brutal Volcker-style disinflation with much higher unemployment than if they had acted early. The Phillips curve tradeoff that policymakers thought they were exploiting did not hold once expectations adjusted.
[P1.3] That is the central mechanism PRO relies on: inflation that is allowed to drift above target does not buy you durable jobs, it buys you a worse recession later to fix it.
[P1.4] Second, consider who absorbs the cost of each error. Letting inflation run above target is a regressive tax: it hits wage earners on fixed contracts, pensioners, and savers hardest, while asset holders who can reprice quickly are shielded. A "modest rise in unemployment" from disciplined policy is painful but visible, targeted, and typically temporary — unemployment insurance, fiscal transfers, and labor markets can respond. Inflation erosion is diffuse, regressive, and much harder to reverse once embedded in contracts and expectations.
[P1.5] Third, credibility is the scarce asset here. Central banks only get a few chances to prove they mean their target before households and firms stop believing it. Once expectations de-anchor, every future recession requires more unemployment to re-anchor them, not less. Tolerating above-target inflation now to shave off a bit of unemployment is borrowing against future employment at a punishing interest rate.
[P1.6] The core comparison for this debate: a bounded, known cost now versus an unbounded, compounding cost later. PRO wins that comparison.
