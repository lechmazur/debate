[P1.1] Public money for research is not buying headlines; it is buying claims that doctors, engineers, teachers, and other scientists can safely build on. A novel result that no one has checked is not yet that product. It is a promissory note. Reserving a fixed share for replication is how funders make sure some of those notes are actually redeemed.
[P1.2] Left to open competition, replication will almost always lose, and not because it is less valuable. Careers advance on firsts, journals prefer surprising positive findings, and grant panels reward ambitious new directions over careful re-testing. Each individual reviewer can agree verification matters while still ranking it below novelty. The result is a systematic gap: literatures full of fragile effects discovered with small samples, flexible analyses, and publication bias, with no reliable mechanism to sort signal from noise.
[P1.3] That gap is expensive. A false lead does not sit harmlessly in a journal. It attracts follow-on grants, graduate years, attempts at clinical translation, and sometimes real-world interventions. Psychology, biomedical, and social-policy research have all seen prominent findings shrink or disappear under closer re-testing, after substantial downstream money was already spent.
[P1.4] Replicating early is comparatively cheap insurance against funding an entire tree of work rooted in an error.
[P1.5] A meaningful, ring-fenced share — not leftover money when budgets allow — solves the incentive problem. It creates a stable career path for replication teams, builds expertise in high-quality re-testing, and gives journals and institutions a reason to value the work. Voluntary encouragement has had decades to work; exhortation without protected funding is a wish.
[P1.6] Yes, this means fewer novel grants at the margin. But novelty without credibility is not progress, it is accumulation. Knowing that findings face a real chance of independent checking also improves the novel work that remains: preregistration, better power, clearer methods, and less hype become rational. Fewer, sturdier discoveries compound.
[P1.7] Unchecked discoveries merely pile up.

[C1.1] Replication deserves funding; a compulsory budget quota does not follow. The motion is not “check important findings.” It orders funders to protect a meaningful share before knowing which findings matter or what discoveries will be displaced. PRO’s catalogue of bad incentives shows a problem, not that this rigid instrument is the solution.
[C1.2] Verification should follow expected value. A result heading toward clinical guidance may warrant expensive independent trials; an exploratory result may be tested efficiently through ordinary follow-on work. Some portfolios will need substantial replication, others very little. A fixed share can therefore be simultaneously too small for urgent failures and too large for fields where the reserve buys low-value repetition.
[C1.3] Worse, it guarantees the novel-project cuts even when the marginal replication would teach us little.
[C1.4] Nor is replication “cheap insurance.” Exact re-runs can fail because conditions differ; small replications can leave everyone uncertain; successful ones do not certify every downstream use. A protected pot also creates its own incentives: attractive, feasible studies get repeated while costly, consequential checks remain unfunded. Spending stability is not the same as reliability.
[C1.5] PRO assumes the displaced novel grants are the weakest and that reserve spending produces more knowledge. Neither follows. Novel work supplies methods, datasets, negative results, and fresh tests of old assumptions. Panels under pressure may sacrifice interdisciplinary projects while the quota funds repetition with few downstream users.
[C1.6] Funders have stronger, discriminating tools: dedicated calls for high-stakes checks, replication as an explicit review criterion, confirmation built into research programs, and requirements for usable data and methods. These are funded decisions, not mere exhortation, and can expand or contract with evidence of need.
[C1.7] The choice is not novelty versus truth; it is intelligent verification versus spending by formula. A percentage is easy to administer precisely because it avoids the hard scientific judgment of what to check, when, and at what cost. Funders should make that judgment, not outsource it to a quota.

[P2.1] The opposition’s central mistake is to treat a fixed share as a substitute for scientific judgment. It is the opposite: a floor that lets judgment operate. Inside the reserve, funders still rank proposals by stakes, uncertainty, feasibility, and downstream use. Nothing in a share requires repeating obscure findings or capping urgent checks — urgent work can also draw on the wider budget.
[P2.2] The quota decides that verification will not be zero when novelty is exciting; it does not decide which verifications win. [C1.2]
[P2.3] That distinction answers the appeal to flexible tools in [C1.6]. Dedicated calls, review criteria, and program-level confirmation sound discriminating, but if the money remains fungible, each check must again beat a novel proposal in the competition that systematically devalues checking. A criterion without protected funds lets reviewers praise rigour and then fund the first claim anyway. A ring-fence is a commitment device against that drift, not an escape from judgment.
[P2.4] Nor does imperfection defeat insurance. Of course one replication does not certify a finding, and a poorly powered rerun can muddy the waters [C1.4]. That is an argument against occasional, amateur replication — and for the stable expertise, larger samples, and better protocols that only predictable funding creates. We do not abandon clinical trials because one trial is fallible; we fund a system capable of learning across trials.
[P2.5] Finally, displacement is asserted, not weighed. Public funders turn away many fundable novel projects; the grant lost at the margin is usually another competent, incremental proposal, not the sole breakthrough of a generation. The replication gained at the margin, if selected for influence, protects every grant, paper, and intervention built on that claim. One verified foundation can be worth more than several unchecked branches growing from it.
[P2.6] Flexibility without a floor has a track record: verification is everyone’s priority and no one’s funded project. A protected share changes that equilibrium while preserving choice about what matters most.

[P4.1] A1: A meaningful share is one large enough to sustain a replication programme, not a symbolic pilot. Each funder's board would set it for a budget cycle after auditing its portfolio, and fix it within that cycle so panels cannot raid it. It can be reset between cycles if need changes, and unspent money can roll forward toward larger, better-powered, multi-site checks rather than forcing weak reruns. Commitment lives inside the planning horizon; revision between horizons is governance, not abandonment.
[P4.2] A2: No mechanism certifies in advance that a displaced grant is dispensable, and none is needed. Peer review already ranks novel proposals; a reserve draws from the bottom of that ranked list, exactly as a funded replication would under any system. Replication proposals then compete inside the reserve on influence, uncertainty, and feasibility. The comparison is therefore not guesswork about a named breakthrough, but portfolio judgment: verifying load-bearing claims is worth more than funding still more unchecked claims at the margin.
[P4.3] Those answers expose the clash. CON demands perfect foresight from a floor, while accepting the same uncertainty when panels make identical trade-offs informally. But informal trade-offs are where replication loses: praised in principle, ranked below novelty in practice. A floor removes only that repeated veto; it does not remove judgment about which checks deserve money.
[P4.4] Nor does this entitle every neglected activity to a quota. Replication is not another topic competing for attention. It is quality control on the rest of the portfolio, telling funders whether other spending built knowledge or error. That cross-cutting role justifies protection a new subfield does not.
[P4.5] Finally, the risk asymmetry favours PRO. Overfunding replication for one cycle wastes some money on confirmations. Underfunding it, as now, lets false foundations attract years of grants, training, and translation before correction. A bounded, reviewable floor buys protection against the costlier error while preserving competition where competition works: among replications, and among novel projects above a more credible base.

[C4.1] A1: The concrete mechanism is a multi-year, budgeted replication programme: audit the portfolio, commission the highest-stakes checks, award the grants, and require confirmation within suitable research programmes. Those are enforceable funding commitments, not review-panel exhortations. Failure is not falling below an arbitrary percentage; it is leaving influential, feasible checks unfunded after that audit. A quota supplies no better guarantee of success: a funder can spend its entire share on attractive reruns while the load-bearing claim remains unchecked.
[C4.2] A2: The judgment remains useful inside a reserve; what becomes unreliable is the budget decision surrounding it. Panels inside the floor can rank replication A against replication B, but cannot conclude that both are worth less than novel project C. Cross-category comparison is precisely what the ring-fence forbids. PRO’s answer that displaced grants come from the bottom of a ranked list also concedes that no mechanism establishes they are dispensable—and those grants are never compared directly with the replications ultimately selected.
[C4.3] PRO’s new governance details do not repair that gap. Resetting the share between cycles admits that need varies; fixing it within cycles locks in the error when the audit is wrong. Rolling unspent money forward protects the allocation, not necessarily scientific value. The same audits can instead determine the next targeted commitment without compelling a fraction of every budget.
[C4.4] Calling replication “quality control” explains why it deserves funded priority, not why its priority must be expressed as a fixed share. Quality control can also be built into original projects, commissioned when stakes rise, or expanded when failures appear. Nor has PRO established the claimed risk asymmetry: underchecking can propagate error, but overreserving sacrifices projects that might produce methods, evidence, or verification themselves. The opportunity cost is real, not merely “some money on confirmations.”
[C4.5] PRO wins, at most, the case against allowing verification to be zero. CON already funds targeted checks. The motion goes further: guaranteed cuts and compulsory reserve spending before their marginal value is known. That rigidity remains unjustified.

[C5.1] The decisive defect is that a fixed share guarantees spending on replication without guaranteeing that replication is the best use of the money. PRO protects it from competition with novelty, then calls that protection “judgment.” But judgment inside the reserve can only choose which replication to fund; it cannot decide that every available check is worth less than the novel project being displaced. That is precisely the comparison the ring-fence forbids.
[C5.2] Calling replication “quality control” establishes its importance, not its correct budget fraction. Quality control should be strongest around load-bearing, uncertain claims and weakest where checks are cheap, redundant, or already embedded in ongoing research. PRO’s audits and periodic resets concede that need varies; the quota nevertheless locks in one answer while conditions change.
[C5.3] Nor is the cost merely the “lowest-ranked” proposal, as though that meant dispensable. It is an unfunded project never compared directly with the replications ultimately selected. Meanwhile, a filled reserve can still leave the crucial claim unchecked while attractive reruns proceed.
[C5.4] The winning principle is therefore simple: fund verification through enforceable, multi-year commitments when an audit shows that particular checks outperform their alternatives. Do not confuse a protected allocation with protected truth. A quota guarantees activity and cuts; targeted judgment guarantees that both must earn their place.
