[P1.1] Resale platforms do not charge hefty commissions merely to host photographs; they charge those fees to sell trust. Yet across the online clothing market, platforms collect their cut while pushing the real burden of counterfeits onto the party least equipped to handle it: the individual buyer.
[P1.2] Consider the asymmetry in capability. Modern counterfeits are no longer obvious knockoffs with misspelled logos. High-grade fakes—spanning designer handbags, vintage jackets, and everyday streetwear—replicate stitching, hardware, and tags closely enough to fool ordinary shoppers. A buyer sees only a few compressed photos before purchasing.
[P1.3] Once the package arrives, they typically face a rigid three-day window to inspect the item and prove it is fake, sometimes even having to pay out-of-pocket for third-party verification just to unlock a refund.
[P1.4] Platforms, by contrast, are the least-cost avoiders. They possess seller histories, pricing anomaly data, and the scale to run digital screening and physical authentication hubs. Authenticating more inventory upfront—rather than restricting checks to ultra-expensive luxury tiers—and absorbing liability when fakes slip through places the burden on the only actor able to stop fraud systematically.
[P1.5] Moreover, current incentives are broken. Every time a counterfeit fools an unsuspecting buyer, or a buyer misses a short dispute deadline, the platform keeps its commission. Platforms literally profit from undetected fraud. Requiring platforms to absorb counterfeit risk through broader vetting and durable authenticity guarantees turns counterfeits from a quiet revenue stream into a balance-sheet cost they must eliminate.
[P1.6] Finally, pushing risk onto buyers creates a market for lemons. When shoppers know they are on their own if a coat turns out to be fake next month, they discount what they are willing to pay across the board. That punishes honest sellers of genuine clothing while rewarding scammers. If platforms want marketplace commissions, they must shoulder marketplace risk.

[C1.1] The proposition treats clothing resale like sneaker or handbag authentication, but garments are fundamentally different assets. Unlike a Birkin with a serial number or a limited-edition sneaker with a production code, the vast majority of clothing lacks unique identifiers. A vintage denim jacket, a washed silk shirt, or a distressed leather boot cannot be "authenticated" by a platform employee with a checklist. The proposal demands that generalist marketplaces become forensic experts across thousands of brands, seasons, and fabric variations—an impossible standard that would either require prohibitively expensive physical inspection of every listing or force platforms to delist anything uncertain to avoid liability.
[C1.2] Economically, this mandate would strangle the secondary market. Authentication is not free. If platforms must absorb counterfeit risk and vet more inventory upfront, they will recover those massive costs by raising seller fees and buyer premiums across the board, so honest sellers subsidize fraudsters. Worse, to minimize exposure, platforms would restrict listings to easily verified current-season goods with tags, banning vintage, artisanal, and rare pieces that lack documentation.
[C1.3] The open marketplace becomes a curated department store, destroying the treasure-hunt value that drives resale.
[C1.4] The proposal also creates a dangerous moral hazard. When buyers bear no risk of loss, they stop scrutinizing listings. Sellers, knowing the platform guarantees authenticity regardless of what they disclose, have less incentive to reveal flaws, alterations, or uncertain provenance. The so-called burden on buyers is not a design flaw; it is the price of agency.
[C1.5] Buyers already possess powerful protections: credit card chargebacks, PayPal purchase protection, and platform dispute windows. The true least-cost avoider is the buyer who can inspect the physical garment, feel the fabric weight, and compare stitching against retail references—capabilities no remote authentication hub possesses.
[C1.6] Finally, if buyers want insurance against counterfeits, specialized third-party authentication services and escrow already exist. Platforms are marketplaces, not insurers. Forcing them to absorb counterfeit risk does not eliminate fraud; it merely socializes the cost across every honest transaction while shrinking the variety that makes resale valuable.
