[P1.1] Consumer-price targeting has a well-known blind spot: the most dangerous inflation can show up in balance sheets, not shopping baskets. In the mid-2000s United States and several other economies before 2008, consumer inflation looked manageable while housing credit and prices ran far ahead of incomes. Central banks that waited for CPI to signal trouble waited until the crash was already built.
[P1.2] Housing is not just another asset. It is the largest leveraged purchase most households make, the collateral behind banking systems, and the transmission belt of monetary policy. A housing boom is also a credit boom: new mortgages create purchasing power, push prices higher, and validate more lending against the same collateral. That feedback loop is barely visible in a CPI basket where housing enters through lagging rents.
[P1.3] When prices detach from rents and incomes, the correction destroys net worth, freezes credit, and forces mass unemployment.
[P1.4] An explicit target does not mean setting a house-price number and popping bubbles. It means publishing a framework: track credit-to-GDP, price-to-income, rent yields, and underwriting standards; lean against broad-based housing leverage with rates and macroprudential tools; and be accountable when the boom is allowed to run. Symmetry matters. Central banks cut after crashes; they should also act during the boom.
[P1.5] The objection that asset prices are noisy is an argument for better rules, not for blindness. A fire alarm can have false positives; that does not justify removing it from a building full of gasoline. The objection that housing is politically sensitive is exactly why an explicit mandate is needed: discretion gets captured and delayed. Financial stability is already a central bank duty, but without an explicit anchor it is subordinated to CPI until too late.
[P1.6] CPI stability is necessary but not sufficient. A central bank that ignores housing can hit its inflation target while presiding over the biggest credit bust in a generation. The motion asks it to name the risk it already has to manage.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a real problem for the right remedy. Housing bubbles can build while consumer inflation is calm, but the remedy is not to make central banks explicitly responsible for asset-price stability. That would turn monetary policy into a blunt instrument aimed at the wrong variable. House prices can rise because of scarce supply, migration, lower long-term rates, or changing household preferences, not just speculative credit.
[C1.2] In real time, no central bank can reliably separate a healthy revaluation from a bubble. If it must react to housing prices as a target, it will often raise interest rates against supply-driven price gains, slowing jobs and investment while doing little to increase homes. The cost of false alarms is not a nuisance; it is recessions.
[C1.3] The deeper danger is leverage and bank fragility, and those are better handled by tools designed for them. Loan-to-value caps, debt-service limits, counter-cyclical capital buffers, stress tests, and mortgage-underwriting rules can cool credit without making the policy rate hostage to one asset class. If housing finance is overheating, regulate the mortgage, not the price index. A central bank can monitor housing fiercely without making price stability a formal objective.
[C1.4] The proposition says this adds accountability, but explicit targets work best when the instrument can control the variable. Interest rates cannot steer local land values, zoning shortages, or global risk appetite without creating large side effects.
[C1.5] There is also a moral-hazard trap. Once a central bank is formally charged with asset-price stability, markets will assume it must cushion falls as well as curb rises. That promise lowers risk premia, encourages leverage, and makes the financial system more fragile. Housing is also distributional and political; assigning its stability to technocrats invites backlash and undermines the credibility needed for inflation control.
[C1.6] The safer path is a focused inflation mandate, supported by macroprudential regulation—not an overloaded target that blurs responsibility and pressures central banks to manage what they cannot control.
