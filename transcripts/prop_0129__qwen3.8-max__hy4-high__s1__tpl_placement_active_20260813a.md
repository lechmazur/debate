[P1.1] The safest standard is not whether any policy makes prostitution disappear, but whether it reduces coercion, violence, and trafficking while giving people currently selling sex more safety and exit options. Criminalizing purchase and decriminalizing sale does both. It treats the seller as the vulnerable party, not the criminal.
[P1.2] Full prohibition fails because it punishes people who are often poor, migrant, LGBTQ+, or otherwise precariously housed. A seller with an arrest record is less able to rent, work elsewhere, or leave; fear of prosecution makes them less likely to report rape, extortion, or theft. Fully legal markets create different harms: a licensed sector with health checks and taxes, beside a larger informal sector because many people cannot or will not register. That two-tier result is not a technical glitch; it follows from treating sex as a commodity while trying to regulate intimate access.
[P1.3] Legalization can also expand demand and give buyers a stronger sense of entitlement to access bodies.
[P1.4] The asymmetric model attacks demand without attacking the person supplying the service. Police resources can focus on traffickers and violent buyers rather than street sweeps. Sellers can keep earnings, seek medical care, and call police without confessing to a crime. Over time, fewer buyers means fewer people recruited into exploitation, especially where exit services are funded.
[P1.5] It also gives governments a clearer tool: divert and educate buyers, while funding housing, health care, and alternatives for sellers. That burden belongs on demand, not on those least able to absorb it.
[P1.6] This is a compromise, not a fantasy. It acknowledges that many people sell sex because of constrained choices, while refusing to treat purchase as a neutral consumer right. The real question is whether the state should punish the exploited, license the market as ordinary commerce, or deter the side with more power. The last is the safer and more just balance.
