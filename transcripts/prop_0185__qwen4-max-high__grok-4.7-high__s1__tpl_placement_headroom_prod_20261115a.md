[P1.1] Legislative discretion has a defect that no amount of goodwill repairs: the benefits of borrowing are concentrated and immediate, the costs diffuse and deferred to a future legislature and a future electorate. That asymmetry produces a ratchet. In expansions, when tightening is cheap, surpluses are politically unrewarding; in contractions, restraint is impossible. So debt climbs across cycles regardless of which party governs.
[P1.2] Handing fiscal discipline to the very institution whose incentives generate the bias is not a solution — it is a description of the problem.
[P1.3] Notice what the motion actually says. It is not a rigid annual balance requirement. Automatic stabilizer exceptions mean that when output falls, tax revenue drops and unemployment claims rise, and the deficit widens *legally, automatically, without a vote*. The countercyclical response is built into the rule, not sacrificed to it.
[P1.4] Any attack on "austerity in a recession" targets a provision nobody proposed.
[P1.5] What the constitution adds is a burden of proof. Discretionary borrowing beyond the stabilizers requires an explicit, supermajority, recorded override with a repayment path — Switzerland's debt brake works roughly this way, with an emergency clause and an amortisation account; Germany's rule adjusts for the cycle. Legislatures still legislate. They simply must justify departure rather than drift into it.
[P1.6] And consider where procyclicality really bites. Governments that entered 2008 and 2020 with heavy debt had less room, and several eurozone countries ended up cutting spending *during* downturns because markets, not constitutions, imposed the constraint. A rule that forces saving in booms is what makes stabilizers affordable in busts. Discretion gave those countries neither discipline nor flexibility.
[P1.7] The choice is not rules versus flexibility. It is whether borrowing must be justified, or merely won.
