[P1.1] Morality must be grounded in what we share as human beings, not the arbitrary lines drawn by historical accidents. The lottery of birth—where and to whom we are born—carries no intrinsic moral weight, whereas the capacity to suffer and thrive is universal.
[P1.2] First, consider the asymmetry of stakes. When national loyalty conflicts with universal human welfare, we are almost always weighing fundamental survival against marginal privileges. If a wealthy nation prioritizes its own citizens above all else, it hoards lifesaving resources during global crises or refuses asylum to people fleeing violence. In these cases, national loyalty demands that the absolute survival of a foreigner be sacrificed for the comfort or economic convenience of a compatriot.
[P1.3] A coherent moral framework cannot justify letting one person die simply because they were born on the wrong side of a political border.
[P1.4] Second, elevating national loyalty to a primary moral claim logically justifies exploitation. If our highest duty is to the advancement of our own state, then policies that actively harm other populations—such as fighting proxy wars, ignoring catastrophic climate emissions, or exploiting vulnerable labor markets—become not just permissible, but morally required, so long as they enrich the homeland. History is littered with the catastrophic results of placing the nation-state at the apex of moral concern. Universal human welfare is the only ethical constraint that prevents patriotism from decaying into imperialism.
[P1.5] National loyalty certainly has practical utility, much like loyalty to a family or local community. It can foster social trust and civic participation. But these local loyalties are conditional. They must exist within a broader, uncompromising moral architecture.
[P1.6] You cannot protect your family by murdering your neighbor, and a nation cannot justly advance its interests by trampling on the basic welfare of humanity. Universal human welfare is the foundation that gives all other moral claims their boundaries. When the two conflict, the universal claim must always take precedence.
