[P1.1] A minimum wage protects one worker at a time; a sectoral agreement protects an entire industry. That difference in scale is the whole case.
[P1.2] First, coverage. Firm-by-firm bargaining assumes workers can organize at the entity that sets their pay — but the modern economy has dissolved that entity. The hotel cleaner works for a contractor, the driver for an app, the fast-food worker for a franchisee whose margins are dictated by headquarters. Where bargaining is purely enterprise-level, as in the United States, coverage has collapsed toward one worker in ten, and those with the least individual leverage — cleaners, carers, guards, warehouse staff — are locked out permanently.
[P1.3] France, which extends sectoral agreements across industries, covers the large majority of its private workforce. The mechanism is obvious: set the floor where the terms are actually set.
[P1.4] Second, floors take wages out of competition. Under enterprise bargaining, every decent employer is a fool: raise pay and a low-road rival undercuts you and takes the contract. When every firm in an industry must meet the same minimum, competition shifts to service, quality, and productivity — where it should be. Good employers stop being punished for decency, and the race to the bottom loses its fuel.
[P1.5] Third, labor markets are not competitive. Employers hold concentrated power over low-wage workers, and the evidence on wage floors shows pay can rise without the employment collapse critics always predict. A negotiated sectoral floor is more surgical than a statutory minimum: set by the industry, for the industry, adjustable to regional costs and trade realities.
[P1.6] Finally, note the word "minimum." These are floors, not ceilings — bargaining above them continues, which is why Denmark, Austria, and the Nordics pair sectoral systems with world-class competitiveness. Sectoral bargaining does not replace enterprise negotiation; it gives it a foundation.
[P1.7] The question is not whether workers deserve fair standards, but whether any mechanism can deliver them at scale. Only one can.

[C1.1] An industry is not a workplace. It is a swarm of firms that differ in size, location, capital, and customers. Sectoral bargaining pretends they are interchangeable, then asks the state to make that fiction binding.
[C1.2] Coverage is the strongest-sounding claim, and the most misleading. Extending a deal signed by large incumbents to every cleaner, driver, and franchisee does not restore a vanished table. It writes a wage schedule for people who were never in the room. Small firms and thin-margin operators then face a floor built for someone else's cost structure.
[C1.3] That is not protection at scale. It is a tax on entry.
[C1.4] Floors do not merely take wages out of competition. They take adjustment out of it. Good employers are not fools for paying more; they are firms that can. Forcing the rest to match does not civilize rivalry.
[C1.5] It deletes the jobs that cannot. When demand falls or a region has weaker sales, a sector-wide schedule cannot bend. Hiring freezes, hours shrink, and the least experienced — the young, the newly arrived, the formerly unemployed — never get the first job. Extended-agreement systems have long produced dual markets: insiders inside the deal, temps and contractors queued outside it.
[C1.6] Nor is this surgical. Someone must draw the industry line. Is a hotel kitchen the same sector as a hospital cafeteria? A logistics app the same as a warehouse?
[C1.7] Those borders become political prizes. Incumbents write classifications that freeze yesterday's jobs and raise rivals' costs.
[C1.8] Denmark and Austria are not a plug-in. High skills and tight labor supply cannot be decreed. Jumping from American enterprise coverage to government-extended sectoral deals skips the real costs: rigidity, outsider exclusion, and bargaining converted into industrial regulation. Minimum standards can be honest and general.
[C1.9] They should not be one contract exported across an entire industry.
