[P1.1] Nobody gets to choose whether people drink on a Saturday night. They already do. The only real choice is where, and under whose eyes. The answer should be: in the open, under management, where help can reach them.
[P1.2] First, banning drinking on the street doesn't reduce drinking; it relocates it. When a bottle can't leave the bar, people drink fast before they arrive. They pregame in cars, parking garages, and apartment kitchens, downing cheap spirits quickly because bar prices punish moderation. They drink in alleys and side streets precisely because those places are hidden from enforcement.
[P1.3] Hidden is the problem. A person who collapses in a stairwell or is assaulted at an unregistered house party has no bouncer, no street medic, no camera, and no witness.
[P1.4] Second, a tightly managed zone turns visibility into safety. Picture a few downtown blocks with defined boundaries and hours, plastic cups only, no glass, and sales cut off at a set time. Add staffed first-aid points, extra lighting, late transit, and a concentrated police and ambassador presence. Problems still happen, but they happen where help is thirty seconds away rather than thirty minutes.
[P1.5] New Orleans and Savannah have lived with versions of this for decades, and much of Europe treats a beer on a plaza as ordinary rather than criminal.
[P1.6] Third, the alternative wastes enforcement. Blanket open-container bans generate endless low-level citations. They are applied selectively, often against the least powerful people on the street, while doing nothing about the dangerous drinking happening out of sight. A zone lets police concentrate on violence and drunk driving instead of confiscating cans.
[P1.7] So the opposition carries a heavy burden. It must show that banning street drinking actually makes people drink less, not just drink somewhere worse. If it can't, regulation beats displacement.
