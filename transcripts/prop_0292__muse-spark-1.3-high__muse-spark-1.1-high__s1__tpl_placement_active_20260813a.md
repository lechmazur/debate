[P1.1] Imagine a 16-year-old finding a realistic nude video of herself shared in a class group chat. She never made it. An app stitched her Instagram face onto porn. The school can't stop the spread, the site hosting it ignores her emails, police say no existing harassment or porn law cleanly fits AI fakes.
[P1.2] Her humiliation is real even though the image is fake.
[P1.3] That gap is why an explicit private right of action is necessary. Criminal bans depend on prosecutors who lack resources and prioritize other cases, and they do nothing to get images down quickly or compensate victims. Old tools like copyright fail when the victim never owned the source photo, and defamation fails when everyone knows it's AI yet shares it to degrade anyway. Only a direct civil claim — allowing the depicted person to sue creators and knowing distributors for damages and fast court-ordered removal — puts power and speed where needed: in the victim's hands.
[P1.4] Opponents will warn about speech. But nonconsensual fake porn contributes almost nothing to public debate, artistic commentary, or democratic self-government. We already accept limits for defamation, fraud, and revenge porn because falsehoods used as weapons are not the speech a free society exists to protect. An explicit statute actually guards expression better than stretching old laws, because legislators can write clear consent requirements, knowledge standards, and exemptions for legitimate parody or newsworthy matters.
[P1.5] The tradeoff in the motion is therefore lopsided. On one side, a small narrowing: people lose a supposed right to manufacture realistic sexual images of real, non-consenting people. On the other, millions — overwhelmingly women and girls — gain ability to stay in school, run for office, or simply exist online without fear that anyone with a phone can strip them naked to the world. Protecting that participation expands freedom far more than it restricts.
