[P1.1] Public money should buy public obligations, not merely private promises. A team asking a city to help finance a new stadium should accept enforceable revenue sharing or neighborhood benefits in return. This is not a ban on stadium subsidies. It is a minimum bargaining standard: taxpayers should not bear negotiated costs while receiving only hoped-for benefits.
[P1.2] The central problem is the gap between stadium activity and public gain. A packed restaurant beside an arena does not establish that the city became richer; some customers would otherwise have spent money elsewhere locally. Construction work is temporary, while financing and maintenance obligations can endure. And money committed to a stadium cannot also repair streets or support housing.
[P1.3] Cities need more than impressive attendance projections to justify that trade.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, the parties capture different benefits. Teams can monetize premium seating, sponsorships, and stadium-related revenues. [P1.5] Neighborhood requirements can instead direct benefits toward those bearing local costs—for example, funded transit improvements, affordable commercial space, or targeted hiring commitments.
[P1.6] The word “enforceable” does essential work. A meaningful agreement specifies obligations, deadlines, verification, and remedies such as repayment or withholding future subsidies. Revenue-sharing provisions need clear definitions and audit rights; neighborhood commitments need measurable delivery requirements. [P1.7] Cities retain discretion over which route fits and whether to subsidize at all.
[P1.8] The strongest objection is that conditions might drive a team away.
