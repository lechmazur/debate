[P1.1] Employers should drop MBTI and DISC from hiring and team-building not because personality is irrelevant, but because these instruments answer the wrong question. Hiring asks: who will perform? Team-building asks: what working arrangement improves outcomes? MBTI and DISC give people a flattering four-letter or color-coded identity.
[P1.2] That is not a forecast.
[P1.3] The central flaw is validity. MBTI sorts continuous traits into binary types, so people near a cutoff can flip categories. Many test-retest repeatedly and get different types. DISC has a similar architecture: a short forced-choice profile yields a quadrant or style.
[P1.4] If a measure cannot reliably classify the same person twice, it cannot reliably predict performance across candidates or time. A hiring tool that unstable is noise with a brand.
[P1.5] Worse, it feels like insight. Barnum-style descriptions—“you value harmony but need independence”—fit almost anyone. Managers then overtrust the label. In team-building, that produces real decisions: who leads, who sells, who gets the detail work.
[P1.6] The result is stereotype laundering: a person is assigned a role based on a quiz, not evidence.
[P1.7] Compare the alternatives. Structured interviews, work samples, job-knowledge tests, and reference checks have far more defensible links to performance. Even within personality testing, broad traits like conscientiousness have some predictive signal; MBTI and DISC do not capture job-relevant traits in a way that supports selection. If the other side says these tools are for communication, then keep them in a low-stakes workshop—if at all—not in hiring or team design.
[P1.8] The reason to stop is not that labels are unpleasant. It is that employers are making consequential decisions with instruments whose predictive validity is too weak to justify the risk, cost, and bias. Fun taxonomy is not a hiring rationale.

[C1.1] The proposition attacks a straw man: a manager who hands out MBTI or DISC and then hires by profile alone. That is misuse, not the instruments’ only or primary legitimate function. These tools are descriptive frameworks for how people prefer to communicate, process decisions, and respond to pressure. Judging them solely by predictive validity is like rejecting a meeting agenda because it cannot forecast quarterly earnings.
[C1.2] For team-building, the relevant outcome is not performance prediction but shared language. A team that learns someone prefers to finish one task before switching can stop misreading that behavior as rigidity. Another team that discovers its conflict comes from different pacing can adjust how it assigns collaborative work. That is a real organizational benefit, even if the categories are imperfect.
[C1.3] The motion’s focus on “predictive validity” is aimed at the wrong criterion.
[C1.4] Even in hiring, the case for stopping is overstated. Employers can use MBTI or DISC as a structured conversation prompt—asking candidates how they handle ambiguity, deadlines, or disagreement—and then verify those claims with interviews, work samples, and references. The problem is treating a four-letter label as the decision rule.
[C1.5] But rejecting that overuse does not require eliminating every use.
[C1.6] The “stereotype laundering” concern is an argument for better training, not a ban. If managers are assigning roles from quiz results alone, they already have a judgment problem. Removing the tool would not remove the bias; it would just push it back into gut feel and unstructured impressions. A structured team discussion can make those assumptions visible rather than hidden.
[C1.7] The proposition proves that MBTI and DISC should not be standalone predictors. It does not prove employers should stop using them in the broader ways these tools are actually used: as low-stakes frameworks for communication, reflection, and structured dialogue. The correct remedy is clearer guidance and limits on interpretation—not elimination.

[C2.4] The “J/P clash” example is misuse by jumping to a conclusion. Proper use checks the type-based story against what the person actually says: “Is that what happened?” That makes hidden assumptions visible instead of leaving them to fester.
[C2.5] [P2.6] says training cannot turn an unreliable signal reliable. Correct, but irrelevant. The skill being trained is not to interpret the type; it is to use the type as a question prompt and refuse to convert it into a score. [C2.6] The remedy for misuse is clear limits and decision hygiene, not elimination.
[C2.7] The fatal concession only follows if CON said tests should be harmless decorations. They should not. They should be structured scaffolding around a decision that valid tools still make. [P4.1] A1: Yes—deciding which follow-up questions to ask is shaping judgment, and that is precisely the problem. If a DISC label makes a manager probe one candidate on pushback and another on consensus, the interview is no longer comparable, and the label has steered the evidence. Observable effects include different follow-ups, note-taking, and anchoring—reading an ambiguous answer as “classic D” or “typical introvert.” If the profile changes none of that, it is inert and should be dropped. CON cannot have it both ways.
[P4.2] A2: Direct self-report is not exempt, but it does not need to be a fixed type. Asking an employee, “Do you want written context before decisions?” targets a present preference that can be checked, revised, or observed. MBTI and DISC convert that into a category, and then the category travels beyond the moment: “she’s a J,” “he’s a D.” The instability matters because the instrument claims to identify a style, not because teams cannot ask about current preferences. Direct questions are the better version: transparent, revisable, and not dressed up as personality science.
[P4.3] Those answers decide the debate. CON’s last refuge is bounded use: the test as a conversation prompt or shared vocabulary. But the benefit comes from the questions and the conversation, not the branded category. The risk comes from the category steering attention and ratifying assumptions.
[P4.4] Strip away the category and MBTI/DISC are unnecessary. Keep the category influential and their weak predictive validity is exactly what the motion targets.
[P4.5] That is why the overbreadth objection fails. The proposition does not ban discussing work styles; it says stop using these standardized tests in hiring and team-building. In hiring, use structured questions and work samples directly. In team-building, use concrete agreements about deadlines, communication, and decision rights.
[P4.6] If a tool cannot forecast performance and cannot reliably describe the same person twice, it should not be the scaffolding around consequential decisions. Employers should stop.
