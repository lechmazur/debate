[P1.1] Remote work did not move the office into the home. It moved work into bedrooms, kitchens, and shared apartments, and AI-driven video and keystroke monitoring moves surveillance there with it. A webcam does not just see an employee; it sees children walking past, partners, religious objects, medical equipment. A keylogger does not just count work; it can capture personal passwords, a private message typed on a break, a bank login, a note to a doctor.
[P1.2] An intrusion that personal should never be imposed as a silent condition of keeping a job.
[P1.3] AI makes this worse, not more precise. Older supervision was episodic and human; this is continuous, searchable, and scored. Systems flag idle seconds, infer attention or mood, rank workers against each other, and keep records that can be reused for discipline, promotion, or termination long after the original purpose is forgotten. Workers rarely know what triggered a flag, and they cannot meaningfully challenge a model that treats a caregiving interruption, a disability, or a thoughtful pause as lost productivity.
[P1.4] Opt-in consent and strict limits solve the proportionality problem without banning useful tools. Consent must be specific, revocable, and free from retaliation for refusing. Limits must mean work hours only, no continuous bedroom webcam, no capture outside work applications, no emotion recognition, short retention, employee access to their own data, and a human decision before punishment. With those guardrails, a regulated team can voluntarily use monitoring for security or training; without them, consent is fiction and monitoring expands by default.
[P1.5] Employers still get what they legitimately pay for: output. They can set deadlines, review work product, secure their systems, and audit access to sensitive files. Paying a salary buys labor and results, not ownership of a worker's home, body, and every keystroke. Surveillance is also a poor substitute for management: it rewards looking busy, encourages mouse-moving tricks, raises stress, and drives good employees away, while missing the judgment that creates value.
