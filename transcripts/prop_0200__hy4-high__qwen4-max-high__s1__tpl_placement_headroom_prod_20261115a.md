[P1.1] Recognize a narrow rule now: material an operator extracts from the Moon and captures may be owned and sold; the Moon itself, any crater, and any claimed sovereign zone may not. A tank of oxygen from regolith is not a deed to territory. That distinction lets the law support commerce without endorsing appropriation. Title passes only to severed, recorded mass, not to land, permanent exclusion, or sovereignty.
[P1.2] Waiting for a full global treaty sounds orderly only if delay is costless. It is not. A legal vacuum rewards opaque state programs, quiet prospecting, and first movers who turn presence into leverage while everyone else argues about text. It blocks the very conditions the proposition requires: no recognized sale means no clean chain of custody, no licensing hook, no public ledger, no cleanup duty.
[P1.3] Treaty-first purity therefore produces less law, not more; it leaves a commons with rules on paper and behavior in the dark.
[P1.4] The obligations are the answer to the real physical fear. Lunar dust, plume ejecta, discarded stages, and abandoned gear can endanger other missions for years. So legal recognition should be conditional on published plans and coordinates, mass and buyer records, plume modelling, tracked debris, removal or safe disposal plans, and close-out reports. A firm that extracts a small amount of regolith, tests oxygen production, logs what it took and where, keeps the site clear, and sells that oxygen to a lander creates an inspectable event.
[P1.5] An unlicensed scramble creates a rumor.
[P1.6] Interim legality makes a better treaty possible. Negotiators need records on costs, safety zones, environmental effects, and supply chains, not speculation. Compliance can be tied to contracts, insurance, launch permissions, and resale. The comparison is stark: conditional commerce gives information and leverage; treaty-first suspension gives holdouts a veto and first movers the shadows.
