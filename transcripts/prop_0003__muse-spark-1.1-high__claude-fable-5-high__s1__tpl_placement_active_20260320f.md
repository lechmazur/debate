[P1.1] Cities are for people, not for buildings. When a historic-district rule makes it illegal to replace a crumbling three-story rowhouse two blocks from a subway with a six-story apartment housing twenty families, the city has decided to preserve wood and brick at the cost of human shelter.
[P1.2] That trade is now intolerable. We face a housing shortage that drives rents, evictions, and super-commutes, and pushes growth into farmland and wildfire zones. The most sustainable, affordable place to add homes is precisely where historic districts tend to cluster: flat, walkable, transit-rich cores. Yet their rules don't just stop demolition; they cap height, ban lot subdivision, require setbacks and lengthy materials review.
[P1.3] The result is enforced scarcity in the exact places we need abundance. Every home blocked downtown becomes a longer car commute outward, more carbon, more displacement pressure elsewhere.
[P1.4] This scarcity is not neutral. Historic designation exploded after the 1970s not mainly to save true landmarks like Monticello, but to freeze whole neighborhoods of common bungalows and tenements. In practice it functions as a legal superpower for wealthier homeowners to veto change. It locks low density in high-opportunity areas, protecting property values while renters pay the price.
[P1.5] Losing some architecturally significant buildings is a real cost, but a smaller one. Relaxing does not mean erasing cities; it means allowing more homes by right, keeping the best blocks as museums, documenting and salvaging the rest, requiring facadism where it helps. What we cannot do is keep every cornice and also house the next generation. Paris, London, even Greenwich Village itself replaced what came before.
[P1.6] A living city must be allowed to renew.
[P1.7] When preservation blocks needed density, it should yield. Beauty matters, but shelter, access, and climate matter more.

[C1.1] Start with a simple map exercise: overlay any American city's historic districts on its zoning map. The districts cover a sliver — typically a few percent of urban land. The other ninety-plus percent is dominated by single-family-only zoning, parking mandates, minimum lot sizes, and strip malls a bus ride from downtown. The housing shortage lives there.
[C1.2] Demolishing landmarks buys a rounding error of capacity while handing politicians a way to look pro-housing without touching the zoning that actually causes scarcity. That is the core deception in this motion: it trades something irreplaceable for something we could get more cheaply elsewhere.
[C1.3] And the trade is asymmetric in the worst way. A missed apartment building can be built next year, next block, next mayor. A demolished 1880s theater or rowhouse block is gone for every generation that follows. Rational policy does not sell one-way, irreversible assets to solve a reversible, policy-made problem.
[C1.4] Notice, too, what relaxation actually produces on the ground. Demolition permits do not come stapled to affordable units. They come with speculation: buy the protected building, let it rot, plead hardship, flip the cleared lot. Meanwhile the buildings destroyed are disproportionately the city's naturally affordable stock — old walk-ups and tenements with depreciated rents — replaced, if at all, by luxury product years later.
[C1.5] The renters PRO invokes are often the first evicted.
[C1.6] Historic fabric is also not the enemy of density. Rowhouse and tenement districts are frequently denser than the postwar sprawl around them, and they permit gentle intensification — ADUs, rear additions, conversions, infill on vacant lots — without demolition. Add the climate ledger: the embodied carbon in an existing structure makes reuse, not replacement, the green choice.
[C1.7] So the real question is not beauty versus shelter. It is whether we solve scarcity where it's actually created — the vast underzoned periphery — or vandalize the small, finite, irreplaceable core for symbolic yield. Keep the protections; fix the zoning.
