[P1.1] Smartphones are not neutral classroom objects. They are devices engineered by some of the best-funded companies on earth to capture and hold attention. Asking a fourteen-year-old to resist that pull six hours a day, with a different rulebook in every room, is not trusting students. It is setting them up to lose.
[P1.2] So the question is not whether phones distract. Everyone knows they do. The question is who should carry the burden of managing them: the institution, or each individual teacher, period by period. Three reasons say the institution.
[P1.3] First, a patchwork fails even the teachers who try. When one teacher allows phones and the next bans them, the strict teacher becomes the villain and every class opens with negotiation. Students learn that rules are a bargaining position, not a norm. A school-wide default flips that.
[P1.4] The teacher is no longer the phone police acting alone; she is enforcing a rule everyone already knows, backed by the office and by parents who signed off on it. Consistency is what makes enforcement cheap.
[P1.5] Second, teachers do not control the spaces where the worst phone harms happen. Hallways, bathrooms, and lunch are where fights get filmed, where group-chat cruelty spreads in real time, and where kids sit side by side scrolling instead of talking. A teacher-by-teacher model is silent there by design. Only a school rule reaches the whole day.
[P1.6] Third, this is a collective action problem. No single student can put the phone away when everyone else is online, because that means missing the joke, the plan, the drama. A default ban frees all of them at once. That is why schools and governments in many countries have been moving toward bell-to-bell restrictions.
[P1.7] Notice what "default" means: exceptions survive. Students with medical needs keep devices. Teachers can schedule planned use. Parents reach children through the office, as they did for generations.
[P1.8] Opposing this means defending inconsistency, isolated teachers, and unregulated hallways. That is the burden CON must carry.

[C1.2] First, classrooms are not identical. A chemistry lab may need a calculator app; an English teacher may collect quick polls; a language class may use translation or voice recording; a student may photograph homework. Under teacher discretion, these decisions are fast, pedagogically grounded, and reversible. Under a default ban, the easiest response is no, because allowing a phone looks like breaking the rule.
[C1.3] The result is not fewer distractions; it is fewer low-cost teaching tools.
[C1.4] Second, PRO’s consistency sounds orderly, but uniform prohibition makes enforcement more adversarial, not less. When the default is ban, every visible phone becomes a contraband issue. [C1.5] That preserves relationships and keeps minor misbehavior from becoming a disciplinary record.
[C1.6] Third, schools should teach responsible use, not just abstinence. Teenagers will carry powerful devices after graduation. If students never practice managing notifications, using a phone for a task, or putting it away without surveillance, they learn only that rules replace judgment. Teacher-managed norms can model graduated responsibility: phone-free during direct instruction, limited use for collaboration, and explicit norms for group work.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO says exceptions survive, but a default ban still presumes distrust. It burdens students with medical, family, accessibility, or safety needs to prove they deserve an exception.
