[P1.1] The precautionary principle, as commonly applied, has become an engine of paralysis. It inverts the burden of proof: innovators must demonstrate that a product is absolutely safe before it can enter the market, while those who oppose it need only gesture at hypothetical, speculative harms. This asymmetry is the core problem. Regulators see the risk of action—cancer scares, environmental alarms—but are largely blind to the risk of inaction: the patients who die because a therapy is delayed, the farmers who suffer because a crop is banned, the climate damage that persists because cleaner technology is blocked.
[P1.2] This is not a theoretical flaw; it is the operating logic of modern risk regulation.
[P1.3] Consider genetically modified organisms. For decades, European and other regulators treated GMOs as guilty until proven innocent. The result was not a public health triumph; it was the suppression of crops engineered to reduce pesticide dependency and withstand drought. Meanwhile, the status quo—conventional agriculture with its chemical loads and yield volatility—remained unregulated because it was familiar.
[P1.4] Or consider nuclear power. After Fukushima, precautionary shutdowns and moratoria forced reliance on coal and gas, energy sources whose health and climate costs are known, quantified, and enormous. The principle demanded perfection from the new option while granting a free pass to the deadly incumbent.
[P1.5] This pattern repeats because precaution is politically one-sided. A regulatory delay produces no headline, no identifiable victim, no angry constituency. A novel technology that harms, or even appears to harm, produces all three. The incentive structure guarantees over-regulation.
[P1.6] The principle should not be abolished, but its common application is indefensible. It demands immunity from all risk, not protection from disproportionate risk. Genuine harm prevention requires evidence of probability, not possibility. When we regulate on the basis of what might go wrong rather than what is likely to go wrong, we do not buy safety.
[P1.7] We buy stagnation, and stagnation kills.
