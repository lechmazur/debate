A low-income valedictorian from a rural public school competes against an applicant with SAT tutoring since sophomore year, a private counselor shaping essays, unpaid internships in a parent's lab, varsity lacrosse, and early decision that requires forgoing financial aid comparisons.

[P1.4] Even the celebrated mobility stories prove the rule. Yes, the few poor kids who get into Harvard and go to Wall Street see huge income jumps. But there are so few of them that the net effect is trivial. What elite colleges do at scale is protect the already privileged from downward mobility.

[P1.6] That is why policy romanticism is so costly. We grant these institutions tax exemption, tax-deductible donations, and public research money on the premise they are fairness engines. In reality we subsidize an inheritance system. If they were truly engines of mobility, their enrollment would reflect talent distributed across income.
[P1.7] It does not. The honest policy stance is to treat them as private clubs that manage intergenerational transfer: tax their mega-endowments, end legacy preferences by law, and stop counting their tiny cohort of low-income admits as evidence of an open system.
