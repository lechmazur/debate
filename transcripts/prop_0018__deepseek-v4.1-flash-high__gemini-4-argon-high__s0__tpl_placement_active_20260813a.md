[P1.1] Central streets are a finite public asset. When demand exceeds capacity, something must ration them. Today the rationing device is congestion: everyone pays in wasted time, idling fuel, dirty air, slower buses, and delayed emergency response. Congestion pricing replaces that chaotic toll with an explicit one, then uses revenue to expand the alternatives that make less driving realistic.
[P1.2] That is why cities should impose it even when drivers object.
[P1.3] Strong opposition is predictable, not disqualifying. The costs are concentrated on a visible group—peak drivers—while the benefits are spread across millions of bus riders, pedestrians, cyclists, businesses that depend on reliable deliveries, asthma sufferers, and future residents. Democratic government cannot require unanimity from those asked to pay for a shared cost. If it did, no one would ever pay for clean water, fire protection, or road maintenance either.
[P1.4] Drivers may oppose a toll while also benefiting from faster buses and cleaner air; the intensity of their objection does not settle whether the policy is right.
[P1.5] The practical record points the same way. Singapore, London, and Stockholm have used area charges or trials to reduce central traffic and raise transport revenue; Stockholm’s politics famously shifted as residents experienced the results. The mechanism is simple: a price at the bottleneck changes marginal trips—some drivers shift time, route, or mode, and traffic falls enough to speed everyone remaining. Revenue can fund buses, rail, safe streets, and discounts for disabled or low-income drivers, addressing fairness without gutting the policy.
[P1.6] Opposition is a signal to design well: clear boundaries, peak-hour emphasis, transit improvements before or alongside launch, and targeted relief. It is not a veto. The metric is whether the city moves more people with less delay and pollution. On that metric, central congestion pricing wins even when drivers shout.
