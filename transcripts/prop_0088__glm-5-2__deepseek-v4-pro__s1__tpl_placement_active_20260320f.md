[P1.1] The four-day school week trades something irreplaceable—student learning—for savings that are marginal at best. That tradeoff fails on its own terms.
[P1.2] Start with what districts actually save. Cutting one day trims some transportation, food service, and utility costs, but the largest budget lines—salaries, benefits, facilities maintenance—stay essentially the same. Savings typically land around one to three percent of district budgets. For a district spending roughly $10,000 per pupil, that's perhaps $100 to $300 per child annually.
[P1.3] Now weigh that against what each child loses.
[P1.4] Instructional time is the core mechanism through which learning happens. When you remove a day, even with longer remaining days, total instructional hours usually drop. A kindergartener learning to read through daily repetition, a middle schooler who needs guided practice to grasp fractions—these students lose the repetition and structure that make skills stick. The fifth day doesn't become enrichment for most kids.
[P1.5] It becomes unstructured, often unsupervised time, widening the gap between families who can compensate with tutoring and supervision and families who cannot.
[P1.6] The staff retention argument is equally thin. Some teachers report liking the schedule, but liking a compressed calendar is not the same as teaching more effectively. Educators face pressure to cover the same material in fewer hours, which degrades lesson depth and increases stress. Meanwhile, the savings barely dent the structural funding problems most districts actually face.
[P1.7] The proposition asks a straightforward question: does the financial relief outweigh the academic damage? It does not. The harm compounds year over year, falls hardest on the students least equipped to absorb it, and buys districts very little in return.
