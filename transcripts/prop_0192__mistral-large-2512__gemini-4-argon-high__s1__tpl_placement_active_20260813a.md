[P1.1] The case for recognizing great apes, cetaceans, and elephants as non-human persons with rights against captivity and exploitation rests on three pillars: cognitive capacity, moral agency, and the failure of current legal frameworks to prevent egregious suffering.
[P1.2] First, these species possess cognitive capacities that mirror those we use to justify human personhood. Elephants mourn their dead, great apes use tools and language, and cetaceans exhibit self-awareness and complex social structures. If personhood is tied to sentience and intelligence—not species—then denying these rights is arbitrary. The law already recognizes corporations as legal persons; extending this status to beings with proven emotional depth and problem-solving skills is a logical, not radical, step.
[P1.3] Second, their moral agency demands accountability. Elephants retaliate against human threats, dolphins rescue drowning humans, and chimpanzees adopt orphaned young. These actions reflect empathy, justice, and social responsibility—traits we associate with moral beings. If we exploit them for entertainment or research, we ignore their capacity to suffer *and* their capacity to judge us for it.
[P1.4] Personhood isn’t just about rights; it’s about recognizing reciprocal moral relationships.
[P1.5] Finally, current protections fail. The Animal Welfare Act allows elephants to be chained for 16 hours a day, orcas to languish in tanks 1/10,000th the size of their natural range, and chimpanzees to be subjected to invasive experiments. Labeling these practices "humane" is a legal fiction. Personhood would grant enforceable rights—freedom from captivity, bodily autonomy, and protection from exploitation—shifting the burden from proving cruelty to proving necessity.
[P1.6] Opponents may argue that personhood disrupts industries or sets a slippery slope. But the alternative—continuing to treat these beings as property—is a far greater moral disruption. If we accept that intelligence and emotional depth warrant rights, the only question is where to draw the line. These three groups stand on the side of that line where denial is indefensible.
