[P1.1] The modern digital ecosystem operates on an extractive fiction: that by merely browsing the web, using an app, or carrying a connected device, individuals have agreed to have their intimate lives tracked, aggregated, and auctioned. Treating large-scale personal-data harvesting for targeted advertising as presumptively unlawful without explicit opt-in consent does not dismantle the digital economy; it restores basic autonomy against a market failure built on exhaustion and hidden surveillance.
[P1.2] Under current default-extraction regimes, consent is a hollow legal ritual. Platforms deploy dark patterns, cross-site trackers, and impenetrable terms of service engineered to manufacture resignation rather than informed agreement. Everyday users cannot realistically audit the hundreds of invisible data brokers logging their location history, health queries, and daily routines. When the law permits pervasive harvesting until a consumer navigates a labyrinth of opt-out toggles, it protects predatory friction, not consumer choice.
[P1.3] The consequences of this unchecked harvesting are severe and systemic. Pervasive tracking fuels predatory financial targeting, algorithmic discrimination, and behavioral manipulation, all while amassing centralized data troves that inevitably leak or get sold to shadowy intermediaries.
[P1.4] Flipping the legal default to presumptive unlawfulness corrects this asymmetry at its root. It establishes a clear, enforceable standard: entities seeking to commercialize private data must explicitly ask for permission upfront. Companies that provide genuine value can still earn that consent through transparent terms. Where users decline, thriving alternatives like contextual advertising—matching ads to the content being viewed rather than the person viewing it—prove that digital publishing does not require surveillance to remain profitable.
[P1.5] If an advertising model can only survive when consumers are deprived of an honest, frictionless opportunity to say no, it is an extraction scheme rather than a legitimate market. Courts and regulators must establish explicit opt-in consent as the non-negotiable baseline of digital life.
