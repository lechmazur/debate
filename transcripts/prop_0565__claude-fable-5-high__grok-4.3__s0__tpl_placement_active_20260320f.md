[P1.1] Infertility is a disease. The World Health Organization classifies it as one, defined by the failure of the reproductive system to perform its function. That single fact should anchor this entire debate, because once we accept that infertility is a medical condition affecting roughly one in six people worldwide, the burden shifts entirely to the other side. They must explain why this disease—alone among diseases of comparable prevalence and severity—deserves exclusion from basic healthcare.
[P1.2] Consider what we already cover without controversy. We treat erectile dysfunction, repair sports injuries, prescribe hormones for thyroid failure, and perform reconstructive surgery after mastectomies. None of these are about bare survival; they restore normal bodily function and the life people reasonably expect to live. Infertility treatment does exactly the same thing.
[P1.3] A woman with blocked fallopian tubes or a man with low sperm count has a malfunctioning organ system. IVF is simply the effective therapy for that malfunction. Carving it out requires a principle, and no principle survives scrutiny except squeamishness about reproduction itself.
[P1.4] The status quo is also brutally regressive. Where IVF is excluded from coverage, a single cycle costs many thousands of dollars, and most patients need more than one. The result is two-tier medicine in its starkest form: the wealthy buy parenthood while nurses, teachers, and farmworkers with identical diagnoses are told their disease doesn't count. Countries like France, Israel, and Denmark already fund IVF publicly, and they have not bankrupted themselves—fertility care is a small fraction of any health budget, dwarfed by routine chronic-disease spending.
[P1.5] Finally, the harms of untreated infertility are real medicine, not lifestyle disappointment: documented depression, anxiety, marital breakdown, and in many societies, social ostracism and violence against women blamed for childlessness. Healthcare systems exist to address exactly this kind of suffering.
[P1.6] The proposition asks only for consistency: treat a recognized disease the way we treat other diseases. The alternative is discrimination dressed up as budgeting.

[C1.1] Countries should prioritize finite public resources toward conditions that threaten life, mobility, or basic daily function rather than toward the creation of new life. Infertility, however distressing, does not meet that threshold. Every euro or dollar spent on IVF cycles is unavailable for childhood vaccines, insulin access, trauma care, or early cancer screening—interventions whose benefits are both larger in scale and more reliably delivered.
[C1.2] Treating IVF as basic care also distorts clinical priorities inside the system itself. Success rates per cycle remain modest even in good centers, often requiring repeated attempts whose cumulative cost and emotional toll are high. Public budgets would then face pressure to fund multiple rounds or to subsidize add-on procedures whose value is still contested. That pattern crowds out treatments with clearer, measurable returns on health.
[C1.3] The comparison to erectile dysfunction or post-mastectomy reconstruction fails on the same ground. Those interventions restore existing function or symmetry; IVF manufactures a new state that would not otherwise occur. Once the state begins funding the manufacture of parenthood, no principled limit remains when patients request public support for other deeply felt but non-essential goals.
[C1.4] Finally, the claim that exclusion creates two-tier medicine ignores the tiers already created by coverage decisions. When budgets are tight, societies already ration by denying public payment for many safe but low-priority interventions. Adding IVF simply creates a new tier of disappointed patients whose taxes now support a service they themselves may never need or use. Basic healthcare exists to prevent avoidable harm, not to guarantee any particular family outcome.
