[P1.1] Widening urban freeways is the policy equivalent of loosening your belt to cure obesity. It feels like relief for a week, and then the problem comes back bigger. Removal, by contrast, treats the disease.
[P1.2] The core mechanism is induced demand. Add a lane and you don't drain the existing traffic; you invite new trips, longer commutes, and sprawl that locks in tomorrow's congestion. Houston's Katy Freeway, expanded to over twenty lanes in places, became a national punchline because peak travel times got *worse* within a few years. Los Angeles spent over a billion dollars widening the 405 through the Sepulveda Pass and ended up roughly where it started.
[P1.3] This isn't a fluke; it's a pattern transportation engineers have documented for decades. Capacity creates its own traffic.
[P1.4] Removal flips that logic. When San Francisco lost the Embarcadero Freeway after the 1989 earthquake, the predicted carmageddon never came; instead the city got a waterfront worth billions. Seoul tore out the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway and uncovered a stream that anchors a downtown district. Milwaukee removed the Park East spur and turned a moat into tax base.
[P1.5] Traffic dispersed, evaporated, or shifted modes — and the land became something a city can actually use.
[P1.6] That's the second pillar: opportunity cost. An urban freeway is the most expensive land use a city can pick. It pays no property tax, generates noise and particulate pollution that measurably shorten nearby residents' lives, and historically was bulldozed through Black and immigrant neighborhoods whose wealth never recovered. Widening doubles down on that footprint.
[P1.7] Removal recovers it for housing, transit, parks, and walkable streets — the things that actually move people in dense places.
[P1.8] So the comparison is stark. Widening: tens of billions spent, congestion unchanged, neighborhoods further hollowed. Removal: traffic adapts, land returns to productive use, health and equity improve. A reasonable city, facing that ledger, should default to removal and treat widening as the rare exception — which is exactly what the motion says.
