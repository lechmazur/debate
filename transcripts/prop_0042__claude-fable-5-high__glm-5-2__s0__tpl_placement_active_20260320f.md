[P1.1] A hamburger that costs three dollars at the counter is a lie. Its true cost includes methane warming the planet, feedlots polluting rivers, antibiotics breeding resistant bacteria, deforested land, and the healthcare bills for diet-linked heart disease and colorectal cancer. Those costs don't vanish because they're missing from the receipt—they're paid by taxpayers, by hospital systems, and disproportionately by poor communities living downstream of industrial farms and downwind of a destabilized climate. A Pigouvian tax on meat production simply makes the price tell the truth.
[P1.2] Three pillars carry this case.
[P1.3] First, the externalities are enormous and regressive by default. Climate damage, water contamination, and chronic disease already function as an invisible tax—one that falls hardest on low-income households, who are least able to relocate, insure, or afford medical care. The status quo is not neutral; it is a massive transfer of costs from meat producers onto the vulnerable. CON must defend that hidden tax to oppose a visible one.
[P1.4] Second, the equity objection dissolves once you notice the tax raises revenue. Governments can rebate proceeds as a per-household dividend or subsidize legumes, grains, and vegetables. Because wealthier households consume more meat in absolute terms, they pay more in; a flat dividend leaves most low-income families financially better off even at higher shelf prices. British Columbia's carbon tax with rebates shows this architecture works politically and practically.
[P1.5] Third, price signals change behavior where lectures fail. Tobacco taxation cut smoking more effectively than any information campaign, and it too was attacked as regressive—yet the poor gained the most in health. Meat taxes similarly shift diets at the margin, spur producers toward lower-emission methods, and accelerate alternatives.
[P1.6] The motion asks whether we should do this even at some cost in food prices. Yes—because the alternative isn't cheap food. It's food whose real cost is quietly billed to the sick, the flooded, and the poor.

[C1.5] Third, the motion taxes meat production categorically, not the specific practices generating environmental harm. A regenerative pasture operation and an industrial feedlot pay the same penalty. This isn't pricing an externality—it's penalizing a food category.
[C1.6] The motion bites the poor to discipline the system. The rebate is a promissory note from institutions that have never cashed one without taking their cut. That's not justice—it's a regressive tax in an environmental costume.
