[P1.1] Health systems allocate under scarcity. A marginal dollar can go to a community immunization campaign, tobacco cessation support, hypertension screening, or clean-water oversight—or to another ICU day, another late-line drug, another high-intensity procedure for a patient already dying. The proposition asks which marginal use should come first. It should be prevention, because that dollar buys more healthy life, reaches more people, and reduces future pressure on the system.
[P1.2] The efficiency case is decisive. Preventive measures are among the highest-value purchases in medicine: they stop disease before it requires expensive treatment. A vaccine or a blood pressure check costs little compared with the hospitalization it averts. High-cost end-of-life interventions, by contrast, often yield very small extensions of life, sometimes measured in days, while imposing serious suffering and financial strain.
[P1.3] The same budget can protect many people from avoidable disease or add a small, uncertain increment to one patient’s final weeks. At the margin, the social return is not close.
[P1.4] This is not cruelty to the dying. Palliative care, hospice, pain control, and honest communication are comparatively inexpensive and should be guaranteed. The motion does not target that compassionate baseline. It targets marginal expansions of high-cost end-of-life treatment: prolonged intensive care with little chance of recovery, last-line therapies with weak or no survival benefit, repeated emergency admissions that cannot change the underlying trajectory.
[P1.5] Refusing to fund those at the first priority respects both the dying patient and the patients who could still be saved.
[P1.6] Prevention also serves equity. Preventable illness falls hardest on poorer, less-connected populations—the same people least likely to receive expensive rescue care. Shifting marginal resources upstream narrows health gaps instead of widening them. It also makes the system more sustainable: fewer preventable crises mean less overcrowding, lower future costs, and more capacity for high-value treatment when real emergencies occur.
[P1.7] The strongest test is opportunity cost. A health system that automatically privileges the visible dying patient will quietly sacrifice the invisible future patient. Prioritizing preventive public health is not anti-care; it is the only allocation that takes all patients seriously.

[C1.1] The core promise of a health system is that it will not abandon you when you need it most. Prioritizing distant, statistical benefits over the dying person in the bed violates that fiduciary duty. A system that will spend on everyone until they become expensive and then look away teaches patients they are only cost centers.
[C1.2] The efficiency comparison is rigged. Prevention sounds cheap per person, but at the margin we have already picked the easy wins. What remains are mass screenings where hundreds must be screened to help one, lifestyle programs with high dropout, campaigns people ignore. Benefits are probabilistic, decades away, and easily erased by noncompliance.
[C1.3] End-of-life care delivers certain relief and time to an identifiable person now.
[C1.4] And those final weeks are not low-value. The metric that counts them as days times disability insults how humans actually value life. A few lucid weeks to settle affairs, to say goodbye, to hold a grandchild, to try one more line of hope when you choose it — patients and families prize that beyond any spreadsheet. Denying it in the name of population health imposes one philosophy of a good death on everyone.
[C1.5] Trust is also practical. People cooperate with prevention — vaccines, screening, early treatment — because they believe the system will be there if prevention fails. Tell the public that once you are seriously ill and costly you fall to second priority, and that cooperation collapses. You do not get a sustainable, equitable system by sacrificing the dying; you get a cynical one people avoid until too late.
[C1.6] Finally, today's marginal end-of-life treatment is tomorrow's standard cure. Intensive care, transplant surgery, targeted drugs all looked like costly long shots at the edge of death before they became routine. Starve that frontier and you freeze progress for future patients prevention can never save.
