[P1.1] Picture one block of single-family homes near transit in Boston or Seattle twenty years ago. Zoning allowed one house per lot. If the city had then legalized by-right duplexes, fourplexes, and small apartments, every redevelopment, renovation, or vacant lot would have been a chance to add two, three, or ten homes instead of one. Multiply that by tens of thousands of blocks over two decades: hundreds of thousands of missing homes.
[P1.2] That scarcity—not mysterious modernity—is why America’s most productive cities became luxury goods.
[P1.3] Affordability is a supply problem as much as an income problem. When demand rises through jobs and population, prices rise unless supply can expand. For twenty years, cities locked in scarcity: single-family-only zoning, minimum lot sizes, height caps, parking mandates. Builders were not shy about profit; they were barred from meeting demand where it was hottest.
[P1.4] The result was a fixed pool of homes auctioned to the highest bidder. Working- and middle-class renters and buyers lost out; young renters, service workers, and growing families paid the difference.
[P1.5] Two decades is an eternity for compounding supply. Legalize density then and each year’s small projects accumulate: older buildings filter down, land values stabilize, and the stock becomes more elastic. Critics will say costs or the 2008 crash killed construction. But legalization does not need a boom; it works through incremental, profitable projects—accessory units, duplexes on underused lots, fourplexes replacing one aging house—precisely what exclusionary zoning made illegal.
[P1.6] The contrast is visible today. Cities that permitted apartment growth while coastal gateways did not have kept rents far closer to local incomes—not because they are less desirable, but because they let housing be added when people wanted to live there. Follow that path two decades ago and housing would be cheaper today, by the boring arithmetic of more homes where people want them.

[C1.1] The PRO assumes that a zoning change is a housing supply. It is not. Legalizing denser housing twenty years ago would have changed what was permitted, not automatically what was financed, built, rented, or affordable. That distinction decides this debate.
[C1.2] First, permission is not production. Even where dense housing is legal, projects fail because land is expensive, parcels are fragmented, construction costs are high, lenders are cautious, and opposition shifts from zoning hearings to design battles, historic reviews, and environmental challenges. Small builders and missing-middle projects often could not pencil out without subsidy. Upzoning a single-family block near transit might produce a few duplexes, but it does not create the labor force, materials pipeline, or credit conditions needed to add hundreds of thousands of low-cost homes.
[C1.3] Second, earlier legalization could have raised land values before it lowered rents. If a modest house suddenly carried the right to become four units, its price would reflect development potential. That makes entry more expensive for first-time buyers and raises property taxes for long-time owners. It also encourages teardowns of older, naturally affordable housing to make way for newer, smaller, but still market-rate units.
[C1.4] The result can be more churn and displacement, not a cheaper city.
[C1.5] Third, affordability is not just a count of units; it is a relationship between housing costs and incomes. American cities became less affordable in large part because inequality widened, wages stagnated at the bottom, and housing became a financial asset. Even a larger stock would not have guaranteed lower rents for service workers if the new supply was concentrated in expensive neighborhoods or captured by investors.
[C1.6] The PRO must prove not merely that density would have added homes, but that those homes would have been cheap enough, fast enough, and distributed widely enough to make cities more affordable today. Without that proof, the proposition is a planning sketch, not an affordability result.
