[P1.1] A family cannot apply for most jobs, submit schoolwork, book a medical appointment, claim benefits, or run a small business today without reliable internet. That is not convenience; it is the doorway to work, education, health care, and citizenship. When a service becomes necessary for ordinary participation in society, leaving it to whatever the market finds profitable is not neutrality — it is exclusion by design.
[P1.2] Water and electricity followed the same path. Private companies wired profitable cities while rural towns and poor neighborhoods waited, because the return on the last mile is weakest where need is often greatest. Universal service changed the rule: connection became an obligation of the system, not a favor for attractive customers. Internet providers face identical incentives.
[P1.3] They overbuild where affluent subscribers cluster and underinvest, overcharge, or offer degraded service where deployment is harder.
[P1.4] Making access legally enforceable creates the missing floor. Every household could demand a connection meeting a defined standard of speed, reliability, and affordability, and regulators could hold providers and governments to it. That standard need not be frozen in law; broadband thresholds can be reviewed and raised as demand grows, just as electricity rules evolved. Nor does enforceable mean free unlimited luxury.
[P1.5] Like water and power, it means a basic lifeline connection for all at an affordable price, with costs shared through universal-service funds, provider duties, or public investment.
[P1.6] Without that right, we keep the present patchwork: children doing homework in fast-food parking lots, patients unable to use telehealth, rural firms cut off from markets, and jobseekers screened out before an interview. Subsidies that can be cut and corporate promises that can be withdrawn have not closed those gaps, because no one is finally answerable when they persist.
[P1.7] We did not wait for electricity companies to find every village profitable before deciding people deserved light and heat. We should not wait for broadband companies to find every household profitable before deciding people deserve to participate in modern life.

[C1.1] Notice what the motion promised and what the opening delivered. The motion says "high-quality internet," legally enforceable. By [P1.5], that has already shrunk to "a basic lifeline connection." That retreat is not a slip; it is the whole problem.
[C1.2] A legally enforceable right has to be defined precisely enough for a court to order someone to satisfy it. Define it high, and you have created a bottomless obligation. Define it low enough to be satisfiable, and you have enshrined the parking-lot homework connection as the legal standard and declared the job done.
[C1.3] Water and electricity are not moving targets. A liter of clean water in 1950 is a liter of clean water now. Internet "quality" doubles its own demands every few years. Freeze a threshold in statute and it is obsolete before the regulations are drafted; let regulators raise it on their own authority, as [P1.4] suggests, and you have an open-ended mandate that nobody voted on and every provider will litigate.
[C1.4] Either way, the enforceability that is supposed to be the selling point becomes the mechanism of failure.
[C1.5] Then ask who the right runs against. If it runs against providers, you have told every company that entering a market means inheriting a duty to serve its most expensive corners. That is how you get fewer entrants, entrenched incumbents, and cross-subsidies loaded onto everyone's bill. If it runs against the state, you have handed judges the power to order infrastructure spending ahead of schools and hospitals, triggered by whoever files first.
[C1.6] The household at the end of a twenty-mile track can compel a six-figure trench; the thousand families in town who need a cheaper plan cannot.
[C1.7] The real question is how to connect the last five percent. The honest answer is targeted, technology-neutral public money: subsidize satellite, fixed wireless, municipal networks, device and skills programs, wherever each dollar connects the most people. That approach adapts as technology changes. A legal right cannot.
[C1.8] It buys the symbolism of universality at the cost of the flexibility that actually delivers it.

[P2.1] Calling a guaranteed lifeline a retreat from “high-quality” [C1.1] mistakes the standard. High-quality does not mean unlimited premium speed; it means a connection that reliably does what participation now requires — two-way video for class and telehealth, cloud tools for work, forms and files without timing out, for a household at once. A slow, capped, unreliable link is not a lesser version of that right; it fails it. The floor is demanding precisely because it is functional.
[P2.2] The moving-target argument also proves too much. Clean water is not a timeless liter: safety rules have tightened as we learned about lead, bacteria, and chemical contaminants, and electricity service now includes voltage, safety, and reliability standards unimaginable when networks began. Legislatures set the duty and the tests — safety, capability, affordability — and regulators update technical thresholds subject to legislative override. Broadband speed is no harder to revise than contaminant limits.
[P2.3] Obsolescence is an argument for periodic review, not for having no floor at all.
[P2.4] Nor does enforceability require a judge to order a six-figure trench for the first claimant, or one company to wire every hillside. The right runs first against the state as guarantor. Government must produce a funded plan, set deadlines, and answer complaints through a regulator; providers meet shared duties as a condition of licenses, spectrum, and market access, with high-cost areas supported from a common fund. Courts police unreasonable failure to act rather than writing budgets line by line.
[P2.5] That spreads cost instead of deterring entry: no firm bears the last mile alone, and incumbents cannot use expense as an excuse to cherry-pick.
[P2.6] Finally, targeted subsidies are not the alternative to a right; they are how a right gets implemented — by satellite where trenching is wasteful, wireless or fiber where each works best. Without the legal duty, those subsidies remain optional, expirable, and unanswerable when they miss. Enforceability does not remove flexibility. It removes the option to abandon the unprofitable.

[P4.1] A1: Yes — a connection meeting the floor by a fixed date, or an interim service that meets it until permanent infrastructure arrives. A household left out can complain to the regulator, which can name a provider of last resort, order delivery by fiber, fixed wireless, or satellite, impose a deadline and penalties, and require a bill credit if affordability is breached. That individual standing is what enforceability adds beyond a subsidy: under a subsidy, being missed is bad luck; under a right, being missed is a breach that must be cured.
[P4.2] A2: The shared system pays, as for the first build — but progressively, not through a flat surcharge on struggling households. Funding can come from general revenue, spectrum and licence fees, and contributions scaled to provider revenues, paired with an affordability cap and low-income exemptions. Town families therefore gain more than they pay: a legally protected affordable rate outweighs a modest shared contribution. Raising the floor also rarely means re-trenching everywhere; it often means upgrading electronics, capacity, or switching the hardest homes to improved wireless or satellite.
[P4.3] That is how ordinary utility rights actually work. My opponent calls this just a program with a complaints desk [C2.2], yet no water or electricity right works by a judge personally laying pipe. It works through a guarantor state, a regulator, a provider of last resort, and a remedy when a household is denied. That is enforceability in practice, not its absence.
[P4.4] The alternative still has no answer for the child in the parking lot. Targeted money without a floor cannot say when she has received enough, and without a duty it cannot say who must act when she has not. A right turns flexible tools — subsidies, satellite, wireless — from discretionary spending that can lapse into obligations that must finish the job.

If not, the state must identify who will provide it, by what means, and by when.
[P5.3] That duty does not require courts to lay fiber or one company to bear an impossible mile alone. Utilities have never worked that way. They work because the state guarantees the outcome, the regulator assigns delivery, costs are shared, and technology can vary by place. The enforceable floor is what prevents flexibility from becoming abandonment.
[P5.4] Without it, being missed is misfortune and no one bears an individual obligation to cure it. With it, being missed is a breach with a remedy.
[P5.5] Participation in modern life should not depend on whether a household is profitable to serve or lucky in a funding cycle. A right makes universality the requirement, not the aspiration.
