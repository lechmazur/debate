[P1.1] A plane ticket is not a seat alone. It is the promise to get a person and the ordinary belongings they need for the trip to their destination. A carry-on is part of that promise. The proposition simply requires airlines to price the core trip honestly, with a reasonable carry-on baseline included, instead of turning the basic act of flying into a scavenger hunt through add-on menus.
[P1.2] That matters because fragmented baggage pricing defeats real competition. If Airline A advertises $79 and charges $60 at checkout for the bag, while Airline B advertises $119 with the bag included, the consumer cannot meaningfully compare. The first number is not a lower price; it is bait. At checkout, the traveler is tired, the trip is booked, and the fee appears after the emotional and practical commitment is made.
[P1.3] That is not a market serving consumers; it is a market exploiting their limited attention.
[P1.4] The harm is ordinary, not exotic. A student flying home for a funeral, a parent packing a child’s clothes, a business traveler on a two-day trip—none is asking for luxury. They are asking that the advertised ticket get them and a small bag to the destination. When that is not true, every basic fare becomes a negotiation with a gate agent, and boarding slows while passengers argue over sizers and overhead space.
[P1.5] A reasonable baseline also leaves airlines plenty of room to sell extras. Charge for a second carry-on. Charge for checked bags. Charge for oversized items or priority boarding.
[P1.6] But do not pretend the first ordinary bag is an optional add-on when the customer cannot travel without it.
[P1.7] CON will say unbundling lowers fares. If so, include the baseline and let the airline prove the lower fare. Transparency is not a price control; it is the precondition for genuine choice. The side defending hidden fees is defending the right to win by confusing customers.
[P1.8] PRO defends the right to know what the ticket actually buys.
