[P1.1] A university seat is not an inheritance, and it is not a receipt for a parent's check. Sharply limiting legacy and donor preferences means no standing plus, no donor flag, no special review lane; family history may be context, and generosity may earn gratitude, but not a material admissions advantage. The reason is simple: a scarce educational opportunity should turn on the applicant's ability, preparation, curiosity, character, and likely contribution: what this student will do with the education, not what the family already did for the institution.
[P1.2] The unfairness is not symbolic. Selective alumni are not a random sample; they are people who already cleared earlier admissions barriers and often have the wealth, stability, and networks to give their children better schools, tutoring, activities, and confidence. A legacy boost piles a second advantage on top of the first. Donor preference is plainer still: it exists only where a family has enough surplus wealth to make a gift consequential.
[P1.3] Both make published standards conditional, so a stronger applicant can lose to a thinner file because of lineage or a check. That corrodes social mobility, student trust, and the meaning of the degree.
[P1.4] The likely defense is institutional benefit: legacies build loyalty, donors fund aid and buildings. But that confuses a budget model with an educational mission. A seminar does not gain a sharper participant because a parent pledged a gift; a physics class is not more rigorous because a grandfather attended a reunion. Universities can raise money through transparent philanthropy, named scholarships, research support, and alumni programs without selling access.
[P1.5] If the price of a donation is a weaker admit over a stronger one, the gift is not generosity; it is a private purchase of a credential backed by public trust and, often, tax advantages.
[P1.6] Sharp limits work: identical thresholds, audited exceptions, no development-officer advocacy. The opposite principle is indefensible: ancestry or family money may buy a break.

[C1.5] Furthermore, these preferences do not hand degrees to the unqualified. Universities are communities, not state civil-service testing bureaus. Forcing institutions to sharply sever ties with the families and benefactors who sustain them dismantles the financial engine of higher education.
