[P1.1] Death does not convert a person into public raw material. Intimate personality and voice traits are not neutral data; they are the audible signature of a self. A grief bot that simulates them is ventriloquizing the dead, giving them new lines and apparent consent. The question is who may authorize that.
[P1.2] The motion's rule supplies the answer: prior consent from the deceased, or authorization from their estate. Anything less treats the dead as content mined by whoever holds the data.
[P1.3] Because the dead cannot consent in the moment, advance or estate authorization is the only way autonomy survives death. Wills dispose of property, donor registries direct bodies, and estates already manage likeness and publicity; intimate simulation deserves at least that protection. A cloned voice or inferred personality can make the dead endorse products, resolve old arguments, or comfort one relative while wounding another, all without their agreement. Without a consent gate, the default is exploitation by whoever has the data.
[P1.4] The harm is concrete. A sister who uploads a mother's voicemails and asks, "Did she love me less?" may get a fluent answer shaped by the archive's cruellest fragments or the prompter's steering. An abuser with old recordings could build a bot that says, "I forgive you," overriding boundaries the person held in life. The estate exists to represent the deceased's values and stop identity being rewritten by whoever grieves loudest or manipulates best.
[P1.5] The rule is narrow. It does not ban grief technology, photo archives, or quoting letters; it governs only simulation of intimate personality or voice traits—the features that make a bot feel like the person. Companies can still offer generic remembrance. The burden is on CON to explain why the most intimate simulation should be least protected.
[P1.6] Comfort bought by possessing a voice against unknown wishes is not healing; it is a sanctioned haunting. Require consent, and mourning stays human rather than a product.
