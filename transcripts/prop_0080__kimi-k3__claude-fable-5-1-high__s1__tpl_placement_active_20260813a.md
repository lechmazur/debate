[P1.1] When a school pays an athlete a share of the revenue their performance generates, the question of whether this is labor is settled. The only remaining question is whether the people doing the work get a voice in its terms—or whether schools keep writing the rules unilaterally.
[P1.2] Collective bargaining is not radical; it is the ordinary mechanism every other labor market uses to convert unequal power into enforceable terms. Look at what the ad hoc status quo actually delivers: conflicting state laws, booster collectives making promises athletes cannot enforce, no standard contracts, no grievance process when a deal collapses, and eighteen-year-olds negotiating alone against institutions with legal departments.
[P1.3] Schools suffer too: every compensation rule they impose unilaterally is an antitrust target, which is why college sports drowns in litigation. Formal bargaining fixes both failures. Athletes gain what ad hoc systems structurally cannot provide—negotiated medical coverage beyond eligibility, injury protection, transfer rules they actually consented to, and a neutral arbitrator instead of an appeal to the same department that disciplined them. Schools gain the one thing no compliance office can supply: stability, because terms reached through genuine collective bargaining are shielded from antitrust attack.
[P1.4] That is how every professional league governs itself, and it is the exemption college sports keeps begging Congress for—earned rather than gifted.
[P1.5] My opponent will warn that bargaining professionalizes college sports or crushes smaller programs. But bargaining is a process, not an outcome: the parties remain free to negotiate academic standards, roster limits, and revenue splits that sustain non-revenue teams. What it removes is unilateralism—one side dictating every term and calling it amateurism.
[P1.6] The weighing standard is simple: which system produces legitimate, enforceable, stable rules? Ad hoc NIL fails all three tests; collective bargaining is designed to pass them. Once revenue flows directly from school to athlete, representation is not a threat to college sports—it is the missing institution that makes the new reality governable.

Michigan can recognize a union; Alabama, Texas, Georgia, and Florida largely cannot. A "national" bargaining unit for college football is a fiction under existing law.
[C1.3] What PRO actually proposes is a fractured landscape where some conferences bargain and others can't—the opposite of the uniform, stable rules promised in [P1.6]. And the antitrust shield in [P1.3] only covers terms reached with a genuine union of genuine employees. Where that's impossible, the lawsuits continue exactly as before.
[C1.4] Now the second-order effects. Employees pay income tax on compensation, so scholarships become taxable. International athletes on student visas generally cannot be employed—they'd be pushed out overnight. Workers' compensation, overtime, and hiring law follow.
[C1.5] Athletic departments facing that bill respond the way every employer does: fewer positions. The swimmer, the wrestler, the walk-on—these are the people cut to fund a football payroll. PRO calls bargaining "a process, not an outcome," but the precondition dictates the outcome.
[C1.6] Then ask who bargains. A union is dominated by whoever has leverage: revenue-sport starters. They cycle out in three years and bind incoming freshmen who never voted. Professional unions have a long record of trading away rookies and minor leaguers to protect veterans.
[C1.7] Import that dynamic and you've built an institution structurally hostile to the very athletes PRO says it protects.
[C1.8] The genuine problems—unenforceable collective promises, absent grievance channels, inconsistent medical coverage—are contract and rule problems. They can be fixed with standardized agreements, court-supervised settlement terms, neutral arbitration, and mandated protections that bind schools without making teenagers taxable employees. Fix the terms. Don't blow up the classroom to get them.
