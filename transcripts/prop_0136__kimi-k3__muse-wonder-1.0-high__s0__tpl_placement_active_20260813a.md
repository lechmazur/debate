[P1.1] A couple in Seoul deciding whether to have a child does not ask: can we afford the hospital bill? They ask: can we ever afford an apartment big enough for a family, and who will care for the child while both of us work? Those are the questions East Asian governments must answer, and a one-time cheque answers neither.
[P1.2] First, the cost structure. Childbearing in East Asia is gated by recurring, structural costs. Housing is the prerequisite: in Seoul, Tokyo, Taipei and Shanghai, home prices have run far ahead of incomes, and young adults respond by delaying marriage and first births — delays that quietly become permanently foregone births. Childcare is the recurring burden: where affordable full-day care is scarce, motherhood means leaving the workforce, and couples rationally stop at one child or none.
[P1.3] A bonus of a few thousand dollars is a rounding error against decades of these costs; it cannot move a decision anchored in them.
[P1.4] Second, the record. South Korea has spent enormous sums on pro-natalist policy for two decades, much of it weighted toward lump-sum baby grants from central and local governments — and fertility kept sliding to the lowest in the world, well under one child per woman. Where bonuses do anything, studies of baby bonuses find they mostly shift the timing of births that would have happened anyway: a bump one year, a dip the next, no change in completed fertility.
[P1.5] Third, the contrast. Societies that built out affordable childcare and family housing — France and the Nordics are the familiar cases — sustained fertility markedly higher than East Asia's, and within Japan, municipalities with better childcare access show smaller fertility declines. The mechanism is consistent: remove the recurring constraint and births follow; hand over cash and nothing structural changes.
[P1.6] The question is which lever raises birth rates more. Bonuses buy timing; housing and childcare buy decisions. Only the second changes how many children East Asia's couples actually have.
