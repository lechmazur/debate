[P1.1] Every traffic death is, at bottom, a physics problem. A pedestrian struck at roughly twenty miles an hour usually walks away; struck at forty, they usually die. Speed is not one risk factor among many—it is the multiplier on every other mistake a driver makes. So the question is simple: which tool actually changes the speed of the traffic on the road?
[P1.2] Police stops cannot. An officer catches perhaps one speeder in hundreds, at one spot, for a few hours, and drivers know it. The ticket feels like bad luck, not consequence. Deterrence research has said for decades that certainty of punishment matters far more than severity, and a stop-based system is the definition of uncertain punishment.
[P1.3] Cameras invert that. Every car, every hour, every day. Drivers slow down not because they fear a patrol car they will probably never meet, but because they know the road itself is watching. Where cameras have been deployed at scale—France's nationwide rollout in the early 2000s, Britain's fixed camera network, New York City's school-zone program—speeding and serious crashes fell sharply and stayed down.
[P1.4] That is what a real safety gain looks like: behavior change across a whole population, not a handful of citations.
[P1.5] Now the externalities. A camera photographs a license plate. It does not know the driver's race, cannot smell anything, cannot claim an air freshener was hanging suspiciously. The traffic stop is the single most common way ordinary people encounter armed police, and it is where Philando Castile and Daunte Wright died over a taillight and an expired registration.
[P1.6] Stops are also where officers themselves are killed roadside. Scaling stops up scales all of that up. Scaling cameras up scales none of it.
[P1.7] Cameras are also cheaper per infraction, freeing officers for what machines cannot do—impaired driving, reckless behavior, actual crime. The motion asks for larger gains and fewer harms. Automated enforcement delivers both, and traditional stops fail on both.
