[P1.1] A continent that grows cocoa but imports chocolate, pumps oil but buys back petrol, is not trading—it is being taxed by someone else's factory. Prioritizing intra-African trade and manufacturing is not autarky; it is the route from price-taker to price-maker.
[P1.2] Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire dominate global cocoa exports, yet most of the grinding, branding, and retail profit is captured in Europe. Nigeria exports crude yet imports refined fuel. This is not comparative advantage; it is a value-capture gap created by colonial infrastructure built to move raw goods to ports, not to connect African producers to African consumers.
[P1.3] Commodity dependence also concentrates risk. A slump in copper, oil, or cocoa can gut budgets, currencies, and jobs, because drilling and mining employ few people and buy little from local suppliers. Agro-processing and manufacturing spread demand across transport, packaging, finance, and services, creating the tax base governments actually need.
[P1.4] The better model already has scale: the African Continental Free Trade Area links over a billion people. The obstacle is not African demand; roads, rails, and regulations still point outward, making it easier to ship raw cobalt to Asia than to assemble batteries in the DRC or Zambia.
[P1.5] Opponents will call raw exports "realistic" and factories "premature." But "realistic" means cheap for distant buyers and costly for African sellers: someone else captures processing and branding. "Premature" is a choice to export unemployment along with copper and cocoa.
[P1.6] The question is not whether Africa can abandon all commodity sales tomorrow. It is which priority builds resilience, jobs, and bargaining power. If the measure is steady employment, broad diversification, and real leverage in global markets — not merely foreign-exchange earnings — prioritizing regional manufacturing and trade beats continued dependence on exporting raw materials to former colonial powers and distant rich markets. That is the case PRO will defend.

[C1.1] The fatal word in this motion is "over." Nobody denies that more processing, more factories, and more regional trade would benefit Africa. The question is whether governments should deliberately shift priority away from the one engine that currently pays for everything else. They should not — because that engine funds the very transformation at issue.
[C1.2] Start with the balance sheet. Raw material exports remain the only proven, at-scale source of foreign exchange for most African states. Power grids, ports, technical schools, industrial credit — every input manufacturing requires is bought with commodity earnings. Botswana did not build its institutions by de-emphasizing diamonds; it used diamond revenue to finance everything else.
[C1.3] A country does not get richer by downgrading its only reliable payer.
[C1.4] Second, this experiment has been run. In the 1960s and 70s, Ghana, Tanzania, and others prioritized state-led manufacturing for protected markets. The result was high-cost goods nobody bought voluntarily, drained treasuries, and the debt crises of the 1980s. Rebranding import substitution as "intra-African" does not fix its core defect: insufficient purchasing power.
[C1.5] Intra-African trade sits at roughly 15 percent of the continent's commerce — not mainly because of colonial railways, but because neighboring economies produce similar things and their consumers are poor. A free-trade area cannot manufacture wallets.
[C1.6] Third, notice the loaded geography. "Former colonial powers and distant rich markets" is meant to sound like a trap; rich is the operative word. South Korea, then China, then Vietnam industrialized precisely by selling manufactures to wealthy, distant buyers while upgrading at home. The customer with money is not a neocolonial plot; he is the revenue.
[C1.7] Steering sales toward poorer neighbors instead is sentiment paid for in lost jobs.
[C1.8] Fourth, risk cuts both ways. Anchoring industry to thin regional demand concentrates shocks just as surely as commodity cycles do. Resilience means diversifying markets — adding regional trade atop a strong export base, not building on its rubble.
[C1.9] The serious path is sequencing: extract, earn, invest in capability, process where competitive, and sell to whoever pays most. Prioritize competitiveness, not compass [clipped]
