[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether artificial intelligence can imitate artistic influence. It is who must authorize a synthetic performance sold to the public as the work of a particular dead artist.
[P1.2] Imagine a label releasing a “new” track generated from a deceased singer’s voice, phrasing, imagery, and musical persona. Listeners may reasonably believe the artist participated, approved the release, and would have wanted its profits to benefit their estate. If no one authorized this commercial resurrection, the label has appropriated more than a general style: it has manufactured a new performance around a marketable identity and captured revenue that properly belongs with the artist’s representatives. That creates both deception for audiences and unfair competition for labels that negotiate honestly with estates.
[P1.3] Explicit permission establishes a clear chain of authorization. It would not grant estates exclusive ownership of artistic styles, nor prevent living artists from learning from earlier work. One permission could authorize one project without restricting other creators. It simply prevents a studio from commercially deploying an artist’s distinctive performance identity without consent.
[P1.4] This standard also preserves AI’s benefits. Studios and labels would still be free to use licensed models, commission consenting estates, create wholly synthetic artists, or develop performances that evoke broad genres without impersonating a specific dead artist. What they could not do is appropriate another creator’s persona for profit without approval. Requiring permission turns consent from an abstract principle into a routine contract and approval step.
[P1.5] The alternative is a self-help regime in which whichever platform can manufacture the most convincing performance captures the market. Consent prevents that race to commodify dead artists before their estates can decide whether their identities should be used, how, and for whose benefit. That is a narrow safeguard against synthetic exploitation, not a ban on artistic imitation or technological innovation.

A living tribute act performs with its own voice and labor and can bargain with the label; an AI “extension” uses the dead artist as the performed source without any bargained performer. If a label can manufacture new releases in that persona merely because it labels them synthetic, the artist’s representatives are excluded from a product that appropriates that persona and substitutes for an authorized release.
[P5.2] CON insists this is merely competition [C4.2]. But competition between two new works is not permission to create the first work in someone else’s commercially recognizable voice and manner. Copyright, trademark, and false-endorsement law do not answer that act: no recording need be copied, and the lie can be avoided. The product’s market appeal comes precisely from the dead artist’s identity.
[P5.3] The motion should therefore operate around artist-specific resemblance: when a generated performance is marketed around a particular deceased artist’s recognizable vocal or performative manner, permission is required; shared genre techniques remain free. A required yes is not a perpetual estate veto: another studio may seek its own license, and refusal affects that project rather than all cultural influence. That is the line that distinguishes legitimate imitation from commercial appropriation.
