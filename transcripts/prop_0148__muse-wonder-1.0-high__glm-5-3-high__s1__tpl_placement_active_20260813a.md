[P1.1] A sixteen-year-old does not become competent at midnight on her eighteenth birthday. If she has never sent a message unmonitored, chosen a route on her own, or spent an afternoon with friends without location tracking, adulthood arrives not as a step but as a cliff. A growing right to private communication and movement is how families avoid that cliff: freedom expands with age and demonstrated responsibility, instead of child-style monitoring holding firm until it suddenly disappears.
[P1.2] That growth is not indulgence; it is training. Adolescence is when judgment is built by using it — deciding who to trust, how to answer a cruel text, whether to leave a party early, how to get home safely. Constant surveillance outsources those decisions to a parent watching a screen. The teen learns performance under observation, not responsibility when no one is watching.
[P1.3] Small, survivable mistakes at fourteen and fifteen, reflected on with support, are precisely what prevent larger, unsupervised mistakes at nineteen.
[P1.4] Privacy also protects the relationships and safety monitoring claims to serve. A teenager who expects every diary entry, search, and conversation with a friend to be inspected will not become more honest; she will become more secretive, using deleted threads, borrowed phones, and false destinations. She will hesitate to seek help about bullying, mental distress, sexuality, or a friend in trouble if disclosure means total exposure. Parents learn more, earlier, from a child who volunteers information because trust is reciprocal than from data extracted after trust is gone.
[P1.5] This right is graduated, not absolute. A twelve-year-old needs tighter boundaries than a seventeen-year-old, and concrete evidence of serious danger can justify a focused intervention. What it rejects is the default of indefinite, blanket surveillance — reading all messages, tracking all movement, approving all contacts — regardless of maturity. Families should be raising future adults through progressively real freedom, not keeping teenagers as monitored children until the law abruptly declares them free.

[C1.1] The proposition's own words give the game away: it asks us to convert parental judgment into a teenager's "right." Once it is a right, it is no longer something parents can expand, pause, or revoke based on the child in front of them — it is something the child is entitled to enforce. That is the real choice here, not "cliff versus ramp."
[C1.2] Parents are not just observers of adolescence; they are legally and morally accountable for it. If a fifteen-year-old is groomed online, cyberbullied into crisis, or hurt wandering at midnight, it is the parents who answer for it — to the school, to the hospital, sometimes to the law. It is incoherent to hand full responsibility for outcomes to parents while removing their access to the information they need to discharge it. A right to unmonitored communication and movement, even a "growing" one, severs exactly that link.
[C1.3] And the risks are not hypothetical parental anxiety. Sextortion, predator contact through anonymous apps, drug purchases arranged by message, suicidal ideation hidden in private chats — these are the concrete ways unsupervised teen communication goes wrong, and they are precisely the cases where early parental sight of the messages is the difference between intervention and tragedy. PRO says surveillance drives secrecy; I say secrecy driven by fear of exposure is survivable, while a crisis nobody adult ever sees is not.
[C1.4] Notice, too, that PRO's own standard — "graduated, not absolute," exceptions for "concrete evidence of serious danger" — collapses into what parents already do. Families have never monitored a seventeen-year-old like a seven-year-old. Norms have always loosened with age and maturity. The only thing the motion adds is a litmus line the teen can invoke against her own parents: you owe me this privacy, now.
[C1.5] Good development of judgment happens inside relationships of accountability, not inside spaces adults are forbidden to enter. The burden is on PRO to show why making privacy enforceable against parents helps teens more than it blinds the adults responsible for them.

[P2.1] A "growing right" is not a lock parents can never open. It is a shifting presumption: as teenagers mature, parents need an increasingly good reason to read their messages or track every movement, rather than teenagers needing permission for any privacy at all. Intervention remains possible for concrete danger. What ends is revocation based on anxiety, curiosity, or habit — the indefinite child-style monitoring at issue.
[P2.2] The accountability argument proves too much. If responsibility for a fifteen-year-old required total information, it would justify listening to every call and searching her room daily until eighteen. Parental duty means reasonable, proportionate oversight, not omniscience or a guarantee against every bad outcome. Responsibility must also transfer gradually; retaining total control teaches a teenager she is never finally answerable for her own choices.
[P2.3] Grooming, sextortion, and suicidal crisis justify targeted access, not blanket surveillance. Those harms migrate to disappearing messages, coded language, and second accounts when teenagers expect total inspection, while thousands of ordinary texts bury warning signs in noise and create false confidence. Fast intervention depends on a teenager bringing a frightening message to a parent early, confident she will receive help rather than confiscation and exposure of every unrelated conversation. Privacy is often the condition for disclosure.
[P2.4] If families already loosen controls with maturity, that concedes the developmental logic; the real dispute is the default. Without a growing right, loosening is a revocable gift granted when parents feel comfortable, which can lag years behind actual competence. With it, expansion is the normal trajectory and restriction needs justification by the teenager in front of them, not by the mere technical ability to monitor everything. Private also does not mean unaccountable: teenagers can still report where they are going, meet curfews, and face consequences — without surrendering the content of every conversation.

[P4.1] A1: Parents do — immediately. A growing right within a family is not a court order or a teenage veto; it is the standard parents judge themselves by. If a parent sees cause, she checks first, acts first, and no dispute suspends intervention. What changes is what she must be able to say afterwards: not “because I can,” but “because your behaviour changed in this specific way.” The teenager can ask for that reason, and should get it.
[P4.2] The power to intervene stays; the entitlement to permanent, unexplained access does not.

Blanket collection also makes warning signs harder, not easier, to see: panic stands out against a trusted baseline; it disappears in a haystack of routine messages that create false reassurance while the real conversation moves elsewhere.
[P4.5] Parents are not made responsible by omniscience. Offline, they remain responsible while not hearing every classroom conversation or following every walk home; they watch behaviour, set curfews, and intervene on change.
