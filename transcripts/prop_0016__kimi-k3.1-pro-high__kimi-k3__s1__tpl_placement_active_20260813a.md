[P1.1] A city can clear a sidewalk by morning and still have exactly the same number of people sleeping outside that night. That is the difference between managing the appearance of homelessness and reducing homelessness. Housing and service access must lead, with policing reserved for genuine threats rather than used as a substitute for solutions.
[P1.2] First, match the tool to the cause. People become and remain homeless through combinations of unaffordable housing, lost income, illness, disability, violence, and discharge from institutions without support. An arrest, citation, or move-along order repairs none of these. It often makes escape harder: belongings and medication are discarded, identification is lost, appointments are missed, and outreach workers must find people again.
[P1.3] A tent moved from one block to another is not a public-safety success; it is the same crisis with a new location.
[P1.4] Second, housing makes services work. Someone managing psychosis, addiction, or diabetes cannot reliably keep appointments, store medication, or plan recovery while being displaced between sidewalks and shelters. Low-barrier housing, paired with persistent outreach, mental-health care, addiction treatment, and income support, turns contact into continuity. A home is not a substitute for treatment; it is the platform that makes treatment and employment attainable.
[P1.5] Nor does this mean tolerating violence, fires, or harassment. Police should address dangerous conduct. But conduct enforcement is a backstop, not a homelessness strategy. When sleeping or camping becomes the central offense, cities cycle vulnerable people through courts, jails, emergency rooms, and encampment clearances, while residents still face the next encampment.
[P1.6] Public safety also includes homeless people themselves, who are exposed to assault, exploitation, and lethal weather.
[P1.7] The decisive test is not whether a city can produce a temporarily clear street. It is whether fewer people remain unsheltered, more retain housing, and more can actually reach care. A housing-and-services approach can improve order by removing its underlying cause; an enforcement-first approach can, at best, improve order briefly by hiding it. Cities should stop asking police to make poverty disappear and build the exits that make street life unnecessary.

[C1.1] Notice what that opening image concedes: the sidewalk matters. Clear it by morning and something real happened — a child walks to school, a shop opens, a bus gets boarded. PRO calls that "managing appearance." The millions who depend on public space call it a city doing its first job.
[C1.2] Homelessness policy is not only about the homeless; it is about whether parks, transit, and libraries stay usable for everyone, including the working poor one paycheck from the street.
[C1.3] Three reasons housing-first fails as the primary strategy.
[C1.4] First, it has been tried at scale, and it lost. California poured tens of billions into homelessness while the unsheltered population grew; supportive units in Los Angeles and San Francisco have run well over half a million dollars each and taken years to build. A strategy that cannot house its way out at plausible cost or speed cannot be primary; it can only be one tool.
[C1.5] Second, the cause framing misdescribes who is on the street. The visibly unsheltered — the encampments driving this debate — are disproportionately people gripped by fentanyl, methamphetamine, or untreated psychosis, not families one rent check short. Voluntary offers are routinely refused, and housing without treatment requirements too often becomes a place to keep using, sometimes to die. Enforcement is not the enemy of treatment; it is the leverage — camping bans, drug courts, civil commitment create the pressure that pure outreach cannot.
[C1.6] Third, order is the precondition of compassion, not its competitor. Cities that let disorder metastasize — San Francisco, Portland, Los Angeles — watched employers flee, tax bases shrink, and voters revolt against the very spending PRO wants. New York, pairing a shelter guarantee with rules actually enforced, keeps the overwhelming share of its homeless off the street; the tolerance-first coast left them in tents.
[C1.7] The motion asks what comes first. Every failed experiment gives the same answer: not the waiting list.
