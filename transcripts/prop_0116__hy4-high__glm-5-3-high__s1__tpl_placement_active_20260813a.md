[P1.1] The core issue is who gets to treat a failing program as costless. A university can collect tuition up front, set cohort size, design the curriculum, promise career relevance, and certify that the program is worth borrowing for. If several graduating cohorts still earn too little relative to the debt they were encouraged to take on, students carry the damaged credit, families carry the anxiety, and taxpayers carry the default. The institution keeps the revenue.
[P1.2] That is a one-way bet.
[P1.3] Requiring schools to bear part of default costs repairs that incentive without treating every low salary as institutional fault. The trigger is narrow: repeated outcomes, at the program level, comparing earnings with debt. A recession, a bad year, or a graduate choosing low-paid public service does not create liability. A program that keeps enrolling students at a price its graduates cannot sustain does.
[P1.4] Universities control the variables that make that pattern predictable: admissions claims, tuition levels, program expansion, employer links, and whether warning signs are acted upon or buried beneath fresh recruiting.
[P1.5] Risk-sharing also answers the information problem. An applicant sees a brochure, an aid package, and borrowed money that feels like access; the school sees completion rates, job placement, borrowing levels, and prior cohorts. When the better-informed party can externalize the downside, weak programs are too easy to sustain and too easy to market. A modest default charge would make a dean ask whether another cohort should be admitted, tuition cut, the curriculum changed, or the program closed.
[P1.6] Those are decisions we want before students borrow, not after they default.
[P1.7] Nor is this an attack on access. Loan subsidies make enrollment look cheap while hiding program failure until the bill comes due. If universities want loan dollars to fill classrooms, they should stand behind programs that repeatedly leave graduates with earnings too weak to carry the debt. Shared liability is the price of honest pricing.

[C1.1] The proposition fails on its own terms: it uses default as a proxy for institutional fault, and that proxy is broken. Graduate earnings reflect labor markets, family wealth, regional economies, and career choice far more than they reflect curriculum quality. A theater program in a depressed city and a nursing program in a boomtown can teach equally well and land on opposite sides of any earnings-to-debt trigger. Punish the school for the first, and you have not disciplined a bad actor—you have punished geography.
[C1.2] The deeper problem is who actually gets squeezed. Faced with default liability, universities will not heroically cut tuition. They will manage risk the way every lender does: avoid it. That means creaming admissions—fewer first-generation students, fewer students from under-resourced high schools, fewer adult learners returning after a layoff.
[C1.3] The students this policy claims to protect are precisely the ones whose earnings trajectories are hardest to predict. Risk-sharing becomes a quiet admissions ban dressed up as accountability.
[C1.4] Consider what the proposal does to low-wage, high-mission fields. Teachers, social workers, counselors, clergy, artists: these programs reliably produce modest earnings. If repeated low earnings-to-debt ratios create liability, rational universities shrink or close them—not because they fail students, but because society underpays the work. The policy taxes exactly the programs a civil society most needs.
[C1.5] There is also a fiscal shell game here. Most enrollment in this country is at public universities. Making "the university" pay means making state legislatures pay—through appropriations, tuition hikes, or program closures. The taxpayer default cost PRO worries about doesn't vanish; it circulates back with administrative friction attached.
[C1.6] For community colleges and regional publics operating on thin margins, a default charge is a closure accelerant, and mid-degree closure is the worst outcome a borrower can face.
[C1.7] The one-way bet PRO describes is real, but it runs through lending policy and consumer information—loan limits, transparent outcome disclosure—not through forcing schools to underwrite debts they did not originate. Fix the information gap at the point of borrowing. Don't rig outcomes by penalizing schools for wages they don't [clipped]

[P4.1] A1: The trigger fires on earnings relative to debt because that ratio is the harm, not a verdict on teaching. Price alone cannot measure sustainability: high tuition with strong earnings is different from modest tuition still financed by living-cost borrowing against weak wages. The rule distinguishes by repetition, program-level results, actual defaults, and a partial charge scaled to debt. So a high-debt predatory program has large exposure; a low-tuition public has smaller exposure and can respond through net price, borrowing limits, placement, or warnings.
[P4.2] If both keep producing unpayable debt, both should feel some cost.
[P4.3] A2: The feature is that liability follows program debt outcomes across cohorts, not an admissions label. Excluding "risky" students does not erase defaults already embedded in current cohorts, does not earn relief from repeated triggers, and forfeits enrollment revenue. Cutting price or adding aid reduces the debt that creates the charge, keeps students enrolled, and lowers defaults. A dean may still cream-skim, but risk-sharing gives no discount for it; it rewards lowering unaffordable borrowing.
[P4.4] CON's "cheap compliance strategy" [C2.2] is cheap only if the school may keep the same price and pretend risk vanished.
[P4.5] These answers settle the comparison. CON demands perfect moral sorting before any consequence, yet disclosure and loan caps do not sort: caps ration credit without asking why the price was set; disclosure can mark a poor-region program as risky and scare away the students CON wants to protect. Neither places recurring default cost on the institution that set tuition, recruited the cohort, chose its size, and held prior-outcome data. Liability does.
[P4.6] Under CON's approach, the school keeps revenue and enrolls the next cohort; under shared liability, repeated unaffordable outcomes become a budget consequence before the next cohort borrows. The issue is not proving every low wage is a school's fault. It is refusing to let the seller of a repeatedly unpayable debt load be the only party insulated from the loss.
