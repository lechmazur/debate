[P1.1] Democracy thrives when those affected by the laws have a say in making them. Lowering the voting age to sixteen aligns our democratic principles with the reality of adolescent capacity and responsibility.
[P1.2] First, sixteen is the optimal age to foster lifelong civic habits. When a person turns eighteen, their life is in maximum transition. [P1.3] At sixteen, however, most teenagers are in a stable environment. They are still rooted in their communities, living with family, and attending high school, where civic education can be directly paired with the act of casting a ballot. Empowering them at sixteen bridges the gap between learning about democracy and participating in it, creating lifelong voters rather than disengaged young adults.
[P1.4] Second, sixteen-year-olds are already full stakeholders in our society. They can legally work, and when they do, their income is taxed. Denying them the vote is literal taxation without representation. [P1.5] Excluding them from the ballot box denies them a voice in the very systems that govern their lives.
[P1.6] Finally, the standard objection—that sixteen-year-olds lack the maturity to vote—misunderstands cognitive development. While "hot cognition," which governs impulsive, heat-of-the-moment decisions, matures later, "cold cognition" is fully developed by sixteen. Cold cognition involves gathering information, weighing options, and making deliberate choices. Voting is the quintessential exercise of cold cognition.
[P1.7] A sixteen-year-old takes weeks or months to research candidates and deliberate, utilizing the same logical reasoning capacity as an older adult.
[P1.8] By lowering the voting age to sixteen, we strengthen democratic legitimacy, permanently boost voter turnout through early habit formation, and give a disenfranchised group of taxpaying stakeholders the voice they both earn and deserve.
