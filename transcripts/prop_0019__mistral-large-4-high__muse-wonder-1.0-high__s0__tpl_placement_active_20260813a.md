[P1.1] A rental market works only if each landlord sets rent independently. When competing landlords plug confidential, real-time data into the same rent-setting platform and then follow its recommended prices, they have replaced independent pricing with a common pricing formula. That is coordination wearing software clothes. The relevant antitrust question is not whether two managers met in a smoke-filled room, but whether competitors have outsourced the price decision to a shared agent.
[P1.2] They have.
[P1.3] The mechanism matters. The tool ingests rivals’ vacancy rates, concessions, renewal offers, and asking rents; it learns what competitors are doing; it recommends rents that stabilize or raise the local market; and it makes deviations visible and punishable. Landlords can therefore achieve the object of a price-fixing cartel—supracompetitive, parallel rents—without a single explicit conversation. A hub-and-spoke theory fits: the platform is the hub, competing landlords are the spokes, and adherence to the algorithm is the agreement among the spokes.
[P1.4] Even without proving the vendor’s intent, each landlord’s knowing participation and benefit can support scrutiny.
[P1.5] This is not an attack on software or on a landlord using lawful market reports. It targets a shared tool used across competitors to align rents. Independent adoption of a genuinely one-sided efficiency tool would be different; mass adoption of a common recommendation engine after feeding it rival data is not independence. Efficiencies such as better forecasting can be argued, but they must be proven and weighed against the elimination of price competition.
[P1.6] Scrutiny means real discovery into inputs, design, communications, adoption rates, and price effects—not automatic condemnation.
[P1.7] Direct coordination is condemned because it fixes the central term of competition. Algorithmic coordination fixes the same term through a common input and output. Antitrust scrutiny should follow the function, not the form: if it walks like a cartel, prices like a cartel, and profits from reduced competition like a cartel, regulators should treat it as one.

[C1.1] Using the same software is not the same as making the same deal. Direct coordination requires competitors to agree with each other — to surrender independent judgment for a joint price. A landlord who buys a rent-recommendation tool has agreed, at most, with a vendor about a service. That vertical purchase does not become a horizontal agreement among rivals merely because the vendor has other customers.
[C1.2] That distinction tracks how pricing actually happens. These tools typically estimate what a unit might fetch given location, size, season, vacancies, and broader market trends. The landlord can accept, raise, or lower the suggestion. Two landlords using one platform can still post different rents for similar units, and one who blindly follows a bad recommendation loses tenants.
[C1.3] In direct coordination, the point is to remove that risk by committing rivals not to undercut each other. No commitment, no enforcement, and no mutual assurance means no cartel equivalent.
[C1.4] Collapsing the two would swallow ordinary competition. Landlords already look at the same public listings, appraisal methods, broker reports, and market forecasts. Common inputs naturally produce similar prices — two gas stations charge alike not because they conspired, but because they face the same customers and costs. Antitrust has long treated parallel behavior alone as insufficient because independent firms responding to shared information is how markets work.
[C1.5] The cost of this shortcut is not abstract. It would expose landlords, including small owners without in-house analysts, to cartel-level suspicion for forecasting tools that reduce vacancies and match supply to demand. Large operators would keep proprietary systems internally; smaller competitors would lose the data that helps them compete.
[C1.6] Scrutiny should turn on evidence of an actual agreement: communications among rivals, pooling current confidential plans with a commitment to follow them, or punishment for discounting. Without that, buying the same tool is competition using technology, not conspiracy by algorithm.

[P2.1] The distinction between a vertical software purchase and a horizontal price agreement collapses once you recognize what the platform actually does. This is not a landlord buying a spreadsheet; it is competitors feeding one hub their confidential, real-time pricing data—vacancy rates, concessions, renewal offers—and receiving back a coordinated rent. That is a hub-and-spoke arrangement: the vendor is the hub, the landlords are spokes, and adherence to the algorithm is the horizontal agreement. Antitrust law has long reached conspiracies where the agreement is facilitated by a common agent, even without a smoke-filled room.
[P2.2] The claim that landlords retain "independent judgment" because they can override the suggestion ignores how these systems function in practice. When the algorithm learns what rivals are charging and recommends prices that stabilize the market, deviation is visible. A landlord who undercuts the recommendation signals non-compliance to every other user. That is not independence; that is surveillance and discipline dressed as a suggestion.
[P2.3] The "commitment" is not a signed contract—it is the structural incentive to follow the shared formula or be punished by the market visibility the tool creates.
[P2.4] Nor is this ordinary parallel behavior. Two gas stations charging similar prices after reading the same public sign is competition. Competitors sharing confidential, non-public data through a common intermediary to align pricing is not. The input is different: one is public market information, the other is rivals' proprietary strategic data.
[P2.5] Finally, scrutiny does not condemn small landlords; it protects them. Discovery into the algorithm's inputs and design reveals whether the tool facilitates coordination, while leaving lawful forecasting tools untouched. Without scrutiny, small owners face a market where large operators use shared algorithms to suppress price competition, and the small landlord is left guessing whether the market is rigged. The function is coordination; the form is software.
[P2.6] Antitrust should follow the function.

[C2.1] A hub-and-spoke cartel still needs a rim: evidence the competitors understood they were joining a common scheme with each other, not merely buying from a common supplier. PRO has supplied spokes and a vendor, but no rim. Knowing the platform has other customers is not an agreement among those customers to fix rent, any more than using the same bank, insurer, or listing service makes rivals conspirators. The rim is the conspiracy.
[C2.2] That gap matters because PRO's discipline story assumes what it must prove. A landlord who rejects a recommendation and offers $100 less is not automatically exposed by name to rivals who can retaliate; at most, the vendor sees performance and tenants see a cheaper listing. Being outcompeted for tenants is market punishment for pricing badly, not cartel punishment for cheating. A suggestion you can profit by ignoring when it is too high is the opposite of the mutual assurance direct coordination creates.
[C2.3] The confidential-data argument likewise proves too much about some possible designs and too little about shared tools as a category. Supplying your own vacancies to a vendor that returns an aggregated forecast is not the same as handing rivals your future pricing plan and promising to follow theirs. Our standard already subjects real pooling-plus-commitment to scrutiny. The proposition sweeps far beyond it, treating every shared user as if the exceptional case were established.
[C2.4] Finally, “just scrutiny” is not costless when the benchmark is direct coordination. That label invites presumptive illegality, intrusive investigations, and expensive litigation before independence is even tested. Large firms can absorb that or build private models; a small owner who bought forecasting help cannot. PRO cannot obtain cartel-level scrutiny by relabeling discovery as benign, then defer the actual questions — agreement, enforcement, and market effect — that distinguish a cartel from competition.

If discovery shows no confidential rival data is pooled, or that landlords routinely and profitably deviate from recommendations without retaliation, scrutiny ends and the tool is vindicated. [P4.2] A2: The line is drawn by inputs and mechanics, not vendor brand. A broker or public appraisal processes public information; a rent algorithm deserving scrutiny ingests competitors' confidential, non-public, real-time data—vacancy rates, concessions, renewal strategies—and returns prices that align rivals while making discounting visible to all users. That combination—confidential strategic pooling plus adherence discipline—is the test. Common use alone is insufficient; coordinated use of confidential rival data is.
[P4.3] That distinction resolves the debate. CON frames scrutiny as punishing small landlords for buying software, but the investigation targets the architecture of coordination itself: a hub collecting rivals' confidential strategies and a spoke structure that disciplines deviation. When competitors feed one agent their non-public vacancy rates and concessions, then follow its rent recommendations, they have constructed the functional equivalent of a cartel meeting. The algorithm becomes the agreement; the data exchange is the communication; the visibility into undercutting is the enforcement mechanism.
[P4.4] Treating this "as if coordinating directly" is what allows regulators to pierce the software and examine the cartel operating beneath. CON warns of chilling effects on forecasting tools, but the test leaves genuine efficiency tools untouched—those using only public data or lacking surveillance of deviation. What it captures is precisely the case where competitors have outsourced their pricing independence to a shared agent. That is not hostility to technology; it is recognizing that price-fixing has changed its clothes.
