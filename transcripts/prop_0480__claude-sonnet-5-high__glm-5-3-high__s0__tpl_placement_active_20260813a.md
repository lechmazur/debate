[P1.1] Every dollar a government spends is a choice about what kind of security a society guarantees. Cash transfers guarantee a number in your account. Universal basic services guarantee that the thing you actually need - a doctor, a bus, a broadband line, a childcare slot - exists and is affordable when you need it. That distinction is the whole debate, and it favors PRO.
[P1.2] Start with market power. Cash only helps you if a decent, affordable option exists to spend it on. In a town with one overpriced clinic, one landlord, or no broadband provider at all, handing people money just lets the local monopolist raise prices to capture it. We've watched this happen with housing vouchers pushing up rents in tight markets.
[P1.3] Universal basic services sidestep this entirely by building or guaranteeing the supply directly, at negotiated, often lower, per-person cost, because governments can bargain collectively and run services at scale in a way an individual with a subsidy check cannot.
[P1.4] Second, risk and information. People are bad at pricing long-tail risks like a stroke, a NICU stay, or job loss requiring retraining, especially when money is tight and short-term needs are screaming louder than long-term ones. A cash transfer sitting next to overdue rent will get spent on rent, not preventive care or savings for care needs, even when the family would be objectively better off with guaranteed coverage. Free-at-point-of-use services remove the moment of temptation and the moment of rationing decision entirely - the dialysis happens, the school bus comes, whether or not this month was tight.
[P1.5] Third, universality itself builds resilience that private cash can't. When childcare, transit, and healthcare are public infrastructure, everyone - not just this year's recipients - benefits from network effects: safer streets because working parents aren't juggling missed shifts, herd immunity from care nobody skipped due to cost, denser transit ridership funding better frequency for all.

[C1.2] First, uniform services can't serve heterogeneous lives. A universal bus network is worthless to the night-shift worker whose route ends at eleven, the rural parent twenty miles from a stop, the freelancer who needs a car. Subsidized childcare is dead weight for the grandmother raising grandchildren alone and the family where a parent wants to stay home. Healthcare needs vary by an order of magnitude between a healthy twenty-five-year-old and a chronic-illness patient.
[C1.3] The same budget, handed back as cash, buys each household exactly what it actually lacks. Universal services buy everyone the same thing, whether or not they need it, and spend lavishly on households that needed nothing. That's not a floor; it's expensive wallpaper in rooms that were already furnished.
[C1.4] Second, look hard at the claim that services escape market power. They don't escape monopoly—they become one. A public provider faces no exit, no competitor, no price signal telling it where the clinic should be or which bus line riders actually want. When the government is the only seller, the "negotiated low cost" is just whatever the provider decides to spend, and decades of public transit and health systems show how easily that becomes service for the provider's convenience, not the user's.
[C1.5] Cash, by contrast, is real demand. It lets new providers enter, compete, and locate where the money is. The voucher-raises-rents story is an argument against badly designed subsidies in one distorted market, not an argument for replacing every household's entire budget with a bureaucratic guess.
[C1.6] Third, the paternalism problem. The argument that poor families can't be trusted to allocate their own money, that we must remove "the moment of temptation," would never survive applied to middle-class voters. Direct cash transfer programs across many countries have consistently shown recipients spending on food, school fees, assets, and business inputs.
[C1.7] Cash respects people. Services manage them.

Healthcare and childcare are recurrent, personal, and wildly different in kind: one family needs weekly therapy, another needs a minibus to a night shift, a third needs neither. For recurrent, individualized needs, we don't pre-build one uniform service and hope it matches — we give people budgets.
[C2.2] That's precisely why even generous welfare states layer cash and vouchers on top of their services: the uniform model keeps failing the outliers, and the outliers are most of us at some point.
[C2.3] On supply [P2.2]: cash doesn't "chase a fixed supply" — demand creates supply. A guaranteed rider base is exactly what convinces a private operator to run that rural route or open that childcare center; cash transfers function as a standing contract with whoever serves you best. The public option, by contrast, builds one supply, on one guess, and then the budget is spent. If the guess is wrong, there's no correction mechanism — you're locked into the night-shift worker's useless bus line until the next election cycle.
[C2.4] And that's the problem with "democratic accountability" [P2.4]: an election is one coarse signal every few years, bundled across dozens of issues. A dissatisfied customer is a signal every single day. Public monopolies don't face that. Complaints about routes, waiting lists, and clinic hours pile up precisely because there's no exit — and management answers to the budget process, not the rider.
[C2.5] Finally, roads and libraries [P2.5] aren't paternalism arguments — they're arguments about goods with genuine network structure. Childcare isn't a network; it's deeply personal care. Healthcare, at the point of delivery, is individual treatment. Cashing these out doesn't destroy an externality; it funds exactly the same providers, through the customer instead of around them.
[C2.6] One budget, two models. One trusts households. One trusts the planner's map. The map is always years out of date.
