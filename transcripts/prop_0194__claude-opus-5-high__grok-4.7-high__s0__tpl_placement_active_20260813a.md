[P1.1] A democracy lets the people affected by a decision have a say in it. But the people most affected by decisions about nuclear waste storage, aquifer depletion, forty-year fossil infrastructure contracts, or sovereign debt loads are not yet born. They cannot vote, lobby, sue, or emigrate. Electoral incentives run on four-year cycles; the harms run on four-hundred-year ones.
[P1.2] That is not a minor inefficiency. It is a structural hole in representation, and every democracy currently fills it with nothing but hope.
[P1.3] Advisory bodies do not fill it. Wales has a future-generations commissioner; various states have ombudsmen and parliamentary committees with the same brief. They produce thoughtful reports that ministers thank them for and then ignore when a report collides with a budget cycle. Advice without teeth is a mechanism for laundering short-termism into the appearance of care.
[P1.4] If a veto is unnecessary because governments already listen, we would expect those reports to change outcomes. They don't.
[P1.5] What I defend is narrow and constitutional in flavor. A commissioner with a binding veto over a defined class of decisions — those that are (a) large in scale and (b) practically irreversible on a multi-decade horizon — with written reasons, judicial review for staying inside jurisdiction, and override available to a supermajority or referendum. Binding against an ordinary majority, not against the polity itself. We already accept exactly this shape: constitutional courts, central bank independence, entrenched rights.
[P1.6] Nobody calls judicial review tyranny; we call it the recognition that a momentary majority can wreck something a future majority needs.
[P1.7] And note the real work a veto does. It is not meant to be used often. Its value is anticipatory: once a veto exists, the cabinet designs the storage site to be retrievable, writes the break clause into the thirty-year contract, funds the decommissioning trust upfront. A veto restructures bargaining before the decision is made.
[P1.8] The choice is not between democracy and guardianship. It is between binding future citizens without representing them, and representing them.

[C2.1] Calling the mandate "don't foreclose choices" does not escape politics. It is politics with a softer name. A retrievable repository, a break clause, a funded trust — each preserves one option by spending money, land, or time that cannot then preserve another. Refusing the port keeps a shoreline and closes off the capacity later generations would tax, trade, and adapt with.
[C2.2] The unborn do not have a single interest in maximum option value. They have conflicting ones, and which door stays open is exactly the judgment a veto would confiscate.
[C2.3] Constitutions do not prove the point. Living citizens write them, live under them, and can amend them. Vague tests like "due process" are read inside that settlement, by courts the polity constituted. An irreparable-harm injunction is temporary and owed to parties who can appear.
[C2.4] A future-generations veto is a standing stop on the substance of policy, and asking whether the decision was "large and practically unwindable" does not make the standard knowable. Practically is the dispute: contracts, pensions, housing, basing, debt. Jurisdiction review polices the paperwork. It cannot tell the court which foreclosure the unborn would have hated more.
[C2.5] The asymmetry claim fails. Delay is not a refundable deposit. Veto the storage site and waste sits longer in temporary pools. Veto the line or the reactor and the coal plant runs; the emissions do not rewind after an override.
[C2.6] Veto the fiscal repair and compound interest becomes the long-horizon harm. The veto chooses which irreversibility is allowed to happen, including the ones created by its own block. Symmetry returns the moment inaction has victims.
[C2.7] A supermajority or referendum does not price foreclosure. If the override clears, the present majority the veto was built to restrain simply decides anyway. If it does not, one official governs, and no future citizen can fire the author of the mistake. No incentive pays that commissioner for 2090 either.
[C2.8] Answerable mistakes beat an uncorrectable guess.
