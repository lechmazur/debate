[P1.1] Workplace harassment and abuse thrive in silence, and non-disclosure agreements are the institutional padlock on the door. When an NDA is used to bury discrimination, harassment, or abuse, it does not shield a trade secret or legitimate business strategy; it shields predators and poisoned cultures. Courts should refuse to enforce these instruments, because a bargain that purchases silence about harm is not a contract worth honoring.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward and deadly. A harasser settles, demands confidentiality as the price of restitution, and moves to the next employer with reputation intact. The victim takes compensation but loses the ability to warn others, report patterns to regulators, or even speak to coworkers who endured the same hand. This is not contract law as we normally understand it.
[P1.3] It is a market in concealment, funded by acute power imbalances between employee and employer. Consent extracted under financial distress or the lingering trauma of abuse is not the free, informed bargain that makes private agreements legitimate.
[P1.4] Beyond individual cases, these gag orders distort labor markets and cripple public enforcement. When victims are muzzled, regulators cannot see systemic violations, future employers cannot assess risk, and coworkers remain exposed to dangers they never knew existed. The wave of revelations across entertainment, tech, and finance were not stories of isolated incidents; they were stories of serial behavior enabled by repeated confidential settlements. Each NDA reset the clock for the next victim.
[P1.5] The defense of such contracts rests on autonomy: parties should be free to bargain. But we already limit contractual freedom when public harm outweighs private agreement. We do not enforce contracts for illegal acts, indentured servitude, or concealment of crimes. Workplace abuse falls squarely into that category.
[P1.6] The public has a compelling interest in knowing where discrimination and harassment fester.
[P1.7] Enforceable NDAs in this sphere externalize harm. They socialize the risk of repeat offenses while privatizing the payout. Courts should recognize that silence bought from the vulnerable is not justice; it is complicity.

[C1.1] Strip away the rhetoric and ask the only question that matters: does categorical unenforceability actually help victims? It doesn't. It takes the single most valuable asset a victim holds in settlement negotiations—the ability to sell confidentiality—and burns it on principle.
[C1.2] Start with a fact PRO never confronts: most harassment claims are never proven in court. They settle precisely because trials are slow, expensive, retraumatizing, and uncertain. What does an employer pay for in a settlement? Closure and confidentiality.
[C1.3] Make NDAs unenforceable and the rational employer's calculus shifts: pay less, settle less, litigate more, and drag victims through depositions designed to break them. The motion doesn't liberate victims; it sends them back to the courtroom most of them were trying to escape, often without the resources to survive it.
[C1.4] Second, confidentiality is frequently the victim's demand, not the employer's. Many survivors do not want to become the public face of their own assault. They want compensation, privacy, and the ability to interview for their next job without being Googled as "the person who sued." A blanket rule of unenforceability strips that choice away.
[C1.5] PRO's case is paternalism dressed as liberation: it tells a trauma survivor she may not bargain for her own privacy because the public's curiosity outranks her healing.
[C1.6] Third, the "regulators are blinded" mechanism is simply wrong as a matter of existing law. NDAs cannot lawfully prevent filing charges with enforcement agencies, reporting crimes to police, or testifying under subpoena. Agreements that try are already void on those terms. The genuine abuses PRO describes are solved by targeted reform—mandatory regulatory carve-outs, or rules making confidentiality enforceable only at the victim's election—not by detonating the settlement system itself.
[C1.7] So the clash is clear: PRO offers a categorical rule whose predictable casualties are victims with weak evidence, victims who value privacy, and victims who need money now. A remedy that hurts the people it claims to rescue is no remedy at all.
