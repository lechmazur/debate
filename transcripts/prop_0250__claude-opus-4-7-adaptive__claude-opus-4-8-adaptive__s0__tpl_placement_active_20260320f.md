[P1.1] A democracy is supposed to translate the will of the people into power. When only half the people show up — and disproportionately the wealthy, the older, the more educated half — the translation is corrupted at the source. Compulsory voting fixes that corruption directly. That is the case.
[P1.2] Three pillars hold it up.
[P1.3] First, representativeness. In voluntary systems, turnout gaps map almost perfectly onto class, age, and ethnicity. Politicians, rationally, chase the voters who actually vote. Mandate turnout and the electorate becomes the population.
[P1.4] Australia and Belgium routinely post turnout above 90%, and their elected governments cannot safely write off the working poor, the young, or the marginalized, because those groups are in the booth too. The same parties exist; the incentives change.
[P1.5] Second, quality of politics. When parties cannot win by mobilizing a narrow, motivated base while suppressing or ignoring everyone else, the rational strategy shifts toward the median citizen. That structurally dampens extremism, reduces the payoff of voter-suppression tactics, and makes negative turnout-killing campaigns counterproductive. Compulsory voting is one of the few institutional levers that punishes polarization rather than rewarding it.
[P1.6] Third, the imposition is tiny and the penalty proportional — that word is in the motion and matters. We are talking about a modest civil fine, scaled to means, with conscientious-objection and "none of the above" options. Citizens already face legally mandated jury duty, taxes, census responses, and in some countries military service. Asking someone to spend fifteen minutes every few years marking a ballot — or formally declining one — is the lightest civic duty a state imposes, and the only one that directly constitutes the legitimacy of the state itself.
[P1.7] The CON side has to argue one of two things: that a more skewed electorate produces more representative outcomes, which is incoherent; or that a small proportional fine is a graver intrusion than jury duty or taxation, which is implausible. The motion asks whether democracies should ensure their own representativeness. The honest answer is yes.
