[P1.1] The motion turns on three words: per dollar. Not which method stores the most carbon in a textbook ideal, but which buys the most carbon and the most living things for each unit of scarce conservation money. On that question, rewilding abandoned farmland wins by a margin that is hard to overstate.
[P1.2] Start with the denominator. Active reforestation is expensive by design: nursery seedlings, site preparation, planting crews, tree guards, watering, replanting after the first dry summer kills a third of the stock. Costs commonly run into the thousands of dollars per hectare, and large-scale monitoring of planting programs repeatedly finds survival rates far below what was promised. Rewilding abandoned land costs close to nothing.
[P1.3] Fencing out livestock, blocking a drainage ditch, perhaps reintroducing a few grazers or beavers. Nature supplies the seedlings for free, and they are already adapted to the site.
[P1.4] Now the numerator. We have a continent-scale experiment already run. New England was mostly cleared farmland in the mid-1800s; today it is overwhelmingly forest, holding enormous carbon stocks, and nobody planted it. The same process is unfolding across rural Spain, Italy, the Balkans and post-Soviet Eastern Europe, where millions of hectares have simply been left.
[P1.5] Succession works.
[P1.6] On biodiversity, the gap is wider still. Most planting programs put even-aged, often single-species stands on land, sometimes non-native species, sometimes on grasslands that should never have become forest. Rewilding produces a mosaic: scrub, wet meadow, old-field grassland, forest edge, dead wood. Mosaic habitat is what supports pollinators, ground-nesting birds, and the messy middle of the food web that tidy plantations exclude.
[P1.7] Yes, there are sites where planting is necessary: severely degraded soils, no nearby seed source, arid zones. But the motion says most, and most reforestation money is spent on land that would regenerate on its own if we simply stopped interfering.
[P1.8] When the cheapest option is also the one with the deepest track record, the comparison is not close.

[C1.1] The motion rests on a seductive accounting trick: make rewilding sound free, make planting sound wasteful, and the cheapest option looks like the winner. But compare programs on the two outcomes the motion actually demands—carbon sequestered and biodiversity restored—and rewilding abandoned farmland loses, even per dollar, because it buys marginal land and slow, risky outcomes rather than the intensive sites where every dollar works hardest.
[C1.2] Start with the land itself. The abandoned farmland that dominates the European and North American backcountry is not New England's rich valley soil; it is marginal, eroded, nutrient-depleted ground on uplands, slopes, and dry sites. Passive succession on these depleted soils often stalls at low-productivity scrub, and early soil carbon can decline as agricultural inputs stop while decomposition continues. Active restoration can pair planting with soil amendment, drainage repair, and site preparation, rebuilding the carbon reservoir faster and deeper than passive succession on the same parcel.
[C1.3] Per dollar, the denominator is not the only thing that matters; the numerator must be earned.
[C1.4] Now consider time and risk. A young, passively regenerated thicket sequesters carbon slowly. Climate targets are measured in decades, not centuries. Meanwhile, rewilding creates real wildfire and drought exposure: unmanaged regrowth becomes fuel, and a single dry summer can release in days what took years to draw down.
[C1.5] Active programs can thin, diversify, and maintain stands, reducing catastrophic-loss risk that turns a cheap hectare into a zero-carbon hectare.
[C1.6] Biodiversity is not automatically higher. Abandoned fields often become dense monocultures of bracken, ragweed, or invasive vines, not the rich mosaic PRO imagines. Many endangered species need early-successional or agricultural habitats that active management maintains—not wild scrub.
[C1.7] Rewilding has a role. But as a replacement for most active reforestation, it delivers less carbon, more slowly, with greater risk, and with biodiversity outcomes no better than well-designed ecological restoration. The cheap option is not the best buy.
