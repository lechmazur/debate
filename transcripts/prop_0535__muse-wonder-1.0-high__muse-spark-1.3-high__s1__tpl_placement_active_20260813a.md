[P1.1] An advance directive is not a prediction about how dementia will feel. It is a decision about what kind of ending a person is willing to accept as their own.
[P1.2] When someone still has capacity, watches a parent linger for years unable to recognize family, and writes that they do not want antibiotics, hospital transfers, or tube feeding once they reach that stage, they are exercising the most consequential autonomy they have left. Later, when they cannot remember writing it, cannot weigh risks, and cannot advocate for themselves, that document is the only place their competent voice still exists. If we treat it as merely advisory, we do not protect the patient in front of us — we erase the person who saw this moment coming.
[P1.3] Strong binding is necessary because the alternative is unworkably subjective. A smile at music, eating ice cream, or resistance to a needle will be read by exhausted relatives and cautious clinicians as contentment, fear, or consent depending on what they already believe. People with dementia adapt and live in fragments of experience; adaptation is not a repudiation of a lifetime of values about dignity, independence, and not burdening children. Without a presumption that the directive controls, every hard case collapses into projection and the loudest surrogate wins.
[P1.4] The exception makes this humane, not rigid. If, while still competent, a person revises their views, repeatedly embraces a faith that now requires maximal treatment, or tells multiple witnesses they have changed their mind, that is clear evidence of material change and the directive should yield. What should not count is inferring a new philosophy from fleeting affect in advanced disease.
[P1.5] People complete directives precisely because they fear being kept alive in a condition they regard as intolerable. If families can set those instructions aside whenever the demented self seems peaceful, planning becomes pointless and the competent self is betrayed when powerless to protest.

[C1.1] A competent signature should not become a life sentence for a person who can no longer appeal it.
[C1.2] Someone at 65, haunted after watching a parent die badly, signs a form refusing antibiotics or feeding once dementia is ‘severe.’ She is imagining emptiness, humiliation, being a burden. She is not imagining herself at 82 — forgetful, wordless at times, yet lighting up when her daughter sings, reaching eagerly for breakfast, resting comfortably in the sun. That later woman is not a memory of a person. She is a living patient with real pleasures, pains, and fears, and strong binding tells us to sacrifice her actual welfare to honor an earlier abstraction about dignity.
[C1.3] That is backward because advance forecasts about dementia are notoriously unreliable. Healthy people predict unrelenting misery and insist they would rather die. Yet many people with even moderate dementia report contentment, affection, humor. Adaptation is not incompetence.
[C1.4] The fact that joy comes in fragments does not make it false. To dismiss a smile, appetite, or calm as mere ‘fleeting affect’ while treating a decade-old checkbox as sacred turns autonomy into cruelty: we would force treatable pneumonia to kill a contented person who is not asking to die.
[C1.5] Directives are also too blunt to rule. They use vague triggers like severe decline or loss of recognition, written without knowledge of which infection, which treatment burden, which prognosis will actually arise. Strong binding strips clinicians and families of the discretion needed to ask the only humane question: will this treatment help or harm the person in this bed today?
[C1.6] And the proposition’s supposed safeguard is an illusion. Demanding clear evidence of changed values from someone who can no longer reason or testify guarantees the old document almost always wins. That is not respect for autonomy; it is rule by paperwork over a living patient who deserves protection.

[P2.6] That would make every directive revocable by interpretation, and none reliable.
