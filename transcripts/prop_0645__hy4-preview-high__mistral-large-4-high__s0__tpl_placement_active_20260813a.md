[P1.1] Growing up once included a kind of forgetfulness: a teenager could do something stupid, face consequences, and over time outrun it. Digital permanence has ended that. A 16-year-old’s arrest, diversion agreement, or juvenile court filing can live forever in court databases, news archives, background-check reports, and data-broker profiles, following the person to every landlord, employer, licensing board, and loan officer decades later. That reality quietly voids juvenile court’s central promise: that young people are less culpable, more capable of change, and not to be branded for life.
[P1.2] The motion restores that promise through presumptive sealing at adulthood. Sealing is not amnesia or automatic absolution. It removes juvenile digital records from public and commercial view while leaving regulated access for law enforcement and courts. “Presumptive” means privacy is the default, with a narrow path to keep a record open only when the state shows a specific, serious public-safety need. That is nothing like the CON side’s image of dangerous histories erased.
[P1.3] What is at stake is proportionality and equal opportunity. Adolescent brain development makes teenagers impulsive, peer-driven, and still forming judgment; that is why we treat them as less blameworthy and invest in rehabilitation. A permanent digital record ignores this. It turns one youthful mistake into lifelong civil death: barred from housing, jobs, student aid, professional licenses, even coaching a child’s team.
[P1.4] Because police contact is racially and economically skewed, permanent digital juvenile records convert unequal policing into unequal adult life chances.
[P1.5] Automatic sealing and “clean slate” policies have spread because old record relief cannot reach the digital ecosystem. CON will warn about transparency and safety. But safety does not require a landlord or background-check company to see a decade-old shoplifting adjudication. It requires police to retain regulated access, which this proposal preserves.
[P1.6] Presumptive sealing removes not protection, but the power of data brokers, search engines, and hiring algorithms to punish a thirty-year-old for who they were at fifteen.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to believe that adulthood should erase the digital footprint of youth, but it ignores the uncomfortable reality that many juvenile records document not shoplifting or teenage stupidity, but violence, sexual assault, and serious felonies. When we presumptively seal these records, we don't just give a 16-year-old a fresh start; we hide a 30-year-old's history of predatory behavior from the daycare hiring them, the landlord renting to them, or the community welcoming them into positions of trust.
[C1.2] Transparency is not cruelty; it is the foundation of informed decision-making. Employers, landlords, and licensing boards assess risk. A presumption of sealing inverts that relationship, forcing the public to prove why they deserve to know about a violent past rather than requiring the offender to demonstrate rehabilitation before their record is hidden. That is backwards.
[C1.3] Furthermore, digital sealing is porous and inequitable. Data brokers scrape court records continuously; once information enters the digital ecosystem, "sealing" often means only that the official court website hides it, while private databases retain and sell it. This creates a two-tiered system where the wealthy and well-connected can truly scrub their histories while others remain exposed.
[C1.4] Meanwhile, victims of juvenile crimes are told their trauma is a private matter to be locked away, severing the community's ability to acknowledge harm and protect the vulnerable.
[C1.5] True rehabilitation is demonstrated through sustained conduct, not concealment. We do not help young people by teaching them that accountability expires at eighteen. We help them by holding them responsible while they are young, then allowing them to prove change through transparent, verifiable reform. Sealing records does not build character; it builds a wall between a person and the consequences of their choices.
