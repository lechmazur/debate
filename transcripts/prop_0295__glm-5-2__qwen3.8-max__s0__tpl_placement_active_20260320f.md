[P1.1] Restitution is not a delivery; it is a repair. When former colonial powers treat the act of shipping artifacts back as the end of their obligation, they reduce a moral and historical reckoning to a logistics problem—crates loaded, receipts signed, conscience cleared. That is not justice. That is a transfer of liability.
[P1.2] Consider what colonial extraction actually dismantled. Objects were not simply taken; the institutional ecosystems that produced, preserved, and interpreted them were disrupted or destroyed. Museums, training systems, conservation infrastructure, and scholarly networks in colonized regions were systematically underdeveloped while metropolitan institutions grew rich on the proceeds. Shipping an object back into that hollowed-out context and calling restitution "complete" recreates the original harm in a new form: the object returns, but the capacity to care for it, study it, and make it meaningful to its community does not.
[P1.3] Provenance research makes the point sharpest. Many returned objects arrive with fragmented or deliberately obscured ownership histories. The archives, acquisition records, and colonial-era documentation that could reconstruct their journeys sit in European institutions. If a former colonial power returns a Benin bronze but withholds the archival work needed to trace which British punitive expedition officer took it, from which compound, through which intermediary, it has returned an object without its history.
[P1.4] That is not restitution; it is repatriation of a mystery.
[P1.5] Funding conservation and museum capacity is not charity grafted onto restitution. It is part of restitution itself, because the capacity gap is a product of the same colonial relationship that produced the theft. To acknowledge the taking while refusing to repair what the taking broke is to accept responsibility for the beginning of the harm but not its continuation.
[P1.6] The proposition does not demand perpetual dependency. It demands that the power that caused the damage fund the conditions under which the damage can actually be undone. Anything less lets the thief choose the scope of his own apology.

[C1.1] The quickest way to turn restitution into renewed control is to declare it incomplete until the former colonizer has rebuilt the recipient’s museum. The core wrong of colonial taking is continuing possession by the taker. The cleanest remedy is to end that possession unconditionally and transfer title, custody, and interpretive authority. Once the object is back where the claimant authority wants it, restitution has occurred.
[C1.2] Additional assistance may be welcome, but it must be separate, requested, and negotiated—not a standing condition that lets the former power remain manager of the wrong it caused.
[C1.3] PRO’s rule hands former colonial powers leverage at the moment they should lose it. If restitution is not complete until conservation capacity, provenance programs, and museum infrastructure are funded, the returning institution can shape standards, staffing, access, insurance, display, and even the narrative attached to the object. A government that took a sacred mask can now decide what climate controls are adequate, which curators must be trained, and what provenance questions deserve funding. That is not repair; it is continued stewardship under a moral label.
[C1.4] This approach also makes returns less likely. If every restitution triggers an open-ended budget line and bureaucratic oversight regime, legislatures and museums will treat claims as liabilities rather than obligations. The safest political path becomes delay: study the provenance, build the capacity plan, negotiate the partnership. Claimants then wait longer for the object, while the former holder remains indispensable.
[C1.5] Where capacity gaps exist, the right answer is voluntary cooperation: share archives, provide digitized records, honor training requests, and respond to needs identified by the receiving community. But those measures should strengthen a completed restitution, not define it. The proposition mistakes a useful add-on for the essence of justice, and in doing so keeps the thief in the conservation lab.
