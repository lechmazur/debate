[P1.1] A child who inherits $10 million at twenty-five has not outcompeted anyone. She can buy a home outright, fund a business without convincing a bank, take the unpaid internship that leads to the glamorous career, survive three failed startups, and still live off investment returns. Her equally talented rival from a poor family starts with student debt, rent due every month, and no room to fail. They are not running the same race with a small head start; they are running different races.
[P1.2] That explains why dynastic wealth, not most high salaries, is the deeper threat to equality of opportunity. A surgeon, engineer, founder, or senior manager still trades time for money. The pay stops when work stops, it is taxed every year, and for many high earners it is the first generation's route out of insecurity — paying a mortgage, supporting parents, building modest savings. Punish that income too heavily and you squeeze people still climbing.
[P1.3] Inherited capital behaves differently. It earns while its owner sleeps, buys access to elite schools and networks, provides collateral and confidence to take risks others cannot afford, and then passes to children and grandchildren. Each untaxed transfer does not merely preserve advantage; it compounds it into housing power, educational monopoly, and political influence. That is how meritocracy quietly becomes inheritance with better public relations.
[P1.4] Taxing large inheritances much more aggressively is also the fairer and less damaging place to raise revenue. The recipient did nothing to earn the windfall, so a steep tax discourages far less productive effort than a comparable tax on work. Protect ordinary family support with a meaningful exemption, then apply sharply progressive rates above it, close trust and valuation loopholes, and end rules that let appreciated assets escape tax at death.
[P1.5] Either society taxes the unearned transfer of entrenched privilege, or it keeps loading the burden onto salaries. Only the first defends genuine equality of opportunity.

[C1.1] Inherited billions make dramatic villains, but dynastic permanence is largely a myth. Most large fortunes split among heirs, are consumed, mismanaged, or diluted within two or three generations, while top wealth lists constantly churn with first-generation founders. The persistent gap in opportunity for ordinary children comes not from a small caste of Rockefellers compounding forever, but from millions of differences in parental income, neighborhood, schooling, and connections during childhood — none of which an estate tax at death touches.
[C1.2] That timing failure is fatal to the case for aggressive transfer taxes. By the time the $10 million heir is twenty-five, the decisive advantages have already been delivered: safe neighborhood, tutoring, debt-free college, internships, social network, confidence to fail. Taxing the bequest afterward does not rerun her rival's childhood. It confiscates the receipt while leaving the process that produced unequal opportunity intact.
[C1.3] Worse, steep death taxes do not land only on idle aristocrats. They land hardest on the strivers the other side claims to protect: the contractor, farmer, or shop owner who never earned a surgeon's salary but saved, reinvested, and built a business or property to hand down. That wealth was already taxed as income and profit. Forcing a sale to pay a 50 or 60 percent bill does not create meritocracy; it destroys continuity, jobs, and the most accessible path for modest families to move up in one generation.
[C1.4] And the incentive effects spread outward. People save, build, and hold productive assets partly to provide for children. Punish that motive and you get less long-term investment, more end-of-life consumption, and an arms race of trusts, gifts, valuation games, and emigration that only the richest can afford to win.
[C1.5] Taxing the heir does not house her rival, cancel debt, or fund a better school. Opportunity is built, not redistributed at the coffin.
