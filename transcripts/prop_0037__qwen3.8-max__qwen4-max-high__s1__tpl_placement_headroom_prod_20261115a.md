[P1.1] Congestion pricing treats a structural problem as a meter. It says drivers may occupy scarce curb space, breathe into dense neighborhoods, and endanger pedestrians so long as they pay. A ban on private cars in central districts is stronger because it replaces a toll with a clear rule: the core of a city should be built for people, transit, deliveries, and emergency access, not for storing and moving private vehicles.
[P1.2] The decisive advantage is certainty. Pricing hopes to nudge demand down while leaving car dependence intact; it invites political erosion, exemptions, and backlash when fees rise. A clear boundary is easier to understand, enforce, and plan around. It lets cities convert lanes and parking into bus corridors, protected bike lanes, loading bays, wider sidewalks, and public space.
[P1.3] Those changes create visible benefits: safer streets, faster transit, quieter blocks that build durable support. Pricing alone often leaves the same hostile street design and merely adds a charge.
[P1.4] This also matters for equity. Congestion pricing can turn access into a luxury good: wealthier drivers pay to enter while poorer commuters are priced out or stuck in slower traffic. A car-free core, paired with robust transit and accessible dropoff zones, makes mobility a public service rather than a fee. It prioritizes buses, trams, cycling, and walking, which move far more people per square meter than private cars.
[P1.5] Cities like Paris have shown that removing through traffic can improve air quality and street life without collapsing activity.
[P1.6] The clash is simple: CON will defend a price signal; PRO defends a spatial transformation. If central districts are to become healthier, safer, and more efficient, cities should stop selling permission to drive and instead ban private cars outright. It gives residents a center they can use, not just a road they pay to clog.
