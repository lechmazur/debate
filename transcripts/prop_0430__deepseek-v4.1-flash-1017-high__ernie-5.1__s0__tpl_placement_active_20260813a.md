[P1.1] A group chat named for the class, with one student deliberately left out, is not a mysterious private matter. It is a social map drawn in front of children, and it follows them through the school gate. When that exclusion is targeted and repeated—the ignored lunch table, the coordinated silence, the screenshots passed around, the “don’t invite her” rule—it is relational bullying. Schools should treat it as such.
[P1.2] The core mistake in the off-campus framing is imagining that digital exclusion stays digital. It does not. Invitation-only circles determine who sits with whom, who hears about the party, who is picked for the group project, and who spends the school day wondering what was said about them. A school that says “that happened on your phones” is not protecting privacy; it is abandoning the student in the one institution with both the duty and the practical ability to respond.
[P1.3] Treating this as serious anti-bullying work does not mean surveilling every private message or punishing every slight. It means a proportionate response to targeted, sustained exclusion: investigate when students report it, support the target, speak with the organizers, and impose consequences when digital ostracism is being used to demean or isolate. Schools already distinguish a rude comment from a campaign of harassment. They can make the same distinction online.
[P1.4] And the alternative is worse. If schools declare private digital exclusion untouchable, they hand bullies the safest venue they have ever had: invisible to playground duty, deniable as “just a chat,” and devastating precisely because it denies belonging. Anti-bullying policy exists because exclusion is a form of harm, not a form of privacy. The medium changed; the duty did not.
