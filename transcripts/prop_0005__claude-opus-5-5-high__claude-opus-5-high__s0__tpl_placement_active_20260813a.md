[P1.1] Prescription drugs are prescription-only for one reason: society has decided that ordinary people cannot safely judge them alone. We require a trained professional to weigh dosage, interactions, and risk. Direct-to-consumer advertising exists to route around that safeguard. It spends enormous sums persuading the one person in the exam room who is not qualified to choose, then sends them in to pressure the person who is.
[P1.2] That is why almost every developed nation bans it, and why the United States and New Zealand stand nearly alone in allowing it.
[P1.3] My case rests on three harms.
[P1.4] First, these ads are persuasion dressed up as information. Watch one: thirty seconds of a grandfather laughing on a sailboat while a narrator speed-reads the risks of liver failure and suicidal thoughts. The images carry the message; the warnings are legal cover. A company's incentive is never to tell you whether you need a drug, only to make you want theirs.
[P1.5] Second, advertising distorts medicine. Ads promote the newest, most expensive branded products, not the best treatment. They push patients toward a patented pill when a cheaper generic, or no drug at all, would serve. Doctors report patients arriving with a brand name already chosen, and many prescribe to keep them satisfied.
[P1.6] Vioxx is the cautionary tale: aggressively marketed to the public, taken by millions, then withdrawn over heart risks. Mass advertising amplifies a drug's reach before its dangers are fully known.
[P1.7] Third, costs. Every dollar spent on primetime slots is recovered from patients and insurers, while pressure toward brand-name drugs inflates what entire health systems pay.
[P1.8] Now the likely reply: people need to learn about untreated conditions. Nothing in this motion stops that. Public health bodies can run unbranded awareness campaigns. Doctors, pharmacists, package inserts, and non-mass-media information remain available.
[P1.9] We ban only the branded sales pitch aimed at the crowd.
[P1.10] The question is simple: should drug choice be driven by clinical judgment, or by whoever buys the most airtime? Clinical judgment should decide, and that means banning these ads.
