[P1.1] Asylum in Europe today is a lottery of geography. A person fleeing war who lands in Greece or Italy faces overcrowded camps, slow processing, and the constant threat of destitution; the same person landing in Luxembourg or Sweden has a radically different chance of a fair hearing and a stable life. That is not a migration policy. It is a postcode raffle, and it is collapsing the frontline.
[P1.2] The proposition is modest: share responsibility through binding quotas. A fair allocation formula—population, GDP, existing asylum numbers, perhaps unemployment—would move a predictable share of arrivals from overstretched frontline states to the rest of the Union. States that genuinely cannot host can meet obligations through financial or operational contributions, but they cannot simply free-ride while Greece and Italy absorb the pressure.
[P1.3] Why does this beat the status quo? First, capacity. Small frontline states cannot process and integrate most arrivals; concentrating people there produces slow decisions, poor conditions, and more irregular movement north anyway. Second, legitimacy.
[P1.4] When costs fall only on a few, those states either demand EU money or flirt with pushbacks and border closures; that erodes Schengen and asylum law for everyone. Third, fairness. Responsibility for a common European commitment should not depend on which coast someone reaches. Quotas turn a permanent emergency into a managed, predictable system.
[P1.5] CON will say sovereignty and that quotas are unpopular. But the status quo is also coercive: it imposes a massive burden on frontline states by default. Unpopularity is not an argument for unfairness, and opt-outs can be structured as financial solidarity rather than forced hosting. CON may say quotas create a pull factor, but people flee war and persecution, not spreadsheet allocations; the real pull factor is chaotic, exploitative border zones.
[P1.6] Without quotas, the EU asks a handful of states to guard and absorb a continental responsibility. With quotas, it shares it. That is the choice.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes redistribution for reform. Binding asylum quotas do not cure the frontline crisis; they politicize solidarity, coerce member states, and create an allocation machine the EU cannot credibly run.
[C1.2] PRO’s real complaint is uneven capacity and uneven standards. But moving people by formula does not by itself create fair hearings, fast decisions, or integration. If Greece and Italy are overwhelmed, the answer is stronger common processing at the external border: more caseworkers, shared facilities, uniform procedural standards, faster returns when claims fail, and targeted emergency funding. That helps arrivals where they are without forcing a political confrontation over permanent relocation.
[C1.3] Quotas are also unstable in practice. Asylum seekers are not parcels: they have family links, language ties, and destination preferences. A compulsory allocation scheme will either ignore those ties—producing chaos and secondary movement—or respect them, which means the quota collapses into voluntary matching. The EU’s previous relocation attempts showed that compulsory numbers invite non-compliance, court battles, and domestic backlash, while doing little to improve reception conditions.
[C1.4] This matters because legitimacy is PRO’s own test. A system perceived as Brussels imposing migrants on unwilling communities will not build European solidarity; it will strengthen nationalist parties, harden borders, and make common asylum policy less viable. The status quo is bad, but quotas turn a difficult administrative failure into a constitutional crisis.
[C1.5] Finally, PRO’s escape hatch—pay or contribute instead of hosting—concedes the point. If solidarity can be met through money, logistics, and processing support, then the motion’s core mechanism, binding host quotas, is unnecessary. If it cannot, then quotas require coercion the Union is not equipped to enforce.
[C1.6] The EU should relieve frontline states, but through capacity, common procedures, and flexible solidarity—not compulsory quotas that fracture the very system they claim to save.
