[P1.1] Student debt is the only major form of consumer debt in America that survives almost everything—divorce, death of a spouse, even a house fire that destroys your job prospects—because current law demands borrowers prove "undue hardship," a standard courts have interpreted so narrowly that it effectively requires proving your life is already ruined and will stay ruined forever. That is the wrong line to draw, and it's why bankruptcy law needs a much easier path to discharge when repayment is genuinely, durably impossible.
[P1.2] Here is the core case. Bankruptcy exists for exactly one purpose: to let people whose debts have permanently outrun their capacity to pay start over, so they can keep working, spending, and contributing instead of being crushed indefinitely. We apply this logic to credit card debt, medical debt, business debt, even gambling debt. Student debt gets walled off for reasons that don't survive scrutiny anymore.
[P1.3] The original justification—fear that students would borrow lavishly then discharge debt the moment they graduated, before any lender could assess risk—assumed a system of unsecured, discretionary borrowing. But today's student debt is federally underwritten, non-dischargeable by statute, and taken on by eighteen-year-olds who cannot evaluate labor-market risk or verify a college's true earnings outcomes. The moral hazard argument is backwards: it's an argument for better underwriting, not for permanent debtor's prison.
[P1.4] Consider the person who borrowed for a nursing degree, graduated into a market crash, worked for a decade, and today earns wages that can service groceries or the loan but not both.
