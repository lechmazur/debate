[P1.1] Universities today collect tuition upfront and bear almost no risk when their graduates default. A student signs the loan, the school keeps the cash, and if the degree fails to deliver a livable wage, the lender and taxpayer absorb the loss. That one-sided arrangement is the core problem our proposition fixes.
[P1.2] When a program repeatedly leaves graduates with earnings too low to service the debt it encouraged them to take, the university is not a bystander. It controls admissions, curriculum, marketing, and career pipelines. It knows placement rates and salary ranges before an eighteen-year-old applicant ever sees a brochure. Yet because the institution faces no financial consequence when loans go bad, it has little reason to prune a dead-end certificate, stop promising returns that never arrive, or invest in advising that actually matches students to viable fields.
[P1.3] Skin in the game changes that. If the university pays a share of default costs, incentives shift immediately. Programs that consistently produce graduates unable to repay will be reformed or closed. Schools will publish honest earnings data, invest in career services, and stop recruiting students into degrees that cannot support the debt.
[P1.4] Programs that genuinely work—engineering, nursing, quality liberal arts curricula—will pay nothing, because their graduates repay. This is a targeted, corrective rule, not a broad tax on higher education.
[P1.5] Consider a vocational program charging students into five figures of debt while its own graduates cluster in low-wage jobs. Under current rules, the institution profits regardless. Under shared-risk rules, it either fixes the program or exits the market. That is not punishment; it is accountability for a predictable outcome the school helped create.
[P1.6] Taxpayers and students should not be the only parties bearing losses from institutional failure. Make universities share the risk they currently outsource, and the market for higher education becomes honest.

[P2.1] CON's emotional case collapses on a misreading. The proposition does not tax a school when one borrower is laid off or one graduate cuts hours to care for a sick parent. It applies when a *program repeatedly* leaves graduates with earnings too low to service the debt it sold them. Program-level aggregation is precisely the instrument that filters out CON's anecdotes: layoffs, divorces, illnesses, and unfinished degrees average out across cohorts and years.
[P2.2] What survives is a pattern — a school charging five figures for credentials its own graduate earnings cannot repay. That is not a risk the institution cannot control; it is a product outcome it does control, through price, curriculum, marketing, and placement pipelines.
[P2.3] Cream-skimming is the louder objection but the weaker one. A university facing shared risk has cheaper levers: honest pricing, shorter credentials, employer pipelines, real advising. Note that CON concedes the design: elite flagships pay nothing because their graduates repay.
[P2.4] That is not a flaw, it is targeting.
[P2.5] The metric objection confuses debt-to-earnings with salary worship. A teacher carrying modest debt pays nothing under this rule; so does a social worker whose wage covers her loans. [P2.6] They simply cannot load them with debt the degree cannot carry.
[P2.7] CON's "cleaner instruments" are complements, not substitutes. Disclosure rules have existed for years without making a dead-end certificate honest, and income-driven repayment leaves taxpayers holding the bag for institutional overpricing.
