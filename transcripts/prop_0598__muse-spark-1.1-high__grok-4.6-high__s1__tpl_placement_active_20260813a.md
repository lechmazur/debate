[P1.1] Gulf and North African economies run on migrant labor their formal systems refuse to admit. The Gulf needs millions of builders, drivers, domestics and technicians; Egypt, Morocco, Tunisia and Algeria need seasonal workers, remittances and an outlet for youth unemployment while also policing transit from the Sahel. Irregularity is the market filling a legal vacuum. Mass deportations pretend to solve that by removing workers, but they only create the next vacancy for another irregular entrant.
[P1.2] Deportation also fails on its own security logic. It is staggeringly expensive to detain, prosecute and fly out hundreds of thousands of workers across deserts and seas, only for many to return through smuggling networks that profit from closure. When Algeria pushes migrants into Niger or Saudi Arabia runs sweeping raids, the underground moves deeper, not away - less documented, less screened, more exploitable. A legal corridor does the opposite: it puts the state back in control with contracts, biometrics, health checks and employer liability.
[P1.3] You cannot monitor a population you have driven into hiding.
[P1.4] Finally deportations squander development and diplomacy while corridors compound it. Corridors generate visa fees, social contributions and employer-paid costs; deportations generate only detention bills, lawsuits and pariah headlines from desert expulsions. Gulf reforms from Qatar to the UAE already show that legal, mobile permits reduce absconding and raise productivity - Vision 2030 megaprojects cannot survive stop-start purges. In North Africa tested models like Moroccan seasonal farm work to Spain prove that circular legal channels cut irregular stays, send money home, and give governments bargaining power with Europe instead of condemnation.
[P1.5] If the goal is fewer irregular migrants next year, not just fewer this month, build the hallway. Don't keep kicking people out the window.
