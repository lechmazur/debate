[P1.1] The case for output-based evaluation begins with a simple fact: organizations do not pay for mouse movements, green status dots, or hours logged in a chair. They pay for work that solves problems, serves customers, ships code, closes cases, and helps colleagues. In remote and hybrid settings, activity and presence signals are especially weak proxies because they measure visibility, not value.
[P1.2] Consider two employees. One keeps a status light green, replies instantly, and attends every call, but misses deadlines and produces shallow work. Another works asynchronously, blocks deep-focus time, and delivers excellent work on schedule. Constant monitoring rewards the first and punishes the second.
[P1.3] That is not a minor measurement error; it is an incentive to perform busyness. Once people know keystrokes, mouse movement, response speed, and camera time are being scored, rational workers optimize for those signals. The result is surveillance theater: mouse jigglers, unnecessary meetings, instant but low-quality replies, and anxiety instead of focus.
[P1.4] Output evaluation points the other way. It asks what was delivered, at what quality, by when, and with what effect on customers and teammates. Those criteria can be defined for knowledge work: project milestones, code review quality, case resolution, sales outcomes, research findings, reliable handoffs, and peer feedback on whether someone unblocks others. Measurement is imperfect—so is monitoring.
[P1.5] But imperfect measures of the right thing beat precise measures of the wrong thing.
[P1.6] The proposition says “mainly,” not “only.” Security, safety, and availability constraints can still be monitored. But the core evaluation should track contribution, not conspicuous activity. Hybrid work makes this unavoidable: presence is split, schedules vary, and the best contributor may be invisible for hours. Judge the work, not the performance of being watched.

[C1.1] Outputs sound clean in theory. In practice, they are lagging indicators, and lagging indicators are exactly the wrong tool for managing live, interdependent work. By the time a quarterly output review reveals a problem, the damage is already done: the deadline is blown, the client is gone, the teammate who was quietly struggling is now burned out or already job-hunting. Activity and presence signals exist precisely to catch problems while they are still cheap to fix.
[C1.2] Take PRO's own example in reverse. The asynchronous worker who "delivers excellent work on schedule" is the easy case. The harder, more common case is the remote hire in week three who has gone quiet, isn't asking questions, and won't produce a visible output for another month because the project has a long cycle. An output-only manager finds out something is wrong only when the output fails to appear.
[C1.3] A manager with reasonable presence and check-in signals finds out in week one, when a fifteen-minute conversation fixes it.
[C1.4] Outputs also aren't gaming-proof; they just shift the gaming to a different target. If only shipped deliverables count, workers rationally neglect everything that doesn't produce a countable output: mentoring juniors, writing documentation, reviewing a colleague's code, covering a teammate's emergency, being available when a client calls outside the "deep work block." These are exactly the glue tasks that keep distributed teams functioning, and they are invisible to output metrics while being highly visible to presence and collaboration signals.
[C1.5] There's also a fairness problem PRO never addresses. Output quality depends heavily on what tasks someone was assigned, team dependencies, and sheer luck of the project. Two equally hardworking people can produce very different "outputs" for reasons that have nothing to do with effort or skill. Process signals - responsiveness, availability, collaboration patterns - are often the only honest evidence of effort when output was constrained by factors outside someone's control.
[C1.6] "Mainly outputs" isn't a modest refinement; it strips out the early-warning and coordination infrastructure that makes remote work viable at all.
