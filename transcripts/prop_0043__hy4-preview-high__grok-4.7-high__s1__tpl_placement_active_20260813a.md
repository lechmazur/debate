[P1.1] Democracy rarely dies because a voter misses a suspicious URL. It dies when citizens cannot distinguish a strong argument from a confident one, or a real risk from a scary number. Media literacy attacks the packaging—check the source, notice the emotion. But packaging changes faster than any curriculum.
[P1.2] Deepfakes mimic credible sources; partisans weaponize "bias" labels; neutral-looking misinformation still misleads if its statistics are wrong. A curriculum built on surface cues is obsolete on arrival. It mistakes the messenger for the method.
[P1.3] Formal logic, probability, and statistical reasoning attack the machinery instead. They train citizens to ask whether conclusions follow from premises, whether correlation is causation, whether a sample is biased, a risk framed relatively, a rise just regression to the mean. A nine-year-old can grasp that "most crashes happen near home" does not make home dangerous, or that a "50% surge" may mean one case became two. Those tools generalize to every new platform.
[P1.4] Media literacy can even weaken resilience. It often teaches suspicion without a standard for resolving doubt. If every source is "biased," the honest broker and the propagandist look alike, and rational response becomes cynical withdrawal. Formal reasoning supplies a content-neutral standard—validity, evidence, Bayesian updating—that lets citizens disagree productively and revise beliefs when evidence changes.
[P1.5] Democratic resilience is calibrated trust, not blanket distrust.
[P1.6] Starting early matters because these habits must form before motivated reasoning hardens. Teach probability with dice, causation with simple experiments, logic with argument maps in playground disputes. By voting age, such citizens see that "99% survival" and "1% death" are the same fact, and that anecdotes are not data. Media literacy can ride on that foundation; it cannot replace it.
[P1.7] The citizen who can only "check the source" is defended against last year's tricks. The citizen who can reason is defended against next year's.
