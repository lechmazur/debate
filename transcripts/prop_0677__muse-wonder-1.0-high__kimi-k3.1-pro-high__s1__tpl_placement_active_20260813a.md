[P1.1] Imagine being rejected for a job, or fired from one, and never knowing a machine made the first — and effectively final — call. No one explains, no one can be asked, and you cannot tell whether the reason was your qualifications, a coding error, or a proxy for age, sex, race, or disability. Requiring disclosure of meaningful automated influence fixes that unfairness without banning the technology.
[P1.2] Disclosure is the prerequisite for every other protection workers have. Anti-discrimination law, disability accommodation, and correcting a mistaken record are meaningless if the process is hidden. A warehouse worker flagged for low productivity during equipment downtime, or an applicant screened out because a parser misread parental leave as unreliability, cannot seek human review or supply missing context if they do not know a system was involved. Secrecy makes a bad outcome unanswerable.
[P1.3] That danger multiplies at algorithmic scale. A biased manager harms people one by one and can be questioned. A flawed model can silently reject thousands, learning from historically skewed data and laundering old patterns into apparently neutral scores. Disclosure breaks that silence: regulators, applicants, and workers can spot patterns, ask the next questions, and prevent discrimination from hiding behind a vendor's black box.
[P1.4] Disclosure also improves decisions before anyone is harmed. A firm that must tell people will vet its tools, check their data, and keep a human able to override them. A firm that can stay silent has the opposite incentive: buy the cheapest system, follow its ranking, and enjoy plausible deniability. Transparency rewards responsible vendors and punishes careless deployment.
[P1.5] This is a modest, targeted duty. "Meaningfully influenced" excludes spellcheckers and scheduling software, and disclosure means stating that a system was used and what role it played — not publishing source code. It is a sentence in a job posting, rejection, or termination notice, using information the employer already knows from buying the tool. Against that trivial burden stands a person's livelihood — too powerful to operate in secret.
