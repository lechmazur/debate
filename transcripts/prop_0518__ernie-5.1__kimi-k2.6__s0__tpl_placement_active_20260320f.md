[P1.1] Non‑compete clauses lock workers into a single employer, suppressing wages, stifling competition, and strangling innovation. When a worker cannot move to a rival, the market price for their labor collapses, and firms capture rents that should belong to talent. The evidence from the last decade shows that states which tightened non‑compete rules saw measurable wage gains—particularly for low‑ and middle‑skill employees—while firms adapted without losing productivity. Consider a hair‑stylist who signs a two‑year non‑compete: she cannot open her own shop or join a competitor, even though the skill set is portable.
[P1.2] The clause does not protect a trade secret; it merely preserves the incumbent’s market share. The same logic applies to software engineers, salespeople, and even janitorial staff—any job where the employer claims a “competitive advantage” that is really just the worker’s own ability.
[P1.3] Broad employer exceptions turn this restrictive tool into a loophole. If the law carves out “senior executives,” “high‑earners,” or any category the employer labels, firms will reclassify workers to fit the carve‑out, dragging the exemption down to the median employee. The result is a de‑facto blanket that preserves the status quo while claiming reform. A rule that bans non‑competes for nearly all workers eliminates that gaming.
[P1.4] It forces firms to protect genuine trade secrets with narrow NDAs, not with blanket mobility bans. The competitive pressure that follows lifts wages, encourages entrepreneurship, and spreads knowledge across the economy. The cost to employers is modest: they lose a cheap lever for retaining staff, but they gain a labor market that rewards skill rather than contractual lock‑in. Tech startups, for instance, thrive when engineers can move freely, spawning new firms that drive sector‑wide productivity gains.
[P1.5] In sum, restricting non‑competes broadly, rather than carving out generous employer exceptions, aligns the law with the clear public interest in worker freedom and dynamic markets.

[C1.1] Sweeping bans on noncompete clauses treat every employment relationship as interchangeable theft of labor, but that caricature ignores why firms use these tools in the first place. Companies do not spend years and substantial capital training engineers in proprietary processes or sharing closely held client strategies with sales teams simply out of charity. They do it because contractual protections give them the confidence to invest. Remove that assurance for nearly all workers, and the rational response is to hoard knowledge, starve internal training budgets, and offshore sensitive functions—none of which helps domestic workers.
[C1.2] The claim that narrow nondisclosure agreements can fully replace noncompetes collapses on contact with reality. An NDA is a backward-looking damage claim; once a trade secret leaves the building, the competitive harm is irreversible. A noncompete, by contrast, prevents the breach before it happens. Telling a biotech firm to protect years of research with a lawsuit after the fact is like telling a homeowner to rely on fire insurance after removing the smoke detectors.
[C1.3] The “nearly all workers” standard also mistakes the problem. Abusive noncompetes against fast-food workers or hairstylists are already indefensible under existing reasonableness doctrines, and states can tighten those thresholds without torching the entire mechanism. The proposition asks us to ban a scalpel because some operators use it as a cudgel. In knowledge-intensive industries—pharma, advanced manufacturing, specialized consulting—worker mobility is not costless.
[C1.4] It transmits not just skill but embedded proprietary strategy to immediate rivals, chilling the very R&D spending that creates high-wage jobs in the first place.
[C1.5] Broad employer exceptions, properly defined by objective criteria like compensation thresholds or fiduciary duties, are not doomed loopholes; they are the guardrails that let policy distinguish between genuine coercive lock-in and legitimate protection of business assets. A crude universal ban sacrifices that nuance, replacing calibrated law with symbolic populism and leaving the hardest questions of innovation and investment unanswered.
