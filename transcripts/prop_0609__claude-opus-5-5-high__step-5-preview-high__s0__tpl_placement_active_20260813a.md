[P1.1] A country that spends more servicing old debts than protecting itself from the next storm is not solvent. It is waiting for a scheduled default. That is the trap this motion breaks.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Small island states and low-lying economies borrow, get hit by a hurricane or flood, borrow again to rebuild, and emerge poorer and more indebted. Dominica lost roughly a year's worth of economic output to a single hurricane in 2017. Pakistan's 2022 floods pushed an already strained debt position toward crisis.
[P1.3] Each disaster converts climate exposure into sovereign distress. Relief that ignores this simply resets the clock until the next disaster.
[P1.4] So tie the two together, automatically. When a vulnerable country commits freed fiscal space to resilience, such as sea walls, drought-resistant irrigation, early-warning systems and hardened power grids, relief should follow by rule, not by years of bargaining. Automaticity is the point. Today's case-by-case restructuring is painfully slow.
[P1.5] Zambia waited years for creditors to agree. A standing rule replaces that hostage negotiation with a predictable trigger that governments can plan budgets around.
[P1.6] This also aligns creditors with debtors. Creditors write down debt reluctantly because they fear the money is simply lost. But money spent on resilience protects the economy that repays the remaining debt. A seawall in Barbados protects the tourism revenue that services Barbados's bonds.
[P1.7] The condition is what makes relief rational for lenders rather than charity they will keep postponing.
[P1.8] None of this is untested. Belize and Ecuador restructured debt in exchange for conservation commitments. Grenada's hurricane clause paused payments after Hurricane Beryl. Earlier, the HIPC initiative linked relief to social spending, and recipients' health and education budgets rose.
[P1.9] The motion scales a proven idea and makes it reliable.
[P1.10] Expect CON to cry "sovereignty." But this condition sets a category of spending, not a policy script. Each country chooses its own projects. It is far less intrusive than the austerity conditions debtors already accept.
[P1.11] The real choice is between relief that builds survival and relief that buys time until the next flood.

[C1.1] The motion tries to fix a coordination failure by adding a gatekeeper. Debt relief is slow because creditors are fragmented and each holdout has leverage. A mandatory resilience condition does not remove that leverage; it multiplies it. Every creditor can now ask whether a project is sufficiently climate-resilient, whether spending was timely, whether the country is still “vulnerable,” and then withhold relief while the dispute is resolved.
[C1.2] It also gets the fiscal sequence backwards. Relief is needed because a government cannot fund broad recovery and debt service at the same time. After a disaster, urgent spending includes hospitals, imports, salaries, roads, and bridges—not only assets a lender will certify as resilience. A seawall may protect tourism for decades, but it does not pay next year’s coupon or stop arrears from compounding.
[C1.3] Condition first, relief later means the country may default before the first resilience milestone is audited.
[C1.4] Automaticity is therefore mostly nominal. There is no single global sovereign-debt authority that can bind bondholders, multilateral banks, bilateral lenders, and donor agencies to one definition. Either the rule is negotiated contract by contract—the same bargaining we have now—or a subset of creditors imposes it, creating fragmentation, litigation, and holdouts. Zambia’s delay shows the problem; another condition would be another veto, not a shortcut.
[C1.5] Belize and Ecuador show that targeted debt-for-nature instruments can be useful [P1.8]. They do not show that all relief should be conditional on climate spending. Climate adaptation deserves dedicated grants and concessional finance; debt relief should turn on sustainability and urgent need. Otherwise we tell a drowning borrower to install better drainage before we throw a rope.
