[P1.1] Urban noise and acceleration rules should track the damage a vehicle can impose, not treat a supercar and a subcompact as identical risks. A vehicle designed and marketed to be loud and brutally quick has a different external-cost profile. That difference justifies a second tier of enforcement, not a universal exemption for ordinary cars.
[P1.2] This is not about punishing wealth. It is about internalizing concentrated harm. A high-performance car’s value proposition is often precisely the noise and acceleration that ordinary traffic suppresses: valved sport exhausts, crackle maps, launch control, track modes. The same feature that is a selling point on an empty road becomes an externality on a street with pedestrians, homes, schools, and outdoor diners.
[P1.3] The owner chose a machine engineered to produce more of the harm; the urban public did not choose to absorb it.
[P1.4] The harm is nonlinear. One hard-accelerating performance car can dominate a block’s soundscape and create a sudden high-risk event at a crossing in a way a modest hatchback cannot. That is why uniform enforcement is incomplete: a universal dB limit still lets factory-tuned sport exhausts sit right at the legal edge, and a universal speed limit is weaker against vehicles that reach illegal speed in seconds before enforcement can react. Tighter testing, acoustic-camera thresholds, and acceleration-sensitive enforcement for that identifiable class are proportionate.
[P1.5] The efficient response is layered: ordinary vehicles face the baseline rules; high-externality vehicles face an additional, stricter tier because their expected marginal harm is unusually concentrated. That is the same principle behind stricter rules for heavy trucks near bridges or industrial machinery near residences. It is not discrimination; it is matching regulatory burden to actual external cost. If we ignore that, we let a small number of vehicles consume a disproportionate share of the urban commons.
