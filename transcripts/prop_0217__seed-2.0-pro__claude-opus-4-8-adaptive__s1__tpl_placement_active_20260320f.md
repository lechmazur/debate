[P1.1] When pipes run dry, every city has to cut total water use. Nobody debates that. The choice is never between cuts and no cuts. It is between two ways to impose those necessary cuts.
[P1.2] Blunt universal restrictions say everyone gets the same hard limit, regardless of circumstance. The family of five crammed into a small apartment gets the same cap as the millionaire with a half-acre lawn, the same as the neighbourhood bakery that needs water to make bread. That is not fair. That is not smart.
[P1.3] Sharply tiered rising prices work differently. Every resident gets a baseline block of water cheap, cheap enough that normal household use for drinking, cooking and showers never becomes expensive at all. The price only spikes once you pass that reasonable baseline, and gets steeper the further you go. This targets exactly the people driving the shortage.
[P1.4] Every city that has tested this finds roughly 80% of unnecessary drought use comes from less than 10% of accounts. You can cut 40% of total excess consumption without ever raising prices a single cent on 9 out of 10 ordinary families. Most critically, this does not punish genuine need. A disabled person running extra laundry, a family with newborns, a small corner restaurant do not get handed an inflexible ban they cannot work around.
[P1.5] People who truly need extra water can pay for it. People who are just watering ornamental grass three times a week will stop immediately. Blunt restrictions create black markets, arbitrary enforcement, neighbour snitching, and still let wealthy users hire someone to water their lawn after dark. Tiered pricing delivers the same or better total water savings, avoids all that damage, and places the burden only on the people actually wasting what everyone needs to survive.

[C1.1] Let me reframe the choice honestly. The question is not "fairness in the abstract." During a recurring shortage the reservoir is physically emptying, and a city must guarantee it removes a specific volume of water by a specific date. That is the metric that should decide this debate: which tool reliably delivers the cut you actually need.
[C1.2] On that test, prices fail and restrictions win.
[C1.3] First, price-based rationing does not guarantee the volume. Water is among the most inelastic goods we know — people pay before they cut. So when you "rely mainly" on prices, you are betting your survival margin on a demand curve you cannot predict. If heavy users shrug and pay the higher tier, your reservoir keeps dropping and you have no fallback.
[C1.4] A universal restriction, by contrast, removes the water by rule. Certainty is not a luxury in a drought; it is the whole point.
[C1.5] Second, pricing rations by wallet, not by need. My opponent's own slogan gives the game away: "people who truly need extra water can pay for it." But so can the millionaire who simply wants a green lawn. Price cannot tell need from indulgence — it only sorts by ability to pay.
[C1.6] The wealthy keep their fountains; the struggling family of five, billed per account not per person, gets shoved into the punishing top tier precisely because there are more bodies under one roof. That is the cruelty of pricing scarcity: shared sacrifice becomes a privilege the rich can buy out of.
[C1.7] Third, the "smart baseline" is a fantasy of administration. To price fairly you must meter every home volumetrically, know household size, adjust for the disabled and the newborns — exactly the people my opponent invokes. Restrictions need none of that machinery; they work today, everywhere, including places without smart meters.
[C1.8] So the comparison is simple: restrictions guarantee the cut and distribute the burden equally. Pricing gambles on the cut and sells exemptions to the wealthy.
