[P1.1] The core question is simple: what number should sit at the top of the dashboard, the one that ministers defend in Parliament, that journalists track, that drives the next policy? I argue it should be a measure of national well-being—how people's lives are actually going—rather than GDP growth alone.
[P1.2] Start with what GDP is. It counts market transactions. It rises when we rebuild after a flood, when traffic jams burn more fuel, when families pay for childcare they once provided themselves. It says nothing about whether people are healthier, safer, less anxious, or whether the air is breathable.
[P1.3] A country can post handsome growth figures while loneliness, overwork, and ecological damage quietly mount. The problem is not that GDP lies; it's that it answers a narrow question and we have wrongly treated its answer as the answer.
[P1.4] Headline targets are not neutral. What you put at the top of the dashboard is what gets optimized. Make well-being the headline, and you force the system to ask the right question every budget cycle: are lives getting better?
[P1.5] This is not anti-growth. Growth remains a powerful instrument—often the best one—for raising well-being. But an instrument should serve the goal, not impersonate it. Treating well-being as primary simply puts the means and the end back in their proper order.
[P1.6] Where growth advances well-being, a well-being target endorses it. Where growth comes at the cost of health, security, or environment, a well-being target catches the trade-off that GDP conceals.
[P1.7] And this is doable. Composite well-being measures already exist and are being adopted by serious governments; they track income alongside health, education, environment, and social connection. The question before us is not whether such measures are perfect—no single number is—but which flawed number better steers a country toward lives worth living. On that comparison, well-being wins.
