[C2.2] On [P2.1], "published reasons and judicial review" sounds reassuring until you ask what courts actually review: jurisdiction and procedure, not whether the commissioner's substantive judgment about future harm was correct. Courts can confirm the commissioner followed the rulebook while rubber-stamping a contestable value call about what future people would want. Procedure doesn't resolve indeterminacy — it just launders it.
[C2.3] [P2.2]'s comparison to rights and justice actually cuts against PRO. Rights disputes are adversarial: both sides get representation, courts hear counter-argument, precedent constrains future rulings. A future-generations veto has no one arguing the other side's case — no advocate for the future who wants the reactor built, the grid connected, the emissions cut now rather than later. It's one person's uncontested interpretation elevated to a binding block.
[C2.4] [P2.4] says delay-caused harms belong "within the commissioner's assessment" — but that just smuggles back in the same problem it was supposed to solve: one official deciding, with no electoral check, which irreversible harm — contamination or locked-in emissions — matters more. PRO wants this both ways: indeterminate enough to need a dedicated guardian, determinate enough for that guardian to referee energy-versus-ecology tradeoffs correctly.
[C2.5] And the reversibility framing in [P2.5] quietly assumes refusal is costless. Delayed grid connections and shelved storage facilities also produce extinguished options — just ones PRO doesn't picture as vividly. A veto that can only say no is a coin that always lands on inaction, dressed as caution.

CON calls this a ratchet, but repetition is not paralysis unless every alternative fails the same narrow test of severe, enduring harm.
[P5.4] A government unable to meet that test has not been denied necessary action; it has been denied permission to shift lasting risk onto voiceless people.
[P5.5] No commissioner can guarantee wisdom. The motion does not require that. It requires one real constraint where electoral accountability structurally runs out. Between a contestable, reasoned “not this way” and an irreversible “too late,” democracy should choose the decision it can still revisit.
