[P1.1] A core purpose of an advance directive is to let a competent person govern the later period when dementia undermines deliberative capacity. If such directives were only weakly binding, they would be mere hints that families and clinicians could discard whenever the patient seemed content, difficult, or expensive. The proposition protects the autonomy of the whole person across time, not just the fragmented present self.
[P1.2] Dementia erodes memory and executive function, but it often leaves deeper emotional and evaluative dispositions intact. Values—what makes life worth living, how much dependency one accepts, what one owes to faith or family—are formed over decades and are more stable than passing desires. A woman with Alzheimer's who now smiles at music but cannot recall her diagnosis has not shown a material value change; she has shown the disease’s narrowed lens. Her competent self foresaw exactly that narrowing and bound it.
[P1.3] Strong binding therefore does not ignore later change; it demands clear evidence of a material value shift—an explicit, sustained repudiation or a coherent new pattern contradicting the directive—not a momentary refusal born of fear or confusion. Without that filter, surrogate decision-makers project their own guilt, hopes, or cost concerns onto a vulnerable patient.
[P1.4] Consider a man who, before dementia, refused antibiotics for pneumonia once he could no longer recognize his children. In a lucid hour he may say he feels fine. That is not clear evidence that his values about dependency and personhood changed; it is evidence he is having a good hour. His prior directive should govern.
[P1.5] The opposing view must defend a world where every transient preference overrides a lifetime of considered judgment. The respectful rule is to honor the prior self unless the later self clearly becomes a different valuer. That is the difference between respecting a person and replacing her.

PRO's own safeguard—"clear evidence of material value change"—requires the kind of coherent, articulate, sustained self-report that late-stage dementia by definition destroys. A person who can still explain "my values have shifted" in a way courts or families would accept is, almost by construction, not yet impaired enough to trigger the dispute. So the standard isn't a real filter; it's a one-way ratchet that guarantees the prior directive always wins, no matter how contented, communicative in simpler ways, or clearly enjoying life the current person is.
[C1.3] "Strongly binding unless clear evidence" quietly collapses into "always binding," because the disease erases the only kind of evidence that would count.
[C1.4] Second, prior directives are often bad predictions, not settled values. A directive written in fear of an imagined future is not a deeper truth about the self; it's a guess, frequently wrong, frozen into policy.
[C1.5] Third, and most fundamentally: the person eating breakfast, humming along to music, reaching for a hand, has interests now. Enforcing a stranger's fear against a contented present being isn't protecting autonomy—it's sacrificing an experiencing person to a document. PRO must defend that sacrifice even when the current person is manifestly not suffering. That is not respect.
[C1.6] That is erasure dressed up as principle.

The proposition does not require a lucid late-stage essay: "My values have changed." It requires clear evidence of a material value change, and that evidence can take forms dementia does not erase. The person can revoke or amend the directive while she still has capacity; she can express a simple, sustained change—"I was wrong to fear this; I want treatment"—in early or moderate decline; or a new pattern of commitments can contradict the old instruction.
[P2.2] What does not count is a good hour. Humming, eating breakfast, and reaching for a hand [C1.5] are experiences, not verdicts on whether she now accepts permanent severe dementia as an acceptable life. That is not a ratchet; it is a demanding filter.
[P2.3] The disability-paradox point [C1.4] mistakes prediction for conviction. Yes, people mispredict how bad a future will feel; adaptation research shows they find more contentment than expected. But a directive is not only a forecast of misery. It is a statement about identity: what recognition, independence, faith, or relationship the person regards as non-negotiable.
[P2.4] A woman can enjoy breakfast and still be someone who did not want to be sustained in a condition she called "no longer me." Enjoyment within a tragedy is not consent to the tragedy.
[P2.5] So the "stranger's fear" [C1.5] is actually the earlier self's authorship, and the "experiencing person" is not automatically a different valuer. Their phrase, "erasure dressed up as principle" [C1.6], reverses the case: the erasure is reading transient affect as a new authoritative self and using it to cancel the only first-person judgment we have. Honoring the directive unless values clearly change protects both. That alternative lets any observer say, "She seems happy now," which is precisely when families and clinicians are most tempted to substitute their hopes—or their burdens—for the patient's considered wishes.
