[P1.1] When soldiers burned Benin City in 1897 and carted thousands of bronzes to London, that was not collecting. It was armed robbery. A robber does not gain ownership because his house has air conditioning. Looted means stolen, and stolen property remains stolen even if the thief can polish it better.
[P1.2] If better preservation created title, any museum could justify taking anything.
[P1.3] The preservation argument is colonialism in conservation language. It says: you are too poor, too unstable, too incompetent to care for your own heritage, so we must keep it for you. The instability it invokes was often created by the very colonial extraction that took the objects. And the premise is false.
[P1.4] Western institutions suffer thefts, basement flooding, mis-catalogued crates unseen for decades. Keeping a sacred object in a London storeroom while its community mourns its absence is not preservation. It is hoarding.
[P1.5] This is about who gets to decide. Cultural heritage is not just material to be climate-controlled. For the Edo people, the Benin Bronzes are records, law books, altars. For many communities, an object decaying through ritual use at home is more preserved than one sterilized behind glass abroad.
[P1.6] Sovereign peoples have the right to take risks with their own inheritance. Paternalism denies that right and tells formerly colonized nations their culture belongs to humanity only when humanity is white.
[P1.7] Return is justice, not charity. It restores legal order, restores spiritual and historical continuity, and it builds real preservation. Repatriation has spurred funding, training, and new museums across Nigeria, Ethiopia, and Cambodia. Museums that return do not empty; they finally become credible as institutions of education rather than trophy cases for empire.
