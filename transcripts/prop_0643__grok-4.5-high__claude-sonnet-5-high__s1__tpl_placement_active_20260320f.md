[P1.1] Corporate boards of large public companies hold decisive power over jobs, investment, and community survival, yet they answer almost solely to capital. Workers who create the value have no formal seat. Legally reserving a significant percentage of those seats for worker-elected directors corrects a structural failure of accountability and produces better governance.
[P1.2] Shareholder primacy rewards short-term extraction: deferred maintenance, skill-destroying layoffs, and executive compensation detached from durable results. Workers cannot diversify their risk the way shareholders can; a single plant decision can erase a decade of earnings and local tax base. Elected representatives inject operational knowledge boards otherwise filter out—real data on bottlenecks, safety, and morale—while forcing longer horizons into the room. The result is fewer value-destroying surprises and decisions that protect the human capital the firm actually runs on.
[P1.3] Co-determination systems in Europe have already demonstrated the mechanism at scale. Firms with substantial worker board participation have remained globally competitive, not less so, while showing more employment stability and fewer destructive confrontations. Capital retains control; labor gains voice sufficient to block pure predation. That balance works because the board becomes a genuine forum of the two factors that produce surplus rather than a closed capital caucus.
[P1.4] Public listing already grants limited liability and access to public capital markets in exchange for accountability. Extending seats to the people whose lives are staked on the enterprise completes that bargain. Without a legal floor, concentrated ownership and manager capture keep boards sealed. A significant reserved percentage—large enough to matter, short of veto control—supplies the missing counterweight.
[P1.5] Companies improve, inequality narrows, and corporate power gains democratic legitimacy instead of extracting it.

[C1.1] Corporate boards exist to solve one specific problem: monitoring managers on behalf of the people who bear residual risk and cannot walk away when things go wrong. Shareholders cannot get their capital back if a board fails; workers, however painful a layoff is, can and do move on, draw unemployment, retrain, sue for violations. Baking a permanent worker bloc into board composition doesn't fix that structure—it creates a second, conflicting fiduciary duty inside the one body that's supposed to have a single clear mandate.
[C1.2] A worker-director elected by and answerable to colleagues will face constant pressure to protect current jobs over the firm's long-term health, exactly the insider-outsider dynamic that entrenches inefficient plants and blocks restructuring the company needs to survive at all. That doesn't protect workers—it protects incumbents at the expense of future hires and the company's competitiveness.
[C1.3] Notice, too, what a "significant percentage, legally required" mandate actually does: it applies one governance template to companies as different as a five-person biotech spinoff that just IPO'd and a hundred-thousand-employee industrial conglomerate. Germany's co-determination, which PRO leans on, sits inside a two-tier board system, sector-wide bargaining, and decades of specific legal infrastructure—it isn't a bolt-on seat quota transplantable into a fundamentally different unitary-board legal system. Cherry-picking that outcome ignores how much of its success depends on institutions this proposal doesn't build.
[C1.4] And mandates have exit costs. Firms facing a binding board-seat requirement can go private, redomicile, or avoid public listing altogether—stripping away the disclosure, audit, and securities-law oversight that public status currently forces on them. You'd shrink the public company sector precisely where public accountability is strongest, in exchange for a governance seat that creates internal conflict rather than resolving it.
[C1.5] Real worker voice—works councils, information and consultation rights, profit-sharing, sectoral bargaining—delivers protection without freezing an adversarial structure permanently into corporate law. That's the case this debate should turn on.
