[P1.1] In the race to cut emissions between now and 2036, the only scoreboard that matters is tons of CO2 avoided per public dollar spent. [P1.2] Life-extension is the fastest, cheapest emissions bargain in the power sector. These plants are already grid-connected, permitted, staffed, and largely depreciated. A modest injection of public capital into relicensing, targeted equipment upgrades, and fuel procurement keeps them online for another ten to twenty years, instantly preventing their replacement by methane gas. No wind or solar project can match that speed or carbon return, because none can repurpose existing transmission and baseload infrastructure at comparable cost or regulatory speed.
[P1.3] The true public cost of scaling wind and solar is not captured by the price of panels and turbines alone. Consequently, each public dollar spent on renewables buys a part-time energy source that still relies on fossil backup. Nuclear buys firm, around-the-clock displacement in a single package, delivering far more avoided emissions for every dollar of public money.
[P1.4] New-build programs compound this advantage within the decade. Standardized designs and reformed licensing in active jurisdictions can bring reactors online before 2036, locking in sixty-plus years of near-zero output. Unlike renewable assets that require perpetual replacement and firming, nuclear projects amortize their public subsidy across decades of continuous baseload generation, squeezing more carbon out of the economy per dollar than any intermittent alternative.
[P1.5] Maximum emissions reduction per taxpayer dollar demands keeping existing reactors alive and adding new ones. That is the fastest path to 2036.
