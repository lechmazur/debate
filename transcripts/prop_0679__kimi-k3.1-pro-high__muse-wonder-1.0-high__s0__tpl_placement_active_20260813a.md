[P1.1] A criminal trial is not the place to make defendants prove that the state’s machine made a mistake. The question is not whether AI can be useful; it is who bears the burden before an AI-generated summary, translation, or reconstruction is presented as evidence. That burden should rest on the party offering it. Presumptive inadmissibility, overcome by independent human-expert verification, is therefore a safeguard—not a ban.
[P1.2] These outputs do not merely reproduce evidence. They transform it. A summary selects and omits; a translation resolves ambiguity; a reconstruction fills gaps or sharpens detail. An apparently clearer image can therefore become a different image, and a fluent translation can conceal uncertainty behind confident prose.
[P1.3] Because fluency and precision are not proof of fidelity, the output needs checking against the underlying material before a jury treats it as fact.
[P1.4] Cross-examination cannot reliably cure that problem. A witness can be asked what they saw; an opaque model cannot adequately explain why it supplied one phrase, omitted another, or generated a particular detail. Nor can we assume every defense team has the resources to replicate the prosecution’s tools and uncover subtle errors. Once jurors see a persuasive reconstruction, telling them to treat it cautiously does not necessarily erase its impression.
[P1.5] The cheapest and fairest point to control the risk is before admission.
[P1.6] CON may answer that humans also err. They do—but that is an argument for verification, not for exempting machines from it. A qualified human expert provides an accountable foundation: examining source material, testing the output, identifying limitations, and defending that work under questioning. Verification need not guarantee perfection; it must replace unsupported confidence with an examinable basis.
[P1.7] The presumption also preserves AI’s legitimate uses. It can generate investigative leads, help locate relevant material, and produce evidence that later clears this threshold. Verified outputs can be admitted; unverified ones remain inadmissible. When the cost of delay is inconvenience, but the cost of error can be imprisonment, convenience should not decide the standard.
[P1.8] Reliability must be demonstrated before the machine speaks against someone’s liberty.

[C1.1] Criminal courts do not need a presumption against a tool; they need to enforce the rules they already have. Any proponent must authenticate evidence, lay a foundation, satisfy hearsay limits, and show a technical method is reliable, testable, and properly applied. Judges must exclude material whose prejudice outweighs its probative value. An AI summary untied to its source, or a reconstruction that invents detail, already fails.
[C1.2] Presumptive inadmissibility adds a slogan, not protection, and triggers exclusion even when that showing is strong.
[C1.3] Independent human-expert verification sounds reassuring, but it can be illusory. Experts are fallible, may defer to fluent output, and cannot realistically redo the underlying work. Verifying thousands of translated messages means retranslating them; verifying a summary of hundreds of hours of recordings means repeating the review. The expert becomes either a rubber stamp or a costly duplicate, and the stamp can create more confidence than the checking deserves.
[C1.4] That cost will not fall evenly. Prosecutors have laboratories, budgets, and retained experts. Defense teams often use AI precisely because they cannot afford armies of reviewers—to translate a witness statement pointing elsewhere, summarize jail calls undermining an informant, or organize records showing the client was elsewhere. Make an outside expert the price of admission, and the state can pay while exculpatory defense evidence stays out.
[C1.5] A rule sold as protecting defendants could help convict them.
[C1.6] The category is also too blunt. Transcribing clear body-camera audio, translating a short document, or summarizing disclosed records with citations jurors can check is not synthesizing a face or filling gaps in video. Courts can demand source material, prompts and settings, error testing, and a sponsoring witness subject to cross-examination about production and checking. That targets the actual risk in the actual case.
[C1.7] Scrutinize AI output because it must earn trust in this trial, not because it came from AI. Require proof of fidelity for this evidence—not a categorical presumption cured only by paying a second, fallible human.

[C2.4] The presumption burdens the checkable and launders the uncheckable.
[C2.5] The defense answer fares no better. [P5.1] CON’s own answer supplies the deciding point: an independent expert “could” perform the sampling, but “could is not must.” That is precisely the gap the presumption closes. Without it, the party seeking conviction—or acquittal—chooses the samples, frames the comparisons, and presents its own process as proof. Cross-examination does not remove that structural interest. Independence does not guarantee neutrality, but it adds an accountable check beyond the advocate’s self-certification.
[P5.2] Verification need not repeat every test. Strong checking can be reviewed, sampling can be defended, and inadequate expert review remains inadequate. But when the only assurance is “our side checked it thoroughly,” the output has not earned the jury’s confidence. And when added details cannot be checked at all, no expert opinion can verify them into existence.
[P5.3] The cost objection cannot reverse that logic. A poor defendant needs a faithful translation, not permission to offer an unverified one; resource support should address unequal access, not lower the reliability standard for everyone.
[P5.4] The choice is not credentials versus accuracy. It is independently demonstrated fidelity versus fidelity asserted by the proponent. Before machine-generated assertions help decide liberty, trust must be independently verified—not presumed.
