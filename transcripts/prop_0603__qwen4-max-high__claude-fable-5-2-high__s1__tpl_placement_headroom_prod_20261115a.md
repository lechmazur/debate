[P1.1] A baseline is the one piece of evidence that cannot be collected later. Every other gap in deep-sea mining knowledge — plume behaviour, noise effects, recovery rates — can be studied while operations proceed. The "before" cannot. Once a collector passes over a patch of abyssal seafloor, the community that lived there is gone as a reference point, and no amount of subsequent funding reconstructs it.
[P1.2] That asymmetry decides this motion.
[P1.3] The ISA's operating standard is preventing serious harm to the marine environment. That is a comparative claim: harm relative to what? Without a verified baseline you cannot say what was there, how variable it was naturally, or whether an observed change exceeds background noise. "Monitor and adapt" becomes monitoring without a control, and adaptation without a trigger.
[P1.4] The regulator is left asking the contractor whether the contractor's own footprint looks acceptable.
[P1.5] Note who currently generates the data: the licence holders. Independence is not a nicety here; it is the difference between evidence and marketing.
[P1.6] And the harms are not obviously repairable. Polymetallic nodules are themselves habitat, growing over millions of years; the animals attached to them cannot recolonise a plain that has been stripped. Disturbance tracks from experimental scraping decades ago remain visible, with communities still altered. Recovery on human timescales is not a safe assumption.
[P1.7] A pause is not a ban. Exploration, science, and baseline collection all continue — and improve, because nobody is racing to call the science "good enough" for a licence. What pauses is the irreversible step: exploitation approvals that create vested rights, sunk capital, and political pressure to keep going whatever the data later show.
[P1.8] The question is not minerals versus nature. It is whether we take the measurement before or after we destroy the thing being measured.
