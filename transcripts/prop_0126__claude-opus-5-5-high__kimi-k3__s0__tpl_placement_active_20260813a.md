[P1.1] Families of incarcerated people are paying what amounts to a private tax on love. In many jails and prisons, a fifteen-minute call has cost several dollars, video visits are billed by the minute, and much of that money has flowed back to the facility as a "commission." That is the real choice here: either the state treats family contact as part of running a prison, or it sells a captive audience to a monopoly vendor and splits the proceeds.
[P1.2] My case rests on three claims.
[P1.3] First, the vendor model is corrupt by design. The family cannot choose another provider. The prison picks the vendor, and vendors have competed by offering bigger kickbacks rather than lower prices. The customer paying the bill has no bargaining power, and the institution signing the contract profits from overcharging.
[P1.4] No functioning market looks like this.
[P1.5] Second, family contact is public safety infrastructure. It is widely accepted among corrections researchers and practitioners that people who stay connected to family behave better inside and are less likely to reoffend after release. They leave with somewhere to live, someone to help them find work, and a reason to stay out. Price calls high and you ration those bonds exactly when they matter most.
[P1.6] The costs don't vanish. They return as reoffending, new victims, and children in foster care.
[P1.7] Third, the burden falls on the innocent. Incarcerated people rarely pay for calls themselves. Their mothers, partners, and children do, and those families are disproportionately poor. Imagine a grandmother choosing between her electric bill and hearing her grandson's voice on his birthday.
[P1.8] That is punishment of people never convicted of anything.
[P1.9] CON will say "free" isn't free. Correct: taxpayers pay. But places that have ended call charges, including several states and major city jail systems, have shown the cost is modest relative to corrections budgets, while call volume rises sharply. Security monitoring works the same whether a call is billed or not.
[P1.10] Public money should fund the policy that actually reduces crime, not a predatory contract the public never needed.
