[P1.1] A student who spends all day in front of a screen should not spend the only hours of freedom in front of another one. Before public schools pour money into purely digital enrichment, they owe children the basics of being children: running, painting, building, and improvising together. The motion is not anti-technology; it is a demand for correct priorities.
[P1.2] The first reason is equity. Digital enrichment is not free in any meaningful sense. It assumes a reliable device, a quiet room, and fast internet at home. A student living in a crowded apartment or a shelter may have all three in name only.
[P1.3] After-school sports, arts, and unstructured play require no subscription, no bandwidth, and no silent workspace. They meet children where they actually live.
[P1.4] The second reason is developmental. Children need movement to regulate attention, sleep, and stress. They need collaborative, face-to-face play to learn negotiation, failure, and trust. A basketball game or a chaotic rehearsal teaches lessons no login can replicate: how to lose gracefully, how to read a teammate’s face, how to invent rules when the adults aren’t watching.
[P1.5] These are not soft skills; they are the operating system of adulthood.
[P1.6] Finally, sequencing matters. A school that expands purely digital enrichment while fields sit locked and art rooms go dark is telling students that their bodies and imaginations are less deserving of investment than the next screen. The child who already has a laptop at home gains little from a second hour of coding. The child who has no field, no paint, no playground, gains everything.
[P1.7] Fund the foundation first. Then talk about pixels.

[C1.1] The proposition assumes that every school’s scarce time is best spent first on fields, studios, and playgrounds, and that digital enrichment is a luxury to be delayed. That is a romantic but costly mistake. The real question is which expansions reach the most students, fastest, with the fewest barriers. Often, that is digital.
[C1.2] Digital enrichment is not merely a substitute for play; it is a modern pathway to opportunity. A well-run after-school tutoring platform, coding club, digital-music lab, graphic-design course, or college-prep seminar can serve students who cannot stay for athletics because of jobs, sibling care, transportation, disability, or safety concerns. It can reach rural schools with no theater and urban schools with no safe field. It can offer translation, captions, adjustable pacing, and individualized feedback in ways a crowded gym or rehearsal cannot.
[C1.3] PRO’s equity argument is backwards. Yes, home access is uneven, but that is exactly why schools should expand school-based and school-supported digital programs now, not postpone them until every field and art room is upgraded. Delaying digital enrichment punishes the student who needs a device, a hotspot, or a quiet computer lab today. Sports and arts are valuable, but they also carry hidden costs: coaches, facilities, equipment, insurance, travel, weather, and supervision.
[C1.4] Digital programs can scale quickly, reuse content, and connect students to mentors beyond the neighborhood.
[C1.5] The motion also confuses recreation with structured digital learning. A student editing a documentary, practicing algebra through adaptive software, or composing music on a laptop is not passively consuming another hour of screens. She is building skills that school budgets rarely support but that modern citizenship and work increasingly demand.
[C1.6] Schools should not be forced into a single developmental sequence. If a community’s children lack math tutoring, internet access, or safe indoor options, making them wait while fields are unlocked is not child-centered. Enrichment should follow student need, not a nostalgic ranking.
