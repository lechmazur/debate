[P1.1] Climate policy fails when politicians try to pick corporate winners instead of pricing the actual harm. Raising carbon prices and returning the revenue as equal per-capita dividends cuts emissions across the entire economy while protecting working families. Green industrial subsidies, by contrast, breed lobbying, waste, and regressive wealth transfers.
[P1.2] First, carbon pricing is vastly more effective at cutting emissions than subsidies. Industrial subsidies require bureaucrats to guess which technologies deserve public billions. In practice, this invites rent-seeking, directing money toward politically connected firms rather than the fastest ways to decarbonize. Worse, subsidies only make selected green products cheaper; they place no penalty on burning coal or gas, which can simply expand total energy consumption without forcing dirty plants offline.
[P1.3] A rising carbon price is technology-neutral. By embedding the cost of pollution into every transaction, it rewards whatever genuinely cuts emissions—whether breakthrough grid storage, simple building insulation, or cleaner cement. If a factory cuts emissions by fixing steam leaks rather than buying a subsidized machine, a carbon price rewards it while subsidies miss it entirely.
[P1.4] Second, equal dividends make climate policy fair and politically durable. Decarbonization collapses the moment ordinary citizens feel squeezed by energy bills, as seen whenever uncompensated fuel taxes spark mass backlash. Green subsidies worsen this resentment because payouts flow overwhelmingly to corporate shareholders and affluent households buying new electric vehicles or suburban solar panels. Equal per-capita dividends flip that dynamic.
[P1.5] Because wealthy households have much larger carbon footprints than poorer ones, returning revenue in equal checks means low- and middle-income families receive more money back than they pay in higher energy costs.
[P1.6] Crucially, because each person’s dividend is fixed, every household still pockets the savings whenever they shift to cleaner choices. Governments should price pollution directly, skip the corporate middlemen, and return the money to the people.
