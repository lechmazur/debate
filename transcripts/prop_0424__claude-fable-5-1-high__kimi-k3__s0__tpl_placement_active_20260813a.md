[P1.1] Loneliness is not an information problem. Nobody in a city is lonely because they couldn't find out other people exist. They are lonely because there is nowhere to be near those people without paying for the privilege, and no reason to keep showing up. That is a problem of place, and cities are in the business of place.
[P1.2] Three reasons the balance should tip toward rooms and hours rather than downloads.
[P1.3] First, the mechanism. Friendships form through repeated, low-stakes, unplanned exposure: the same faces at the same table on Tuesdays. A rec center that stays open late produces that automatically. The widower who comes for the newspaper drifts into the chess game; the teenager with no quiet room at home meets the other teenagers with no quiet room at home.
[P1.4] An app must manufacture each meeting from scratch, ask users to schedule it, and hope nobody cancels. Apps generate introductions; places generate habits. Only habits cure loneliness.
[P1.5] Second, who gets reached. The loneliest residents, older people, the poor, the digitally excluded, people whose phones are the very thing isolating them, are precisely the people who don't download a wellbeing app or keep opening it past week two. Retention on such products is notoriously thin. A library card requires no smartphone, no data plan, no onboarding funnel.
[P1.6] It reaches the cases the app never even sees.
[P1.7] Third, comparative advantage and durability. The private market already floods people with connection software: dating apps, Meetup, Discord, group chats. A city commissioning another one is competing where it is weakest and subsidizing a vendor whose product can be shut off, repriced, or breached. A refurbished branch library still works in twenty years, serves a thousand purposes at once, and costs very little per user-hour to keep the lights on later.
[P1.8] So the test is simple: per public dollar, which option produces durable, repeated, in-person contact among the people most likely to be isolated? Open, warm, free rooms win that test. At best, an app points toward them.
