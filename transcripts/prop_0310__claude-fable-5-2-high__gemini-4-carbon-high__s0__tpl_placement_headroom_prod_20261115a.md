[P1.1] Every student in every classroom will, with near certainty, do three things as an adult: feed themselves, live in a home that breaks, and manage money. Schools exist to prepare young people for the lives they will actually lead, and no part of the curriculum has a higher guaranteed usage rate than these. That is the test for what counts as core: not prestige, but whether lacking the skill predictably damages a life.
[P1.2] Start with what "optional" really means. An elective reaches students who already know they need it, have room in their timetable, and attend a school that offers it. Everyone else depends on the family lottery. A teenager whose parents discuss interest rates and fix a leaking tap inherits competence for free.
[P1.3] A teenager whose parents work nights, or never learned themselves, inherits nothing. Calling these skills extras does not make them less necessary; it makes them hereditary.
[P1.4] Then look at what ignorance costs. The eighteen-year-old who signs a car loan without understanding compound interest pays for it for years. The adult who cannot cook pays a daily premium for takeaway and pays again in health. The renter who cannot unclog a drain or spot dangerous wiring pays a callout fee or lives with the hazard.
[P1.5] These costs are routine, they compound, and they fall hardest on those with the least margin for error.
[P1.6] Nor does core status mean gutting academics. A modest required strand is enough, and it reinforces traditional subjects: budgeting is applied mathematics, cooking is chemistry and fractions, repair is measurement and physics made tangible.
[P1.7] So the choice is stark. The other side must defend a system where learning to feed, house, and fund yourself depends on luck and self-selection. Competence at adult life should be something every school guarantees.

[C1.1] If universal adult usage defined the core curriculum [P1.1], schools would mandate driving, laundry, parenting, and swimming. They do not, because core schooling is governed by comparative advantage, not adult ubiquity. Schools exist to build foundational capacities—literacy, numeracy, and analytical reasoning—that demand years of sequential instruction and cannot easily be acquired later. Master those foundations, and a young adult can dissect a loan agreement, calculate compound interest, follow a recipe, or use a step-by-step guide to clear a drain.
[C1.2] Miss those foundations, and a half-remembered tenth-grade budgeting unit will save nobody from a predatory contract [P1.4].
[C1.3] Making cooking, repair, and finance core for every student collides with zero-sum budgets and timetables. Calling this a "modest required strand" [P1.6] ignores physical reality. Schools cannot teach entire cohorts to cook and fix homes with worksheets; they need commercial kitchens, ventilation, tools, lumber, groceries, safety insurance, and specialist teachers. Every dollar spent building mandatory workshops and every hour cut from the schedule comes directly out of reading remediation, sciences, languages, or arts.
[C1.4] That turns the equity argument [P1.3] upside down. Working-class students rely most on public schools for rigorous academic credentials and catch-up instruction—the exact lifelines cannibalized when cash-strapped schools must stretch scarce funds across capital-intensive domestic mandates.
[C1.5] Mandatory life-skills classes also fail on timing. Procedural habits decay rapidly without real-world stakes. Teaching a fourteen-year-old with no paycheck or lease how to budget a household or patch drywall delivers instruction years before it matters, and renters are barred from most structural or electrical work anyway. Practical routines stick when learned at the point of need or by personal motivation.
[C1.6] Keeping these courses optional lets schools fund rich, hands-on electives for students who want them, rather than imposing shallow box-ticking at the expense of core mastery.
