[P1.1] Pedestrians are the only people in this system with no control over the risk. A rider who hits someone on a sidewalk has broken a rule; but the company chose the vehicle, unlocked it, placed it in the public right-of-way, set the speed, designed the app, drew the service area, and kept the data. It knows where misuse is predictable: near stations, crowded blocks, campuses, tourist areas, and no-ride boundaries. This is not a freak accident; it is a recurring hazard generated by a paid service.
[P1.2] That gives the criterion: liability should follow preventable control. Shared e-bike and scooter firms are not passive sellers of a neutral product. They operate a fleet remotely, app by app. They can require rule acknowledgment, slow or shut down devices in restricted zones, detect sidewalk patterns, lock out repeat violators, and insure the risk.
[P1.3] When the law leaves an injured pedestrian to chase one individual rider, it puts the loss on the least powerful party and lets the cheapest cost-avoider keep the upside while exporting the harm.
[P1.4] More legal responsibility should mean a rebuttable presumption or shared liability when a rider injures a pedestrian on a sidewalk or in a clearly restricted zone, unless the operator shows it used reasonable prevention and enforcement. Not absolute liability; rider fault remains. But "the user did it" is not enough when the operator makes rule-breaking foreseeable, profitable, and technically manageable.
[P1.5] The predictable objection is that adults who break rules should pay. That confuses moral blame with practical responsibility. A pedestrian struck near a crosswalk cannot inspect app terms, identify an uninsured tourist, or reconstruct geofence logs; the company can. Suing one rider does little to change deployment, speed settings, warnings, or repeat-offender enforcement.
[P1.6] Operator liability works before the collision. If a company earns revenue by putting thousands of motorized vehicles into pedestrian space, it should carry the legal burden when its system sends them where they are forbidden.

[C1.1] Liability should track immediate, voluntary control over the harmful act, not distant capacity to adjust system settings—and shifting more legal responsibility to shared e-bike and scooter companies for rider misconduct on sidewalks and restricted zones fails that test, while producing worse outcomes for both safety and access. The rider who chooses to swerve onto a crowded sidewalk, ignore posted no-ride signs, or blow past explicit in-app warnings about restricted zones is making a deliberate, split-second choice no remote fleet operator can block in real time.
[C1.2] Geofence technology is not precise enough in dense, building-lined urban corridors to reliably distinguish a sidewalk from an adjacent bike lane, and holding companies presumptively liable for every such violation will force operators to either overcorrect by throttling or locking devices across swathes of legitimate travel space, or pull out of dense neighborhoods entirely, cutting off low-cost, low-carbon travel for low-wage commuters, people with mild mobility limitations, and people who would otherwise pick those devices over car trips. This shift also undermines, rather than improves, safety.
[C1.3] When the law treats companies as the default responsible party for deliberate rider rule-breaking, it dilutes personal accountability for the person actually operating the vehicle: reckless riders face less reason to follow the rules if they know injury costs will fall on the company, not them. It also misapplies the “cheapest cost avoider” standard: companies already use every scalable, practical tool available, from in-app rule reminders to repeat-violator lockouts to secondary insurance coverage for at-fault crashes. Existing tort law already lets injured pedestrians recover from either the negligent rider or that existing coverage, without tilting the scales to create open-ended company fault for choices they did not make.
[C1.4] Piling extra legal responsibility on them does not unlock some unused safety fix—it just creates a deep-pocketed lawsuit target, while doing nothing to deter the split-second bad choices that cause nearly all these collisions.
