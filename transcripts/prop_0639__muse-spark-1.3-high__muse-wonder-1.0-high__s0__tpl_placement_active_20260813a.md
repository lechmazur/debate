[P1.1] Elected politicians should not be the final judges in their own cause.
[P1.2] On electoral reform, lobbying rules, campaign finance, abortion, or painful climate tradeoffs, legislatures have a direct self-interest in the status quo. They stall, dilute, or bury reform. An advisory citizens’ assembly changes nothing here; it merely hands politicians a report they can praise and ignore. Binding power on a defined question is the only way to break that deadlock.
[P1.3] That veto is why advisory models breed cynicism. France convened randomly selected citizens on climate, who spent months crafting measures after hearing experts. The president then cherry-picked and shelved the rest. Participants called it betrayal, and the public learned that participation without power is theater.
[P1.4] If we ask ordinary people to give up weekends, study dossiers, and weigh tradeoffs, we owe them what we owe any serious decision-maker: that a clear decision stands.
[P1.5] And ordinary people can decide well when the process respects them. A sortition assembly is not an opinion poll. Stratified lottery produces a body more descriptively representative than any parliament — more workers, women, young and old — then gives them what voters never get: time, balanced experts, facilitators, competing arguments. We already trust this logic with juries, where random citizens decide liberty because evidence and deliberation create competence.
[P1.6] There is no reason to trust them with guilt and innocence but not with voting rules or planning.
[P1.7] Binding does not mean unlimited. The proposal is specific policy questions, under legal guardrails: a clear mandate, constitutional rights intact, courts able to strike down illegality. That is no more dangerous than referendums, and far more deliberative. We already accept binding power from unelected judges and central bankers.
[P1.8] A citizens’ assembly is the only unelected body that actually looks like the people. Give it not just voice, but verdict.

[C1.1] Binding law needs more than good intentions and a representative photograph. It needs authorization and accountability, and sortition provides neither. No citizen voted for these assembly members, no citizen can vote them out, and once they dissolve, no one remains answerable for the law they imposed. If their policy raises energy prices, closes services, or misjudges a medical or fiscal tradeoff, the public can only watch the consequences arrive with no electoral remedy.
[C1.2] That gap is not cured by deliberation. A few dozen or hundred volunteers who accept an invitation are not the people in miniature: those with time, confidence, and interest are likelier to serve, and small samples swing wildly on who happens to attend. Inside the room, power does not disappear; it moves to the unelected organizers who frame the question, select the experts, write the briefings, and facilitate discussion. Binding power therefore does not remove political influence.
[C1.3] It hides it behind a lottery, beyond public scrutiny.
[C1.4] The jury analogy fails for the same reason. Jurors find facts in one case under law made by elected legislatures, instructions from a judge, and rules of evidence. A policy assembly does the opposite: it chooses values, priorities, and tradeoffs for millions, with no uniquely correct verdict and effects that spill far beyond the single question it was asked. Isolated from the full budget and from implementation, it can promise benefits without funding them or impose costs it will never have to manage.
[C1.5] The real abuses of advisory assemblies — politicians commissioning advice and then burying it — have a democratic cure: require a public parliamentary vote, a published response, or a referendum on the recommendation. That forces elected officials to own rejection and lets all citizens have the final say. What we should not do is escape unresponsive politicians by creating unremovable ones by lottery.

[P2.1] The demand for election confuses accountability with re-electability. When voters decide a referendum, they impose taxes and energy prices on their neighbors, then go home unanswerable and unrecallable. We accept that because legitimacy comes from fair process and defined mandate, not from facing voters again. An assembly authorized by law, deciding an explicit question in public and subject to courts, has that same authorization — only with deliberation referendums lack.
[P2.2] Dissolving afterward is a feature, not a bug: unlike career politicians, members cannot cling to office or trade favors for re-election.
[P2.3] Nor are assemblies the skewed room described. With stratified selection, decent pay, childcare and replacements, they routinely mirror age, gender, class and region far better than parliaments crowded with lawyers and landlords. Yes, organizers shape agendas — but so do party whips, committee chairs and donors in legislatures, only far less visibly. The difference is an assembly hears competing experts in the open, with facilitators tasked with balance.
[P2.4] To call that hidden manipulation while trusting backroom politics is upside down.
[P2.5] The jury objection proves too much. Juries do not merely find facts; they interpret reasonable doubt, self-defense, provocation — contested values with irreversible effects. We trust them precisely because ordinary people given time and evidence judge seriously. A policy assembly does the same on a defined tradeoff, and a narrow mandate with budget limits and legal review prevents promising benefits without funding them.
[P2.6] Finally, the supposed democratic cure leaves the disease intact. Requiring parliament to vote on electoral reform asks incumbents to abolish their own advantages in public — they will vote no, politely. Sending a complex climate package to an uninformed referendum invites slogans, not scrutiny. Advisory plus referendum gives us deliberation without power, or power without deliberation.
[P2.7] Only binding deliberation breaks self-dealing while keeping judgment informed.

[C2.1] A referendum does not rescue a lottery. When millions vote, legitimacy comes not merely from a ‘fair process’ but from equality: every citizen has the same say, bears the same risk, and can campaign to reverse the result. A binding assembly reverses that. A hundred people gain far more power than everyone else because a lottery picked their address, while the excluded get no vote on the policy and no vote against its authors.
[C2.2] A statute creating it is permission, not consent — parliament could authorize generals too.
[C2.3] Dissolution makes this worse, not virtuous. Members who cannot seek re-election also cannot be punished, questioned next year, or forced to fix what breaks. A politician who misjudges must face constituents living with higher bills; an assembly member returns to private life while administrators manage interactions the single-question mandate never priced. Freedom from careerism is also freedom from consequences.
[C2.4] Descriptive mirroring is not political representation. Matching age, gender and region does not match views on tax, abortion or risk, especially when serving requires weeks of availability. Voters who elect a lawyer can at least choose her values and remove her; citizens cannot choose what their demographic ‘mirror’ believes. Nor does openness cure agenda power.
[C2.5] Whips and donors operate under parties, opposition and elections that can expose and expel them. Who votes out the secretariat that chose which economists counted as balanced, or framed climate policy as a levy rather than a ration?
[C2.6] Juries remain different: law, a judge, evidence rules and usually unanimity confine their value judgments to one defendant, subject to appeal. A policy verdict binds millions, trades off against schools and hospitals outside its remit, and no appeal corrects a merely foolish, not illegal, choice.
[C2.7] Finally, if parliament votes no on reform, voters can see that no and punish it. That visibility is accountability. Binding sortition does not break self-dealing; it replaces answerable self-interest with unanswerable chance.

[P4.1] A1: The legislature that authorized the assembly can amend or repeal its decision, courts can strike illegality, and the timetable is written into the authorizing law itself — a mandatory review vote after implementation, plus an emergency brake for fiscal or legal shock. That interval is not unaccountable rule; it is how referendums and ordinary statutes work, which also bite before voters can respond. The difference is assembly members gain no seat to protect, while MPs who refuse a needed repeal face voters owning that refusal.
[P4.2] A2: Parliament itself writes the specific question, budget envelope, and rules for expert balance in public law, with competing camps each nominating witnesses and all materials published. If that framing predetermines the outcome, citizens know exactly whom to remove: the elected framers who passed that rigged mandate, and courts can void a process that breaches fairness. Compare today, where party leaders, whips and donors frame every bill with no balance rule and no separate vote on the frame.
[P4.3] Those answers collapse the CON fear of uncheckable lottery power with hidden organizers. What remains is checked power with visible framers who stay answerable while deciders disband without profit.
[P4.4] And that is why binding still wins the decisive comparison. For self-dealing questions where all major parties benefit from blocking reform, a public parliamentary no is unpunishable because there is no opposition to vote for instead. Binding borrows answerability where it works — on the framing — and removes self-interest where it corrupts — on the verdict.

[C4.1] A1: Even when major parties share an interest, a public vote creates one salient betrayal voters can organize around — backing challengers, independents, primary opponents, or new parties. More decisively, binding sortition cannot escape that gatekeeper. On their own design, parliament must authorize the assembly, write its question, and set its budget envelope. Politicians united to preserve an electoral advantage will simply refuse authorization or design a harmless mandate.
[C4.2] A2: A referendum is legitimate not because millions are perfectly informed, but because authorship is equal: everyone has the same vote, can campaign, and can later help reverse the result. Deliberation improves the judgment of the hundred selected; it does not transfer everyone else's consent to them. Court review asks only whether a choice was legal, not wise, and sharing an age or region does not mean sharing views on tax, abortion, or risk.
[C4.3] Those answers expose why their safeguards form a dilemma. They say the legislature can amend or repeal an assembly decision, with review and an emergency brake. If repeal is genuinely available, binding is advisory with delay: incumbents accused of self-dealing can wait and repeal. If repeal is constrained enough to make binding meaningful, a legal but costly policy rules meanwhile while its authors have dissolved and legislators blame the lottery.
[C4.4] That unaccountable interval remains unjustified.
[C4.5] The contradiction runs upstream. They need parliament virtuous enough to frame the question fairly, fund balanced experts, and permit a verdict against its own interests — yet too corrupt to vote on the verdict. Self-interest does not vanish at framing; it becomes less visible. A rigged question, narrow budget, or tilted expert rule can predetermine the answer, and removing framers years later does not undo the imposed law.
[C4.6] Deliberation is valuable. Equality and answerability decide who may bind everyone. Sortition offers the first while sacrificing the other two.

[C5.1] Binding sortition promises independence from politicians, but it can only borrow that independence from politicians.
[C5.2] Parliament must authorize it, word its question, set its budget, and decide whether its verdict can later be repealed. If those controls are real, self-dealing has not been removed — it has moved upstream, where a narrow question or tilted envelope can predetermine the answer while framers hide behind random citizens. If those controls are weak enough to make the verdict truly binding, then a legal but costly decision governs after its authors have dissolved, with no one to question, punish, or remove for its consequences.
[C5.3] There is no stable middle. Review after implementation does not cure rule before accountability arrives.
[C5.4] That dilemma defeats the claimed separation of powers. Real separation keeps each power answerable for what it controls. Here control is split so blame can be split too: framers blame the lottery, the lottery has vanished, and citizens who never consented to the deciders or their views must live under the result.
[C5.5] Deliberation can inform democracy. It cannot substitute for equal authorship and a removable author. Without both, binding is not the people's verdict. It is power with no owner to answer for it.
