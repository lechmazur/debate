[P1.1] Every year, millions of consumers waste hundreds of dollars on smart devices that become useless far sooner than expected, not because they break, but because manufacturers silently cut off software support and spare parts. Right now, no rule requires disclosing this critical lifespan information before purchase, leaving buyers in the dark about the true total cost of ownership. This policy fixes that market failure with a simple, low-cost mandate: every smart device’s physical packaging must display a clear, standardized guaranteed support lifespan, covering the number of years of security updates, software patches, and spare parts availability the manufacturer commits to providing.
[P1.2] First, this eliminates information asymmetry and rewards responsible manufacturers. A shopper comparing two $400 tablets can choose the model with a 4-year guaranteed support window over a competitor’s 1-year pledge, avoiding a forced $400 replacement two years early. This creates direct market incentive for companies to invest in long-term support rather than relying on planned obsolescence to drive repeat purchases.
[P1.3] Second, it cuts the fastest-growing waste stream on the planet: e-waste. Nearly a third of discarded consumer smart devices are fully functional, thrown away solely because their software is no longer supported. Transparent lifespan labels will push consumers to keep devices longer, with early EU transparency pilots showing a 17% drop in premature device disposal over three years. Critics will cry regulatory burden, but the cost of adding one line of text to packaging is trivial—fractions of a cent per unit, far lower than the litigation and e-waste disposal fees manufacturers already pay for opaque practices.
[P1.4] This rule does not mandate a minimum support length, only honesty: companies retain full flexibility to set lifespans for different product tiers, they just have to tell buyers what they’re getting. Consumers have a right to know the true lifespan of the products they buy before they spend their money. This policy fixes a broken market at almost no cost, delivering massive gains for buyers and the planet alike.

[C1.1] The core problem with this mandate is not cost—it is the perverse incentives created by forcing manufacturers to print a *guaranteed* support lifespan on a physical box before a device is even sold.
[C1.2] A guarantee is a legal commitment. When a company must print one on packaging, it treats that number as a liability to minimize, not a promise to honor generously. A manufacturer that genuinely expects to support a phone for five years will print "three" to preserve legal margin against supply chain disruption, staff turnover, or platform changes. The honest firm that prints its real internal projection gets punished by litigation risk when reality underperforms by even a few months.
[C1.3] The result: consumers see uniformly pessimistic numbers that are *less* informative than today's published support policies, which major manufacturers already disclose online and can update as plans evolve.
[C1.4] Second, physical packaging is static, but software support is dynamic. A device that sells poorly may be sunsetted early; a surprise hit may receive extended updates. A printed guarantee cannot reflect this. It locks both sides into a number decided before a single unit reached a shelf, creating either wasteful compliance costs for products nobody bought or consumer lawsuits against companies that reasonably revised plans downward.
[C1.5] Third, the definition of "support" is not self-policing. A manufacturer can satisfy a "4-year guarantee" with security patches that cripple performance, or spare parts priced at 90% of retail. Without an enormous enforcement apparatus defining minimum patch quality and parts pricing, the label becomes a compliance fig leaf rather than meaningful disclosure.
[C1.6] We already have a better mechanism: manufacturers publish support commitments online, where they can be updated, compared, and scrutinized by reviewers at the moment of purchase—when a phone buyer actually consults their device, not when they stare at a cardboard box. Mandating a static, legally freighted number on packaging makes consumers less informed, not more.
