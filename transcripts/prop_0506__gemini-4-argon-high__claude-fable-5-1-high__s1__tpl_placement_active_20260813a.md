[P1.3] That immunity is indefensible given the narrow threshold at issue: liability attaches only when platforms *ignore repeated, verified reports*. This requires no impossible pre-screening, automated crystal balls, or punishment for good-faith moderation errors. Once a report is verified and repeated, ambiguity vanishes. The platform is no longer a passive conduit struggling with scale; it has actual knowledge of an active crime and chooses inaction.
[P1.4] Since synthetic fraud is not protected speech, shielding platforms after verified notice does not protect free expression—it merely subsidizes willful negligence.

When a platform routes verified impersonation complaints into automated dead ends, it cuts operating costs at the public's expense. [P1.6] It forces platforms to internalize the cost of their neglect, making responsive takedown systems a financial necessity rather than an optional courtesy.
[P1.7] Finally, this places the burden on the only actor capable of stopping the harm and making victims whole. AI scammers operate anonymously across borders and are functionally judgment-proof. Once a scam is verified, the impersonated target is powerless, the defrauded user is ruined, and the platform alone can sever the scammer’s reach with a single click. [C1.1] Strip the proposition to its load-bearing word: "verified." Verified by whom? Not by a court—the whole point of civil liability is that judgment comes afterward. Not by a regulator, who isn't in the loop.
[C1.2] In practice, "verified" means the complainant attached something and asserted it proves synthesis. The motion treats that as the moment "ambiguity vanishes." It is the moment ambiguity begins.
[C1.3] Deepfake detection is not a solved problem. Detection tools disagree with each other, generative models improve monthly, and a determined complainant can "verify" a genuine video as fake just as easily as the reverse. Platforms cannot adjudicate that at scale, so under this rule they won't try. They will adopt the rational response: anything reported twice disappears.
[C1.4] That is not speculation—it is exactly what notice-and-takedown produced in copyright, where automated DMCA notices became a routine weapon against criticism, reviews, and parody. Now extend that weapon to anyone who dislikes being depicted. A politician reports the satirical clip mocking him as "AI impersonation." A company reports a whistleblower's screen recording.
[C1.5] Report twice, attach a PDF, and liability does the censoring.
[C1.6] Notice what PRO's retiree story actually requires: that the platform's inaction, rather than the scammer's lies, the money transfer, and the retiree's own decision, be the legally proximate cause of a life savings vanishing. Scammers resurface under new accounts within hours; a takedown is not a cure. Courts would be asked, in hindsight, to assign a fraud loss to whichever intermediary has assets. That converts platforms into insurers of every scam that touched them—and insurers price risk by excluding it.
[C1.7] Who survives that? The incumbents with legal departments and detection budgets. Small forums, open-source hosts, and new entrants face a lawsuit vector per reported post. The motion sold as accountability is a moat.
[C1.8] The real chokepoint is money, not pixels. Payment rails, bank hold periods, and regulator-enforced response mandates target fraud where it is counted. Private tort liability targets whoever is nearest and richest. Those are not the same.
