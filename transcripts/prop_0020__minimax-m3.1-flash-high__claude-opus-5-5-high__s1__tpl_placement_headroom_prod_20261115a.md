[P1.1] Without a common currency, the EU could have preserved national monetary autonomy while deepening the political and fiscal integration that makes European power useful. The euro was not the foundation of European unity; it was one instrument among many. It imposed a single interest rate across economies as different as Germany, Greece, and Ireland. During recessions and banking crises, governments could no longer devalue, adjust rates, or provide targeted support.
[P1.2] Monetary policy became one-size-fits-none.
[P1.3] That loss of steering capacity weakened Europe’s response to the sovereign-debt crisis. Small differences in fiscal positions were suddenly interpreted by markets as threats to the entire currency. States needing consolidation were pushed into austerity, while others faced pressure to support them. Without the shared currency and its moral-hazard dynamics, national exchange rates and central banks could have absorbed shocks more gradually, protecting jobs and public services without surrendering control over national economies.
[P1.4] The euro shifted critical decisions about interest rates and financial conditions to an unelected institution whose decisions can impose severe adjustment on entire societies. Democratic strength is not measured by how many powers Brussels accumulates; it is measured by whether citizens can hold their governments responsible for economic outcomes.
[P1.5] Nor is monetary union necessary for the single market. Exchange-rate risk and transaction costs can be reduced through stable exchange-rate arrangements, central-bank cooperation, and financial rules. The euro increased cross-border financial integration, but that benefit must be weighed against crises that made a nominally integrated Europe less able to govern shocks.
[P1.6] The pro-euro case treats currency as a symbol of unity. Yet unity built on irreversible loss of national control is brittle. Retained monetary sovereignty would let Europe coordinate fiscal support and structural reforms rather than improvise a monetary union. That is a stronger Europe: economically adaptable, democratically accountable, and able to act together.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on an imaginary alternative: sovereign national currencies floating gently, absorbing shocks, answerable to voters. Europe already tried the real alternative, and it failed.
[C1.2] Before the euro, Europe ran exactly the "stable exchange-rate arrangements" PRO proposes in [P1.5]. The European Monetary System and its Exchange Rate Mechanism collapsed under speculative attack in 1992 and 1993. Britain and Italy were forced out, and the peseta and other currencies were devalued under pressure. That is the counterfactual: not orderly adjustment, but hedge funds testing every weak currency and governments raising rates to punishing levels to defend a peg.
[C1.3] And the "monetary autonomy" PRO mourns in [P1.1] was largely fiction. Under the old system, the Bundesbank effectively set interest rates for the continent, answering only to German conditions. When German reunification pushed rates up, Italy, France and Britain paid the price without any vote. The euro converted German dominance into shared governance: Italy, Spain and Greece now sit on the ECB council.
[C1.4] That is more accountability, not less.
[C1.5] Now test PRO's crisis story. Yes, the euro's original design was flawed. But Europe repaired it: the "whatever it takes" pledge ended the sovereign-debt panic, then came the bailout fund and banking union. In 2020, the ECB's pandemic purchases and jointly borrowed recovery funds protected Italy and Spain from a market rout.
[C1.6] Picture 2020 with a lira and a peseta: capital flight, emergency rate hikes, and twenty-seven governments bidding against each other.
[C1.7] Finally, strength means weight in the world. The euro is the world's second reserve currency, and it underpins Europe's sanctions power against Russia. Revealed preference settles the rest: Greece kept the euro at its darkest moment, and Croatia and the Baltic states chose to join. Nations do not queue up for a weakness.

[P2.2] Their institutional argument inverts accountability. [C1.3] says shared ECB governance gives Italy, Spain, and Greece a vote. But voting over one interest rate is not control over their economies. No government can lower rates for a domestic recession, devalue to restore competitiveness, or target support to particular regions without affecting everyone else.
[P2.3] No elected national government can discipline the ECB independently. Collective decision-making multiplies vetoes.
[P2.4] Nor do the euro’s crisis tools prove monetary union was necessary. Joint borrowing and emergency support can belong to a political union with national currencies. Separating currency sovereignty from fiscal solidarity would avoid the design contradiction: states could support one another while retaining tools to manage inflation, employment, and financial conditions.
[P2.5] The pandemic comparison [C1.6] is weakest because it is counterfactual. A lira or peseta would not automatically have produced “capital flight, emergency rate hikes, and twenty-seven governments bidding against each other.” More relevantly, EU members could have coordinated finance measures while retaining monetary tools. The opponent asserts a market melodrama rather than demonstrating it.
[P2.6] Finally, reserve-currency status and voluntary euro adoption show benefits, not that the euro makes the EU stronger. Benefits do not erase the costs of one rate across dissimilar economies or the loss of national policy autonomy. The decisive comparison is not euro versus chaos, as [C1.7] demands, but coordinated European solidarity with national currencies versus coercive monetary integration. The former preserves external unity and room to respond; the latter repeatedly forces adjustment through the ECB.

[P4.1] A1: Floating, not pegged — and that is what separates my alternative from the EMS. The EMS was rigid: narrow bands around hard parities meant shocks had to be absorbed by defending a politically sacred number with rates and austerity; Britain's ERM exit and the lira's 1992 devaluation were consequences of peg defense, not of floating. Under floating currencies with a coordination framework — wider stability zones, common fiscal rules, jointly guaranteed issuance — shocks are absorbed by gradual nominal adjustment, not a scramble to hold parity.
[P4.2] And "running on the lira" works differently: with debt denominated in the state's own currency, a market attack cannot force a sovereign default; it reprices assets and raises borrowing costs while a free central bank cuts or hikes accordingly. Joint guarantees remove the incentive for funding stops, because no member faces redenomination. Note the irony: the EU built the EFSF, the ESM and joint pandemic borrowing only after the euro crisis forced its hand. Currency union starved the political union of fiscal tools, then improvised them late.
[P4.3] A2: Greece stayed because leaving was worse — bank run, redenomination, loss of EU funds — not because the euro made Greece strong. The record actually supports my case: Greece's post-2009 collapse was fiscally brutal precisely because a currency it could not devalue forces the entire shock onto wages, pensions and unemployment. A government refusing to leave under those conditions evidences a coercive system, not a strengthening one. Croatia joined to align with EU law and tourism receipts, surrendering its own central bank: convergence with the bloc, not proof of eurozone strength.
[P4.4] And exit is a rigged test — there is no orderly euro exit clause, so staying measures lock-in, not revealed preference.
[P4.5] The decisive comparison is a political union with national currencies plus enforceable, jointly guaranteed fiscal capacity, against a monetary union that repeatedly imposes undemocratic, deflationary adjustment and left politics to invent fiscal solidarity from scratch. Only the first is a stronger Europe.
