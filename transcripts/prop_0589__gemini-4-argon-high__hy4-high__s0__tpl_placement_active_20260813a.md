[P1.1] Urban shootings are not random; they are overwhelmingly concentrated within tiny social networks and driven by cycles of retaliation. Stopping them cost-effectively requires choosing a targeted, preventative instrument over an expensive generalist force.
[P1.2] Hiring additional police applies a broad, largely reactive tool to a hyper-specific problem. Patrol officers rarely know which interpersonal dispute sparked a shooting unless witnesses talk—and where distrust of law enforcement runs deep, witnesses stay silent. Civilian community violence intervention (CVI) programs bypass this barrier. Employing credible messengers and hospital-based responders with local trust, CVI reaches people police cannot.
[P1.3] When someone is wounded, a civilian responder at the hospital bedside can intercept and de-escalate friends actively planning a retaliatory shooting that night. A uniformed officer in that room gets stonewalled.
[P1.4] On the cost side of the ledger, the comparison is lopsided. Adding a sworn officer is among the most expensive personnel commitments a city can make. Beyond salary and overtime, cities pay for academy training, cruisers, tactical gear, lifelong pensions, and heavy liability costs. Crucially, because police are generalists, only a sliver of a new officer’s paid hours targets gun violence; most of a shift is consumed by traffic stops, routine service calls, and paperwork.
[P1.5] By contrast, civilian interventionists cost far less to employ, carry none of the equipment or pension bloat, and dedicate one hundred percent of their time to the exact individuals most likely to shoot or be shot next. Moreover, while an arrest still leaves the city paying massive emergency medical, court, and prison bills after a shooting happens, CVI stops the bullet from being fired at all. Because a single unresolved feud can easily spiral into multiple shootings, paying trusted civilians to mediate active conflicts delivers vastly more shooting reduction per dollar than adding another patrol car to the beat.
