[P1.1] In 2014, after Ferguson, the promise was explicit: put a camera on every officer and the era of "his word against mine" ends. Advocates pointed to the small Rialto experiment, where use of force and complaints reportedly fell by half or more, and the White House pledged tens of millions of dollars on that basis. Cameras were sold as a structural fix for police accountability, not a modest recordkeeping upgrade.
[P1.2] Measure the delivery against that promise. The largest randomized trial, roughly two thousand officers in Washington, DC, found no meaningful change in use of force or civilian complaints. Reviews pooling dozens of studies have mostly described effects as small, inconsistent, or absent. The Rialto result did not generalize; it was the exception advocates mistook for the rule.
[P1.3] The reason is mechanical, not mysterious. Accountability requires footage to exist, to be seen, and to produce consequences. Each link weakened in practice. Officers routinely fail to activate cameras, or activate them late, often with little penalty.
[P1.4] Departments control release and frequently withhold video for weeks or months, and some states passed laws restricting public access. Many agencies let officers review footage before writing reports, converting a check on narratives into a tool for polishing them. And even damning video runs into the same walls that existed before: prosecutors reluctant to charge, union contracts, arbitration reinstating fired officers, qualified immunity.
[P1.5] Notice which videos actually changed outcomes. Walter Scott, Eric Garner, George Floyd: bystander phones, not body cameras. Laquan McDonald: a dashcam, with the key police audio missing. Meanwhile, body-camera footage is used far more often as evidence against civilians than against officers.
[P1.6] Cameras produced a documentation benefit and a public-relations benefit. They did not produce the accountability transformation that was promised. The gap between expectation and result is the entire point.
