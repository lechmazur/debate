[P1.1] The kafala system ties a migrant worker's legal presence to a single employer. That is the entire design, and it is the entire problem. When your visa, your residence, and your ability to leave the country depend on keeping one person satisfied, your employer is not just your boss — he is your immigration officer, your jailer, and your landlord rolled into one. Portable, worker-held visas sever that tie: the worker holds the permit, changes jobs freely, and the state — not the sponsor — becomes the counterparty.
[P1.2] Three things follow.
[P1.3] First, exploitation under kafala is not a bug of poor enforcement; it is a predictable consequence of monopsony. A worker who cannot legally quit has no bargaining power. Wage theft, passport confiscation, and unpaid overtime thrive not because employers are unusually cruel but because the cost of complaining is deportation. A worker-held visa makes exit real, and credible exit disciplines employers better than any labor inspectorate ever has.
[P1.4] This is basic market logic: when workers can leave, wages and conditions move toward what labor is actually worth.
[P1.5] Second, Gulf states already know this works, because they have run the experiment piecemeal. Qatar, under sustained international pressure before the World Cup, moved toward non-employer-tied visas and a minimum wage with no catastrophic collapse in its labor market. Complaints channels and wage protection systems exist precisely because the tied system generated constant abuse. The direction of travel is clear; the proposition simply finishes the journey deliberately rather than under pressure.
[P1.6] Third, the pragmatic case is as strong as the moral one. Tied visas push workers into illegality the moment a dispute starts — absconding charges, informal work, exploitable underground status. Portable visas keep workers in the formal system, taxable, documented, and visible. Employers who treat workers well will keep them; employers who don't will lose them.
[P1.7] That is not destabilization — that is the labor market finally pricing bad behavior.

[C1.3] Exit sounds like bargaining power until the employer who paid for recruitment simply stops hiring, or hires through brokers who recover the loss in fees the worker cannot escape. Unconstrained switching does not end monopsony. It moves it upstream, beyond Gulf inspectors.

[C1.6] Free switching also misallocates labor. Project employers and households cannot staff contracts if workers may leave the day a better wage appears next door. [C1.7] Informality swells with people between jobs, overstaying, or hired off the books by firms that never took responsibility for anyone.
[C1.8] Abuse is real. This remedy is not. Without notice rules, cost recovery, and enforcement that actually bites, portable visas trade an assignable duty for a freedom these states cannot administer. That is not reform.
