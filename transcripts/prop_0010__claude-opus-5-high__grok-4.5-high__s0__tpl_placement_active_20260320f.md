[P1.1] A non-disclosure agreement in a harassment case does not buy privacy. It buys silence about a fact pattern that other people urgently need to know — and it buys it from the one person with the least bargaining power in the room.
[P1.2] Picture the ordinary version, not the celebrity version. A woman is groped by a regional manager. She has no savings, legal bills mounting, and a lawyer telling her the company will spend three years grinding her down. The company offers eight months' salary — conditional on her never describing what happened to a reporter, a regulator, a future employer, or the next woman who asks her a nervous question in a parking lot.
[P1.3] She signs, because rent is due. The manager keeps his job and his access. Two years later, someone else is groped, and she too is offered eight months' salary. That is not a series of unfortunate coincidences; it is a functioning business process.
[P1.4] Weinstein, Nassar, and a string of newsroom and boardroom scandals all followed that same shape: not one failure of detection, but many successful purchases of silence, each one legally airtight.
[P1.5] Notice what the law already accepts. Courts refuse to enforce contracts to conceal a crime, contracts in restraint of testimony, contracts against public policy. Silencing the victim of an assault about the assault is squarely in that family. The only reason it feels normal is habit.
[P1.6] The crucial move — and I want the rest of this debate to turn on it — is that unenforceability takes nothing from victims. A victim who wants privacy still has it: she can decline to speak, request anonymity in court records, and keep the settlement figure confidential. What she loses is only the obligation to stay quiet forever at the demand of the party who harmed her. Confidentiality that a survivor chooses is a shield.
[P1.7] Confidentiality that an employer purchases is a weapon, and it is pointed at the next employee, who was never at the negotiating table and never got a dollar.

[C1.1] NDAs in harassment and discrimination cases are not a corporate loophole; they are the mechanism that turns a contested claim into cash, finality, and control for the person who needs them most. Strip enforceability and you do not free survivors—you shrink the only exit many of them can actually use.
[C1.2] A settlement without confidentiality is rarely a settlement employers will underwrite at meaningful value. Liability exposure, copycat claims, and reputational risk are precisely why a company pays. If the price of resolution no longer includes silence, the rational response is harder litigation, lower offers, or no deal. The worker with rent due then faces a longer fight, a thinner check, or nothing.
[C1.3] That is not justice reform. It is a wealth transfer away from the claimant who settled and toward whoever can endure years of discovery.
[C1.4] Privacy is not always the employer’s demand. Many complainants do not want their names, medical details, or workplace conflict on the public record, searchable by the next hiring manager. Enforceable mutual NDAs let them take the money and close the chapter. Choice of silence is still a choice; removing the legal force of the bargain does not expand that choice—it collapses the package that made the choice valuable.
[C1.5] Existing law already refuses to enforce contracts that conceal crimes, obstruct justice, or bar testimony to regulators and courts. Serial predators are not a failure of NDA doctrine; they are a failure of reporting channels, internal accountability, and prosecutors. Making every harassment or discrimination NDA unenforceable treats a disputed civil allegation like a proven public danger and torches a tool used in ordinary, non-criminal disputes every day.
[C1.6] The next employee’s interest matters. It does not automatically override the current employee’s interest in a confidential resolution she accepts. Blanket unenforceability is a blunt instrument aimed at Hollywood villains that lands on ordinary workers who need leverage, speed, and privacy more than a press conference. Keep the narrow public-policy carve-outs.
[C1.7] Do not destroy the settlement architecture that actually pays people.
