[P1.1] When a patient refuses chemotherapy, when a woman seeks an abortion, when a transgender person pursues hormone therapy, the real moral question is not whether these choices preserve some abstract dignity. It is whether the individual has the right to decide what happens to their own body. Bodily autonomy is the ground floor of medical ethics; dignity is a second-story decoration that can be pulled down the moment someone else gets to define it.
[P1.2] Consider what happens when dignity takes priority. Religious hospitals have denied emergency abortions on dignity grounds, leaving women to hemorrhage. Governments have used dignity rhetoric to block end-of-life options, forcing people to die in agony against their will. Dignity is a claim that must be interpreted, and whoever interprets it holds the power.
[P1.3] Autonomy, by contrast, is a boundary: my body, my decision, full stop. It does not require a committee to validate whether my choice is dignified enough.
[P1.4] The medical profession already recognizes this hierarchy. Informed consent exists because autonomy matters more than any external judgment about how a patient should live or die. Without autonomy, dignity arguments become a tool of control. A state that says "we protect your dignity by forbidding this procedure" has simply replaced one person's choice with its own.
[P1.5] The history of forced sterilization, involuntary psychiatric commitment, and denial of pain management all traveled under dignity's banner while trampling autonomy underfoot.
[P1.6] This is the central clash: autonomy is concrete, enforceable, and protective of real harm. Dignity is elastic, easily co-opted, and often serves the interests of those imposing restrictions rather than the patients themselves. When the two collide, autonomy must win—because without it, dignity means nothing at all.
