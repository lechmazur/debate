[P1.1] The central fact about commercial spyware is that export is irreversible. A missile can be tracked; a zero-click exploit, once sold, can be copied, resold, repurposed, and turned around. That is why stricter export regulation wins on national security.
[P1.2] When a commercial vendor sells to an authoritarian state, it is not selling a tool to one customer; it is selling a capability into an ecosystem. That state can use it against dissidents, journalists, opposition, and foreign officials; it can share with proxies; and its security services can reverse-engineer or leak. The result is blowback: diplomatic crises when allied governments discover their officials or citizens were hacked, collapse of intelligence-sharing trust, and a global race in which everyone's communications become less secure, including ours.
[P1.3] Stricter regulation targets the export choke point. Licensing, end-use checks, restrictions on zero-click and zero-day sales to states with records of abuse, and hard limits on re-export reduce the chance that tools we help circulate are used against us or our partners. That does not eliminate intelligence; it preserves the most valuable intelligence by protecting alliances and legal authorities. The intelligence options lost are marginal and replaceable—bespoke operations, domestic development under security control, and liaison with trusted partners.
[P1.4] The options gained are systemic: fewer scandals, stronger coalitions, and a slower proliferation spiral.
[P1.5] CON must argue that unrestricted exports produce net security. But every sale without enforceable controls is a bet that a foreign government, its proxies, and its contractors will show restraint forever. That bet has repeatedly failed. National security is not the freedom to sell everything; it is control over what can be turned back on you.
[P1.6] Regulate exports strictly, and we keep the tool while denying the blowback.
