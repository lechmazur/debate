[P1.1] We live in a world where millions of aging parents face destitution because their children walk away—not because of poverty, but because no law compels them to stay. This is not a failure of love; it is a failure of structure. A legally enforceable duty to contribute to parental care costs fixes that failure without replacing it.
[P1.2] The core principle is reciprocal obligation. Parents who raised children made enormous financial and personal sacrifices, often forgoing their own retirement security. When those parents become indigent and infirm, the children who benefited from decades of investment have a concrete, measurable debt—not sentimental, but economic. Law does not create filial love; it ensures that love, where it exists, is not crushed by the practical impossibility of bearing care costs alone.
[P1.3] Consider the practical alternative. Without legal compulsion, the cost of indigent elder care falls on taxpayers. Public Medicaid and social safety nets already strain under the weight of an aging population. A 2020s-era reality is that public systems cannot absorb every case—and they were never designed to replace family responsibility, only supplement it.
[P1.4] When children who can pay refuse, the public subsidizes their choice. That is regressive taxation disguised as family privacy.
[P1.5] Critics will call this intrusive. But the proposition is narrow: it applies only when parents cannot support themselves, and only to adult children with the means to contribute. Courts already enforce child support, alimony, and restitution. Filial responsibility is a thinner, more obvious version of the same logic—you cannot take decades of support and then claim zero obligation when the provider falls.
[P1.6] The motion does not criminalize poverty or demand the impossible. It draws a line: if you received, you contribute. If you cannot, you are exempted. This is fairness codified, and the alternative is a system that punishes the vulnerable twice—once by age, once by abandonment.

[C1.2] Parental care is not a loan. Children never consented to a repayment schedule, and parental investment is wildly uneven. A father who abandoned his child at five and a mother who sacrificed for thirty years are not the same creditor. Yet the motion’s trigger is merely that a parent “cannot support themselves.” That standard asks nothing about whether the parent earned support, whether the child was abused or neglected, or whether estrangement is justified.
[C1.3] Courts would be dragged into adjudicating intimate histories, with money and resentment as the prizes.
[C1.4] Enforcement is equally corrosive. To assess an adult child’s “means,” the state or a litigating parent would need access to income, savings, housing costs, dependent children, and siblings’ contributions. Families would become collection agencies. Siblings would sue each other over who pays more.
[C1.5] Some adult children would reduce contact to avoid liability; others would be trapped in relationships they escaped. A legal duty does not strengthen family care—it makes care a bill and affection a pretense.
[C1.6] The taxpayer argument proves too much. If public systems cannot absorb elder care costs, neither can an adult child suddenly billed for them. Shifting the burden onto one relative is not fairer than pooling it through social insurance; it is arbitrary. [C1.7] The real alternative is not legal compulsion versus abandonment. It is a humane public system versus privatized guilt. Moral duties can be real without being enforceable. The motion crosses that line and makes family life smaller.

Courts already impose child‑support and alimony despite no signed repayment plan, weighing income, dependency, and history. The same tools that set a parent’s obligation to a child can be turned around: a judge can assess an adult child’s earnings, assets, and dependents, then order proportional contribution. The claim that [C1.2] “parental investment is wildly uneven” is why the motion limits liability to those with means and exempts those who cannot pay; law already distinguishes ability from culpability, as in spousal support where abuse is a factor.
[P2.2] Abuse or abandonment can be raised as a defense without destroying the core duty, just as a debtor can claim hardship.
[P2.3] On enforcement, [C1.4] warns families become collection agencies. In fact, filial‑responsibility statutes already exist in several jurisdictions, operating through standard civil‑procedure: a single filing, a means‑test, and a garnishment cap. Sibling disputes are manageable because contribution can be apportioned by income, preventing one sister from shouldering the whole bill. The fear children will cut contact is speculative; compliance in existing regimes is high when the duty is modest and tied to ability.
[P2.4] The taxpayer argument collapses under [C1.6]’s own logic. Public systems are already strained; asking one adult child to pay a slice is no more arbitrary than pooling through social insurance—it simply redirects money that would otherwise be subsidized by all taxpayers to the person who benefited from the parent’s sacrifice. The opposition’s “humane public system” is precisely what is failing now; without a legal back‑stop the burden falls on strangers while children walk away.
[P2.5] Thus the motion does not shrink family life; it protects the vulnerable by codifying a fairness that market and welfare already demand.

The daughter earning a modest salary is not a fair insurer simply because her parent is old. And if she cannot pay, PRO exempts her—so the “duty” protects the public purse only when the child has money, while the poorest families still depend on the state.
[C2.7] The motion does not solve the fiscal problem; it adds litigation, invasion, and estrangement on top of it.
[C2.8] The decisive comparison is this: a moral duty can survive without a courthouse. A legal duty will be enforced against the vulnerable, evaded by the wealthy, and resented by everyone in between.
