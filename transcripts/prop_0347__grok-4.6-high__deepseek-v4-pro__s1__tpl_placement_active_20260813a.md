[P1.1] Customer-facing gig workers live under a score customers can weaponize. A driver who refuses a sexual advance, a courier whose race or accent becomes the customer’s grievance, a cleaner punished for declining unpaid extras—these people do not receive a quality rating. They receive a sanction dressed as feedback. Platforms then treat the number as if it were a neutral measure of work and use it to hide offers or cut access.
[P1.2] The motion targets that regime: not every star, but rating systems strongly shaped by abuse, bias, or harassment.
[P1.3] No worker should have to accept that as the price of remaining on the app. These scores are not peer review among equals. The customer speaks last, often after the job is done, and the worker cannot force a real inquiry into motive. When a scoring channel is systematically contaminated—complaints clustering around protected traits, one-star raids after refused extras, harassment cheaper than a refund—the platform cannot compel continued participation without making discrimination a condition of work.
[P1.4] The alternative is not chaos. Platforms already see on-time data, completion rates, cancellations, and verified complaints. They can use those, plus human review, instead of a star that launders customer power into an employment decision. What they cannot do is say: absorb racist, sexist, or retaliatory scoring or lose the livelihood.
[P1.5] Refusal here is narrow. It is the right not to be graded by a process already functioning as a harassment vector.
[P1.6] That is the clash. Either ratings remain compulsory even when they operate as bias machines, or workers can step off a tainted scoreboard without being locked out of the work itself. Access to the job cannot depend on submitting to abuse recoded as quality.

[C1.3] Platforms cannot separate genuine discrimination from strategic refusal.

[C1.5] Customers may avoid those workers, offers may fall, and workers who never abused the system become harder to distinguish from those who did. The worst actors gain the most from exit.
[C1.6] If a rating system is genuinely “strongly shaped” by abuse, the answer is systemic: remove retaliatory reviews, audit for patterns tied to protected traits, use verified complaints and human review, and penalize platforms that refuse. That protects all workers. The motion instead gives an individual exit while leaving everyone else under the same tainted scoreboard. If the system is polluted enough to justify refusal, it is polluted enough to justify platform-wide correction; if it is not, no individual exemption is justified.
[C1.7] No worker should be forced to accept abuse recoded as quality. But the answer is not letting each worker opt out of the central trust mechanism while keeping platform access. It is forcing platforms to clean the mechanism. A right without a finding and without a corrective process is an escape hatch that shields bad actors and punishes transparent workers.
[C1.8] The debate should be about accountability, not self-exemption.
