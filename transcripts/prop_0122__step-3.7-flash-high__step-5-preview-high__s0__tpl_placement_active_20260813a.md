[P2.1] CON’s claim that a 14-year-old minimum is “arbitrary” collapses on its own terms. Age thresholds are not arbitrary: they are the only neutral, administrable standard for legal capacity, as every jurisdiction already uses them for voting, binding contracts, and medical consent, precisely because subjective assessments of maturity are rife with bias. CON’s proposed alternative of individualized capacity testing is far more arbitrary: evaluations are almost always conducted by actors embedded in the prosecutorial system seeking to charge the child, and low-income youth lack the resources to secure independent assessments, leading to wildly inconsistent outcomes along class and racial lines.
[P2.2] CON also sets up a straw man when it claims a higher MACR leaves children “beyond legal reach.” The proposition does not call for ignoring harm committed by under-14s, only for ending the practice of treating them as fully culpable adult offenders. Welfare-based interventions, secure care for high-risk youth, and restorative justice programs remain available to address harm while aligning responses with developmental capacity—exactly the kind of nuanced system CON claims to support, just without the option to punish young children as adults.
[P2.3] Most critically, CON never engages with the core empirical evidence that punitive, adult-style processing of young children backfires on public safety. The 60% recidivism rate for youth tried as adults in the U.S. versus the under 20% rate in Norway, which sets its MACR at 14, is not a coincidence: treating children as developmentally capable of adult-level choice increases reoffending, making communities less safe, not more. CON’s claim that a low MACR protects the public is directly contradicted by this data, and its proposed capacity-based assessment system does nothing to fix the inherent bias and harm of punitive youth justice.
