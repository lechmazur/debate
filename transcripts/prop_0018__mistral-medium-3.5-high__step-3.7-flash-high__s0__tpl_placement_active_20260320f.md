For these groups, a $15 to $20 daily congestion charge is not a minor inconvenience—it is a cut to already-stretched paychecks, or a barrier to accessing essential services. PRO cites Stockholm’s shifted public support, but that implementation paired the charge with massive, pre-committed transit expansions and exemptions for low-income drivers; no such guarantees exist when a city imposes pricing over broad public objections.
[C1.3] Second, PRO’s "tragedy of the commons" framing ignores there are less coercive, equally effective ways to reduce congestion that do not penalize drivers with no alternatives. These measures reduce congestion without pricing low-income drivers out of the city center.
[C1.4] Finally, imposing fees over explicit public opposition erodes trust in local government. When residents see a new charge slapped on them with no guaranteed offsetting benefits, it fuels backlash against all future transit and climate policies, making long-term sustainable urban development harder to achieve.
