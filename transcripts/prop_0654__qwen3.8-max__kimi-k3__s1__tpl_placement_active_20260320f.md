[P1.1] Governments should require embedded structural-health-monitoring sensors in all new bridges, dams, and power-grid assets because these systems fail in ways periodic inspections are poorly designed to catch. A bridge can develop fatigue cracks in hidden connections, scour around piers after floods, or corrosion inside sealed members. A dam can show internal seepage or piezometric pressure changes long before surface stains appear. Transmission lines can overheat, sag, and ignite faults between patrol cycles.
[P1.2] Manual inspections are snapshots: visual, intermittent, dependent on access, weather, lighting, and inspector judgment. Sensors provide continuous measurements of strain, vibration, tilt, temperature, corrosion, displacement, and leakage, turning invisible risk into a visible trend.
[P1.3] The decisive advantage is time. Continuous monitoring converts sudden emergencies into managed maintenance. When a sensor trend crosses a threshold, engineers can inspect the precise component, dispatch crews, reduce loads, isolate a line, or lower reservoir levels before a small anomaly becomes a collapse, blackout, or flood. That is especially important under climate stress, where floods, heat waves, and wildfires push assets beyond historical design envelopes.
[P1.4] A periodic schedule cannot know that a storm last Tuesday undermined a pier unless someone later goes to look; an instrumented bridge can flag the change immediately.
[P1.5] This is also fiscally prudent. The incremental cost of embedding sensors during construction is small compared with emergency reconstruction, liability, economic disruption, and loss of life. Requiring sensors in new assets avoids the inequity of relying on post-disaster learning. Government should set the rule because the risks are public: a dam failure, bridge collapse, or cascading grid failure does not respect private balance sheets.
[P1.6] The status quo asks inspectors to find hidden deterioration after it has developed; the proposition requires new infrastructure to report its own condition while there is still time to act. That is the safer, cheaper, and more accountable standard.

[C1.1] The proposition is not "sensors are useful." It is that every new bridge, dam, and grid asset — from a major suspension span to a county culvert carrying forty cars a day — must be instrumented by law, regardless of engineering judgment. That blanket mandate is where the case collapses.
[C1.2] First, the mandate spends safety money where risk is lowest. New assets are built to modern codes and are the least likely to fail. The collapses we actually mourn — the I-35W bridge in Minneapolis, the Morandi bridge in Genoa, the Oroville spillway — were aging structures, decades old. A rule confined to new construction does nothing for that dangerous inventory while diverting funds from fixing it.
[C1.3] Every dollar embedded in a low-risk new culvert is a dollar not spent on the corroded 1960s bridge down the road.
[C1.4] Second, sensors are not a one-time purchase; they are a permanent obligation. They drift, corrode, lose power, and become obsolete in ten to fifteen years — while a dam lives for a century. A mandate locks in decades of calibration, data storage, software, and specialist staff that small utilities and rural counties simply lack. And on the power grid, thousands of networked sensors are thousands of new digital doors into critical infrastructure — an attack surface created by statute.
[C1.5] Third, sensors do not replace human judgment; they multiply the demand for it. Data is not insight. False alarms dispatch scarce crews on false trails; missed calibrations create false reassurance. A mayor told "the sensors say it's fine" has less reason to send an inspector, not more.
[C1.6] The system fails the moment the data pipeline does — quietly.
[C1.7] Engineers already install monitoring where risk justifies it: long spans, high-hazard dams, critical corridors. That targeted, risk-based practice is the sensible standard. The proposition replaces it with a rigid legal formula that ignores consequence, ignores lifecycle cost, and freezes today's technology into tomorrow's concrete.
[C1.8] Vote CON for monitoring guided by engineering judgment, not by blanket statute.
