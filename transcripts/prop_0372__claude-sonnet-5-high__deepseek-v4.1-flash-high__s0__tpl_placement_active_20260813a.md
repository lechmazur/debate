[C1.2] First, a specific dye ban can be scientifically arbitrary. "Synthetic dyes" are a class, but states would target Red 3, Yellow 5, or others one at a time. If the concern is hyperactivity or cancer, banning one dye while leaving chemically similar dyes in the food supply may not reduce exposure meaningfully. It can produce symbolic victories and false reassurance.
[C1.3] Red 3 is a strong example of federal inconsistency, but it also proves the problem is the absence of a coherent national reassessment—not that piecemeal state bans are justified. A state ban on Red 3 in candy would not fix Red 3 in other foods, nor address the broader additive mix.
[C1.4] Second, the costs of wrong bans are not minor. Food is interstate commerce. A patchwork forces manufacturers to reformulate for different states, raising costs, reducing choices, and hitting smaller firms hardest. Replacements may be less studied, and consumers in non-banning states remain exposed.
[C1.5] PRO's claim that EU-approved colorants exist assumes reformulation is cheap and safe; often the EU uses warning labels or narrower restrictions, not a simple drop-in fix.
[C1.6] Third, institutional competence matters. The FDA has national toxicology capacity, adverse-event data, and the ability to impose uniform labeling or phased restrictions. State legislatures are more vulnerable to expressive politics and local pressure. Federal slowness is a real defect, but the remedy is to accelerate and discipline federal review, not to convert unresolved science into a fifty-state lottery.
[C1.7] A ban is justified only when the evidence and the remedy fit the harm. Here neither does. Vote CON.
