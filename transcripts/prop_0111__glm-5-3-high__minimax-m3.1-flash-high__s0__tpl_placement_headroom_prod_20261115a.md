[P1.1] Primary care is the part of medicine where small, inexpensive interventions prevent large, expensive catastrophes. A vaccinated child, a managed diabetic, a caught hypertension, a treated depression — these are cheap. The emergencies they prevent — septic complications, amputations, strokes, crises — are devastating and costly. That asymmetry is the entire case for a government guarantee, and it is why guaranteeing primary care while leaving elective and supplementary services to private markets is not a compromise; it is good economics and good ethics at the same time.
[P1.2] Start with the economics. Primary care has the strongest claim to being a public good in health: everyone needs it, demand is predictable, and the cost of exclusion falls on everyone else. When an uninsured person skips a physician visit for chest pain and arrives at an emergency room, the public pays anyway — at a higher price, with worse outcomes. Universal primary care converts these hidden, inflated costs into visible, budgeted, cheaper ones.
[P1.3] It is the one sector where coverage expansion plausibly pays for much of itself through avoided hospitalizations, better chronic disease management, and earlier diagnosis. Elective services — cosmetic procedures, boutique checkups, private rooms — carry no such externality. Nobody's health collapses because someone else paid out of pocket for a nicer hospital suite. The proposition draws precisely the right line.
[P1.4] Second, the moral case. A society that guarantees primary care affirms that no one's basic health should depend on income at the moment of need. This is not a radical claim; it is the principle behind public education and clean water. And crucially, allowing private markets for supplementary services preserves choice, innovation, and personal spending freedom — the values opponents rightly worry about — without sacrificing anyone's access to the essentials.
[P1.5] Finally, this is tested in the real world. Systems built on universal primary care with parallel private supplementary sectors — like Britain's NHS alongside private practice, or Australia's bulk-billing model — deliver broad, affordable front-line access while retaining a private tier. [C1.1] A universal primary-care guarantee can fail even if the government pays every bill. Money buys claims, not consultations. If clinicians are absent from rural districts, if training slots are too few, or if clinics cannot survive on public tariffs, a legal entitlement produces waiting lists, travel, and informal charges rather than access. Governments can guarantee the financing of primary care; delivery requires clinicians and infrastructure.
[C1.2] The second problem is opportunity cost. A guarantee turns an open-ended fiscal promise into a political obligation that must be honoured during recessions, epidemics, and wars. The same tax revenue spent guaranteeing primary care may be needed for mental-health treatment, emergency capacity, maternal care, or targeted subsidies for the poor. The proposition supplies no limit on what “primary care” must include.
[C1.3] If prevention, medication, dental care, and unlimited follow-ups are bundled together, low-value marginal visits can displace higher-value treatment.
[C1.4] The mixed-market boundary is also unstable. “Elective” and “supplementary” are not natural categories; private insurers and providers classify services according to reimbursement incentives. Hospitals can recode a procedure, clinics can expand into specialist work, and employers can route referrals toward reimbursable services. Public primary care may then refer patients into a private system it cannot afford, while private plans steer uncomplicated cases away from shared capacity. Government must define the package and control billing, or the supposed private tier will cream-skim and shift costs upward.
[C1.5] Finally, the emergency-room externality does not prove the proposed solution. Higher use of emergency departments reflects not only missed primary care but also hospital prices, insurance design, and unmet social needs. Guaranteeing more primary-care demand without expanding supply can simply add public spending without reducing emergencies. The sensible objective is universal access to a defined, evidence-based core, with subsidies concentrated where financial barriers are decisive and capacity constraints directly addressed.
