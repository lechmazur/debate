The evidence offered is promising, but it comes largely from small phase II studies with short follow-up and, crucially, functional unblinding: patients usually know whether they received a psychedelic. Expectancy, therapist enthusiasm, and intensive selection are not noise; they may be part of the effect. That does not make the results fraudulent.
[C1.2] It makes them insufficient to justify rolling the model out beyond narrow trials, where exclusion criteria, monitoring, and data collection are precisely what separate hope from evidence.
[C1.3] “Carefully regulated” is doing enormous work in this motion, and it is not a plan. Who trains and supervises therapists? What psychotherapy protocol is required? How are boundary violations, worsening suicidality, psychosis, cardiovascular events, and diversion tracked outside a trial?
[C1.4] Psychedelic-assisted therapy is not a pill dispensed at a pharmacy; it is a relational intervention delivered to people in altered, vulnerable states. Licensing clinics creates commercial pressure to widen indications, lower screening thresholds, and sell hope to desperate patients. The opioid analogy cuts against the proposition: regulated medical channels can still produce mass harm. Ketamine clinics illustrate how quickly off-label practice outruns evidence.
[C1.5] ECT is a hospital-based procedure, not a template for outpatient psychedelic clinics.
[C1.6] Consent is also weaker than the motion assumes. “Severe treatment-resistant” describes desperate people who may pay heavily, travel far, and forgo proven care for an unproven course. Real-world screening cannot replicate trial exclusions for comorbid psychosis, bipolarity, or polypharmacy. And the underground-use argument does not establish medical authorization; it argues for better trials, registries, and harm reduction, not for converting experimental protocols into licensed services.
[C1.7] The responsible path is rigorous trials and research-grade compassionate-use registries that generate data, not routine permission. Until safety, durability, and scalable delivery are demonstrated, governments should not permit this outside narrow clinical-trial settings. The patients the motion claims to protect are exactly those most harmed by premature authorization.
