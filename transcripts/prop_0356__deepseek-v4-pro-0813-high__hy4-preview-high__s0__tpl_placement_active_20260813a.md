[P1.1] The proposition draws a necessary line: continuous in-car behavior surveillance should not be used for pricing unless drivers opt in on genuinely fair terms. Without that line, “choice” becomes a penalty.
[P1.2] First, car insurance is legally required for most drivers and essential to work, school, and care. When the affordable premium requires continuous monitoring, opt-in is manufactured by economic pressure. A night-shift worker can “choose” the surveillance policy or pay far more, but that is not consent; it is a privacy toll. Fair terms require a non-punitive, non-surveilled option, not a discount from an inflated baseline.
[P1.3] Second, continuous behavior data is not a transparent measure of risk. It collects location, time, speed, braking, cornering, phone use, and possibly trip purpose. Algorithms can penalize necessary night driving, dense urban routes, or hard braking to avoid a child. The score is proprietary; drivers often cannot know what raised the premium or contest it.
[P1.4] That is algorithmic discipline, not underwriting. Without fair opt-in, it embeds discrimination by neighborhood, shift work, and life circumstances behind a black box.
[P1.5] Third, surveillance pricing flips insurance from mutual risk-sharing into behavioral control. Drivers learn to drive for the algorithm, avoid certain places, or leave the phone untouched because they fear a rate increase. That chilling effect should not be imposed by default. The insurer gains intimate mobility data valuable far beyond collision risk; the driver bears the risk of leaks, secondary use, and future price changes.
[P1.6] The rule should be strict: no continuous surveillance pricing unless the opt-in is voluntary, informed, revocable, and not the price of normal affordability. Insurers seeking to monetize every trip should bear the burden of proving fairness, not drivers. Drivers should not have to trade routine privacy for legally required coverage.
