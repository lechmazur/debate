[P1.1] Sports fandom is not a bundle. It is a parish. Most supporters care obsessively about one club, one national team, or one competition—not every event a rights-holder happens to own. The current system forces that fan to buy a bloated subscription full of sports they will never watch.
[P1.2] That mismatch is unfair and commercially myopic, which is why broadcasters should offer cheaper team- or competition-specific streaming passes.
[P1.3] The bundle is not a neutral product; it is a pricing mechanism that charges the casual or single-team fan the average cost of everything. A supporter who wants twenty matches a season ends up underwriting endless golf and studio filler. That prices out younger, lower-income, and diaspora fans—exactly the audiences sport needs. When legal access is unaffordable or ill-fitting, piracy fills the gap.
[P1.4] A cheap, focused pass converts that demand into revenue instead of criminalizing loyalty.
[P1.5] This is not a tax on broadcasting; it is smarter packaging. Streaming has near-zero marginal cost, granular data, and targeted advertising. A club-specific pass can be priced low enough to attract volume, while bundles remain the premium option for completists. Formula 1’s own direct streaming service shows the model: competition-focused, cheaper than a full sports tier, and additive.
[P1.6] Broadcasters also gain what bundles destroy: data on what each fan values, enabling upsells rather than resentment.
[P1.7] The standard defense of bundling is that it maximizes rights fees and cross-subsidizes smaller sports. But the proposition does not abolish bundles; it ends coercion. Fans who want breadth can still buy breadth. Smaller sports can be supported through optional bundles, carriage rules, or sponsorship—not by forcing everyone to fund everything.
[P1.8] The real metric is not maximum extraction from a captive audience; it is sustainable funding plus access. Cheaper team- or competition-specific passes align price with passion, grow the fanbase, and still pay for sport. That beats defending an outdated bundle most fans never wanted.

[C1.1] A match looks free to stream only after someone has paid to create it. Rights auctions, cameras, commentary, graphics, and anti-piracy protection are enormous fixed costs, paid before a single fan logs in. The bundle spreads those costs across millions of viewers and many sports. Replace it with cheap team-only passes and the arithmetic collapses: either the pass is not actually cheap, or revenue falls so far that rights fees, production quality, and investment fall with it.
[C1.2] That cross-subsidy is not a flaw; it is the product. A single-team fan does not only consume their team. They consume a league — credible opponents, a fixture list, relegation battles, cup runs, and competitive balance. Bundles help sustain smaller clubs and less glamorous competitions that big clubs need to play against.
[C1.3] Let Manchester United, the Lakers, or Real Madrid fans peel off into discounted silos, and money concentrates where popularity already is. The rich get richer, the rest shrink, and even the loyal fan is left watching a weaker, more predictable contest.
[C1.4] Bundles also protect discovery. Women's football, lower-league football, darts, cycling, and emerging sports gain viewers because they sit alongside headline events, not because millions would separately hunt and pay for them in advance. In an a la carte world, people buy only what they already love. New audiences are never created, sponsors follow the same narrow audiences, and sports outside the elite lose both money and exposure.
[C1.5] Finally, fragmentation punishes the fans it claims to free. Follow a club, a national team, and one European competition and you are juggling three passes, three renewals, and three price rises — often for more than one bundle cost. Broadcasters forced to cannibalize their core product will raise bundle prices for multi-sport households to compensate. Cheaper micro-passes sound like choice, but in practice they mean higher total costs, weaker leagues, and fewer sports surviving to be chosen.
