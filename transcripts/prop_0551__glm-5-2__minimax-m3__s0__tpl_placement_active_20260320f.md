[P1.1] Water and electricity were not always legally enforceable universal services. They became so because societies recognized that without guaranteed access, meaningful participation in civic and economic life collapsed for those left out. Internet access has crossed that same threshold.
[P1.2] Consider what happens without high-quality internet today. A child cannot complete homework assigned through online platforms. A jobseeker cannot apply for positions that only accept online applications. A patient cannot access telehealth.
[P1.3] A citizen cannot file taxes, claim benefits, or participate in government consultations that have migrated online. The exclusion is not merely inconvenient — it is a systematic denial of opportunity.
[P1.4] The argument for legal enforceability, not just policy aspiration, is structural. Where internet access is treated as a market good, providers rationally prioritize profitable urban and suburban areas. Rural communities, low-income neighborhoods, and remote regions get underserved or ignored entirely. Voluntary targets and corporate goodwill do not reach the people who are hardest to serve because that is precisely where the business case is weakest.
[P1.5] Legal enforceability flips the incentive: the right belongs to the citizen, and the provider or state must meet it.
[P1.6] The comparison to water and electricity is not a claim about biological necessity. It is a claim about structural logic. Essential services subject to market failure require guaranteed universal access as a matter of right, not charity. Electricity does not sustain life the way water does, yet we enforce universal access because participation in modern life without it is impossible.
[P1.7] Internet has reached that point.
[P1.8] "High-quality" matters because token access — speeds too slow for video, connections that drop during a work call — does not enable the uses that make internet essential. The standard must match the functional requirement: enough bandwidth, reliability, and latency to work, learn, and access services.
[P1.9] Countries that refuse to make this commitment are not preserving freedom. They are preserving exclusion.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to treat high-quality internet as a legally enforceable universal service, like water or electricity. The analogy fails on definitional instability, structural mismatch, and the perverse consequences of legal entitlement.
[C1.2] First, the term "high-quality" is doing enormous hidden work. Water and electricity have stable, measurable units: liters, volts, kilowatts. A court can order a utility to deliver a defined quantity. Internet quality involves bandwidth, latency, reliability, and data caps — standards that shift every few years.
[C1.3] What counts as high-quality today will be inadequate by 2030. A legal right pegged to a moving technical target invites two failures: courts freeze the standard, entrenching yesterday's internet, or regulators rewrite it endlessly, politicizing the definition. Either way, citizens litigate over a moving goalpost.
[C1.4] Second, the physical economics differ. Water pipes and electrical grids, once built, serve additional households at low marginal cost. Last-mile internet in rural or mountainous terrain can require thousands of dollars per household in buildout. Forcing universal service across Nunavut or the Australian outback imposes costs water and electricity analogies never faced.
[C1.5] The motion does not solve this — it just transfers the bill to whoever the legal duty lands on, while the unserved customer is still unserved.
[C1.6] Third, legal enforceability substitutes bureaucratic rigidity for the flexibility that delivers connectivity. Subsidies, community networks, and municipal broadband have brought service where pure markets failed. A legal right tends to entrench incumbents, slow innovation, and invite regulatory capture by the very providers it is meant to constrain. Courts cannot easily force a company to serve a customer at a loss in a remote area.
[C1.7] The right exists on paper; the connection does not appear.
[C1.8] Finally, a right to access is hollow without devices, skills, and affordability — none of which the motion addresses. A family without a laptop or training cannot use a 100-megabit pipe any more than they can drink from a dry well. Treating internet as legally enforceable universal service promises inclusion while leaving the actual barriers untouched.

[P2.2] The choice is not between a static right and a moving goalpost — it is between a right that gets updated through public process and no right at all, where the standard is set unilaterally by whatever a provider finds profitable.
[P2.3] CON's cost argument actually collapses into the case for enforceability. Yes, last-mile buildout in remote areas is expensive. That is precisely why markets will never reach those households voluntarily. Subsidies and municipal broadband — CON's preferred alternatives — are discretionary, patchwork, and politically vulnerable.
[P2.4] A legal entitlement creates a durable obligation that survives budget cuts and changing administrations. CON says the motion "just transfers the bill" — but that is what every universal service does. Electricity cross-subsidization means urban ratepayers help fund rural connections. That is not a flaw; it is the mechanism.
[P2.5] The regulatory capture point cuts against CON. Incumbents capture systems more easily when no affirmative obligation exists and cherry-picking profitable areas faces no legal challenge. A citizen enforceable right gives communities leverage they currently lack against providers who skip them.
[P2.6] Finally, the "hollow right" objection — that devices, skills, and affordability also matter — could be raised against universal electricity or water. You need wiring and plumbing to use those services. Nobody concludes that therefore universal service is wrong. [P2.7] CON has not shown that discretionary, market-driven approaches serve the excluded. They have shown that enforcement is hard. Exclusion is harder.
