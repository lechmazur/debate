[P1.1] A billing dispute is not a frequently asked question. A cancellation is not a marketing upsell. A safety complaint is not a routine ticket. Yet companies increasingly force people into chatbot mazes precisely at the moments when clarity, authority, and urgency matter most.
[P1.2] First, billing disputes require human judgment. When a charge is wrong, a bot can only match keywords against a script. It cannot reconcile a proration error, untangle a double subscription, or recognize when a vulnerable customer is being misled. The result is a loop of irrelevant menu options that delays the correction of a real financial error and erodes trust.
[P1.3] Second, cancellations reveal the problem starkly. Many firms design chatbot gauntlets as retention theater—endless offers, hidden paths, and artificial friction. A requirement to reach a human is not a refusal of automation; it is a rejection of coercion dressed as service. Businesses profit from friction; customers pay for it with time and anxiety.
[P1.4] Third, safety complaints are categorically different. If a product malfunctions, a drug triggers unexpected side effects, or a vehicle shows a defect, a chatbot cannot triage risk, escalate to engineering, or document a hazard with the nuance a trained human can. Delay here is not an inconvenience; it is a potential injury amplified by corporate indifference.
[P1.5] The objection will be cost. But the status quo already spends enormous resources on failed bot sessions that escalate to a human anyway, after the customer has been exhausted. A fast human path is cheaper, faster, and more humane in the long run. Treating these three categories as ordinary customer service misreads what is actually at stake.
[P1.6] Automation is fine for balance checks and store hours. For disputes, exits, and dangers, people deserve a person. The rule should be simple: if the issue touches a customer's money, freedom to leave, or bodily safety, the chatbot must hand off to a human without a fight.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses reaching a person with obtaining a remedy. A company could comply instantly by attaching a chat button to a three-hour queue, or transferring a customer to an agent who lacks authority to issue a refund or investigate a hazard. That is human access without human accountability. A fast path is valuable only if it shortens resolution; otherwise, it adds one more frustrating layer.
[C1.2] PRO treats billing, cancellation, and safety complaints as categorically beyond automation, but category is not capability. Modern support software can authenticate a customer, retrieve invoices, identify duplicate charges, calculate proration, execute a cancellation while preserving required records, and route safety reports according to product, symptoms, location, and severity. These are structured tasks at which software can outperform a novice or overloaded agent. A human who must then verify the same information may simply type what the bot already knew.
[C1.3] A safety complaint illustrates the opposite: what matters is not merely a human at the front, but competent escalation to product-safety, legal, or emergency functions. A general customer-service agent may be no safer, and an immediate transfer may produce misinformation. For an apparent medical reaction, an automated triage flow can provide emergency guidance while simultaneously alerting the appropriate team. A promise of “human contact” promises none of that.
[C1.4] The blanket rule also ignores incentives. If every dispute becomes a human interaction, fraud and abuse rise: people can repeatedly invoke safety complaints to trigger costly reviews, or exploit billing and cancellation channels intended for vulnerable customers. Companies will either overstaff low-value queues, pass costs to consumers, or create superficial loopholes.
[C1.5] Policy should instead require timely, effective resolution: disclosed escalation, an empowered decision-maker, reasonable response times, and safety-specific routing. The decisive question is not “Did the chatbot offer a person?” but “Did the customer obtain a correct refund, exit, or protective action promptly?” The proposal risks replacing chatbot traps with human bottlenecks.
