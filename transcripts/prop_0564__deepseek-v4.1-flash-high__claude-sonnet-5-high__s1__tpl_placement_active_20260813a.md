[P1.1] Fares are a terrible way to fund a bus. They fall hardest on people with the least money; they burn resources on fare machines, cards, and enforcement that could go to buses; and they price riders off a service that is nearly empty at the margin. This motion doesn't need a ridership miracle. It needs honest accounting — and the accounting favors free transit.
[P1.2] Start with equity. A flat fare is a regressive tax: the same two dollars from a hospital aide and a hedge-fund partner. For a low-income household making several trips a day, that is real money — grocery money. Free transit hands it back on day one, whether or not ridership moves.
[P1.3] Benefits that arrive regardless of the headline number are not "modest."
[P1.4] Second, the farebox is a bad business. In most large systems fares cover only a fraction of operating costs, and collection, fare cards, and evasion policing claw back part of that. Free boarding also speeds buses: no fumbling, no queue at the front door. That improves reliability and lets the same fleet deliver more service.
[P1.5] Third, take the "modest ridership" caveat seriously. If ridership barely moves, we still cut a poverty tax, cut bureaucracy, and cut dwell time; if it grows, we get more of all three. And where fares are dropped, growth tends to come in off-peak and short trips — exactly the trips that matter to people without cars.
[P1.6] Opponents say the money should buy service instead. That is a false choice created by a false funding rule: broad-based taxation — income, property, land value, payroll — can fund both. We already socialize roads; drivers pay nothing at the curb. Transit is the one piece of public infrastructure we charge people to enter.
[P1.7] The right question is not whether free fares alone remake a city's mobility. It is whether taxing broadly to remove a barrier that excludes the poor and slows everyone down is worth it. It is.

[C1.1] Fare-free sounds generous. In practice it is a poor use of scarce transit dollars, and permanence makes the mistake impossible to correct.
[C1.2] Start with the money. Fares are one of the only revenue streams transit agencies fully control, spent by the people who use the service, adjustable for peak crowding, and insulated from annual budget fights. Replace that with "broad-based taxation" and you hand transit's survival to city councils and state legislatures who already underfund it, competing every cycle against schools, police, and housing. When budgets tighten — and they always do — a program funded by contested general revenue gets cut long before a dedicated farebox stream would.
[C1.3] You have not made transit more secure. You have made it a line item fighting for scraps, permanently.
[C1.4] Second, opportunity cost. Every dollar of new tax revenue spent eliminating fares for everyone, including affluent commuters who would happily pay, is a dollar not spent adding frequency, extending hours, or fixing the buses that don't show up. Riders consistently say service quality — how often the bus comes, how reliable it is — matters more to their lives than the price of a ride. A hospital aide waiting forty minutes for an unreliable bus is not helped by that bus being free; she is helped by it coming every ten minutes.
[C1.5] Fare-free-first spends the equity dollar on the wrong problem.
[C1.6] Third, "broad-based taxation" is not neutral. Cities lean on sales taxes and property taxes, both of which land heavily on renters and low-income households who may not even ride transit regularly. You are not eliminating a regressive burden — you are relocating it onto people, including non-riders, through a tax base that is often just as regressive as the fare it replaced.
[C1.7] The better path — targeted, income-based fare waivers for the poor, funded without gutting the entire farebox — captures nearly all of PRO's equity gain, protects a stable revenue base, and lets scarce new tax money go where it does the most good: more buses, more often.
