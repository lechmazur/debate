[P1.3] Consider the asymmetry. A public health campaign gets a poster, a slogan, and a modest budget. [C1.2] More fundamentally, PRO’s evidence does not establish the outcome in the motion. Sugary-drink reformulation can reduce sugar intake, and trans-fat bans can improve cardiovascular health, without demonstrating sustained reductions in obesity. Removing a cartoon from a cereal box does not change what a family buys, how much it serves, or whether children are active.
[C1.3] These policies may improve products while leaving total energy balance—and therefore obesity—largely untouched.
[C1.4] That gap matters because obesity is heterogeneous. Sleep, medication, stress, physical activity, cooking capacity, and eating habits differ across households. [C1.5] A parent who learns to plan default meals, read promotions skeptically, and manage portions carries those skills into every shop and restaurant; a marketing rule protects only the products, media, and places it happens to cover.
[C1.6] Restrictions also bring compliance costs, substitution, lobbying, and backlash; campaigns can be evaluated and revised without redesigning an entire market.
[C1.7] Environmental measures can be useful supporting tools. But the proposition demands greater focus, not mere inclusion.
