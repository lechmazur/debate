[P1.1] Relevance is not a slogan on a mission statement; it is a measurable thing. It means the people who fund you know you exist, encounter your work, and would notice if you vanished. By that standard, public broadcasters are quietly failing an entire generation, and traditional channel expansion cannot fix it, because a new channel only reaches people who already watch channels.
[P1.2] Consider who actually turns on linear television. In most wealthy countries the average viewer of a public broadcaster's main channel is now well past sixty. People under thirty watch a fraction of the live television their parents watched, and they get news, explainers and entertainment from YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, often through individual creators they trust more than institutions. Launching another linear channel into that environment is like building an extra reading room in a library the young have stopped visiting.
[P1.3] The BBC's own experience is instructive: it moved BBC Three online, then brought it back as a linear channel, and the returning channel drew modest audiences in a landscape that had simply moved on.
[P1.4] Now compare the alternative. Germany's funk, the ARD/ZDF youth network, abandoned the idea of a channel altogether and built formats natively for YouTube and Instagram, partnering with creators who already had young audiences. Result: millions of young Germans regularly encounter public service journalism who would never have found a broadcast schedule. That is what relevance looks like in practice.
[P1.5] Three reasons this must be the priority, not a side project. First, legitimacy: licence fees and public funding survive only while voters defend them, and a generation that never met the broadcaster will not fight for it in twenty years. Second, mission: misinformation spreads in ninety-second clips; a public broadcaster absent from that format has ceded the battlefield it exists to contest.
[P1.6] Third, cost: transmission infrastructure and schedule-filling are fixed expenses, while short-form spending scales with reach.
[P1.7] The mission is the content, not the pipe. Prioritise the audience you are losing, not the one you already have.

[C1.5] PRO's legitimacy argument inverts: legitimacy comes from being indispensable, not from being glimpsed. Nobody defends a subsidy for content they can get free from ten thousand creators.
[C1.6] Third, the cost of that reprioritisation lands on real people. "Prioritise" means money and attention move. The pensioner without broadband, the rural viewer, the disabled listener who relies on broadcast — these are the citizens most dependent on universal service, and they are precisely who gets thinned to fund the influencer budget.
[C1.7] And creators come with brand deals, undisclosed sponsors, and personal politics, governed by no editorial code. One partner scandal costs a broadcaster more impartiality credibility than a hundred million views can buy back.
