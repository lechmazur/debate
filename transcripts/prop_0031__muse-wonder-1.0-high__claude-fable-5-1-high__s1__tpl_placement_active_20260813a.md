[P1.1] Cities should stop creating neighborhoods where the law makes a modest home illegal on day one.
[P1.2] A new single-family-only district is not a neutral starting point. It is a decades-long decision, written into roads, sewers, and lot lines, that no duplex, townhouse, or small apartment may be built even if a landowner wants it. A density bonus leaves that prohibition as the default: exclusion is normal, and slightly less exclusion is a favor to be negotiated.
[P1.3] That default is why bonuses fail on cost. Mandating one detached house per lot mandates the most expensive way to provide a home: more land, structure, and infrastructure per family. A bonus helps only if it outweighs extra fees, parking rules, hearings, and delay — and a builder can always ignore it and sell detached houses to the highest bidder. Banning new single-family-only districts flips the incentive: detached homes remain legal everywhere, but duplexes, triplexes, townhomes, and courtyard apartments are legal by right too, letting small builders add missing-middle homes bonuses routinely fail to produce.
[P1.4] The harm is not only price. New exclusive districts reserve the newest schools, parks, and safest streets for buyers who can afford a detached house, while pushing renters, younger workers, and lower-income families to the fringe. That means longer drives, more farmland paved over, and more emissions locked in before anyone moves in. Bonuses worsen this by concentrating allowed density on noisy arterials or district edges, protecting affluent interiors while putting everyone else next to traffic.
[P1.5] A ban is also simpler, more honest government. Cities can still set height limits, setbacks, tree protections, and infrastructure standards, and require developers to pay for growth. What they cannot do is declare that only one household type and one price point belong. Stop creating new exclusion rather than decorating it with bonuses.

[C1.1] Keep the toolkit; reject the taboo.
[C1.2] The motion asks cities to surrender a planning tool in every context, forever, in exchange for a symbolic default. That trade fails on three grounds.
[C1.3] First, single-family-only districts do real work in specific places. Neighborhoods on septic systems without sewer capacity, wildfire-interface edges where evacuation routes are narrow, floodplains, wellhead-protection areas, and the transition belt between a town and working farmland all have reasons to cap households per acre. PRO concedes cities may keep "infrastructure standards" [P1.5], but a minimum lot size that achieves the same thing is the very exclusion PRO denounces. Either the ban is toothless or it forbids cities from matching density to capacity.
[C1.4] "Entirely" is the word that sinks PRO.
[C1.5] Second, the empirical story runs against PRO. Where single-family zoning was abolished outright — Minneapolis, Oregon, California's lot-split law — the by-right duplex and triplex conversions produced a trickle, by most accounts a few hundred units a year. The meaningful gains came from targeted upzoning along corridors and transit, with apartment buildings, which is precisely the graduated approach PRO dismisses as "decorating exclusion." Legalizing a triplex on a $1.2 million lot does not create an affordable home; it creates three expensive ones and a teardown windfall for the landowner.
[C1.6] Third, bonuses are not a "favor to be negotiated" [P1.2]; they are the only lever that attaches public benefit to private density. A bonus can require that the fourth unit be income-restricted, that stormwater be handled on site, that the builder contribute to the school it fills. A by-right ban hands density away with nothing asked in return, then leaves the city begging developers for affordability it just gave up the power to require.
[C1.7] Blanket bans also invite backlash: litigation, charter-city revolts, ballot repeals that freeze reform for a decade. Incremental tools build the coalition that durable density requires. Cities should expand density bonuses, apply them to interiors as well as arterials, and keep the discretion to say where a detached-only district still makes sense.

[P4.5] Prospective does not mean trivial. Every existing exclusionary neighborhood began as a new district; refusing to create more is how lock-in stops compounding.
