[P1.1] Every generation of students meets a tool schools first try to forbid: calculators, spellcheck, Wikipedia. In each case bans failed and integration won, because the tool didn't disappear — only honesty about it did. Generative AI is the same fight, and this motion stands with history: permit by default, require citation and process transparency.
[P1.2] Start with what a ban does. AI detectors are unreliable: they flag polished human writing, often by second-language students, while paraphrased AI text sails through. So a ban doesn't stop AI use; it punishes the honest student who discloses, rewards the one who launders output, and traps teachers in an unwinnable arms race. The real classroom choice is never AI versus no AI.
[P1.3] It is visible use versus invisible use, and only visible use can be taught, graded, and corrected.
[P1.4] That is why transparency is the heart of this motion, not a loophole. When a student must name the model, include prompts, and hand over drafts, teachers see more student thinking than a finished essay ever shows — the questions asked, the dead ends, the judgment about what to keep. Process visibility is real accountability. A ban offers the opposite: a rule everyone pretends works while actual use goes dark.
[P1.5] Bans also deepen inequality. Students with premium tools and the know-how to disguise use gain a quiet edge; students who follow the rules fall behind. Blanket prohibition widens exactly the gaps schools exist to close, pushing AI practice underground where nobody teaches it.
[P1.6] And school is preparation. These students will enter workplaces where AI drafting and analysis are expected, and where the valued skill is not avoidance but verification, judgment, and honest disclosure. Teaching a student to use AI openly and check its output critically is the modern version of teaching mental math alongside the calculator. You don't protect learning by hiding students from the tool; you protect it by keeping their thinking visible while they use it.

[C1.1] Permitting generative AI by default does not modernize schoolwork. It redefines the assignment so the product need not be the student’s own thinking. Citation and process notes do not repair that damage; they only label the substitution.
[C1.2] School assignments exist to build and test independent skill—forming an argument, selecting evidence, revising under friction, finding a voice. Generative models supply fluent structure and language on demand. When the default rule treats that as fine so long as the student names the model and pastes prompts, grades stop measuring the student and start measuring prompting and light editing. Those are different capacities, and most essays and problem sets were never designed to test them.
[C1.3] The familiar calculator and spellcheck analogies collapse. Those tools assist narrow mechanics after the student has supplied the plan and judgment. Generative AI can supply the plan and judgment. The student becomes an editor of machine output.
[C1.4] Process transparency reveals this only when students candidly show the depth of reliance—yet grade pressure rewards the opposite: smoother drafts, fewer visible dead ends, less cognitive struggle.
[C1.5] Default permission also undercuts learning itself. Writing and reasoning improve through generation, revision, and productive difficulty. Outsourcing those steps short-circuits the work schools exist to develop. AI literacy can be taught in dedicated modules without surrendering every graded task to co-authorship by default.
[C1.6] Bans are imperfect, yet they keep a bright line: what you submit must be yours. Unreliable detectors do not magically make an honor system of transparent outsourcing any safer. The motion trades authentic assessment for a ritual that cannot restore authorship the student never exercised. Refuse that bargain.
