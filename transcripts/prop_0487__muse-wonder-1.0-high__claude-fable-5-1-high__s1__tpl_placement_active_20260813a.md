[P1.1] An asteroid does not care about our budget cycle. If a city-killer is found with ten years of warning, a research paper will not move it. Only hardware that already exists, people already trained, and authority already established will.
[P1.2] That is why planetary defense must shift from a research niche to permanent, deployed infrastructure: dedicated survey telescopes that never go dark, a maintained catalog of near-Earth objects, rapid-response interceptors kept launch-ready, and regularly rehearsed deflection missions under clear government command.
[P1.3] We know the threat is real and the physics works. A meteor exploding over Chelyabinsk injured over a thousand people without hitting the ground. The Tunguska blast flattened a vast forest. Larger impacts can end agriculture, cities, or civilization itself.
[P1.4] And a recent kinetic-impact test showed a spacecraft can measurably change an asteroid's orbit. Deflection is not science fiction; it is engineering that becomes harder the later we start.
[P1.5] Permanent infrastructure matters because warning time is useless without lead time to act. Nudging an object years early so it misses by thousands of miles requires only modest force. Wait until it is months away and the same job demands vastly more energy, perhaps nuclear options no one wants to improvise. Treated as occasional grants and one-off missions, we would spend the critical first years after detection arguing over funding, rebuilding expertise, and designing a spacecraft from scratch while the window for a cheap, safe deflection closes.
[P1.6] Governments already accept this logic elsewhere. We maintain standing armies, missile defenses, earthquake monitoring, and flood barriers for rare, devastating events, not because disaster is scheduled, but because building the response after it begins is too late. The cost of a permanent system is a rounding error in major national budgets compared with the uninsurable, irreversible loss it prevents.
[P1.7] Detection without deflection is just a countdown to impact. Fund the shield before we need it.

[C1.1] The question is not whether asteroids are dangerous. It is whether buying rockets that sit in a hangar for forty years is a better defense than finding the rocks early and knowing how to move them. It is not.
[C1.2] Start with what actually protects us. Nearly every large near-Earth asteroid that could end civilization has already been catalogued, and none is on a collision course this century. The remaining gap is mid-sized objects, and closing it is a telescope problem, not an interceptor problem. Surveys like the planned infrared space telescope and large ground observatories are exactly the kind of work a research program does well.
[C1.3] Detection is where the marginal dollar saves lives. Nothing in a standing interceptor fleet finds a single new rock.
[C1.4] Now consider the interceptors themselves. Every deflection is bespoke. The spacecraft that nudged a small moonlet was designed around that specific target's size, orbit, and approach geometry. An asteroid found tomorrow could be a loose rubble pile, a solid metal body, a fast spinner, or a binary.
[C1.5] A pre-built "rapid-response interceptor" is either useless for most of those cases or so generic that it must be rebuilt anyway. Meanwhile the hardware ages, the launch vehicle it was built for is retired, and the engineers who understood it have moved on. Permanent infrastructure for a once-in-centuries event does not stay ready; it rots.
[C1.6] The lead-time argument also cuts against itself. If an object is found with ten years of warning, that is ample time to design a mission for that object, as the kinetic test went from approval to launch in roughly five years. If it is found with months of warning, no stockpiled interceptor helps either; evacuation does. The scenario where a standing fleet is decisive is vanishingly narrow.
[C1.7] Finally, "launch-ready interceptors under government command" means permanent bureaucracies, nuclear options kept on the shelf, and dual-use hardware other nations will read as weapons. Keep the science funded, keep the telescopes watching, and build the spacecraft when there is something to aim at.

[P2.6] Hardware rotting is an argument against neglect, not permanence. Research niches rot fastest: teams disperse and expertise vanishes between one-off missions. Maintained systems with refresh cycles, drills, and replacement stay usable. The choice is not a perfect rocket in a hangar versus perfect knowledge.
[P2.7] It is a practiced system able to act inside the window physics allows, versus hoping warning time survives bureaucracy.
