[P1.1] The question is not whether universities should remember harm; it is whether memory is allowed to become a substitute for repair. When an institution’s early endowment grew from slave labor, segregated admissions, or dispossessed land, the wrong was not only symbolic. It produced durable advantages: campuses, prestige, alumni networks, and endowment dollars that still shape who gets admitted, who can afford to attend, and which neighborhoods are treated as expendable. A plaque or a webpage does not alter that distribution.
[P1.2] The standard should be simple: if a harm remains materially useful to the institution, the remedy must materially restore the people still excluded by it. Long-term scholarships do that by changing access across generations, especially where family wealth was suppressed by the same policies that enriched the university. Community investment does it by repairing the surrounding neighborhoods that were displaced, redlined, or starved so campuses could expand. This is not charity.
[P1.3] It is a targeted transfer from retained benefits to the communities that paid for them.
[P1.4] CON will likely say that today’s universities are not the same people who committed the wrong, or that funds should be spent neutrally. But the proposition does not ask individuals to bear personal guilt. It asks institutions to stop keeping the gains of their own exclusionary past while declaring the matter closed. If a university can finance new dorms, athletic facilities, and administrative expansion through long-term borrowing and donor campaigns, it can finance durable repair.
[P1.5] An acknowledgment that costs little and changes nothing may be sincere; it is still not sufficient. Words can recognize injustice, but only funded scholarships and sustained community investment can begin to reverse it.

[C1.2] The premise that early tainted wealth remains the material base of modern universities fails on the numbers. Those endowments were built overwhelmingly in the last half-century from living donors, federal research funding, and tuition, all earmarked for current education. Stretching 1830s dollars into a present-day biology building is moral arithmetic, not financial history.
[C1.3] If that chain is enough to create a duty to pay, it is enough to make nearly every old American college, bank, church and municipality liable, with no principled limit.
[C1.4] A university is also a fiduciary, not a reparations legislature. Its funds exist to teach whoever is qualified today, of every background. Diverting them into long-term scholarships defined by ancestral injury means taking need-based aid from a low-income first-generation student now to privilege another student later because administrators ranked historic wrongs. That betrays donor intent, collides with non-discrimination law after Students for Fair Admissions, and abandons institutional neutrality for adjudicating bloodlines.
[C1.5] Community investment is even further from the university mission — campuses already anchor jobs, hospitals and schools, but they are not city halls with taxing power to settle national debts.
[C1.6] Acknowledgment is not empty symbolism. Funding archives, rewriting curriculum, opening admissions and supporting research on slavery and dispossession changes who learns and what the nation knows. That is the repair a truth-seeking institution can legitimately offer. Turning transparency into a perpetual financial punishment does not reverse history; it incentivizes silence and offloads onto universities a responsibility that only the state can democratically bear.
