[P1.1] Electronic waste is the fastest-growing waste stream in the world, and the countries generating the most of it have found a convenient solution: ship it somewhere poorer. The proposition is simple. If you produce the waste, you process it—either at home or through certified facilities meeting genuine environmental and labor standards. No more dumping under the label of "recycling."
[P1.2] The core problem is that e-waste exports operate on a fiction. Scrap shipments are documented as "recycling," but in practice, vast quantities end up in informal yards in places like Agbogbloshie in Ghana or Guiyu in China, where workers—including children—burn cables to recover copper, acid-strip circuit boards for gold, and smash leaded glass from old monitors. The soil around these sites is saturated with lead, mercury, cadmium, and flame retardants. This is not recycling.
[P1.3] It is toxic burden-shifting dressed up as commerce.
[P1.4] The exporting countries know this. The Basel Convention recognized the danger decades ago. Yet enforcement remains weak, loopholes allow "repairable" or "secondhand" goods to flow through, and the economic pressure on importing nations makes refusal difficult. A ban with mandatory domestic or certified regional processing closes the loophole rather than endlessly policing it.
[P1.5] The counterargument that poorer countries "need" this industry is the most cynical defense available. Informal e-waste processing pays a pittance while contaminating the workers, the water table, and the next generation. Nations should not have to choose between poverty and poison. Certified regional processing—shared facilities meeting enforceable standards—preserves legitimate material recovery without externalizing the human cost.
[P1.6] The proposition does not demand the impossible. It demands honesty: if the waste is too hazardous to process safely in your own community, you do not have the right to send it to someone else's.

The bulk of electronics flowing to lower-income countries are functioning secondhand goods—laptops for students, phones for small traders, hospital equipment with years of life left. Because a ban must distinguish "waste" from "used," and no customs officer can reliably do that at scale, the proposition forces a choice: either the ban is porous and changes nothing, or it is strict and strangles the affordable-technology trade that connects hundreds of millions of people to the digital economy.
[C1.3] Both outcomes are failures.
[C1.4] Second, prohibition doesn't end flows; it criminalizes them. The Basel regime already restricts hazardous exports, and the result has been mislabeled containers and smuggling networks. You cannot regulate what you have driven into the dark.
[C1.5] Third, the ban confiscates real value from the people who need it most. E-waste is ore: copper, gold, cobalt, rare earths. Telling lower-income countries they may not participate in recovering those materials—while wealthy nations keep the resource stream for their own "certified" facilities—is resource protectionism dressed as environmentalism. Millions of livelihoods in collection, repair, and refurbishment vanish, and nothing replaces them.
[C1.6] Finally, the ban doesn't even solve Agbogbloshie. Much of the e-waste in African scrapyards is domestically generated—devices that were legitimately imported, used for years, and discarded locally. The dumps remain; only the income disappears.
[C1.7] The real solution is investment: formalizing informal recyclers, funding modern smelting capacity in importing countries, extended producer responsibility that follows the device. Build safe recycling where the waste and the workers already are. The proposition chooses a border wall instead—and border walls have never made toxins disappear.
