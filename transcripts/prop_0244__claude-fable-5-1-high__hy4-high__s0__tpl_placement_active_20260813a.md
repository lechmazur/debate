[P1.1] Judge the motion by its own terms: not whether body cameras did anything, but whether they did what their champions promised. On that comparison, the gap is enormous.
[P1.2] Recall the promise. After Ferguson in 2014, cameras were sold as the fix: an objective witness that would deter misconduct, settle disputed shootings, and rebuild trust. Early results from a small department in Rialto, California, showing use of force and complaints falling by roughly half, were cited everywhere. Federal money flowed on exactly that expectation.
[P1.3] The pitch was transformation, not a modest tweak.
[P1.4] Then came the large, rigorous test. Washington, D.C. randomized cameras across roughly two thousand officers and found essentially no detectable effect on use of force or complaints. Reviews pooling many studies since have landed in the same place: effects that are small, inconsistent, or null. The most careful evidence says the deterrence story largely did not materialize.
[P1.5] Why? Because accountability depends on who controls the footage, and departments kept control. Officers decide when to activate and can mute or "forget." Many agencies let officers review footage before writing reports, converting the camera from a check into a script-aid.
[P1.6] Release is delayed or refused under investigation exemptions. Footage is routinely used to prosecute civilians, while charges against officers remain rare. Qualified immunity and deferential juries mean that even clear video seldom produces consequences.
[P1.7] Daniel Shaver was killed on camera in a hotel hallway, begging; the officer was acquitted. Eric Garner's death was filmed; no charges. George Floyd's killer was convicted, but because a teenager's phone, not his own department's cameras, forced the issue, and because Minneapolis initially described the death as a "medical incident." Video did not change the institution's first instinct.
[P1.8] Any side can concede incidental gains: better evidence in some trials, occasional discipline. But the motion asks about expectations, and advocates expected a new era of police accountability. What they got was a surveillance tool whose footage the police own, release, and interpret.

That was never a serious benchmark. A camera is an evidence system: it makes police power answerable to facts that outlast the encounter. Advocates expected routine records for supervisors, prosecutors, courts, and the public to inspect.
[C1.2] That expectation has largely been met.
[C1.3] Accountability is not a punishment count; it is the ability to test the state's story. Before cameras, an alleged shove or threat often became officer report versus civilian memory. Now reports can be checked against audio and sequence, charges tested on a timeline, files subpoenaed, and discipline, training, or exoneration grounded in evidence rather than rank. If video clears a falsely accused officer, that too is accountability: state coercion needs reasons that can be checked.
[C1.4] PRO's null averages at [P1.4] measure deterrence, not this institutional change. Complaints can stay flat as reporting rises or visible misconduct is recorded; falling numbers are not the sole measure.
[C1.5] The failures at [P1.5]-[P1.7] are downstream. Qualified immunity, prosecutorial discretion, union contracts, and jury deference were never going to be repealed by a lens. Shaver, Garner, and Floyd prove visual evidence can force public proceedings, policy fights, and sometimes a conviction when institutions respond; a bystander phone did that by accident, while cameras make the record routine. Department control cuts the other way too: once recording, retention, and release are rules, nonactivation, muting, deletion, and delay become inspectable acts.
[C1.6] The question shifts from “Who do you believe?” to “Where is the footage, and why did the log stop?” Missing video becomes a traceable choice. That is the gain advocates sold: an auditable record constraining officers and departments, not automatic punishment. PRO has shown cameras are not self-executing; it has not shown they improved accountability less than expected.
