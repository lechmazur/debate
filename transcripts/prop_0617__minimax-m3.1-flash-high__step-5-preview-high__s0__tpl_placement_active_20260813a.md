[P1.1] Dominant digital platforms should bear the burden of proving that their acquisitions do not harm competition. The reason is structural: they possess the information, data, and expertise to explain their business strategies, while consumers and smaller rivals cannot easily audit claims about network effects, ecosystem lock-in, or future innovation. A presumption is therefore not arbitrary leniency toward mergers; it is an allocation of proof based on who can realistically supply the evidence.
[P1.2] Consider a dominant platform acquiring a potential rival. Even if the target is currently small, the acquisition can prevent the emergence of a credible substitute, reserve valuable data or intellectual property, and make entry harder for other firms. These harms may not appear immediately in lower prices.
[P1.3] Instead, they appear as reduced innovation, privacy erosion, weaker interoperability, or the disappearance of a route for consumers to switch. A rule requiring immediate, measurable price increases would miss this central mechanism of digital harm.
[P1.4] The proposed presumption also permits a genuine efficiencies defense, but “substantial consumer benefit” should mean more than asserting that the acquirer promises a better product. The firm must show benefits that are merger-specific, credible, durable, and sufficiently large to outweigh the competitive harm. Baseline improvements that would have occurred without the acquisition, speculative claims about future inventions, and benefits controlled by the dominant firm cannot simply erase the presumption. Concrete commitments—such as interoperability guarantees, enforceable access terms, or structural remedies—make the defense more testable.
[P1.5] This framework does not make beneficial acquisitions impossible. It forces the dominant firm to do what ordinary economic reasoning suggests: demonstrate why a transaction that strengthens market power will nevertheless serve consumers substantially. That burden is especially appropriate where the firm’s acquisition removes a future constraint on its own power. The central question is not whether a merger creates some benefit, but whether those benefits are large enough to justify sacrificing the competition the merger is most likely to foreclose.
[P1.6] Dominant platforms should have to prove that case, not ask the public to assume it.

[C1.1] Competition law rests on a simple principle: the enforcer proves harm; the firm does not prove virtue. PRO inverts that. Under [P1.1], the mere fact of dominance plus an acquisition triggers a presumption of illegality, forcing the firm to discharge a burden that should never have shifted. That is not a refinement of merger review; it is a reversal of the inquiry.
[C1.2] The defense PRO offers is illusory. “Substantial consumer benefit” is undefined enough to swallow every transaction. A platform must show benefits are merger-specific, credible, and durable—a standard that sounds rigorous but is nearly impossible to satisfy ex ante. Consider a large platform acquiring a small infrastructure tool or an AI team. The synergies are real but probabilistic, and the counterfactual—what the startup would have achieved alone—is inherently speculative.
[C1.3] Under PRO’s rule, the honest answer, “we expect integration will improve the product, but cannot guarantee it,” becomes an admission of guilt.
[C1.4] This is not hypothetical. Many acquisitions by dominant digital firms have scaled services that consumers now take for granted. The rule treats all of them as guilty by association with size.
[C1.5] Worse, the “potential rival” logic in [P1.2] makes enforcers the arbiters of future markets. If every startup that might have constrained a giant must be preserved as an independent competitor, we will block the very transactions that allow small teams to reach scale. Startups exit through acquisition because building alone is brutally hard. Remove that path, and you reduce incentives to innovate in the first place.
[C1.6] PRO assumes the burden asymmetry favors the firm. It does not. Regulators face uncertainty too, and a presumption institutionalizes error toward false positives, entrenching the status quo. The correct standard is clear: prove anticompetitive effect.
[C1.7] Do not demand proof of consumer blessing.

[P4.1] A1: Pre-closing evidence cannot guarantee a future outcome, but the rule does not require certainty. It requires substantial evidence that the acquisition will produce material, merger-specific consumer benefits. Relevant evidence can include comparable prior integrations, demonstrated improvements from the target’s existing product, milestones and testing already completed, enforceable commitments on price, privacy, interoperability, or access, and a supported account of how those gains depend on the transaction. Uncertainty then affects the weight of the evidence; it does not automatically convert every honest forecast into a failed defense.
[P4.2] CON’s demand for a guaranteed counterfactual would make the proposed defense practically unavailing.
[P4.3] A2: The platform must distinguish scale-building from buying a nascent rival through evidence: its stated acquisition rationale, internal deliberations, the target’s credible independent growth path, the strategic importance of its data, technology, and user relationships, and whether the transaction would occur only after the target became a competitive threat. Founder profit matters only if it translates into consumer benefit; the sale itself proves neither.
[P4.4] Crucially, PRO does not require the firm to prove that foreclosure is certain before a presumption arises. The dominant platform bears the burden of showing substantial benefit sufficient to answer the competitive concern; where the evidence is ambiguous, failure to carry that burden sustains the presumption.
[P4.5] That distinction is the decisive clash. CON treats uncertainty about both competition and benefits as if only the former merits concern, but dominant platforms can produce elaborate forecasts precisely because they control the acquired assets and the relevant data. Burden allocation corrects that asymmetry without demanding certainty from either side.
[P4.6] The rule also does not sacrifice innovation. A platform may still acquire a startup and demonstrate benefits through products, contracts, and credible integration evidence. But the prospect of being bought cannot by itself count as consumer benefit, especially when the purchase removes a potential independent constraint. Because the acquirer is best placed to explain why this deal benefits consumers, requiring it to do so is more reliable than either presuming innocence or presuming unlawfulness.
