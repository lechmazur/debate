[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a real harm for the wrong remedy. Juvenile records can follow a young person online for years, and trivial adolescent conduct deserves protection. [C1.2] First, accountability does not evaporate at adulthood. Many juvenile adjudications involve serious conduct—assault, robbery, sexual misconduct, or repeated offenses—where victims, schools, licensing boards, employers in caregiving roles, and the public have legitimate reasons to know. PRO’s presumption forces those parties to litigate to keep open information that a court has already adjudicated. It shifts the burden from the person seeking secrecy to everyone harmed or endangered by it.
[C1.3] If a seventeen-year-old has a pattern of violent conduct, the public should not need a motion to learn that at eighteen.
[C1.4] Second, the digital reality defeats the remedy. Courts can seal their own dockets, but they cannot seal the internet. News articles, police blotters, screenshots, and data-broker files persist. So the policy creates the worst of both worlds: it hides the official, authoritative record while leaving less reliable copies circulating.
[C1.5] It can also deprive the public of accurate context, because sealed records are harder to correct, explain, or weigh responsibly.
[C1.6] Third, PRO’s burden shift undermines its own goal. A presumption treats age as closure, even though juvenile justice includes rehabilitation, accountability, and public safety. Targeted relief—sealing after a showing of rehabilitation, with carve-outs for serious or recent conduct—protects second chances without making secrecy automatic. PRO’s shoplifting example is already served by targeted expungement; it does not justify a default rule that conceals what the public may need to see.
[C1.7] The real question is whether courts should hide records by default whenever a juvenile turns eighteen. They should not. Vote CON.
