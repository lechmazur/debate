[P1.1] The motion is deliberately narrow: it protects only people who have no safe alternative shelter, not anyone choosing to sleep in a vehicle when other housing is available. That narrow guardrail addresses predictable overreach objections before they are raised, and the case for it rests on three unassailable pillars.
[P1.2] First, punishing survival when no alternative exists is indefensible cruelty with no legitimate public purpose. A person with no open shelter bed, no supportive family to stay with, no ability to pay for a room cannot will themselves into housing. Fining them for sleeping creates a self-defeating cycle: unpayable tickets turn into arrest warrants, which bar access to employment, rental housing, and public benefits, trapping people in homelessness longer rather than connecting them to stability.
[P1.3] Second, towing and targeted police enforcement do not eliminate unsheltered homelessness—they make it more dangerous. For people living out of their cars, the vehicle is not just transport: it holds their medication, work uniforms, children’s school materials, identification, and every possession they own. Towing it strips them of their last remaining shelter entirely, forcing them onto open sidewalks or isolated, unlit areas away from service providers, bus routes to jobs, and safe access to water and restrooms.
[P1.4] Third, routine criminalization is far more expensive for communities than leaving people in peace. Jail stays, emergency room visits for exposure, and repeated police patrols cost far more taxpayer money than low-barrier safe parking options, and deliver none of the stability that reduces long-term homelessness. No community is made safer by forcing people with nowhere else to go to hide just to rest.

Over nights, it brings waste, blocked sightlines, idling for heat, expired registration, and disputes over access, especially in neighborhoods least able to absorb them. A desperate person may also be parked where an ambulance needs clearance or a wheelchair user cannot reach the curb.
[C1.3] PRO [P1.3] counts what towing takes from the sleeper, but never counts what indefinite parking takes from everyone who must use that space.
[C1.4] “Harassment” is the decoy: no one defends arbitrary “move along” pressure. Rule-bound enforcement is its opposite. [C1.5] Banning routine tools leaves more informal discretion, not less.
[C1.6] A city may reject punitive fines and still keep predictable time, place, and manner rules. It may build low-barrier parking without letting every curb become a shelter.
