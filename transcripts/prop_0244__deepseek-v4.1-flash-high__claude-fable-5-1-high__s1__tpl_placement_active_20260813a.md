[P1.1] The body-camera pitch was never just about evidence. Advocates promised a new accountability regime: when police misconduct was recorded, departments would no longer be able to bury it, officers would be disciplined, and public trust would follow. That promise has not been kept at the scale expected.
[P1.2] The core reason is simple: footage does not discipline anyone. Accountability is a chain—capture, review, release, adjudication, consequence—and cameras only strengthen the first link. Police departments and unions control the rest. Officers in many places can view footage before writing reports, shaping the official story around what the camera shows.
[P1.3] Departments can classify footage as confidential, delay release, or refuse to release until public pressure becomes unbearable. Prosecutors depend on police to make cases and rarely pursue officers. Arbitrators and civil-service protections can overturn discipline even when video is damning. A camera can prove what happened; it cannot force anyone to care.
[P1.4] The measurable record is mixed. Some early deployments coincided with fewer complaints or use-of-force incidents, which advocates treated as proof. But falling complaints are not the same as accountability. They may reflect de-escalation, but they may also reflect citizens believing that complaining is futile because the camera will simply be interpreted in the officer’s favor.
[P1.5] Viral footage occasionally produces a firing or a conviction, yet those are exceptions that prove the rule: accountability arrives when the public is already mobilized, not because the camera automatically triggered it.
[P1.6] So the comparison is not cameras versus nothing. It is cameras versus the expectations used to sell them: transparency on demand, misconduct exposed, trust rebuilt. Cameras made police work more visible. They did not meaningfully redistribute the power to judge, punish, and protect officers.
[P1.7] That is why the accountability improvement is real but smaller than the advocates’ case required.

[C1.2] PRO's "new accountability regime" [P1.1] is a straw expectation built from headlines, not from the advocates' stated case.
[C1.3] Measure cameras against the real expectation and they clear it. [C1.4] Now the cases where harm did occur. Before cameras, prosecutions of on-duty officers for killings were nearly nonexistent, because the only surviving witness wore the badge. Since then, body-camera footage has been the decisive evidence in high-profile convictions and in charging decisions that came within days rather than years—think of cases where a deputy was indicted for a fatal shooting largely because his own camera recorded the exchange. PRO calls these "exceptions" [P1.5], but the baseline they replaced was zero.
[C1.5] PRO's chain metaphor [P1.2] cuts against them. Yes, cameras strengthen only the first link—but the first link was where accountability used to break. Arbitrators, unions, and prosecutorial deference existed before 2014; advocates never promised cameras would abolish them, so their persistence is not an unmet camera expectation.
[C1.6] The comparison PRO wants is cameras versus a fantasy. The honest comparison is what was promised versus what was delivered, and on that measure body cameras did what their advocates said they would do.
