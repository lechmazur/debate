[P1.1] Market access is a public license to put a medicine into bodies, formularies, and insurance systems. That license should require the full trial record, including results that show failure, harm, or no added benefit. Negative findings are not private disappointments. They are half the evidence.
[P1.2] Without them, approvals, guidelines, and prescriptions rest on a curated highlight reel.
[P1.3] The bias is structural. Flattering trials reach journals and sales materials; disappointing ones stay in drawers. Clinicians then treat patients as if the missing failures never occurred. A drug can look modestly useful in the published record and barely better than placebo, or riskier than advertised, once the withheld negatives are counted.
[P1.4] Patients absorb the cost and the harm while the sponsor keeps the inconvenient file offstage. Tying disclosure to market access ends that asymmetry at the moment the company most wants something from the public.
[P1.5] The waste is equally concrete. Other laboratories repeat dead ends already falsified in industry data. Public funders chase signals that failed quietly. Meta-analyses and guidelines inherit the skew, so incomplete evidence hardens into policy.
[P1.6] A buried negative trial does not protect innovation. It taxes everyone else and delays the search for treatments that actually work.
[P1.7] Voluntary codes and bare registration do not fix this. Registration without results is a promise, not a record. Incentives remain one-sided: selective silence protects revenue and reputation. Outcome data the public is asked to trust is not a trade secret in any serious sense.
[P1.8] Rivals can often infer a failed program from abandoned filings and quiet pipelines. What doctors cannot ethically ignore, and what competitors cannot invent, is the actual safety and efficacy record.
[P1.9] Disclosure will not block worthwhile drugs. It will block the profitable habit of selling certainty the trials do not support. Countries owe patients that bargain: sell here only if the failures are as public as the successes.

[C1.1] Market access is not a bargaining chip to be held hostage for transparency. The proposition before us makes the right to sell a medicine conditional on surrendering proprietary data, and that coercion will cost patients the very treatments they need. When a country threatens to block a drug unless its sponsor publishes every negative result, the sponsor faces a simple choice: comply with demands that may expose sensitive commercial information to competitors, or withdraw from the market entirely. The loser is not the pharmaceutical company.
[C1.2] It is the patient with no remaining options.
[C1.3] This confuses two distinct regulatory goals. Transparency belongs in the realm of trial registration and publication law. Market access belongs in the realm of safety and efficacy review. Bolting transparency requirements onto the market access gate does not strengthen either function.
[C1.4] It creates a perverse incentive: rather than risk forced disclosure of ambiguous or preliminary data, companies may simply avoid seeking approval in strict jurisdictions, creating a two-tiered world where rigorous regulatory environments lose access to new therapies while lax ones gain them. Patients in wealthy nations with aggressive disclosure mandates may find themselves waiting years longer for drugs available elsewhere.
[C1.5] Consider what counts as a "negative" result. A trial that misses its primary endpoint but shows promise on secondary measures is not a failure to be buried. An underpowered study is not evidence of inefficacy. A subgroup analysis that fails to replicate is not proof of harm.
[C1.6] Forcing sponsors to label and publish every disappointing interim analysis floods the medical literature with noise, invites misinterpretation by payers and press, and can prematurely kill drugs that might yet prove valuable in larger, better-designed trials. Clinicians need curated, peer-reviewed evidence, not a raw data dump of every exploratory endpoint.
[C1.7] Countries can achieve transparency without this hostage-taking. Mandatory trial registration, public results databases, and publication requirements for approved drugs accomplish the goal without threatening access. The proposition trades patient welfare for bureaucratic purity. We should reject it.

[C2.1] The PRO side mistakes a regulatory license for a hostage negotiation, but the distinction matters. Conditioning market access on safety and efficacy protects patients from harmful products. Conditioning it on public disclosure of every trial outcome is a different transaction entirely: it converts the approval process into an engine for transparency policy, extracting concessions that belong in the legislative domain of publication law. [P2.1] treats these as the same gate, but bolting transparency mandates onto market access does not strengthen safety review; it weaponizes it.
[C2.2] Consider the claim that disclosure is "not a veto" [P2.5]. In reality, the moment a negative trial result becomes public, payers, insurers, and prescribing physicians react. A drug can hold regulatory approval while becoming commercially unviable, effectively withdrawn from formularies. The sponsor faces not a choice between disclosure and silence, but between disclosure and financial ruin.
[C2.3] That is a veto dressed in regulatory clothing.
[C2.4] PRO insists we are discussing only "outcome record, not manufacturing methods" [P2.3], but even aggregate efficacy data reveals R&D strategy, patient population insights, and competitive positioning. More critically, the threat of forced disclosure chills investment in trials within strict jurisdictions. Sponsors will run studies in countries with weaker transparency demands, exporting the evidence base and delaying access for patients in nations that impose these conditions.
[C2.5] The argument that voluntary registration has failed [P2.7] ignores the actual regulatory architecture. Agencies already receive complete trial data, including negative results, as a condition of review. The deficiency is not regulatory blindness but publication. The remedy is mandatory publication requirements tied to approval or journal standards, not a market access ultimatum that risks patient access for bureaucratic leverage.
[C2.6] When PRO suggests that ambiguity about "negative" results [P2.9] merely requires better reporting standards, they underestimate the litigation and compliance nightmare of defining disclosure triggers ex ante. Every interim analysis, every underpowered null, becomes a potential liability trap. The market access gate should remain a safety gate. Transparency belongs elsewhere.
