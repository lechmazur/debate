[P1.1] When a person is experiencing a mental health crisis, the primary objective must be safely resolving that crisis, not merely rushing a badge to the scene. Cities must expand non-police crisis response teams because the safety and effectiveness of the intervention fundamentally outweigh the raw speed of the dispatch.
[P1.2] Currently, cities default to sending armed police officers to behavioral health emergencies. Officers are trained primarily in law enforcement, compliance, and threat neutralization, not psychiatric triage. The sudden arrival of uniforms, weapons, and command-and-control tactics routinely escalates distress, too often turning a medical emergency into a lethal confrontation or an unnecessary arrest.
[P1.3] By contrast, non-police crisis teams—comprising medics, social workers, and peer support specialists—are specifically equipped to de-escalate. They treat the individual as a patient in need of care, not a suspect to be subdued.
[P1.4] We must embrace this transition even if it temporarily increases response times in some neighborhoods. A fast response is actively harmful if it is the wrong response. Sending a plumber to a cardiac arrest just because they are five minutes closer than an ambulance defies medical logic; similarly, dispatching an armed officer to a severe depressive episode just because a cruiser is around the corner actively endangers the caller. We should gladly trade a few extra minutes of waiting for the assurance that the person arriving brings clinical expertise rather than a firearm and handcuffs.
[P1.5] Furthermore, expanding specialized teams improves the overall public safety ecosystem. Police currently waste countless hours waiting in emergency room lobbies or processing minor arrests for behavioral health calls. By diverting these emergencies to specialized responders, we connect individuals to long-term community care, addressing root causes and breaking the cycle of repeated 911 calls.
[P1.6] A delayed resolution that ends in targeted treatment is undeniably better than a rapid response that ends in trauma, incarceration, or a fatal shooting. Expanding non-police crisis teams is a vital, life-saving evolution of public health and public safety.
