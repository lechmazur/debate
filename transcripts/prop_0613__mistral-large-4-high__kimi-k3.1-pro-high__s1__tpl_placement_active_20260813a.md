[P1.1] The internet was built on open protocols. Email works between Gmail and Outlook. Phone calls connect across carriers. Yet today's dominant social platforms are walled gardens, trapping users and their relationships inside proprietary silos.
[P1.2] This is not an accident of technology but a business model of lock-in. When your social graph—the map of every friend, family member, and colleague you have ever connected with—belongs to the platform rather than to you, switching costs become insurmountable. You cannot leave without abandoning your community.
[P1.3] Mandating interoperable, user-portable messaging and social graph access for qualified third-party services dismantles this artificial captivity. It transforms social networking from a monopoly extraction game into a competitive marketplace where services compete on privacy, design, and moderation rather than on who can hold your relationships hostage. When a user can export their social graph to a rival platform or an independent service, incumbents must earn retention through quality, not coercion. New entrants can offer stronger encryption or ad-free experiences without first convincing millions of users to rebuild their networks from scratch.
[P1.4] Critics will warn of privacy risks, but qualified third-party access means vetted services operating under strict regulatory standards, not open season for data miners. The alternative—continued lock-in—is already a privacy disaster, as platforms monetize captive audiences with no competitive pressure to protect them. Interoperability forces platforms to treat users as customers rather than assets.
[P1.5] This is not about breaking up companies; it is about restoring the fundamental right to take your relationships with you. Without interoperability, we accept a digital feudalism where we are tenants on corporate land. With it, we reclaim sovereignty over our own social lives. The question is not whether we can afford to mandate interoperability, but whether we can afford not to.

[C1.1] This mandate treats your friendships as luggage you can pack and take elsewhere. But a social graph is not solely yours: it reveals other people’s relationships, group memberships, contact details, and sometimes their beliefs or vulnerabilities. You may consent to export your own posts; you cannot consent on behalf of every friend whose connection to you becomes portable. “Qualified” access does not solve that consent problem. Once information is copied elsewhere, later breaches, changed policies, corporate sales, or weak deletion practices can make exposure irreversible.
[C1.2] Messaging interoperability compounds the danger. Encryption is not a label that automatically survives every bridge between services. Secure cross-platform communication requires compatible identity verification, key management, metadata handling, and abuse controls. Every new connection expands the attack surface, while vetting a company at admission cannot guarantee the security of every subsequent update or prevent compromised accounts.
[C1.3] PRO’s “strict regulatory standards” are an assertion, not a mechanism for making a distributed system as safe as its weakest participant.
[C1.4] The competition argument also cuts both ways. Continuing compliance, documentation, security reviews, and support for mandated interfaces are easier for dominant firms to absorb than for small community platforms or encrypted start-ups. A mandate aimed at today’s giants can become tomorrow’s barrier to entry. Standardized interfaces can also freeze design choices and discourage services from differentiating themselves through novel privacy or safety architectures—the very competition PRO promises.
[C1.5] Email is a misleading analogy. Sending a message deliberately to an address on another network is not equivalent to compelling continuous messaging access or bulk portability of relationship maps. Open protocols can be valuable; that does not establish that governments should impose this particular bundle on every covered service.
[C1.6] There are less dangerous ways to reduce lock-in: export of a user’s own content, consent-based contact portability, transparent switching tools, voluntary protocols, and ordinary competition enforcement. The motion goes much further by conscripting platforms and exposing non-consenting users. A right to leave a platform should not become a duty to help copy your entire community into someone else’s database.

[P2.1] The opposition treats the social graph as unilateral property, but relationships are inherently bilateral. Portability does not mean dumping every friend’s private data into a third-party database; it means letting a user transfer their own connections where those connections have consented to receive messages or be discoverable elsewhere. We can mandate bilateral consent and granular controls—friend requests re-accepted on the new service, visibility settings that travel with the user—without exposing non-consenting parties. The alternative is not safer; it is a world where platforms monetize your entire network indefinitely because you cannot take your half of the relationship with you.
[P2.2] On security, the claim that interoperability creates vulnerability ignores that open protocols allow public auditing and rapid patching, while proprietary encryption remains an untestable black box. Email proves cross-provider security is achievable when standards are rigorous. "Qualified" access means ongoing regulatory oversight, not a one-time vetting that expires.
[P2.3] The competition argument inverts the actual market failure. Today’s compliance-free lock-in is the barrier to entry. A startup cannot out-innovate an incumbent when users cannot bring their friends. Mandated interoperability lowers the barrier by neutralizing network effects; yes, there are compliance costs, but they are dwarfed by the monopoly rents extracted by walled gardens.
[P2.4] Tiered obligations can protect small services.
[P2.5] Finally, the proposed alternatives—exporting your own posts or voluntary protocols—have already failed. Voluntary interoperability is what we have now, and it has produced silos. Exporting content without your graph is like being allowed to leave a party while abandoning everyone you came with. We are not conscripting platforms to copy communities; we are requiring them to let users leave with the relationships they built.

This mirrors telecom number portability: you keep your number, carriers coordinate, and your contacts do not relearn it.

[P4.3] A2: Portability mandates can require strict data minimization and continuous certification to address "irreversible exposure." Third parties receive only cryptographic identifiers and connection status, not message histories, photos, or profiles. Access is API-based and instantly revocable; when a recipient is breached, acquired, or decertified, regulators can suspend their keys and compel deletion under legal penalty.
[P4.4] Crucially, this risk is not created by interoperability—it is the status quo concentrated in unaccountable monopolies that already suffer massive breaches and sell user data. Interoperability distributes oversight and grants exit power; it introduces accountability where today there is none.
[P4.5] The opposition's dilemma collapses once we recognize that effective portability and bilateral consent are compatible through technical design, not mutually exclusive. They demand interoperability prove itself risk-free before adoption, while defending a status quo where platforms monetize captive graphs, suffer breaches, and lock users in indefinitely. That asymmetry decides the debate. We do not need perfect recall mechanisms; we need better accountability than black-box monopolies, plus the fundamental right to exit.
[P4.6] The motion does not conscript communities into third-party databases; it restores the user's right to move their own half of the relationship, with granular safeguards for the other half. We choose sovereignty over captivity, distributed oversight over concentrated secrecy.
