[P1.1] A worker who drives for two rideshare apps in the morning and fulfills short-term warehouse contracts at night works full-time hours, yet receives zero benefits because no single company claims forty of those hours. Our safety net still assumes one boss for decades, allowing modern firms to slice full-time labor into fragments and pocket the difference. Requiring a portable benefits floor for workers earning substantial income across platforms and short-term contracts fixes this structural failure. Under this mandate, each hiring firm contributes a pro-rated percentage of pay into a worker-owned account that follows the individual from job to job, funding essentials like paid sick leave, injury protection, and retirement.
[P1.2] Without this floor, the contract economy relies on an unpriced public subsidy. Workers still get sick, injured, and old; when platforms shed those inevitable lifecycle costs, the burden falls on public assistance and families pushed into debt. This loophole also distorts market competition. Traditional businesses that hire permanent staff and pay benefits are penalized and undercut by competitors that assemble the exact same labor through fragmented gigs purely to dodge benefit thresholds.
[P1.3] A portable floor restores competitive neutrality: an hour of substantial labor carries a baseline benefit cost, no matter how cleverly a firm structures its contracts.
[P1.4] Crucially, portable benefits escape the false binary between rigid traditional employment and total precarity. Opponents argue that protecting independent workers destroys the scheduling freedom they value. Pro-rated portability does the exact opposite: it makes flexibility sustainable. Workers can mix platforms, switch contracts, or decline shifts without forfeiting their safety net.
[P1.5] Furthermore, tying the mandate to substantial income targets individuals who genuinely rely on this labor while sparing casual side-earners unnecessary friction. Flexibility should mean autonomy for workers, not a corporate loophole to erase basic economic security.

[C1.4] Next, incidence. [P1.4] frames the contribution as a firm paying its share. But gig pay is set by an algorithm with no bargaining counterparty. A pro-rated levy is a per-ride cost, and the fastest adjustment is a lower per-ride rate.

[C1.6] Then administration. Thousands of hiring firms, including foreign platforms, must track each worker's aggregate earnings across competitors to know whether the floor applies. [C1.7] The honest conclusion of PRO's own diagnosis, that the one-boss model is obsolete, is a universal floor funded publicly and tied to the person, not a new employer mandate. The proposition picks the design most exposed to gaming, with workers paying the bill.
