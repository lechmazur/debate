Those effects spill beyond the person choosing. The fact that a feed has values, as [P1.1] says, is not a defect; it is why those values should be set through common, accountable rules.
[C1.2] Millions of user-selected “recommendation goals” turn a governance problem into a private menu.
[C1.3] PRO’s key step—[P1.4]’s claim that “User control supplies information”—is too thin for the burden. A checkbox saying “learn a skill” or “show several sides” does not tell the system what is reliable, age-appropriate, non-manipulative, or worth finite attention. People mispredict future wants: they endorse sober news in a setting, then reward gossip in the moment, or request diversity while clicking only identity-affirming posts. [C1.4] The [P1.3] lunch/headlines, evening/bicycle-repair, Sunday/family example proves only that context matters. [C1.5] Goal controls would also be gamed. If “local news” is a target, spam farms chase it; if “viewpoint diversity” is a target, propagandists demand inclusion as balance; if “relax without outrage” is a target, urgent public information is demoted as unpleasant. Declared objectives are easier to manipulate than common quality and safety standards. Mutes, unfollows, “not interested,” resets, and limits are feedback, not a transfer of objective-setting.
[C1.6] The proposition asks platforms to hand over the objective while keeping responsibility for the consequences. One managed feed, balancing relevance, safety, well-being, and diversity under shared rules, keeps authority where responsibility already sits.

[P2.1] CON’s central argument proves the wrong conclusion. Feeds’ public effects justify enforceable safety and quality rules; they do not imply one recommendation objective for everyone. A speed limit is a common rule; it does not tell drivers where to go. [P2.2] That distinction answers the responsibility objection. Platforms remain responsible for safeguards and execution, but responsibility for constraints does not create superior knowledge of a user’s purpose. CON collapses two questions: what a feed may promote, and what it should accomplish for this person now. Shared governance can answer the first while leaving the second meaningfully steerable.
[P2.3] The claim that centralized inference is enough is especially weak. A click records a response, not a goal. Someone opening a shocking post may be investigating a rumor, hate-reading it, or unable to look away; treating that as an instruction to supply more is precisely the problem. Search, follows, and “not interested” help rank items, but do not let users say, “For this session, prioritize reliable headlines and stop optimizing my engagement.”
[P2.4] Nor does control mean becoming a recommendation engineer. A default plus a few session goals, limits, and explanations is no second job. Stale settings can expire; indecisive users can keep the default. People requesting sober news but clicking gossip are not consenting to gossip as their objective.
[P2.5] Stated preferences can restrain ranking instead of letting every impulse define success.
[P2.6] Gaming also cuts against the status quo: spam farms already chase engagement. Declared goals do not require accepting every claimant as “local news” or every propagandist as “balance”; quality standards still decide eligibility. Once content is eligible, its ranking can serve the user’s purpose rather than the company’s.
[P2.7] CON offers common rules as a substitute for user authority. They are complements. Safety without goal control is paternalism with guardrails; goal control within safety rules is accountable personalization.
