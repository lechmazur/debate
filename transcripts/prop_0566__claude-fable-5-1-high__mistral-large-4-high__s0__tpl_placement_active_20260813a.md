[P1.1] Every selective university tells the same story: seats go to the students who earned them. Legacy and donor preferences are the one admissions practice that openly contradicts that story. They reward a single fact about an applicant—who their parents are—that the applicant did nothing to achieve and that predicts nothing about what they will contribute.
[P1.2] Start with the arithmetic. At highly selective schools, admissions is zero-sum. Every seat reserved for a well-connected eighteen-year-old is a seat taken from someone with a stronger file and no connections. Litigation data from Harvard showed legacy applicants admitted at several times the rate of everyone else, and that pool skewed heavily white and wealthy.
[P1.3] This is affirmative action for the already advantaged, and it was quietly running in the background the entire time universities claimed they were fighting for opportunity.
[P1.4] The asymmetry is now untenable. After the Supreme Court barred race-conscious admissions, a university may not weigh whether a Black applicant's grandparents were excluded from campus, but it may weigh whether a white applicant's grandparents attended. No institution can defend that pairing with a straight face.
[P1.5] Then the money. CON will say donor loyalty funds financial aid. But this is a sale dressed as philanthropy, and the price is the university's own integrity. Donors give because they love their school and because the tax code rewards them, not because a seat is dangled.
[P1.6] MIT, Caltech, Johns Hopkins, and Amherst have dropped or sharply curtailed legacy preference without their endowments collapsing. The Varsity Blues scandal showed how the public reacts when parents buy seats through a side door; donor preference is simply the same transaction through the front.
[P1.7] Finally, legitimacy. Universities enjoy tax exemption, public research funding, and enormous cultural authority precisely because they claim to sort by talent. Each legacy admit spends that credibility.
[P1.8] The motion asks only for sharp limits—a tie-breaker at most, never a thumb on the scale. CON must defend giving real weight to parentage. That is the one thing a meritocratic institution cannot justify.

[C1.1] Universities are not sorting machines. They are multi-generational communities that must fund their own educational mission, including need-blind admission and full financial aid for low-income students. Legacy and donor preferences are not corruption of that mission; they are the revenue engine that makes it possible. Every seat filled through a donor relationship or legacy connection cross-subsidizes scholarships for students who could never pay tuition.
[C1.2] When MIT or Caltech drops legacy preferences, they act with endowments built over centuries and donor bases concentrated in technology and science philanthropy. Most selective universities—especially those serving broader publics—lack that cushion. A sharp limit would not create more seats for the disadvantaged; it would shrink the financial aid budget and force cuts to the very programs that admit low-income students.
[C1.3] The zero-sum arithmetic is a fiction. Admissions committees do not rank applicants on a single meritocratic scale and fill seats from the top. They construct a class: they need trombone players, debate champions, students from rural Montana, and graduates who will serve on alumni boards and fund the library. Removing these preferences does not automatically redirect those dollars to financial aid; it simply removes the incentive for families to give.
[C1.4] Legacy status signals institutional loyalty—a form of social capital that benefits every student on campus, not just the admit.
[C1.5] The motion asks us to treat legacy as uniquely illegitimate while preserving preferences for athletes, musicians, and geographic diversity. That is arbitrary. It severs one form of institutional contribution while celebrating others. After the Supreme Court barred race-conscious admissions, universities need every lawful tool to maintain diverse, financially sustainable communities.
[C1.6] Sharp limits would deliver neither meritocracy nor equity—only scarcity, and the students who suffer will be the ones without connections to pay for their own seats.
