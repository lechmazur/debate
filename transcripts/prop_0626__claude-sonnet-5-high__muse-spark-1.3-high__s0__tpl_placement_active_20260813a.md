[P1.1] A machine that decides, on its own, who dies on a battlefield is a machine that has crossed a line no legal or moral system was built to handle. That is the case for banning fully autonomous lethal weapons systems, and it rests on three pillars: accountability, the irreducibility of moral judgment, and the danger of runaway escalation.
[P1.2] Start with accountability. Every existing framework of war crimes law, from the Geneva Conventions to command responsibility doctrine, assumes a human decision-maker who can be identified, tried, and punished. If an autonomous system misidentifies a wedding party as a militia convoy and kills forty civilians, who answers for it? The programmer who didn't foresee that scenario?
[P1.3] The commander who deployed a system whose decision process is opaque even to its designers? The manufacturer, thousands of miles away? This isn't a hypothetical gap, it's a structural one: autonomy severs the causal chain that responsibility law depends on. A ban preserves the only thing that has ever deterred atrocity: the certainty that a human being will be held to account.
[P1.4] Second, targeting decisions require judgment that machines cannot perform, not just faster processing. Distinguishing a fleeing combatant from a surrendering one, or a farmer's shovel from a weapon, requires contextual, cultural, and ethical reasoning under uncertainty. Pattern-recognition systems are brittle exactly where war is messiest: crowded urban terrain, deception, mixed civilian-combatant environments. Removing the human "in the loop" doesn't remove error, it removes the last check against catastrophic, unaccountable error at scale.
[P1.5] Third, autonomous weapons collapse the time and friction that currently restrain escalation. Human soldiers hesitate, verify, sometimes disobey unlawful orders. Machines don't. An arms race in split-second-reacting killer robots invites flash conflicts triggered by sensor glitches or spoofed data, with no human able to intervene before catastrophe compounds.
[P1.6] A ban on deployment doesn't ban research, defensive automation, or human-supervised systems. It draws one clear, enforceable line: no machine gets to autonomously decide that a human being dies today. That line is worth defending.
