[P1.1] The veto is not a safeguard of international order; it is an institutionalised exemption from it. It lets any one of five states prevent the Security Council from condemning, investigating, or coercing even while mass atrocities unfold. The result is not cautious stability but paralysis: the Council has repeatedly failed to act decisively in Syria, Myanmar, Ukraine, and other crises because a permanent member or its ally was implicated. A body whose central task is stopping threats to international peace cannot be taken seriously when the states most capable of threatening peace hold a procedural shield.
[P1.2] When major powers threaten to withdraw over losing the veto, that is not a reason to preserve it. It is the clearest demonstration of what the veto is for: a demand that the institution remain organised around the impunity of the strong. Accepting that demand turns the Security Council into a protection racket, keeping the language of collective security while selling its substance to the highest-capacity obstructionists. The proposition rightly treats that threat as extortion, not as a legitimate constraint on reform.
[P1.3] Nor should the withdrawal threat be accepted at face value. Permanent members gain enormous practical benefits from the UN system: legitimacy, burden-sharing, sanctions coordination, peacekeeping, and influence over smaller states. Most cooperate in many bodies where they have no veto. If a major power nevertheless leaves, the remaining Council can act with greater coherence, and regional or coalition arrangements can fill the gap.
[P1.4] A smaller but functional Council is better than a universal but self-neutralising one.
[P1.5] The decisive question is whether international institutions exist to manage power or to legitimise domination. The permanent-member veto chooses domination. Abolishing it, even against threats, is necessary because the current rule guarantees the very failure it claims to prevent.

[C1.1] The Security Council exists to keep the states that can start or stop a world-scale war inside one room. The veto is the admission price. Abolish it after those states threaten to leave and you do not purify collective security. You evict the only actors who can make it real.
[C1.2] That bargain is ugly, but it is not accidental. The League of Nations proved what happens when the strongest powers are absent or alienated: the institution talks, and order is made elsewhere. The United Nations was constructed to avoid that fate. The veto is why American, Chinese, and Russian representatives still sit on the Council instead of building rival forums, rival sanctions, and rival facts on the ground.
[C1.3] Withdrawal is not a bluff we should dare for moral effect. The Council's authority is borrowed from the states that fund peacekeeping, enforce sanctions, move force, and restrain clients. If they walk, the remaining members can draft cleaner resolutions. They cannot make those resolutions bite.
[C1.4] Benefits in other UN bodies do not substitute: those bodies do not license war or Chapter VII coercion. A smaller Council without the major powers is not more coherent. It is a committee with stationery.
[C1.5] Paralysis is real. So is a worse failure: a Council that votes to coerce a nuclear-armed permanent member and then discovers it has authorized a war it cannot contain. The veto is a circuit-breaker, not merely a shield. Removing it under threat of exit does not end domination.
[C1.6] It relocates power to coalitions, veto-free summits, and unilateral action, while the UN keeps the language of universality and loses the substance.
[C1.7] The decisive test is not whether five states enjoy impunity. It is whether international order is stronger with the great powers constrained inside a flawed institution or free outside a purified one. The proposition chooses the second. That is not reform.
[C1.8] It is demolition dressed as principle.
