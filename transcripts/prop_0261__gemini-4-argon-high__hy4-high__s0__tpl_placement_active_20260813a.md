[P1.1] If a parent spends decades building a library of physical books, records, and films, the law guarantees that collection passes to their children. Yet if that same parent builds the exact same collection on Kindle, Apple, or Steam—clicking a button explicitly labeled "Buy" and paying full retail price—their library legally evaporates the moment they die. Corporations use buried, non-negotiable terms of service to quietly convert consumer ownership into a terminal lease. Digital inheritance law must end this forfeiture.
[P1.2] First, guaranteeing transferability restores basic economic fairness. Digital platforms intentionally mimic the language and pricing of physical ownership. A purchased digital movie, game, or e-book frequently costs as much as its physical counterpart, starkly distinct from low-cost rentals or subscriptions. Allowing platforms to pocket full purchase prices while extinguishing the asset upon death hands tech monopolies an unjustified windfall—forcing heirs to repurchase media their family already paid for.
[P1.3] Consumer law routinely overrides fine-print contracts when boilerplate terms strip families of paid-for value; inheritance law must do the same.
[P1.4] Second, without this guarantee, cultural and familial heritage is being erased. As books, music, cinema, and games migrate to digital storefronts, the modern middle-class estate is becoming intangible. A parent’s curated library is both a substantial financial investment and a personal heirloom. Letting that legacy vanish simply because the medium shifted from paper and plastic to pixels arbitrarily punishes modern consumers.
[P1.5] Finally, post-mortem transfer poses no threat to copyright. Passing a digital library to next of kin does not multiply copies or enable piracy. Platforms already manage access credentials and license verification instantly. Transferring a deceased person’s library or account credentials to an heir simply replaces one user with another, preserving the exact one-to-one footprint of a physical inheritance.
[P1.6] When someone buys a digital asset, that enduring value belongs to their family, not the platform.

[C1.1] The guarantee rests on a false material equivalence: a “Buy” button is taken to turn an account into a bookshelf. But a paperback is a finite object; a Kindle book, Steam game, or downloaded film is an ongoing service relationship—authentication, storage, updates, DRM, support, and permissions from rights holders. What the deceased had was personal access under a non-transferable license, not a transferable chattel. A guarantee of inheritance would not pass existing property.
[C1.2] It would create a new property right the buyer never bought and force platforms and creators to honor it.
[C1.3] That destroys the “windfall” claim. People pay full price for theater tickets, software seats, gym memberships, and annual passes without acquiring heritable assets. Digital licenses are priced and structured around personal use; platforms can maintain large catalogs because access is not treated as a perpetual heritable entitlement. If every such license must be made inheritable, sellers will adapt: subscriptions instead of purchases, higher prices, stricter identity checks, less offline access.
[C1.4] The guarantee buys a symbolic estate transfer by worsening terms for living consumers.
[C1.5] The “one-to-one footprint” argument also hides the legal change. Replacing a dead user with an heir is not copying a key; it is authorizing a new person, then potentially their heirs, creditors, or several next of kin. A physical book imposes no further duty after delivery. A digital library requires continual service and verification from a platform that never agreed to recognize that successor chain, plus copyright permissions from rights holders.
[C1.6] Nor is a law-guaranteed transfer right respectful of the deceased. Libraries and annotations can expose health worries, religion, politics, sexuality, or private reading habits to estranged relatives. Clear disclosure, authorized exports, legacy contacts, and opt-in beneficiary designations where allowed protect deliberate preservation. A misleading “Buy” button should invite consumer-law scrutiny, not a universal mandate that overrides contract, privacy, testamentary intent, and the economics of non-transferable access.
