[P1.1] The question is not whether a two-dollar shirt feels like a bargain. It is who pays for the bargain. Fast-fashion marketplaces have perfected regulatory arbitrage: sellers ship small parcels across borders faster than inspectors can track them, and the platform then calls itself a mere intermediary.
[P1.2] But when a company controls the listing, ranks results, takes payment, and sets delivery terms, it is the gatekeeper. Gatekeepers should face obligations.
[P1.3] Start with safety. In ordinary retail, an importer or retailer is answerable for dangerous goods. In the direct-to-consumer model, often no one is: the seller is distant, the parcel low-value, and border checks thin. The result is hazardous products in homes—children’s sleepwear that burns like paper, cheap chargers that overheat.
[P1.4] We do not let a restaurant sell contaminated food because the meal was cheap. We should not let an app sell a fire hazard because it shipped from overseas for less.
[P1.5] Labor transparency is the same. Ultra-low prices often rely on squeezed wages, excessive hours, or hidden coercion. A shopper sees fabric and price, not the factory. Requiring platforms to map suppliers, disclose audits, and stop hiding behind anonymous subcontractors will not fix global abuse overnight, but it makes exploitation visible and contestable.
[P1.6] Refusing to map the chain is a demand to keep abuse invisible.
[P1.7] Critics will call this a tax on poor shoppers. Be honest about the tradeoff: yes, stronger rules slow some ultra-cheap shopping. Deliveries get slower and prices edge up. That is not tragedy; it is the market pricing in harms it now dumps on children, workers, hospitals, and honest competitors already paying for safety and fair wages.
[P1.8] Slower shopping filters out items cheap only because they skipped tests and squeezed labor. Low-income consumers deserve safe products and dignity, not a race to the bottom in a thin plastic bag. A marketplace that profits from cross-border scale should carry the obligations that make it accountable.

[C1.5] None of this means shrugging at unsafe goods. If chargers overheat, ban the charger. Targeted enforcement on demonstrable hazards beats blanket paperwork on millions of safe parcels.
