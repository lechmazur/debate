[P1.1] Theaters are increasingly weaponizing artificial scarcity to funnel consumers into expensive Premium Large-Format (PLF) screens, transforming moviegoing from an accessible cultural event into an exclusionary luxury. While IMAX and Dolby formats offer unique experiences, they should not dominate blockbuster scheduling at the direct expense of standard-price screenings.
[P1.2] First, PLF dominance creates a severe affordability crisis. Blockbusters are the lifeblood of the film industry, uniquely designed for mass audiences. However, when cinemas allocate the vast majority of prime-time slots to premium formats, they impose a de facto tax on the public. A standard ticket is already a significant expense for many households; adding a steep PLF surcharge per ticket makes a simple weekend family outing prohibitively expensive.
[P1.3] Preserving a robust schedule of standard screenings ensures cinemas remain accessible to all demographics, rather than acting as gated communities for those who can afford VIP pricing.
[P1.4] Second, PLF dominance relies on the illusion of choice. Defenders of premium pricing often claim they are merely meeting consumer demand for bigger screens. In reality, when standard screenings for a highly anticipated film are relegated to 10:30 PM on a weeknight or 11:00 AM on a Sunday, consumers are not organically "choosing" the premium upgrade—they are forced into it because it is the only convenient option. This is a coercive upselling tactic.
[P1.5] It strips audiences of their agency, forcing them to pay an unwarranted premium simply to participate in the cultural zeitgeist of an opening weekend.
[P1.6] Finally, squeezing out standard screenings is a myopic strategy that harms the theater industry's long-term health. By pricing out casual viewers and families to pad short-term margins, theaters actively train audiences to wait for streaming. Sustaining the theatrical model requires high volume and habitual attendance, both of which are destroyed when ordinary moviegoers are treated as secondary citizens. Blockbusters belong to the broader public, and exhibition schedules must guarantee that standard, affordable access remains the baseline, not an afterthought.

[C1.2] Forcing theaters to cut PLF slots to add more standard showings would backfire spectacularly, pricing out the very budget-conscious viewers PRO claims to defend.
[C1.3] PRO’s claim of "coercive upselling" is equally unmoored from market reality. Theatrical scheduling is driven by concrete audience demand, not manipulative scarcity: when a PLF screening sells out days before release, adding more PLF showtimes is a response to consumer choice, not a restriction of it. Standard screenings remain widely available at off-peak times for cost-conscious viewers, and the peak showtimes PRO deems "inaccessible" are sold out across all formats because of overwhelming demand, not PLF favoritism. A family that wants a cheap weekday matinee standard screening can still book one with no extra hassle, even as PLF options give them the choice to splurge on a premium night out if they wish.
[C1.4] Far from harming long-term theatrical attendance, PLF screens give audiences a compelling reason to choose the cinema over streaming for event films. The immersive experience of formats like IMAX or Dolby justifies the higher ticket cost for a once-a-year blockbuster, driving foot traffic that benefits every part of the theater, including concession sales that fund standard screening operations. Restricting PLF dominance would not expand accessibility—it would shrink it.
