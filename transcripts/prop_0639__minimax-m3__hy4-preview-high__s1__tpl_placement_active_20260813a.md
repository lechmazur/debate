[C1.1] The issue is not whether elected elites should veto "the people." It is whether a body that merely resembles the public can bind it. Descriptive resemblance is not democratic authorization. [P1.4] says "if it is representative, vetoing it is unjust."
[C1.2] That conflates mirroring a population with being accountable to it. A hundred strangers may look like the country; they have still received no mandate from anyone, they answer to no constituency, and after deciding they disappear. Binding power without continuing accountability is not self-government. It is rule by temporary guests.
[C1.3] Picture an assembly with binding authority over a carbon tax or pension reform. It hears experts, deliberates for weeks, then disbands. If household budgets collapse or pension changes disrupt work, no voter can turn those members out. They bear no responsibility for administration, budgeting, legal fallout, or the next election.
[C1.4] An elected legislature is flawed; it can be slow. But it can be replaced, and it must defend its choices at the ballot box. That feedback loop is not an "elite veto"; it is the device that keeps binding decisions connected to those who live with them.
[C1.5] Its claim that advisory status selects for "caution" while binding status selects for "responsibility" gets the incentive backwards. An assembly that cannot be held responsible has less reason to weigh long-term costs. Sortition works when tightly constrained. Juries use random selection to find particular facts under law, with judges, rules of evidence, appeals, and no power to make general rules.
[C1.6] Binding policymaking is different in kind: it governs everyone, including future citizens and outvoted minorities, and it requires execution by agencies. Heavy reliance on briefings and facilitators merely shifts influence to unelected experts who draft the options.
[C1.7] Advisory assemblies can still be consequential: they can force public, reasoned responses from elected officials and expose cherry-picking. The remedy for ignored recommendations is transparency and a duty to answer, not handing sovereignty to an unaccountable sample. Sortition should inform and press binding power; it should not exercise it.
