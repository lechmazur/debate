[P1.1] A country is not a hotel. It is a political community whose public language and civic norms make self-government among strangers possible. Permanent settlement is joining that community. Treating integration as optional asks the host to dissolve the conditions of shared civic life while still delivering its benefits.
[P1.2] Core civic norms are not cuisine or festivals. They are the operating rules of a liberal order: equality before the law, peaceful transfer of power, and individual rights that override clan, caste, or clerical veto. If those remain optional, the result is not pluralism but parallel societies—honor codes in family disputes, sex segregation imposed on public services, foreign political loyalties imported into domestic contests. A democracy cannot remain coherent if sizable communities treat its constitution as one menu among many.
[P1.3] The common public language is civic infrastructure. It is how a parent talks to a teacher, a worker reads a safety notice, a voter understands a ballot, a witness speaks in court. Permanent translation as a substitute creates a two-class public: those who can participate fully and those who cannot. Children who never master it inherit a ceiling.
[P1.4] Expecting newcomers to learn it is not cruelty; it is the only way equal membership becomes real rather than ceremonial.
[P1.5] Successful immigration has always paired welcome with expectation. Private culture can remain private. Public membership cannot. Without that line, social trust erodes, institutions fragment, and natives correctly see a one-way deal.
[P1.6] The fair bargain is simple: the country opens its institutions; settlers accept the civic language and the civic creed that make those institutions worth joining.

Europeans who arrived in America speaking no English had grandchildren who couldn't speak the old tongue. That transformation required no "expectation" stamped on their admission; it required only opportunity.
[C1.2] The demand is redundant where it matters and corrosive where it isn't.
[C1.3] Notice what the proposition actually does. Civic norms—equality before the law, peaceful transfer of power—are already binding on everyone through legislation and courts. An immigrant who violates them faces the same legal system I do. So what does "expected to adopt" add?
[C1.4] Only a loyalty test aimed at newcomers, a suggestion that the native-born are trustworthy by default while settlers are suspects. That is not a civic creed; it's a two-tier membership, which is precisely what the proposition claims to oppose.
[C1.5] History also contradicts the panic about optional integration. The American Southwest was Spanish-speaking before it was American; Amish and Hasidic communities have kept their own languages and internal norms for over a century; Quebec's anglophones never adopted French en masse. None of this dissolved self-government. Parallel societies that genuinely threaten democracy are the product of exclusion and economic lockout—ghettos created by discrimination—not of insufficient cultural expectation.
[C1.6] When natives see immigrants denied jobs and housing, they get parallel societies. When immigrants get access, their children speak the language within a generation and marry across lines. Integration is a function of openness, not of demands.
[C1.7] Worse, coerced integration backfires. Research on citizenship tests shows stricter requirements tend to reduce naturalization, especially among the less educated—locking people out of exactly the civic membership the proposition says it wants. Demands don't speed integration; they signal that belonging is conditional and grudging.
[C1.8] Cultures integrate best when invited, not conscripted.
