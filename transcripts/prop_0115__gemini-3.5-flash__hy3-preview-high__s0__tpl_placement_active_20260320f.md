[P1.1] Buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) services are not neutral checkout features like a shipping calculator or a promotional coupon. They are unsecured consumer loans. Yet, by masquerading as simple payment processing buttons, BNPL providers exploit a regulatory loophole that leaves consumers exposed to severe financial risks. To protect consumers and ensure market stability, BNPL must be regulated under the same robust frameworks that govern credit cards.
[P1.2] The primary danger of BNPL is the illusion of friction-free debt. Because they escape traditional credit card regulations, these platforms are not required to meaningfully assess a borrower’s ability to repay. This regulatory blind spot enables "loan stacking," where a consumer opens multiple parallel loans across different BNPL providers. Unlike credit cards, which aggregate a user’s liability into a single, visible limit and report to credit bureaus, BNPL allows debt to accumulate silently in the shadows.
[P1.3] This leads directly to debt spirals, particularly among financially vulnerable demographics who increasingly use BNPL for everyday essentials.
[P1.4] Furthermore, BNPL deprives consumers of vital protections that credit card users take for granted. Under established credit laws, cardholders have federally mandated rights to dispute charges, initiate chargebacks for undelivered or defective goods, and receive clear, standardized disclosures regarding fees and terms. BNPL users, conversely, find themselves trapped in a maze of shifting merchant policies and platform terms of service, with little legal recourse when a transaction goes wrong.
[P1.5] Regulating BNPL like credit cards is not about stifling retail innovation; it is about recognizing economic reality. When an entity extends credit and profits from late fees, consumer default, or merchant fees on debt-fueled purchases, it is a lender. We must strip away the digital paint job. If it functions like a credit card, it must be regulated like a credit card.

[C1.1] The core premise of the proposition fails: buy-now-pay-later products are not equivalent to credit cards, and forcing them into the same regulatory box will harm the very consumers proponents claim to protect. Credit cards are revolving, long-term debt vehicles with variable interest, open-ended borrowing, and minimum monthly payments. BNPL is a short-term, fixed-installment checkout option, almost always interest-free, tied to a single purchase with no revolving balance. This structural difference is not a loophole, but a deliberate design choice to serve consumers who cannot access traditional credit.
[C1.2] The two products serve fundamentally different needs, so a one-size-fits-all regulatory approach is mismatched.
[C1.3] Over-regulating BNPL like credit cards would eliminate the accessibility that makes it valuable for credit-constrained shoppers. Credit card rules require hard credit inquiries and strict limit assignments. Most BNPL providers skip hard pulls for small, short-term plans, letting consumers with thin or damaged credit access payment flexibility without damaging their scores. Unlike credit cards, BNPL does not incentivize long-term debt accumulation, as plans are automatically drafted from linked payment methods with no option to revolve balances.
[C1.4] Imposing credit card compliance costs would force providers to add interest, cut off marginal borrowers, or both. That pushes millions of cash-strapped shoppers back to predatory payday loans or high-interest products they cannot sustain.
[C1.5] Proponents cite "loan stacking" as a risk, but credit card-style aggregate limits break BNPL’s purchase-specific design. A consumer financing a $200 fridge should not have borrowing capacity tied to a $50 clothing purchase elsewhere. BNPL providers already use internal risk checks to prevent overextension, and many now voluntarily report repayment data to credit bureaus. Forcing identical dispute rules as credit cards is unnecessary: BNPL users get merchant-level refund protections that often exceed credit card chargebacks, since merchants remain directly liable for sold goods.
[C1.6] Regulating BNPL like credit cards strips away the features that make it a safer alternative to other short-term credit.
