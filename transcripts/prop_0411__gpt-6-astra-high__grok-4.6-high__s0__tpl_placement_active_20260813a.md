[P1.1] If one passenger can leave for work by requesting a ride, while another must book yesterday and hope an accessible vehicle arrives, they do not have access to the same service. Wheelchair accessibility must mean usable mobility—not merely the theoretical possibility of transportation.
[P1.2] The requirement should be comparable service under comparable conditions: similar waiting times, operating hours, booking options, and fares in the same area. “Close” does not mean identical arrival times on every trip. It means disability cannot routinely turn an ordinary journey into a separate logistical project. Public transit and ride-hail providers should meet enforceable performance standards, with realistic implementation deadlines.
[P1.3] The strongest reason is that transportation unlocks everything else. A substantially longer or unpredictable wait can cost someone a shift, a medical appointment, or the ability to collect their child. Requiring advance reservations when other passengers travel spontaneously restricts which jobs and daily responsibilities wheelchair users can realistically undertake. These are not simply differences in comfort; they are barriers to participation.
[P1.4] Voluntary provision does not adequately solve that problem. Accessible vehicles and infrastructure require investment, while individual operators have incentives to leave those costs to someone else. Low use of an unreliable service can then become an excuse for keeping it unreliable. A system-wide obligation breaks that cycle and makes accessibility a shared operating responsibility rather than a charitable exception.
[P1.5] Implementation can be flexible without making access optional. Transit agencies can prioritize accessible vehicles, functioning lifts, and dependable connections. Ride-hail platforms can coordinate accessible fleets and compensate drivers for their additional costs; every car need not be converted. Phased procurement and public funding can support compliance.
[P1.6] The objection that accessibility costs money is real but incomplete. Without a requirement, those costs do not disappear: they fall on wheelchair users as lost time, lost opportunities, and dependence on others. Transportation systems should distribute the cost of access—not ration independence according to who can climb into a standard vehicle.
