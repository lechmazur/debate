[P1.1] Tourist taxes are not free money for whatever the city budget needs. They pay for burdens visitors impose: fuller trains, delayed waste collection, strained sanitation, and housing pressure where short-term rentals compete with residents. Visitors who pay should help finance the services that make their presence sustainable, and residents should have the first claim on that revenue.
[P1.2] Pooling that revenue into a general budget reverses the priority. Nothing then ensures that transit gets an extra bus, sanitation gets a worker, or residents get affordable housing. Officials can claim credit for spending while the visitor surcharge disappears into a surplus used for pet projects or tax cuts. General-budget accounting makes both tourism’s costs and residents’ compensation politically invisible.
[P1.3] Earmarking creates a traceable bargain. Each pound or euro identifies a claim: reliable transit on crowded routes; better waste collection and sanitation in visitor-heavy districts; and affordable resident housing in neighborhoods where short-term rentals raise rents. These are not decorative amenities. They directly address the burdens tourism creates.
[P1.4] Opponents will say money is fungible and earmarks waste flexibility. But fungibility does not erase opportunity cost. The question is not whether the same euro could theoretically pay a teacher; it is whether a tax on visitors should become a general transfer whose benefits residents cannot identify. Tourism supports shops and jobs, but that does not make every city expense a legitimate charge on visitors.
[P1.5] If tourism’s wider benefits justify public spending, that should be an explicit budget choice—not concealed inside a levy ostensibly paid by outsiders.
[P1.6] Earmarking need not freeze money in a vanity project: housing, transit, and sanitation can face transparent targets, audits, and independent oversight. But a protected priority for these essentials is stronger than unrestricted absorption. The decisive question is whether cities may collect more from visitors while shifting the resulting costs onto residents. Earmarking makes the tax fair, visible, and materially connected to the harms it addresses.

[P2.1] The opponent’s strongest point is substitution: a council can call tourism money “transit” while cutting its ordinary transit grant. That is a real design flaw, but it is an argument for enforceable additionality, not for unrestricted absorption. If earmarked spending must add to, rather than replace, the relevant baseline service, and service outcomes are audited, the shell game is blocked. Unrestricted revenue actually permits the same substitution in reverse: collect from visitors, quietly reduce ordinary transit or sanitation spending, and divert the freed money elsewhere.
[P2.2] Housing, transit, and sanitation also need not be an exhaustive map of tourism’s costs. The motion says “primarily,” not exclusively. Crowding-related policing, parks, and toilets can receive residual tourism revenue or be financed from general funds. Crucially, the protected categories cover the most direct burden-and-benefit relationship: crowded transport, waste in visitor-heavy districts, and housing pressure where short-term rentals compete with residents.
[P2.3] Different needs can be addressed without sacrificing that priority. Ordinary emergencies likewise remain a general-budget responsibility.
[P2.4] Cyclicality does not make absorption fairer. Pooling can cushion volatility only when other revenue grows; otherwise a tourism downturn means either spending cuts or higher taxes. Earmarking need not freeze every pound into one permanent project: multi-year plans, reserves, and periodic review can smooth fluctuations, while housing, transit, and sanitation remain a protected floor rather than a blank cheque.
[P2.5] The decisive comparison is therefore not “rigid earmarks versus competent budgeting.” It is a visible visitor contribution versus an invisible cross-subsidy. If a hotel tax is absorbed, nobody can tell whether visitors’ money bought an extra bus, more sanitation, or merely permitted spending elsewhere to rise. A protected fund creates a concrete resident claim and exposes whether the promised benefit actually arrives. Flexibility is valuable; untraceable freedom to spend visitors’ money on unrelated priorities is precisely what this motion rejects.

It means the three favoured purposes must still take priority even when policing, emergency care, parks, or another response would relieve tourism’s costs more effectively. [C2.4] If general revenues must always absorb them while visitor revenue remains protected, the tax’s priority survives precisely when reprioritisation may be most justified.
[C2.5] Reserves and multi-year plans can smooth an earmarked fund, but they cannot outperform pooling volatile tourism receipts alongside broader revenues. A reserve accumulated during a boom is money withheld from current needs; a protected spending floor during a downturn still competes with more urgent claims.
[C2.6] Finally, PRO’s “visible contribution versus invisible cross-subsidy” is a false choice. Cities can publish tourism-tax receipts and report housing, transit, sanitation, and other tourism-related outcomes without making those categories the automatic winners. [C2.7] Accountability should constrain how well money is spent, not dictate in advance where it must mostly go.

[P4.1] A1: Audited baselines can guarantee service levels, but not determine which revenue must pay for them. A council can meet a transit baseline with ordinary receipts while diverting hotel-tax receipts to an unrelated festival; published outcomes look excellent. If a general-budget rule truly binds tourism receipts to eligible purposes and prevents offsets, it is an earmark in substance; if it does not, substitution remains possible. Mandatory earmarking adds that binding source-of-funds rule: visitor money is committed to the burdens tourism creates, and diverting it requires a public decision to change the rule.
[P4.2] Audits ask whether spending works; earmarking makes diversion visible.
[P4.3] A2: The principle is not “greatest immediate harm per euro,” because that ignores causation, incidence, and recurrence. Visitor revenue should first finance marginal costs or externalities caused by tourism and borne by residents, with recurring system pressures ahead of episodic responses. Crowded transit and sanitation are direct visitor-driven demands; where visitor demand encourages short-term rentals and housing conversion, housing becomes a distributional externality. Policing and emergency response may receive earmarked funds if directly attributable to tourism, but exceptional event costs remain residual; ordinary policing and disaster response stay in general budgets. “Primarily” therefore has an objective causal test, not an arbitrary list.
[P4.4] This is why earmarking wins. CON concedes that general budgets can publish, audit, and protect baselines [C2.7], but those controls operate after revenue enters a common pool. They do not prevent residents financing tourism’s infrastructure or visitors financing unrelated benefits. The choice is not competence versus rigidity; it is whether the levy has a traceable fiscal bargain.
[P4.5] Additionality rules, reserves, periodic review, and residual tourism costs prevent rigidity. Earmarking these resident-facing burdens first prevents shifting congestion, waste, and housing pressure onto residents or converting the levy into a general transfer. Changing the linkage should require a visible political choice, not disappear inside a balanced-looking budget.
