[P1.1] Political incentives systematically erode legislative fiscal discipline. When parliaments retain sole discretion over budgets, the shortcut of deficit spending wins elections today while its costs arrive after the next vote. That is why so many democracies accumulate structural deficits even when statutes nominally require balance. The pattern repeats because the political clock is shorter than the fiscal one.
[P1.2] A constitutional balanced-budget rule changes the default. It forces the debate from 'should we borrow' to 'where do we cut or raise,' because the legal baseline is balance. Entrenching the rule makes suspension costly, denying lawmakers the easy escape of a simple majority or a weak sunset clause. Statutory balance laws are suspended at convenience; constitutional status prevents that.
[P1.3] Critics fear rigidity, but the automatic stabilizer exception answers that. In a recession, tax receipts fall and unemployment outlays rise without any new law; the exception permits the resulting deficit automatically, acting as a built-in shock absorber. This avoids the familiar con of discretionary regimes: panicked, pro-cyclical austerity that deepens slumps because legislators delay stimulus until confidence collapses. The exception is mechanical, tied to economic conditions, not to a partisan vote.
[P1.4] Compare the alternatives concretely. Under pure legislative discretion, a government facing a mild downturn can still manufacture a large deficit for partisan projects, then normalize the higher debt as the new baseline. With our rule, only the stabilizer-driven gap is allowed; intentional overspending requires a constitutional amendment, not a budget vote. During expansions, the rule compels balance, so the boom builds room for the bust.
[P1.5] The mechanism also lowers long-term borrowing costs. Credible constitutional constraints signal to markets that debt will not drift upward indefinitely, reducing risk premia and freeing revenue for public services rather than interest.
[P1.6] Legislative discretion is not neutral; it is a bias toward borrowing. A balanced-budget constitution with automatic stabilizer carve-outs delivers discipline without sacrificing recession response. That is the correct design.

[C1.5] The real choice is not constitutional rigidity versus reckless borrowing.
