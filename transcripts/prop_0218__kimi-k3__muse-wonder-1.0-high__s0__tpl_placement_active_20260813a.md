[P1.1] Today, a cracked screen on a three-year-old phone often costs nearly as much to fix through the manufacturer as the phone is worth. That is not an accident; it is a business model. Manufacturers restrict parts, pair components to serial numbers so even genuine parts fail after an independent swap, and withhold diagnostic tools. The result is a repair monopoly, and monopolies price accordingly.
[P1.2] Right-to-repair laws attack this directly: they require parts, manuals, and tools to be available at fair cost. When independent shops can compete, repair prices fall toward the cost of the work, not the cost of a new device. This is already visible where laws exist: France's repairability scoring pushed manufacturers to redesign products and publish prices, and early US state laws forced companies to open parts programs they long refused.
[P1.3] Cheaper repair is also the single most powerful lever on e-waste. The world generates an estimated 60-plus million tonnes of e-waste a year, and only around a fifth is properly recycled. Recycling is a weak fix; reuse is a strong one. Most of a phone's environmental footprint is locked in at manufacture, so every extra year of life avoids nearly the full footprint of a replacement.
[P1.4] A battery swap that costs $40 instead of $250 means a device lives instead of being shredded.
[P1.5] Now weigh the other side. The risks invoked are real but small and already governed: battery and charger safety sit under existing certification regimes that repair laws do not repeal, and anti-theft activation locks are preserved. The claim that a schematic in a repair shop unlocks hacking is speculation, conveniently advanced by the firms that profit from selling you a new device. Security researchers routinely note that devices are already repaired everywhere — just badly and expensively.
[P1.6] So the comparison is not close: certain, mechanical savings for hundreds of millions of consumers and measurably less waste, against hypothetical harms that regulators already have tools to contain.

[C1.2] Right to repair does not create a competitive parts market; it leaves the manufacturer as the sole supplier. A company ordered to sell parts, manuals, and tools on “fair” terms can still charge a high wholesale price, bundle parts into expensive assemblies, and litigate what fair means while deadlines slip. The independent shop must then add rent, labor, and warranty risk. Repair becomes legally possible, not cheap.
[C1.3] Manufacturers can also recover lost repair revenue where they still control the transaction—in higher upfront device prices.
[C1.4] That is why e-waste will barely move. People discard phones and laptops mainly because software support ends, performance falls behind, carrier deals subsidize a new model, or a trade-in makes replacement feel free. Fixing a screen does not restore security updates, and a repaired phone kept as a drawer backup does not prevent a new purchase. Add failed do-it-yourself attempts, individually shipped parts, and counterfeit components that fail early, and the waste avoided in five years is marginal.
[C1.5] Genuine redesign takes multiple product generations, beyond this window.
[C1.6] The harms do not wait. Weakening parts pairing and widening access to diagnostic, calibration, and unlocking tools lowers the value of theft protections. A stolen device that can be re-paired, reset, or harvested through a loosely vetted shop is worth more, so more devices are worth stealing, and prior owners’ data passes through more hands.
[C1.7] Meanwhile lithium batteries, chargers, and glued assemblies move from trained facilities to kitchen tables and undertrained storefronts using copycat cells. One punctured pack can cause a fire no modest saving repays.
[C1.8] Within five years the plausible result is costly compliance, modestly wider repair, little change in replacement behavior—and an immediately larger attack and accident surface.
