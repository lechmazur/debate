[P1.1] "No safe alternative" is the hinge. This isn't about camping in front of homes or blocking fire lanes. It is about what happens when the shelter system has no bed, or the only bed is violent, crowded, or denies a person's partner, pet, or disability needs. Then a vehicle is not a lifestyle choice; it is the last wall between a person and exposure.
[P1.2] Sleeping is not optional. A law that fines, tows, or rousts people for meeting a biological necessity when every alternative is unsafe is not a neutral parking rule. It is punishment for existing.
[P1.3] The mechanism is cruel and self-defeating. A fine on someone with no money becomes debt; unpaid debt becomes license loss, court entanglement, worse credit. Towing is worse: the car is often the only shelter and the only way to get to work, medical care, or a job interview. Cities spend public money to seize the one asset that could help someone exit homelessness, then pay again to store or destroy it.
[P1.4] Harassment does not create a bed. It just moves people from one block to the next, more exhausted, more hidden, less employable.
[P1.5] And the alternative is not chaos. Cities can regulate genuinely dangerous conduct—blocking travel lanes, dumping waste, idling in residential zones—and create safe parking with bathrooms, outreach, and time limits. The motion bars routine fines, towing, and police harassment; it does not immunize every parking violation or bad behavior. It says: when there is no safe shelter, don't criminalize sleep.
[P1.6] The central question is proportionality. If the state cannot provide a safe place to sleep, it should not punish people for finding the least dangerous one. A just city can ask people to move a few feet; it should not make survival a ticketable offense. That is the line to decide: not whether vehicles can be regulated, but whether sleeping becomes a crime when no safe bed exists.

[C1.2] That makes the entire city street a de facto campground.
[C1.3] Remove routine fines and towing and you remove the low-level tools that stop a few vehicles from becoming an entrenched encampment. The proposition says dangerous conduct can still be regulated, but danger is usually discovered after waste, propane, blocked sidewalks, and school curbs are already affected. Routine enforcement prevents problems from hardening; it does not merely punish after harm. Without it, officials wait for crisis, then resort to heavier evictions—worse for everyone.
[C1.4] Towing is not automatically cruelty; it is sometimes the only way to move a leaking, unregistered, or unsafely parked vehicle that cannot move itself.
[C1.5] The fairness problem is just as serious. The motion creates an exemption that a family paying rent, a business owner, or a neighbor with an extra unregistered car does not get. Because no one can verify who truly lacks alternatives, it invites abuse and resentment.
[C1.6] None of this denies the housing crisis. The answer to a truly unsafe shelter is emergency shelter capacity, safe-parking sites with sanitation, and targeted assistance—not a legal right to occupy public streets indefinitely. A car lacks a toilet, running water, safe heat, and security. It lets the state avoid building enough beds while neighborhoods absorb the fallout.
[C1.7] The motion should be rejected.
