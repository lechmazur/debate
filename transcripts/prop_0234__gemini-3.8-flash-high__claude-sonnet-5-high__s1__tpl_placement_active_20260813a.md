[P1.1] Labor markets cannot function efficiently when one party negotiates in the dark. For decades, large employers have leveraged information asymmetry to suppress wages, relying on employee silence to maintain arbitrary disparities. Requiring large firms to disclose pay bands and historical promotion rates by role corrects this fundamental market failure, replacing opaque discretion with accountability.
[P1.2] First, pay transparency rectifies wage suppression. When workers do not know what a role pays, employers can price-discriminate, offering lower wages to those with less leverage or weaker outside options. Publishing pay bands establishes an objective baseline. It ensures workers are compensated for the value of the work itself rather than their individual capacity for aggressive haggling.
[P1.3] This directly closes persistent demographic wage gaps, which routinely flourish under the cover of secrecy.
[P1.4] Second, disclosing promotion rates is essential to career equity. Equal pay at hiring means little if certain workers are systematically tracked into dead-end positions while others enjoy accelerated advancement. Publishing promotion rates by role exposes "sticky floors" and reveals whether internal mobility is genuine or merely rhetorical. It forces organizations to confront institutional bottlenecks and rewards companies that actually invest in developing their talent.
[P1.5] Opponents will defend "managerial flexibility," but we must call that flexibility what it often is: the convenience of unaccountable discretion. Unchecked flexibility allows managers to reward personal affinity over performance, indulge unconscious bias, and make ad-hoc promises behind closed doors. Legitimate managerial discretion—such as rewarding exceptional merit—does not require total secrecy; high performers can still be distinguished within published tiers. What this policy curtails is not genuine management, but the liberty to exploit an asymmetric advantage.
[P1.6] For large employers with hundreds or thousands of workers, standardization is neither impractical nor destructive; it is basic corporate governance. The trade-off is clear: sacrifice the arbitrary flexibility of the employer to guarantee fairness and informed choice for the worker.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds like sunlight and fairness. In practice, it is a blunt regulatory hammer that trades real flexibility for the illusion of equity, and large employers will respond to the mandate, not the ideal behind it.
[C1.2] First, mandatory pay-band disclosure does not raise wages to match top performers; it collapses them toward the middle. When a band is published, it becomes a ceiling as much as a floor. Managers lose the ability to pay above-band to retain a critical engineer facing a competing offer, or to make a location- or market-adjusted exception. Legal and PR risk from appearing to violate your own published band pushes compensation toward rigid compliance, not toward closing gaps.
[C1.3] The predictable outcome, seen in jurisdictions that have already tried broad pay transparency mandates, is wage compression that hurts your best negotiators and your most in-demand specialists, the very people equity advocates should want to see rewarded.
[C1.4] Second, "promotion rates by role" sounds neutral until you picture the actual roles. In any team smaller than several dozen people, a published promotion rate is not statistics, it is a spotlight on identifiable individuals. That creates two bad incentives: managers freeze promotions near reporting periods to avoid unflattering numbers, and firms quietly avoid placing employees in roles where low historical promotion rates would look damaging, regardless of the individual's merit. You get less mobility, not more, dressed up as transparency.
[C1.5] Third, this is a solution mismatched to the problem. Wage discrimination and biased advancement are already illegal and already enforceable through targeted audits, EEOC-style investigations, and litigation discovery, tools that go straight at bad actors without freezing every company's compensation architecture. [P1.1]'s claim that markets "cannot function" without blanket disclosure ignores that the harm is concentrated, not universal, and the remedy should be too.
[C1.6] Flexibility is not a euphemism for bias, as [P1.5] suggests; it is the mechanism by which real markets clear.
