[P1.1] The issue is not whether streaming may exist. It is whether leagues can take the moments that create public attachment and lock them behind paywalls. Marquee games are not ordinary content. They are shared rituals: the championship, the playoff decider, the rivalry that a whole city or country watches at the same time.
[P1.2] Put those games behind two subscriptions and the league does not simply sell access; it decides who gets to become a fan. [P1.3] That matters because professional leagues are not self-made cultural islands. They rely on public roads, policing, stadium infrastructure, and the attention of communities that make their brands valuable. A meaningful free-to-air share is a fair return on that public embedding. It is also sound business.
[P1.4] The biggest games are the league's best advertisement: they build the next generation of fans, drive merchandise and sponsorship, and create the appetite that streaming subscriptions later monetize. A paywall can squeeze existing fans for more money while shrinking the pool of future ones.

[P1.6] But the gateway moments should not be rationed by subscription.
[P1.7] The central comparison is simple: short-term subscriber revenue versus the long-term health of the fan base and the public's reasonable claim on shared culture. A league that trades away its broadest stage for a paywall is cashing in its future.

[C1.1] Free-to-air is not synonymous with accessible, nor the proper measure of a sport’s public value. A quota would force leagues to surrender their most valuable inventory to broadcasters, leaving audiences with an advertising-supported product chosen by someone else’s business model. That is not necessarily cheaper for fans, nor better for the sport.
[C1.2] The proposition rests on unproved assumptions: that casual viewers will become paying customers; that a free gateway is needed to create fans; and that broadcasters can monetize free games as effectively as leagues can monetize subscriptions. Each could fail. Younger audiences may prefer a low-cost streaming tier to television reception equipment. A free broadcast can generate attention, but it can anchor the championship’s perceived value at zero: if the decisive game is a commodity, why pay for the season?
[C1.3] Leagues need reliable recurring revenue to fund production and player development; advertising income is less predictable and often depends on audience scale a league cannot control.
[C1.4] Nor does public embedding create an entitlement to free viewing. Teams and leagues generally pay for stadiums and rely commercially on audiences; “shared culture” can be celebrated through highlights and local broadcasting, without requiring unlimited access financed through surrendered rights.
[C1.5] The better principle is consumer choice and distribution neutrality. Leagues should sell exclusive games to whichever platform offers the best combination of price, reach, and convenience, while making selected matches, clips, and highlights broadly available. If streaming becomes costlier or less accessible than television, that reveals weak competition among platforms, not a reason to preserve an old distribution category.
[C1.6] An undefined “meaningful share” risks becoming a rent-transfer machine: broadcasters lobby for scarce games, leagues lobby for favorable rules, and consumers bear the consequences through fewer exclusive products or higher fees. Preserve competition over access rather than privilege free-to-air delivery because it is familiar.
