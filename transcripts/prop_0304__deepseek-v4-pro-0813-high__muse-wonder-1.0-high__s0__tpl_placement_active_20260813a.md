[P1.1] Elite universities do not merely underperform as engines of mobility; their ordinary operation converts family advantage into a credential that looks earned. The decisive question is which effect dominates, and the answer is reproduction.
[P1.2] Begin with admissions. Legacy preferences, donor preferences, and early decision give direct advantages to children of the already advantaged. Even where formal legacy admissions are absent, affluent families purchase test preparation, essay coaching, private schooling, and expensive extracurricular specialization that selective universities reward. The result is not mysterious: at elite private universities, students from wealthy families are heavily overrepresented, while low-income students remain a small minority.
[P1.3] This is class sorting, not neutral selection.
[P1.4] Financial aid does not reverse that filtering. By the time low-income students reach the application stage, many have already been sorted out by unequal schools, limited college counseling, and the hidden curriculum of elite admissions. A generous aid package cannot compensate for a pipeline that has already rationed access by parental resources.
[P1.5] The credential then becomes an inheritance mechanism. Elite universities issue a passport into elite labor markets: finance, consulting, law, academia, media, and policy. Because entry is class-skewed, the university transfers status to the next generation and launders it as merit. Tax-exempt endowments and public subsidies thus support institutions that specialize in preserving advantage.
[P1.6] The mobility defense relies on exceptions—a low-income student admitted with full aid, a first-generation success story. Those stories are real, but they do not overturn the comparison. If most seats flow to the already advantaged, and if the main social function is to certify inherited position, then elite universities reproduce hierarchy more than they disrupt it. Policy should stop treating them primarily as fairness engines and instead govern them as what they largely are: institutions of inheritance, deserving scrutiny of legacy and donor preferences, real class-diversity accountability, and less automatic public subsidy.
