[P1.1] History classrooms decide which wars, conquests, and founding myths children inherit. When those decisions are left mainly to shifting political control, the textbook becomes an elected board’s commemorative message rather than a record of contested evidence. The narrative changes with the legislature: one cycle foregrounds forced removal and colonial atrocity, the next softens them as divisive or unpatriotic. A politically driven text can turn forced migration into mere relocation, colonial rule into modernization, or military failure into betrayal.
[P1.2] Students then receive radically different national histories within a decade, not because the archives changed but because the officeholders did.
[P1.3] Stronger independent scholarly review is the remedy. It subjects textbook claims to peer scrutiny, documentary testing, open disagreement, and revision when evidence fails. It does not promise neutrality; no account of a civil war or an empire is neutral. But it moves authority from raw majority power toward disciplined argument.
[P1.4] A review process shaped by working historians is less likely to erase civilian casualties, the facts of dispossession, or the role of forced labor in building national wealth. It is more likely to present national identity as a struggle over principles—revolution, equality, self-rule—and their repeated betrayal. That is not anti-national. It is the intellectual foundation for a civic identity that can withstand honest disagreement rather than collapse into cynicism when students later meet the excluded evidence.
[P1.5] The deeper choice is therefore between two filters. Political control filters history for loyalty and electoral convenience; scholarly review filters it for evidence, internal consistency, and explanatory power. For subjects as high-stakes as war, colonialism, and national identity, public schools need the stronger filter. A democracy should not train children to treat the latest political majority as their best available historian.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false rescue: it treats every democratic dispute over textbooks as corruption and every guild of historians as correction. But public schools are civic institutions, not archives. When communities decide how to narrate war, colonialism, and national identity, they are not only selecting facts; they are deciding what obligations the present inherits. That is a value judgment, and in a democracy it belongs to accountable representatives, not to an insulated panel that claims authority from expertise alone.
[C1.2] Stronger independent scholarly review sounds modest until it becomes a gate. Historians agree on many documents, but they disagree fiercely about emphasis and meaning: whether the atomic bombings ended a war or inaugurated terror, whether colonial railroads were infrastructure or extraction, whether a founding was liberation for some and dispossession for others. A “scholarly” filter can quietly elevate one interpretation by labeling rivals unscholarly. The result is not neutrality; it is professional pre-commitment, often more difficult to challenge because dissent must first pass the experts.
[C1.3] PRO’s real target is caricature: an elected board rewriting history for loyalty tests. We should reject that. But the alternative to abuse is not abdication. Open meetings, competing textbooks, teacher judgment, public comment, elections, and judicial review are messy, yet they let citizens contest the story in their name.
[C1.4] Political change can expose elite omissions as readily as political capture: immigrant, indigenous, regional, and religious communities have often won recognition by appealing to voters and legislatures against established academic consensus.
[C1.5] The deeper risk of PRO’s model is legitimacy. If students are taught that the official narrative comes from independent scholars rather than from a public process they can amend, they learn history as verdict, not argument. Better to let schools use politics openly—constrained by evidence rules and transparency—than to surrender contested memory to an unaccountable priesthood.
