[P1.1] Risk regulation exists to protect people, not corporate registries. When an algorithmic tool makes automated decisions—denying a mortgage, triaging an emergency room patient, or filtering job applicants—the harm is suffered where the decision takes effect. If a candidate in Lyon or Warsaw is unlawfully discriminated against by a biometric screening model, it matters not a whit whether the software’s parent company is headquartered in Paris, California, or Singapore. The injury occurs in the European Union, to a person under the protection of EU law.
[P1.2] Tying high-risk AI rules to corporate headquarters rather than where impacts materialize creates an indefensible governance failure on two fronts.
[P1.3] First, it leaves European residents defenseless against foreign harms. If the EU restricted high-risk obligations to EU-headquartered firms, offshore developers could deploy unsafe, un-audited systems across the single market with absolute impunity. The most consequential foundation models and predictive engines driving global tech do not originate within the bloc. An establishment-based regime would surrender jurisdiction precisely where the greatest systemic risks lie.
[P1.4] Second, it generates a perverse incentive for regulatory arbitrage. If compliance burdens attach to headquarters, every ambitious European tech firm faces a structural incentive to re-incorporate abroad. A brass-plate address in a lax jurisdiction would instantly liberate a developer from testing, transparency, and human-oversight mandates, while companies remaining in Europe bear the full regulatory cost. Rather than curbing risk, headquarters-based rules would simply export European enterprise while importing foreign, unregulated danger.
[P1.5] Europe’s modern legal tradition—from competition law’s effects doctrine to the territorial scope of data privacy—has long recognized that sovereignty requires regulating the impact within your borders, not the location of a boardroom. High-risk AI rules must follow that same ironclad principle: if your system shapes the rights, safety, and livelihoods of people in the European Union, you answer to European standards.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes the moral importance of harm for a workable legal trigger. Of course a Warsaw job applicant or a Lyon patient deserves protection. But “where impacts occur” is an outcome, not a jurisdictional handle. A single model can affect millions of people across dozens of countries in different ways; its impacts are diffuse, delayed, and often unknowable before deployment.
[C1.2] High-risk duties—conformity assessment, documentation, human oversight—must attach before a system goes live. A provider cannot certify compliance against a map of harms that has not yet been drawn. A regulator cannot audit a moving target. That is retrospective blame dressed up as prevention.
[C1.3] The headquarters-versus-impact framing is also a false choice. The serious alternative is market access and control: anyone placing an AI system on the EU market, or putting it into service here, answers to EU rules whatever its headquarters. That reaches foreign providers through authorized representatives, importers, deployers, and market-withdrawal powers. It does not require treating every downstream effect as a separate jurisdiction.
[C1.4] Impact-based scope would export chaos. A global model quoted in Brussels, or a foreign hospital tool that incidentally processes an EU traveller’s data, could be swept into Europe’s high-risk regime while also facing its home regulator. Faced with overlapping and unpredictable duties, providers will geoblock Europe or over-comply defensively—hurting the very patients and workers PRO invokes. And impact triggers are manipulable: a provider can deny foreseeable EU effects, fragment deployment, or design around the trigger, while enforcement arrives only after the harm.
[C1.5] PRO’s arbitrage worry is real, but it is solved by anti-circumvention rules and local representatives, not by impact. Their foreign-harm worry is solved by market-access conditions, not by impact. Harm should guide enforcement priorities; it should not define the scope of obligations that must be met ex ante. A rule that cannot identify its duty-holder until after the injury is not stronger protection.
[C1.6] It is a recipe for uncertainty, conflict, and delay.
