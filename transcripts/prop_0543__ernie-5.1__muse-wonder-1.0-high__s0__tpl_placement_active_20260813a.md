[P1.1] Every day, millions of minors open social media apps into a world designed to extract attention, harvest data, and expose them to strangers. The default settings on these platforms are not neutral—they are engineered for engagement, not safety. Privacy-by-design mandates simply flip the starting point: accounts private by default, minimal data collection, restricted contact from unknown adults, and age-appropriate content barriers. These are structural safeguards, not speech police.
[P1.2] The harm reduction is measurable and well-documented in broad terms. Predatory contact drops sharply when minors cannot be messaged by strangers. Cyberbullying decreases when audiences shrink by default. Data exploitation—the core revenue model for platforms targeting kids—shrinks when collection is curtailed at the design level.
[P1.3] When platforms have experimented with tighter teen defaults, internal metrics have consistently shown reduced negative interactions without users fleeing the platform.
[P1.4] Critically, this is not censorship. Privacy-by-design does not remove lawful content from public feeds. It does not tell a teenager what to post or what to read. It adjusts who can see them, who can contact them, and what data is stored.
[P1.5] A teenager can still express themselves publicly; the platform simply doesn't hand their location, contact list, and behavioral profile to advertisers and predators on a silver platter.
[P1.6] The market will not solve this. Platforms profit from permissive defaults. Parents cannot individually configure what should be a baseline floor of protection. Government mandates create that floor—the same way seatbelt laws protect children without banning cars.
[P1.7] The question is whether we accept a status quo that measurably harms kids, or adopt a narrow, technical standard that shifts defaults toward safety while leaving expression untouched.
