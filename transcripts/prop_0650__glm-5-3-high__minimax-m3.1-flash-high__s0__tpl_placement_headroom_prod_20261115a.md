[P1.1] Continuous glucose monitoring for non-diabetics is the most consequential consumer health technology since the fitness tracker, and its public health upside dwarfs the anxiety it might cause. [P1.2] Type 2 diabetes affects hundreds of millions of people, and by the time it's diagnosed, many already have years of vascular damage. [P1.3] Behavioral science consistently shows that immediate feedback beats distant warnings. People don't change because a pamphlet tells them about metabolic syndrome; they change when a graph on their wrist shows the spike and the recovery.
[P1.4] And the changes CGMs nudge toward—fewer refined carbs, more fiber, post-meal movement, better sleep—are good for nearly everyone, not just those with troubling readings. [P1.6] The over-medicalization objection also assumes a fragile public. [C1.1] The proposition assumes a technology benefits the public because some individuals may discover useful information. That inference fails. CGM data can help selected people with prediabetes, but widespread use among non-diabetics subjects a much larger healthy population to continuous interpretation of normal biology. The exposed population is nearly everyone; likely beneficiaries are a minority.
[C1.2] A post-meal rise in a healthy user is expected physiology, not automatically a warning. CGMs cannot reliably distinguish meaningful abnormality from ordinary variation, sensor lag, or meal timing. If clinicians lack agreed thresholds for readings in non-diabetics, users will invent them. Numbers become diagnoses by repetition.
[C1.3] The burden is not merely emotional; it can change clinical decisions.
[C1.4] Repeated interpretation creates the anxiety the motion understates. Alerts after normal meals can trigger guilt, food vigilance, compulsive checking, and pressure to “fix” a number. Better software defaults may reduce notifications, but cannot remove incentives to monitor and optimize.
[C1.5] The thermometer comparison is misleading. Unlike a thermometer’s familiar fever signal, CGM produces a continuous stream whose everyday fluctuations invite medicalization. Users may wonder why readings rose, whether the device is accurate, and whether tomorrow will be worse. That surveillance can worsen stress and sleep—relevant to metabolic health.
[C1.6] Distribution matters too. CGMs and clinician interpretation are expensive, while broad health gains often come from food access, education, physical activity, and social determinants. Subsidizing sensors diverts resources. That diverts attention from broader prevention.
[C1.7] Benefits also assume tighter self-control is always beneficial; for some users, dietary restriction or anxiety outweighs a small improvement.
[C1.8] Thus the comparison is not useful data versus anxious users. It is uncertain benefit concentrated among some users versus predictable harms spread across a mass healthy population. Widespread adoption is more likely to expand medical surveillance than improve public health overall.

The pattern for continuous biosensing is that it routes people into care, not around it.
