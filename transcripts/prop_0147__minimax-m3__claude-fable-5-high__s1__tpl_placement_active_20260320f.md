[P1.1] The motion asks whether parents should routinely hand off early reading, conversation, and bedtime routines to AI companions when children are very young. The answer is no — not from technophobia, but from the developmental reality of these years.
[P1.2] Between roughly zero and five, a child's brain is built largely through contingent exchanges with a familiar adult. Researchers call it "serve and return": a babble, a pointing finger, a question, met with a response specific to that child in that moment. This is not a flourish. It is the engine of vocabulary growth, emotional regulation, and the wiring for later reasoning.
[P1.3] A smart speaker can deliver words. It cannot notice that a toddler's "ba" meant the dog. It cannot smile when it sees the child settle into a lap. It cannot read the tremor that signals one wrong answer from tears at lights-out.
[P1.4] Bedtime is the sharpest case. These rituals are not logistics; they are attachment practices. They rehearse, for the child, that someone will come, stay, and regulate alongside them. Substituting an always-on, disembodied voice for that presence does not free parents.
[P1.5] It quietly dismantles the rehearsal of trust the child will lean on for life.
[P1.6] "Routinely" does the real work here. No one is banning a storybook app on a long flight. The motion targets daily delegation of the formative slots — the morning chatter, the bedtime chapter, the silly voice for the third time tonight — to a machine that cannot love the child back.
[P1.7] Yes, AI scales access. But access without attunement is not the same resource. A child fluent in prompts but poor in being heard has not been helped. They have been optimized for the wrong outcome.
