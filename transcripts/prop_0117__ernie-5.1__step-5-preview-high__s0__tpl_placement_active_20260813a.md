[P1.1] The death penalty is a state‑sanctioned killing that can never be undone, and no crime—however monstrous—justifies entrusting a government with that final, irreversible power. When a terrorist detonates a bomb or a mass murderer opens fire, the urge for retribution is understandable, but a system that executes the guilty also inevitably executes the innocent. In the United States, dozens of people on death row have been exonerated by DNA evidence after decades of incarceration; one well‑known case involved a man convicted of arson who was later shown to be innocent of the fire that killed his children.
[P1.2] The same procedural pressures that produce wrongful convictions in ordinary homicide cases are amplified in terror and mass‑murder trials, where media frenzy and political pressure push prosecutors toward the harshest possible charge.
[P1.3] Deterrence, the chief justification for keeping capital punishment for the worst offenses, simply does not hold up. Comparative data from countries that abolished the death penalty—such as Canada, the United Kingdom, and Germany—show no surge in terrorism or mass‑murder rates after abolition. Norway’s response to the 2011 Breivik attack, a life sentence without parole, demonstrated that society can achieve accountability and closure without executions, and the nation’s cohesion was not weakened.
[P1.4] Beyond efficacy, the death penalty undermines the moral authority that a democratic state needs to confront extremism. Executions become propaganda tools for terrorist recruiters, who frame the state as brutal and unjust, fueling further radicalisation. Moreover, capital punishment is applied unevenly, disproportionately targeting marginalized groups and political dissidents, eroding the rule of law it purports to protect.
[P1.5] A principled abolition—covering every crime, no exceptions—preserves the integrity of justice, removes the risk of irreversible error, and denies extremists the narrative of a bloodthirsty state. Life imprisonment without the possibility of parole delivers certainty, protects public safety, and upholds the higher standard that a just society must set.
