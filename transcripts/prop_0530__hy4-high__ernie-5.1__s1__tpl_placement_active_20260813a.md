[P1.1] The principle is dignified truth. These are not ordinary digs or neutral attractions. A mass grave is a broken funeral; a forced-labor site is a landscape where people were turned into tools; an atrocity site often holds those denied burial, names, and mourning. To excavate, display, and explain is to decide again how the dead will be treated in public.
[P1.2] Those who inherit that relation—descendant communities—need substantial authority over the decision.
[P1.3] Substantial control means binding say over disturbing bodies, photographing remains, removal or reburial, names and languages, ceremonies, and the framing of coercion and responsibility. It is not a veto over facts or a licence for comforting myths. Conservation and forensic standards still govern evidence. But technique cannot settle dignity: whether a child’s shoe belongs in a case, whether skulls are lined up for visitors, whether mugshots turn victims into specimens again.
[P1.4] These are value choices, and the community carrying the injury has the strongest claim to make them.
[P1.5] The claim also improves accuracy. Descendant memory can preserve what official archives suppressed: burial customs, kinship links, local place-names, transfer routes, distinctions among prisoners and neighbours, or unmarked grave locations. A skilled team can misread a site without that knowledge. Worse, states, universities, developers, and museums have often been the institutions that reduced the dead to numbers, displayed them as curiosities, or used ruins for flattering national stories.
[P1.6] Calling them the only neutral guardians ignores the conflict of interest in the history.
[P1.7] So the comparison is not community control versus truth. It is accountable stewardship versus a repeat of the old asymmetry: outsiders extract bodies and meanings, then label the result public education. Public education is legitimate only when it avoids a second dispossession. Control may make excavation slower and display more restrained—but that is the point.
[P1.8] At these sites, care is not an obstacle to knowledge; it is a condition of trustworthy knowledge.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds compassionate but is structurally dangerous. "Substantial control" means binding authority over excavation, display, and interpretation—and that authority, handed to descendant communities as a bloc, creates three serious problems that no amount of good intention solves.
[C1.2] First, descendant communities are not monolithic. They fracture along generation, gender, class, and political line. In post-conflict settings from Rwanda to Bosnia to the American South, survivor groups have bitterly disagreed about whether to exhume, whether to name perpetrators, whether to build museums at all. Granting substantial control means one faction can silence others within the very community the proposition claims to empower.
[C1.3] The result is not dignity but a new tyranny of the majority inside a grieving population.
[C1.4] Second, these sites are evidence of crimes with universal significance. Forensic teams need independent access to bodies, stratigraphy, and context to establish what happened and who is accountable. When a community with substantial control decides certain graves stay sealed or certain interpretations get suppressed, it doesn't protect the dead—it buries truth under the guise of protecting it. The forensic work at Srebrenica, for instance, depended on independence from local political pressure; compromise there would have meant impunity dressed as respect.
[C1.5] Third, the proposition ignores what happens when control becomes a tool. Post-conflict elites and local powerbrokers have used "community authority" to whitewash atrocities, redirect blame, or prevent sites from implicating their own side. Substantial control hands them a laundering mechanism, turning memorial sites into monuments to selective memory rather than honest reckoning.
[C1.6] The answer is not exclusion but genuine partnership: mandatory consultation, shared governance, independent scientific oversight. That preserves dignity without surrendering truth to whoever holds local power. The question is not whether descendant voices matter—they do—but whether "substantial control" is the right mechanism. It is not.
