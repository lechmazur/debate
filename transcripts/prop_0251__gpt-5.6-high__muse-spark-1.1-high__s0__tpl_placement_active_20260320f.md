[P1.1] Extreme heat is not merely weather; at work, it is an occupational hazard controlled largely by the employer. Workers rarely choose the shift, pace, protective clothing, ventilation, or access to shade. When the body cannot shed heat, impairment can quickly become collapse or lasting injury. This belongs in labor law because ordinary market choice fails precisely when danger peaks.
[P1.2] Mandatory stop-work protections should create an enforceable duty to suspend labor when objective heat-risk thresholds or serious warning signs are reached, without retaliation against workers. That need not mean one universal temperature. Standards can account for humidity, radiant heat, workload, protective equipment, acclimatization, and indoor versus outdoor conditions. The nonnegotiable principle is simple: at severe risk, production cannot outrank bodily safety.
[P1.3] The first reason is unequal bargaining power. A farmworker, roofer, warehouse employee, or delivery driver may technically be able to refuse, but refusal can mean lost wages, fewer shifts, or dismissal. Managers receive the benefit of continued output while workers bear much of the medical risk. Employers who voluntarily pause can also be undercut by competitors who do not.
[P1.4] A mandatory floor prevents responsible firms from being punished for protecting people.
[P1.5] Second, heat illness can escalate rapidly while degrading judgment. Requiring workers to recognize their own impairment and negotiate a break is therefore too late. Stop-work rules operate like machine lockouts: once exposure becomes unacceptably dangerous, work pauses until safeguards restore tolerable conditions. Businesses can adapt through earlier shifts, rotations, cooling areas, added breaks, and contingency planning.
[P1.6] Those manageable costs are preferable to emergency treatment, disability, and preventable death.
[P1.7] Thresholds will never be perfect, but uncertainty is an argument for well-designed, multi-factor rules—not for leaving vulnerable workers unprotected. Essential services can use special contingency protocols and replacement staffing; “essential” cannot mean expendable.
[P1.8] The decisive principle is responsibility: whoever controls the conditions and profits from the labor must bear the duty to make that labor safe. Without an enforceable stop-work right, economic vulnerability becomes coerced exposure. Labor rights exist precisely to prevent that bargain.
