[P1.1] A family vlog is not a photo album. It is a broadcast business whose main character never signed anything. A toddler's meltdown, a bedwetting confession, a humiliation staged for thumbnails — titled with a real first name, monetized, and left online forever. The only person in that transaction who never consented is the person whose life it is.
[P1.2] Right now the parent is both the guardian obliged to protect the child's privacy and the producer who profits from exposing it. When those roles collide, nobody in the household represents the child. Takedown complaints route to the account holder — that same parent. That is not protection; it is a conflict of interest with a comment section.
[P1.3] Adolescence is when this becomes urgent. At fifteen you can finally name the harm, and the harm is searchable: classmates, coaches, admissions officers and employers meet the archived six-year-old before they meet you. Embarrassment that once faded now has a permalink.
[P1.4] Notice the absurdity in existing law. Several jurisdictions let teenagers erase posts they made themselves, while the far more exposing material a parent posted about them is untouchable. The content the child never chose is precisely the content they cannot remove.
[P1.5] The remedy is modest: a right to request, not an automatic veto. Notice, a parental response, weighing the child's stated harm against genuine expressive or documentary value, then deletion or de-identification where the child's interest wins. Family albums stay. News reporting stays.
[P1.6] What changes is that whoever monetized a child's identity must answer when that child asks.
[P1.7] Parental authority exists to serve children, not to license permanent exposure of them. Give the one person who never got a vote a vote — at the age they can finally cast it.

[C2.5] Yes, exploitatively sexualized or coercive content should be removed under laws aimed at exploitation. Family negotiation, platform privacy settings, and targeted intervention protect without making every shared memory legally provisional.
