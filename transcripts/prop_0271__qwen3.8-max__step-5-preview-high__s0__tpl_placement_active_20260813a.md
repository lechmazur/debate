[P1.1] The proposition is not about banning recommendations; it is about who gets the decisive vote when a child's device is tuned by an algorithm. Today, app makers give parents only pseudo-choices. A parent can often block an app entirely, but not the engagement engine inside it. If the only options are “give my child a social app with a personalized feed” or “deny the app,” consent is coerced by design.
[P1.2] Parents should be able to switch recommendations off, delay them until a child is older, or require explicit approval before a profile is shaped by behavioral data.
[P1.3] The central clash is authority. Children are not ordinary consumers. Their impulse control, susceptibility to social comparison, and sensitivity to intermittent rewards are still developing. Recommendation systems are built to exploit exactly those vulnerabilities: infinite scroll, autoplay, and feeds that learn what holds attention, often pushing more extreme or emotionally charged content because it boosts time-on-device.
[P1.4] The company's incentive is engagement and ad revenue; the parent's incentive is sleep, safety, and healthy development. When those incentives collide, the party with long-term responsibility should outrank the party with quarterly targets.
[P1.5] Opponents will say parents can simply supervise or say no. But supervision cannot compete with billions of A/B-tested interfaces, dark patterns, and network effects that make a child’s app environment addictive by default. More parental power means enforceable, granular controls at the device or operating-system level: a real off switch for algorithmic feeds, age-based delays, and no retaliation by locking children out of basic communication or school tools. That turns parental authority from a slogan into a practical mechanism.
[P1.6] The decisive ground is simple: if a feature is powerful enough to shape a child's attention, emotions, and worldview, the default should not be set by the company selling the child's engagement. Parents deserve more power, because children deserve more protection.

[C1.5] Finally, empowering individual parents as the primary safety mechanism shifts responsibility away from the party that actually has the data, engineering capacity, and legal liability to fix platform-wide harms.
