[P1.1] Cash gives citizens something no bank account has ever offered: the ability to hand a stranger value without asking anyone's permission. A CBDC sold to the public as "digital cash" is a lie if it strips that property away and leaves only the surveillance. That is why the requirement matters: anonymity below a fixed legal threshold preserves what is worth preserving about cash while conceding what is genuinely dangerous about it.
[P1.2] Start with the substitution logic. If the state retires banknotes, it has disabled a capability people held before the reform. That is a taking, and it demands justification. "We need to stop crime" is not a justification for making every grocery purchase, every rent split, every gift to a teenager permanently legible to a database.
[P1.3] Criminals use cars; we do not require odometers to transmit to the state. The burden falls on whoever wants to abolish a freedom, not on citizens to prove they deserve one.
[P1.4] Second, the unbanked and the vulnerable are the ones who lose most. People without stable identification, undocumented workers, refugees, abuse victims escaping a controlling partner who monitors their accounts — for them, cash is not nostalgia, it is exit. A mandatory, fully identified CBDC closes that exit and hands the tracking capacity to whoever compromises the system or inherits it. Historical registries of financial transactions are exactly the infrastructure that authoritarian successors reach for first.
[P1.5] Third, anonymity below a threshold is not lawlessness. The threshold — say, a few hundred currency units per transaction, with cumulative caps and tiered balances — is the compromise. Money laundering, tax evasion, and trafficking move sums that dwarf such limits and remain fully visible. Law enforcement keeps its tools; ordinary privacy keeps its floor.
[P1.6] Finally, this is technically achievable today. Zero-knowledge proofs, blind signatures, and tiered wallet designs let a central bank verify that a note is authentic and unspent without learning who spent it. The question is not whether the state can design privacy in. It is whether it wants to.
