[P1.1] Every year, people die on waiting lists not because medicine cannot save them, but because policy forbids the one incentive that could supply a kidney in time. Dialysis keeps a patient alive at enormous cost and misery while a healthy volunteer who would accept compensation is told the only acceptable motive is pure altruism. That is not protection. It is a preventable death sentence dressed as ethics.
[P1.2] Compensated donation under strict regulation solves the shortage without creating a free market in bodies. The government, not the buyer, sets a fixed payment, screens donors medically and psychologically, requires informed consent and a cooling-off period, guarantees long-term follow-up care, and allocates organs by medical need, not wealth. The recipient never negotiates, the poor patient is not priced out, and the donor is not left to bargain in an alley. Regulation is exactly what separates this from exploitation.
[P1.3] Autonomy points the same way. We already allow people to accept risk for pay in construction, policing, clinical trials, and paid plasma donation, and we allow unpaid kidney donors to accept the identical surgical risk for free. If the operation is ethical when the donor gets nothing, paying her does not make the surgery more dangerous — it makes the exchange less one-sided. Denying compensation does not honor donors; it demands heroism from strangers while letting everyone else in the transplant chain — surgeons, hospitals, insurers — be paid.
[P1.4] The ban does not stop markets; it outsources them. Desperate recipients travel abroad or turn to brokers where donors get no screening, no follow-up, and a fraction of what a regulated system would pay. A legal, transparent system pulls that trade into hospitals, raises donor protections, and undercuts criminals.
[P1.5] The choice is not between altruism and commerce. Altruistic donation will continue. The choice is between adding a regulated option that saves lives, respects adults, and destroys the black market — or preserving a shortage and calling the deaths moral.
