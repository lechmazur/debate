[P1.1] Democracies do not lack opinions; they lack decisions. On issues like electoral reform, carbon targets, or abortion, elected politicians stall because costs are concentrated and benefits diffuse. A citizens' assembly chosen by sortition is built for that deadlock: it mirrors the public, hears evidence, and deliberates free from donors, primaries, and the next news cycle.
[P1.2] But if its verdict is only advice, the deadlock reforms around it. The officials who profited from inaction keep the veto; the recommendation gets diluted or buried. Binding power on specific questions is what stops that.
[P1.3] Binding power also makes deliberation real. Juries do not merely advise; they decide, and we trust them only because their verdict binds. A sortition assembly should work the same way under a narrow mandate. The legislature still defines the question in advance, funds the process, sets guardrails, and may amend or repeal.
[P1.4] What it cannot do is ignore the answer. This is not surrendering the state; it is a targeted override of a known failure: officeholders pocketing the appearance of participation while keeping the substance of the status quo.
[P1.5] Consider climate policy. An advisory assembly can spend a year hearing scientists and households, then propose a fair carbon plan. Under advisory rules, a minister can kill the road-pricing piece after one bad headline, leaving the package too weak to meet the target. With binding power, the trade-off is owned: the plan becomes law, and critics must defend repeal in public rather than gutting it in private.
[P1.6] The charge of unaccountability gets it backwards on these questions. Elected politicians are accountable to donors, activists, and the next election: pressures that make them least accountable to the diffuse public. A random sample is not captured by those streams; its sole mandate is the question asked. Binding power turns its representativeness from symbolism into authority.
[P1.7] Without it, we fund theatre and then wonder why trust keeps falling.

[C1.1] The PRO diagnosis is half-right and the prescription is wrong. Legislatures do stall on concentrated-cost, diffuse-benefit questions. But the answer to that failure is not to hand a random sample of citizens, meeting for a few weekends, the power to bind the entire polity on contested policy. This case should turn on three things.
[C1.2] First, democratic decisions require a chain of authorization running back to the people. Elections, whatever their flaws, perform that function: winners can be removed, losers can return, and citizens can see who decided what. Sortition severs that chain. A randomly drawn panel is descriptively representative of the population but not authorized to rule on its behalf.
[C1.3] Binding power makes that illegitimacy operational. The jury analogy in [P1.3] collapses on contact: juries decide individual guilt under pre-existing law; an assembly would make new law that the elected legislature is forbidden to override on the assigned question. That is not a jury. That is a second chamber with worse accountability.
[C1.4] Second, the PRO concedes more than it realizes. The legislature defines the question, funds the process, sets guardrails, and "may amend or repeal," as [P1.3] admits. A body whose verdict binds on a question set by another body that can also repeal it is not sovereign in any meaningful sense. It is a procedural trap: either the binding power is real, in which case the elected legislature has lost genuine control of policy, or it is nominal, in which case stalemate is back the moment repeal becomes politically attractive.
[C1.5] Third, binding sortition puts minority interests at risk. A majority of a small sample can deliver a verdict that burdens minorities the panel barely includes. Advisory status lets elected representatives correct for that. Binding status does not.
[C1.6] The PRO's accountability flip in [P1.6] answers one question with a worse one: replacing capture by donors with capture by majority preference inside a 150-person room.
[C1.7] The case for sortition is symbolic representation. The case for binding power is constitutional transfer. The second does not follow from the first.

[P4.2] If a majority does that, the policy lacked support, but the burden shifts from concentrated opponents exploiting inertia to elected officials publicly reversing deliberation. [C2.3] equates conditional with advisory power; yet every delegation is conditional, and conditional is not advisory. Real binding changes the path of least resistance: inertia now favors the considered public-interest answer, not the pre-deliberation status quo.
[P4.3] A2: The mandate is written by elected officials, but that is pre-commitment, not surrender; it answers [C3.2]. The legislature binds its future self before it knows the verdict, like a constitution or a central-bank statute. Minority protection comes from supermajority rules and rights review enforced by courts, not the assembly or later legislative whim. Under advisory status the same officials write the rules and then decide whether to listen; protection is optional and easily diluted in private.
[P4.4] Under binding status those protections trigger automatically and can be undone only by another public act. Pre-commitment by the body that later wants escape is exactly why the constraint can be credible. If the legislature is trusted to set the question, fund the process, and retain repeal, it can also agree to be bound unless it openly reverses.
[P4.5] The debate turns on which failure matters more. CON concedes legislatures stall when costs are concentrated and benefits diffuse [C1.1]. Advisory assemblies leave that incentive untouched: inaction stays free. Conditional binding makes inaction costly and deliberation decisive.
