[C1.2] Consumer prices measure a nationwide basket of goods and services. House prices mix land, zoning, tax preference, migration, construction bottlenecks, and credit. When a productive, supply-constrained city becomes expensive, that is not automatically a monetary failure. [C1.3] Dual targets collide, and the collision is political: owners want support, outsiders want cheaper entry. There is no neutral “stable” house price without choosing winners.
[C1.4] The credit-housing loop is real, but it does not justify rewriting the mandate. Loan-to-value caps, debt-service limits, and sectoral capital charges already restrain leverage without using the policy rate as a house-price lever. [C1.5] Nor can officials define “asset-price stability” in real time. Price-to-income and price-to-rent stay high for years where supply is blocked or where rates fall for good reasons. [C1.6] Cleanup after a bust is costly. A standing second target is costlier, because inflation and house prices routinely diverge. The first serious clash will force the bank to abandon one objective or the other. If shelter is mis-measured in the CPI, fix the index.
[C1.7] Do not convert a measurement gap into a housing mandate. Predictability comes from hierarchy: money for consumer prices, credit rules for leverage. Adding housing does not make busts cheaper. It makes the inflation target less credible and the central bank easier to capture.
