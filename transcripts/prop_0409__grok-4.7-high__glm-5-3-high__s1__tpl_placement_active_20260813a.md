[P1.1] Public parks, beaches, and waterfronts are the last large pieces of civic life that do not require a purchase to enter. Sell access and the space stops being public. It becomes a venue. That is the conversion this motion resists.
[P1.2] Keeping them broadly free is first about who belongs. A promenade anyone can walk, a beach a family can reach with a cooler and no reservation, a park with no ticket at the gate: these are places where income does not decide participation. Paid enclosure sorts people by ability to pay. The households least able to buy recreation elsewhere lose the recreation meant to be theirs.
[P1.3] Children without memberships, workers between shifts, and residents on fixed incomes get no substitute. They get less of the city.
[P1.4] Free access is how these places do their public work. They mix strangers and give dense neighborhoods room to breathe. Charge for entry and use falls among the groups policy most wants outside. Cities already fund streets, libraries, and schools without selling admission.
[P1.5] Parks and shores can be kept the same way — taxes, levies, and concessions that sell food without owning the shore. Hand the space to operators whose revenue depends on exclusion and a public good becomes a private product.
[P1.6] Pricing does not manage crowds fairly. It rations by wealth, not by need or time. A neighbor who cannot pay the day rate is shut out so a visitor who can is not. Capacity limits and free timed entry do the job without turning a commons into a club.
[P1.7] Enclosure is already advancing: festival fences on public lawns, resort control of once-open beaches, paths broken by ticketed piers and members-only decks. Each step is called temporary and better maintained. Together they shrink what remains free. Openness is the point of public space.
[P1.8] Enclosure does not improve the commons. It ends it.

[C2.2] Take the incentive argument seriously for a moment. PRO says an operator whose revenue depends on exclusion will fence more, not less [P2.4]. But operators depend on *licenses*, and licenses are renewable, priced, and conditioned. Cities auction the right to run premium experiences precisely because enclosure has value — and spend that leverage on free access, maintenance, and programming.
[C2.3] Public control doesn't vanish when money changes hands; it's often strengthened, because for once the space generates revenue the city can leverage. A park that costs the budget money every year gets defended in every budget fight. A park that helps fund itself gets defended by everyone.
[C2.4] The streets-and-schools analogy [P2.3] actually cuts the other way. We don't sell admission to streets — but we do sell exclusive use of them constantly: film permits, marathons, street festivals, market stalls, advertising. Streets remain public because the *default* is open and the *exceptions* are temporary, priced, and regulated. That is precisely the layered model.
[C2.5] PRO's rule would have to close every farmers' market that blocks a public road.
[C2.6] The ratchet claim [P2.5] — one weekend becomes a season, the cabanas take the shade — is not an argument, it's a slope. The same logic would ban weddings in parks because someone once fenced a lawn badly. Bad contracts are a reason for better contracts, not for starving public space of revenue.
[C2.7] And notice what PRO never answers: the degraded free beach. "A failure of public finance" [P2.3] is a description, not a remedy. Telling the family on a fixed income that the shore is *theoretically* theirs while the restrooms are closed and the pier is condemned is cold comfort. A funded, maintained commons with a paid layer beats a pure, crumbling one.
