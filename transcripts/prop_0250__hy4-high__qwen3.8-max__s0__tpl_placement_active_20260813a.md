[P1.1] Stable democracies do not merely count ballots; they owe citizens an electorate that resembles the public. Optional turnout does not produce a neutral sample. It filters participation through time, money, transport, age, health, and alienation. The retired homeowner is easier to mobilize than the night-shift renter; the confident graduate is likelier to feel invited into politics.
[P1.2] Campaigns then chase reliable voters, and policy follows their preferences. That is government by the most available, not representative self-government.
[P1.3] Mandatory turnout breaks that feedback loop. A legal duty to attend a poll or return a ballot, backed by modest civil penalties, makes participation a universal civic act, comparable to jury service or tax filing. The duty is to participate, not to support a party: secret ballots, blank ballots, and "none of the above" where available protect conscience. Proportionality is built into the motion: a small income-sensitive fine, generous excuses, easy voting access, and no criminal sanction keep the response administrative, not punitive.
[P1.4] The aim is not a fetish for high percentages, but composition. When nearly everyone must show up, parties cannot write off irregular voters; low-turnout primaries and local elections become less distorting; politicians must address the young tenant, the non-college worker, and the busy parent as part of the governing coalition. Picture a city council chosen mainly by older homeowners while shift-working renters stay home: zoning and budgets can proceed as if those residents hardly exist. Mandatory participation makes their absence harder to translate into policy.
[P1.5] The liberty objection is asymmetrical. One abstention is harmless; mass abstention by predictable groups transfers power to others. A stable democracy with courts, secret ballots, and competent administration can protect dissent while refusing to let unequal participation define "the people." The fine is not revenge on nonvoters; it is the modest price of keeping the whole demos visible.
[P1.6] Representative outcomes require a representative electorate, and leaving that electorate to private circumstance is the deeper coercion.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes presence for representation. A democracy is legitimate because citizens are free to govern themselves, and that freedom includes refusing to endorse any candidate, party, or ballot measure. Abstention, blank ballots, and nonparticipation can be political judgments: distrust, alienation, conscientious objection, or satisfaction. Turning that silence into a state-enforced appearance does not make the electorate more authentic; it manufactures consent by penalizing the choice to stay home.
[C1.2] PRO’s core promise—that fines produce representative outcomes—does not follow. Compelling attendance does not compel informed preference. It floods elections with low-information, arbitrary, or protest votes; candidates may still target mobilizable blocs; and governments can claim inflated mandates while actual engagement falls. If the goal is to make shift workers and renters count, forcing them to cast ballots under threat of fine does not give them power.
[C1.3] It gives administrators a bigger list of participants and parties an incentive to manipulate superficial turnout.
[C1.4] The penalties also fall hardest on the people PRO claims to protect. A proportional fine may sound modest, but any penalty for nonvoting is regressive in practice: it burdens precarious workers, caregivers, disabled voters, new residents, and those confused by bureaucracy. Stable democracies already know the remedy for unequal participation is access, not coercion: automatic registration, early voting, mail ballots, polling places, and civic education. If those tools work, compulsion is unnecessary.
[C1.5] If they fail, fining citizens for structural barriers is perverse.
[C1.6] Finally, mandatory turnout corrupts the meaning of electoral mandates. A government elected under universal participation could claim overwhelming turnout while many voters submitted blank or random ballots merely to avoid a fine. That weakens, not strengthens, democratic accountability. The proper standard is not whether every eligible person appears at a polling station, but whether citizens can participate freely and meaningfully when they choose.
[C1.7] Coerced turnout is a bureaucratic substitute for genuine democratic inclusion.
