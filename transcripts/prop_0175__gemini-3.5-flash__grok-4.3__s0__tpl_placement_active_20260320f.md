[P1.1] Occupational licensing exists to protect the public from serious, irreversible harm. We license brain surgeons and commercial pilots because a mistake can be fatal. But when we apply this heavy-handed apparatus to low-risk, everyday trades like interior design, hair braiding, and simple home repairs, the justification of "public safety" collapses. In these fields, licensing requirements do not protect consumers; they protect established insiders from competition.
[P1.2] Consider the sheer disproportion of these rules. In many jurisdictions, a hair braider—performing a culturally traditional, entirely non-chemical technique—must complete up to 2,000 hours of generic cosmetology training. This schooling is expensive, time-consuming, and teaches skills irrelevant to braiding. Similarly, interior designers have lobbied to restrict the simple act of space planning and furniture selection to those with four-year degrees and state-mandated apprenticeships.
[P1.3] The mechanism here is economic protectionism, not risk mitigation. By raising the cost of entry, incumbents limit the supply of competitors. This creates an artificial scarcity that allows established practitioners to charge higher prices at the direct expense of the consumer.
[P1.4] The consequence of this barrier is deeply regressive. It shuts out low-income entrepreneurs, immigrants, and marginalized workers who possess the practical skills to work but lack the capital to navigate redundant, expensive schooling. If safety were the true goal, states would rely on targeted, low-barrier tools like voluntary certification, basic sanitation registries, or standard consumer protection laws.
[P1.5] When the state mandates hundreds of hours of irrelevant training for low-risk work, it is not saving lives. It is locking the gates of opportunity to protect the balance sheets of those already inside. For these trades, licensing is a barrier to entry, pure and simple.

[C1.1] Licensing for trades like hair braiding and interior design exists because even routine work carries concrete risks of lasting physical harm. Interior design decisions touch electrical loads, egress routes, and material flammability, where errors have produced documented building violations and injury claims. These are not hypothetical dangers invented to exclude newcomers.
[C1.2] The training hours cited by opponents are not generic padding. State cosmetology curricula embed infection-control protocols, tool sterilization, and client-assessment steps that directly address the transmission pathways in braiding. Design licensing tests require familiarity with accessibility standards and load-bearing constraints that prevent later retrofits or liability suits. Removing these gates would shift the cost of mistakes onto consumers who lack the expertise to spot substandard work until damage appears.
[C1.3] Economic protectionism is real in isolated cases, yet it does not erase the safety function. Markets respond to fraud after harm occurs; licensing deters it beforehand by raising the reputational and financial penalty for operating without demonstrated competence. Voluntary certification fails for the same reason most voluntary standards do: it leaves informed-consent problems unsolved when the typical client cannot evaluate technical quality in advance.
[C1.4] The proposition treats every entry requirement as presumptively illegitimate unless it matches the risk profile of brain surgery. That standard ignores the cumulative, preventable harms that accumulate across thousands of daily transactions in lower-stakes fields. Licensing calibrates competence thresholds to those harms rather than to an abstract ideal of zero barriers.
