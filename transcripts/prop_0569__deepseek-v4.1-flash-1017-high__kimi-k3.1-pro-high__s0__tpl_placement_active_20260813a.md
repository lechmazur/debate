[P1.1] A chatbot can be a front door to care. It should not be a locked room. The proposition is modest: after a fixed number of sessions, an AI-delivered mental-health service must hand the patient to a human professional—not necessarily end treatment, but ensure a qualified person reviews the case and decides what happens next.
[P1.2] The core danger is indefinite, unsupervised automation. Chatbots can be useful for screening, coping skills, and low-intensity support, but they do not reliably recognize when a presentation has changed: the eating disorder hiding behind “healthy habits,” the trauma response that looks like resistance, the manic confidence that sounds like progress, or the self-harm disclosure buried in session twelve. Without a mandatory checkpoint, no clinician is ever obligated to see that trajectory. The operator can keep the user engaged, and the user can keep confiding, while accountability stays diffuse.
[P1.3] A fixed handoff creates a legal floor, not a clinical ceiling. It forces the system to produce a record, flag risk, and expose the case to someone with licensure, duties, and liability. A human can then say: continue AI support, step up to live therapy, or escalate urgently. The number may be imperfect, just as speed limits and mandatory review periods are imperfect.
[P1.4] The alternative is not individualized perfection; it is allowing companies to decide, case by case and profit by profit, whether a human ever enters the room.
[P1.5] Nor does this ban innovation. It channels AI where it is strongest—access, triage, between-session support—while refusing to let automation become the permanent owner of a vulnerable person’s care. If a service cannot arrange a human review after a defined course of sessions, it is not offering therapy. It is offering a relationship-shaped product with no one accountable when it fails.
