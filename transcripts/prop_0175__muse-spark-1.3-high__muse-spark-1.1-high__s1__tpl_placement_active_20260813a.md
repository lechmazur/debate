[P1.1] Watch what licensing actually demands. To braid hair legally in several states, a Black woman who has braided safely for years was told to complete a thousand-plus hours of cosmetology school — learning chemical dyes, perms, and scissors she would never use — pay thousands in tuition, then pass exams about those chemicals. To call yourself an interior designer in a few states, you need an accredited degree, years of supervised work, and a multi-part exam, even if you only pick paint colors and arrange furniture.
[P1.2] No buildings move, no wires are touched.
[P1.3] Louisiana famously required a license to arrange flowers, with a practical exam that failed most takers — as if a bad bouquet were a public health emergency. Casket sellers, shampooers, eyebrow threaders have faced similar gates.
[P1.4] That mismatch is the tell. Real safety regulation targets a specific, plausible harm with proportionate training. These regimes do the opposite: sweeping, expensive, one-size credentials for low-risk work where injuries are rare, minor, and already governed by general health and sanitation law.
[P1.5] Who benefits from that mismatch? Not consumers. Comparisons across states consistently find no meaningful safety dividend where these licenses exist compared to where they do not. Complaint rates do not fall.
[P1.6] Infections do not fall. What does change is supply: fewer entrants, higher prices, longer waits — textbook incumbent protection.
[P1.7] And the burden is not neutral. The costs land hardest on immigrants, low-income women, and skilled practitioners who learned through apprenticeship or community tradition rather than formal school. A fluently skilled braider becomes an outlaw; a licensed salon owner who lobbied for the rule becomes the gatekeeper who gets to charge more.
[P1.8] When a law blocks competent people from harmless work without making the public safer, calling it protection is wordplay. The primary function is economic: raise the drawbridge, dress exclusion as care.

[C1.5] If licensing were primarily an incumbent cartel, incumbents would exempt themselves. They don't. They pay fees, face inspections, lose their own licenses for violations. Prices rise modestly because meeting a floor costs something — just as restaurants pay for food safety.
[C1.6] The low complaint rate PRO celebrates after deregulation is not evidence of no need; it is evidence of invisible harm where consumers never connect infection to the shop, or poor women simply absorb the loss.
[C1.7] A barrier that forces competence before you touch someone else's body or the public's building is doing its primary job.
