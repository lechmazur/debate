[P1.1] Wars of the twenty-first century are being decided by arithmetic, and America's arithmetic is upside down. In the Red Sea, U.S. destroyers have spent interceptors costing on the order of a million dollars or more to knock down Houthi drones that cost a few tens of thousands. In Ukraine, cheap first-person-view drones built in workshops destroy tanks worth millions and have made much of the front impassable. In a Taiwan Strait fight, the decisive question would be who runs out of munitions and platforms first.
[P1.2] A force built entirely on exquisite platforms is a force with a shallow magazine and no way to refill it.
[P1.3] Three reasons the shift is worth the tradeoff.
[P1.4] First, the cost-exchange ratio. China can build ships and missiles faster and cheaper than we can. If our answer is fewer, more expensive platforms, we are choosing to lose the exchange. An adversary that must spend a multi-million-dollar missile on a thirty-thousand-dollar decoy is bleeding; an adversary that can spend it on a carrier is winning.
[P1.5] Attritable mass flips who bleeds.
[P1.6] Second, concentration is vulnerability. When each aircraft carrier costs over ten billion and takes a decade, losing one is a strategic catastrophe. That makes commanders cautious, which means the exquisite fleet ends up being preserved rather than used. Attritable systems can be sent into contested airspace, lost, and replaced.
[P1.7] A platform you cannot afford to risk is a platform you cannot afford to fight with.
[P1.8] Third, industrial resilience. Exquisite platforms depend on a handful of prime contractors, years-long production lines, and parts that cannot be surged. Cheaper systems bring commercial manufacturers, distributed production, and faster iteration—Ukraine's real lesson: the side that adapts every few months wins.
[P1.9] The motion doesn't ask us to scrap the F-35 or the submarine force. It asks whether the next marginal procurement dollar should buy another slice of a decade-long program or hundreds of expendable systems. On the battlefield that actually exists, the answer is not close.

[C1.2] Ukraine does not prove that mass substitutes for high-end capability. Ukrainian drones inflict losses because they operate alongside artillery, air defense, electronic warfare, satellite intelligence, and command systems. Remove those exquisite enablers and the drones become scattered gadgets. In a Pacific fight, the problem is worse: vast distances, contested spectrum, and hardened targets demand long-range sensors, quiet submarines, penetrating bombers, missile defense, and secure communications.
[C1.3] Swarms cannot hold an island chain, protect sealift, or sink a modern fleet if they lack the platforms and networks to task them.
[C1.4] The Red Sea example cuts against the motion. The uncomfortable interceptor math argues for better layered defense—cheaper effectors, directed energy, more magazines—not for shrinking the fleet that protects shipping and projects air power. [C1.5] They do not carry allies’ confidence.
[C1.6] Attritable systems also create their own vulnerabilities. They require launchers, datalinks, spectrum access, and maintenance; they can be jammed, spoofed, or swamped. An adversary with robust electronic warfare and layered air defense will force us to spend many cheap systems to achieve one effect, reversing the promised cost advantage.
[C1.7] The decisive error is treating exquisite platforms and cheap systems as rivals. They are not. But the motion asks us to reduce investment in the backbone in order to buy more peripherals. That makes America cheaper to field on paper and weaker in the fight that matters.
