[P1.1] Licensing in fields such as hair braiding and interior design functions mainly as a gate that existing practitioners control. Braiders in several states must complete 1,000 or more hours of cosmetology coursework that includes chemical processing and scalp treatments unrelated to their actual work. This requirement adds months of unpaid training and exam fees that disproportionately exclude people who already know the skill through family tradition or informal practice, shrinking the supply of affordable services without reducing documented health incidents.
[P1.2] Interior design licensing similarly demands formal education and passage of tests centered on space planning and decorative standards. These tasks pose no inherent danger to clients or buildings once structural elements are handled by licensed engineers. The rules nevertheless raise project costs and steer work toward established firms that can absorb compliance expenses.
[P1.3] The pattern matches textbook rent-seeking. Incumbent businesses and professional associations lobby for rules that new competitors must satisfy, then staff the licensing boards that enforce them. The resulting barriers produce higher prices and fewer choices while delivering negligible gains in measurable safety. States that dropped or never imposed such licensing for these occupations show comparable rates of consumer complaints and no detectable rise in injury.
[P1.4] By contrast, genuine public-safety licensing, such as requirements for electricians or plumbers, addresses immediate risks of fire, shock, or structural failure that markets alone cannot quickly correct. When the same framework is applied to low-risk activities, the safety rationale weakens and the protection of incumbents becomes the dominant effect.

[C1.1] The safety of the public is not a luxury reserved only for heavy industries; it is a fundamental requirement of any service involving physical contact, public spaces, or structural environments. The claim that licensing in fields like hair braiding and interior design serves merely to protect incumbents ignores the very real, often invisible risks these professions manage every day.
[C1.2] Take cosmetology and hair braiding. These are not merely aesthetic tasks; they are high-touch services that pose direct public health risks. Without standardized training in sanitation, infectious disease control, and scalp pathology, consumers are exposed to bloodborne pathogens, fungal infections, staph, and permanent physical damage like traction alopecia. Licensing ensures that anyone operating on the human body understands basic epidemiology and hygiene, preventing salons from becoming vectors for community disease transmission.
[C1.3] Similarly, professional interior designers do not simply choose color palettes. They make critical structural and material decisions that dictate public safety. Licensed designers must understand complex building codes, Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) compliance, egress pathways, and fire-safety ratings for textiles and materials. A single error in specifying non-compliant, highly flammable upholstery in a commercial space or miscalculating an emergency exit path can have catastrophic, fatal consequences.
[C1.4] To categorize this as "low-risk" is a dangerous misunderstanding of what professional interior design actually entails.
[C1.5] Licensing is a proactive shield. It solves the problem of information asymmetry, ensuring that consumers—who cannot easily inspect a practitioner's sanitary habits or code knowledge beforehand—are guaranteed a baseline level of safety. Relying on post-hoc lawsuits or market reputation only helps after a consumer has already been infected, scarred, or trapped in a fire. Far from a barrier to entry, licensing is the cornerstone of public trust and safety.
