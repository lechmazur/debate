[P1.1] Global supply chains are governed by private codes, and those codes keep failing. Rana Plaza is the emblem: brands with public labor standards were sourcing from a building that collapsed, killing more than a thousand workers. The lesson is not that no firm cares; it is that self-reported caring is not accountability. The proposition closes that gap by requiring the firms with the most power and capacity—large multinationals—to publish standardized, independently audited due-diligence reports.
[P1.2] Three features do the work. Standardized means the same core facts must be disclosed across firms: supplier sites, known risks, audit findings, grievance mechanisms, and remediation. Audited means an independent party tests the claims and the method, so a report is not a brochure. Published means workers, unions, investors, regulators, and journalists can actually use it.
[P1.3] The abused worker may not read the PDF, but her union, a local regulator, or an investigative reporter can.
[P1.4] This changes behavior before it changes law. A factory hidden in tier three can be exposed; a brand that ignores repeated fire-safety failures cannot claim ignorance; a firm doing the right thing is no longer undercut by an opaque rival. Comparability also forces boards to treat human-rights risk as a governance risk, not a donation.
[P1.5] CON will call this costly and box-ticking. Large multinationals already run private audits and ESG reporting; a common mandatory format replaces dozens of inconsistent questionnaires and makes evasion harder, not easier. Audits are imperfect, but the alternative is no verifiable evidence at all. Imperfect transparency can be challenged; manufactured ignorance cannot.
[P1.6] Nor is this extraterritorial policing of every factory: it is a disclosure condition on firms that choose to operate in a market.
[P1.7] Countries should require this because the market alone rewards concealment. Standardized, audited publication is the minimum infrastructure for accountability. That is how decent firms win and abusive ones lose.

[C1.1] Rana Plaza should haunt this proposal, not support it. Several brands tied to that building already had labor codes and social audits. The paperwork existed. The building still collapsed.
[C1.2] The problem was not missing PDFs but paid auditors, announced visits, hidden subcontracting, and inspections that see what they are paid to see. A legal mandate for standardized, audited reports would nationalize that theater.
[C1.3] Standardization makes it worse. A copper mine, a cocoa cooperative, and a third-tier garment workshop do not share a common risk template. Forcing them into one form rewards what is documentable over what is dangerous: certificates over coercion, training logs over freedom to organize. Auditors paid by the firms they judge will do what conflict-mineral and factory auditors have repeatedly done — certify the checkable and miss the human.
[C1.4] The costs will not stay in headquarters. When disclosure creates legal risk, multinationals do what lawyers advise: cut risky suppliers, demand expensive traceability systems, and shift the burden down. Small suppliers in Bangladesh, Congo, or Guatemala who cannot fund compliance teams lose contracts to larger, safer factories or safer countries. American conflict-minerals disclosure famously triggered just such a pullout, leaving artisanal miners poorer, not freer.
[C1.5] Workers pay for the report with their jobs.
[C1.6] And publication does not equal power. Annual, lawyer-scrubbed reports do not help a migrant worker facing withheld wages this week. They help competitors mine supplier lists and help compliance departments prove diligence while changing nothing on the floor.
[C1.7] The honest firm that discloses unresolved forced-labor risks invites prosecution and headlines; the evasive rival that reports ‘no findings’ looks clean. The mandate punishes candor and rewards sanitized ignorance.
[C1.8] Accountability needs enforcement, unions, and liability for actual harm — not comparable brochures that punish honesty, protect evasion, and drive business away from the vulnerable.

[P2.5] Sanitized ignorance becomes a red flag, not a shield.
[P2.6] Costs: The duty falls on large multinationals, not Bangladeshi or Congolese suppliers. They already collect audit data. Disclosure does not mandate divestment; it mandates visibility. [P2.7] CON cannot simultaneously say reports are toothless brochures and that they will cause mass pullouts. If they are toothless, they won't cut suppliers; if they cut suppliers, they are not toothless.

[P4.1] A1: “Standardized, audited” does not mean the firm hires and scopes its own auditor. It means law fixes core scope—supplier sites, risk findings, method, remediation, grievance access—and sets auditor independence and publication rules. A public “no findings” claim is then a factual claim against a common standard, not a private brochure. If workers, a union, a regulator, or another buyer’s report shows blocked exits or withheld wages at that site, the claim becomes actionable.
[P4.2] Who prosecutes? The disclosure regulator, labor authorities, procurement debarment, civil suits, or securities enforcers can treat false or misleading statements as violations. A false clean bill is not a shield; it is a liability waiting for a contradictory fact. Slow enforcement is still better than no verifiable statement to enforce.
[P4.3] A2: The firm can cut unless the regulation requires remediation or explanation. The proposition does not chain a brand to an abusive supplier forever. But the comparison is silent private exit versus visible public exit. Today, a firm can discover forced labor, drop the supplier, and leave workers with nothing and no record.
[P4.4] Under this mandate, the cut is disclosed: site, risk, buyer, date. Unions and regulators can follow the site; the next buyer cannot claim ignorance; pressure can force transition or remediation. Transparency may not save every contract, but it stops the firm from turning a human-rights finding into a quiet cost-saving disappearance.
[P4.5] Those answers show why PRO wins. CON’s alternative—enforcement, unions, liability—is not an alternative; it is downstream of information. You cannot organize a hidden third-tier workshop you cannot find. You cannot prove withheld wages after the workers are gone.
[P4.6] CON’s own questions assume someone must know the site and the risk; the mandate creates that record. And CON cannot call reports lawyer-vetted shields when convenient and litigation files when convenient; if they expose, they deter; if they are inert, they do not cause the pullouts CON fears. The decisive point: accountability requires an auditable public starting point. Vote PRO.
