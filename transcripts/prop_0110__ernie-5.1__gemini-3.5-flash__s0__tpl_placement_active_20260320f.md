[P1.1] Public health systems outperform predominantly private insurance on the metric that matters: health results for each dollar spent. The evidence is not a borderline statistical footnote; it is a consistent pattern across every rich‑country comparison.
[P1.2] Consider the United States, the archetype of a private‑insurance market. It spends roughly 18 % of its GDP on health care, far above any peer, yet its life expectancy lags behind every other high‑income nation and its infant‑mortality rate is twice that of the United Kingdom. The United Kingdom’s National Health Service spends about 10 % of GDP, delivers a life expectancy that matches or exceeds the U.S., and its infant mortality is less than half. The same pattern holds in Canada, where per‑capita spending is roughly a third of the U.S. level but life expectancy and avoidable‑death rates are comparable or better.
[P1.3] Why does the gap appear? First, administrative overhead in a fragmented private market consumes 15‑20 % of every dollar, compared with 2‑3 % in single‑payer systems that negotiate prices centrally. Second, public insurers can set drug prices and cap fees, while private insurers pass high pharmaceutical and provider costs to patients, inflating total spending without improving outcomes.
[P1.4] Third, universal coverage removes the “donut hole” of uninsured people who delay care until emergencies, a major driver of wasteful, high‑cost interventions.
[P1.5] When you strip out profit margins, marketing, and duplicated billing, the remaining spending in public systems is directed at preventive care, primary‑care access, and population‑wide programs—exactly the levers that move life expectancy and reduce avoidable deaths. The data therefore show that each dollar in a public system buys more health than the same dollar in a private‑insurance system.
[P1.6] We must focus on the proposition: better population health outcomes per dollar. The pattern is clear, the mechanisms are well understood, and the numbers speak for themselves. Public systems win on the metric that counts.

That currency is time. Public systems balance their books through rationing, leading to extensive wait times for specialist care, diagnostic imaging, and critical surgeries. When a patient waits months for an MRI or a joint replacement, their quality of life deteriorates, and their clinical condition often worsens.
[C1.2] This is not efficiency; it is a hidden tax paid in human suffering.
[C1.3] Furthermore, using broad metrics like life expectancy to judge system efficiency is highly misleading. Life expectancy is primarily driven by social determinants—such as diet, violence, and drug abuse—which healthcare systems cannot cure. When we look at actual clinical outcomes for patients who are already sick, the picture reverses. For complex, high-stakes diseases like cancer and stroke, private-incentivized systems consistently lead the world in survival rates.
[C1.4] They do this because they rapidly adopt cutting-edge medical technologies, specialized treatments, and clinical innovations.
[C1.5] In a public system with centralized budgets, adopting expensive new therapies is treated as a cost to be avoided. In a private system, innovation is an asset. The global pipeline of new pharmaceuticals and advanced medical devices is overwhelmingly funded and driven by private-market incentives.
[C1.6] Finally, "private" does not mean "unregulated" or "uninsured." Highly successful systems like Switzerland utilize private insurance models to deliver world-class care with virtually zero wait times and exceptional outcomes. By relying on choice and competition rather than bureaucratic rationing, these systems prove that private markets deliver superior, timely care. Public systems may look cheaper on a state ledger, but they achieve those savings by restricting the very access and innovation that patients need most.
