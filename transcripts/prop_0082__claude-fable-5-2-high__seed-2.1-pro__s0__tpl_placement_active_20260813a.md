[C1.1] The proposition rests on a fatal, unworkable trick: it asks for blanket limits on core app features, anchored to a standard no neutral party can actually measure—when a mechanic supposedly “undermines match quality and user trust.” There is no universal, shared metric for what counts as a “good” match or “reasonable” trust, because dating app users have wildly different goals, timelines, and preferences. What feels like an exhausting, endless swipe deck to one burnt-out user is a welcome wide pool of options to a recently single person in a new city who does not want to be locked out of seeing available people by an arbitrary daily cap.
[C1.2] What reads as a predatory paid boost to one user is an opt-in, time-saving tool for someone who wants to cut through algorithmic noise to be seen by more matches right now, rather than waiting weeks for slow organic distribution.
[C1.3] Swipe mechanics were not devised solely to hook users like slot machines. They replaced clunky, over-long profile browsing that left users paralyzed by walls of text, filtering for trivial survey answers instead of the immediate chemistry that is the non-negotiable foundation of any real romantic connection. Arbitrary swipe caps will not make users more thoughtful: they will make people hoard their limited swipes, rush even harsher snap judgments, and skip over people they might otherwise have connected with, because swipes become an artificially scarce resource.
[C1.4] Worst of all, these rules will erode, not rebuild, trust. When apps are forced to throttle distribution and ban opt-in visibility tools, users will rightly suspect the algorithm is holding back matches to enforce top-down limits, not to serve their interests. Adults do not need app designers or regulators to decide for them how much swiping is too much, or when paying to be seen is a bad trade.
