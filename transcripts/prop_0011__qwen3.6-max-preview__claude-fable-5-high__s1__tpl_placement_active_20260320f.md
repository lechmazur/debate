[P1.1] Education policy should measure success by whether families control their children’s futures, not by whether bureaucracies retain their budgets. Vouchers do exactly that: they shift power from institutions to parents. When evidence on test scores is mixed, it tells us that standardized metrics are too narrow to capture what families actually value—safer campuses, better discipline, aligned values, and higher graduation rates. Expanding vouchers respects parents as the primary decision-makers and treats education funding as a student entitlement, not a school subsidy.
[P1.2] The motion explicitly acknowledges mixed academic gains, and that is precisely why expansion is justified. Early voucher cohorts often show flat test scores while dramatically improving parent satisfaction, attendance, and long-term outcomes like college enrollment. Families consistently trade marginal point gains on state exams for environments where their children actually want to attend class. Standardized tests measure a slice of learning; they do not measure whether a child escapes a chronically unsafe hallway or finally receives instruction that matches their pace.
[P1.3] Meanwhile, the claim that public schools “lose resources” mistakes how funding should work. Dollars are appropriated for students, not buildings. When a family leaves, the money follows the child because the cost of educating that child moves with them. Holding funding hostage to force enrollment is a protection racket, not a public good.
[P1.4] Expansion also creates the only leverage that reliably improves district schools: competition. Monopolies stagnate; systems that must earn families respond. Even modest voucher programs force districts to streamline administration, improve student services, and innovate curricula to retain enrollment. Most importantly, vouchers are an equity tool.
[P1.5] Wealthy families already exercise choice through housing markets and private tuition. Denying expansion because test scores are uneven or districts face budget adjustments simply traps low-income students in assigned schools while protecting the status quo. States should expand vouchers because freedom, fairness, and long-term systemic health outweigh short-term institutional discomfort.

[C1.1] When the evidence is mixed and the costs are certain, expansion is the one option a responsible state should reject. That asymmetry decides this debate. The proposition asks states to scale up a policy whose benefits are unproven while admitting its harms—drained public budgets—are real. No serious government runs that trade in any other domain.
[C1.2] We don't expand a drug trial when results are ambiguous; we certainly don't expand it when we know the side effects.
[C1.3] And "mixed" undersells the problem. The largest recent statewide programs—Louisiana and Indiana among them—produced some of the sharpest negative academic effects researchers have measured in education policy, losses comparable in magnitude to pandemic learning disruption. That is what scaling looks like: small pilot programs with motivated families and quality private schools show modest gains; statewide expansion pulls in low-quality, pop-up schools chasing public dollars with none of the testing, certification, or civil-rights obligations public schools carry. Expansion is precisely the mechanism that converts mixed evidence into bad outcomes.
[C1.4] The fiscal harm is not "institutional discomfort." When a handful of students leave each grade, a district cannot lay off a fraction of a teacher, heat a fraction of a building, or run a fraction of a bus route. Fixed costs stay; revenue leaves. The students harmed are the ones who remain—disproportionately poor, disabled, and rural children whom private schools can legally refuse.
[C1.5] Meanwhile, in state after state that adopted universal vouchers, most early recipients were already enrolled in private school. That isn't liberation for the trapped; it's a public subsidy for families who needed nothing, paid for by hollowing out the only schools obligated to take everyone.
[C1.6] So the question is simple: should states make a certain, concentrated sacrifice—weaker schools for the children with the fewest exits—in exchange for a benefit even advocates concede is unproven? Prudence, equity, and the evidence all say no.
