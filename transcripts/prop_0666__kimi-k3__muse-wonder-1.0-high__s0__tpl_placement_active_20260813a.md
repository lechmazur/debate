[P1.1] When a landlord's screening algorithm rejects your apartment application, a hiring platform filters out your resume, or a hospital's triage model deprioritizes your care, the decision arrives without a reason, an author, or an appeal. The question is whether courts will keep one minimal human safeguard in the machine age: an explanation when the stakes are housing, credit, employment, or healthcare.
[P1.2] The right at issue is narrow. It triggers only on significant adverse effects in those four domains; it entitles the affected person to the principal reasons for the decision; it demands no source code, no veto over outcomes, no ban on automation. That matters, because everything CON says about cost must be weighed against a modest, targeted duty — not a fantasy of total algorithmic transparency.
[P1.3] Three reasons to affirm.
[P1.4] First, explanation is the gateway right. Every protection the law already recognizes — appeals, anti-discrimination claims, error correction — presupposes that the affected person knows why the decision happened. A tenant screened out because an algorithm matched her to a stranger's eviction record cannot correct what she cannot see. Without reasons, due process is decorative.
[P1.5] Second, explanations make the systems themselves better. Automated tools err in characteristic ways: stale data, biased training sets, proxies that smuggle in race or disability. Those errors surface only when someone can say, "that reason is wrong." The right to explanation is not anti-technology; it is the quality-control mechanism that lets automation earn public trust instead of burning it.
[P1.6] Third, feasibility is proven, not speculative. American credit law has long required adverse action notices telling applicants why they were denied, and lenders still lend. Courts protect trade secrets every day through protective orders and in-camera review. Extending a familiar, workable principle to the other gateways of economic life is incrementalism, not revolution.
[P1.7] CON must explain why a denied loan deserves a reason but a denied home, job, or treatment does not.

[C1.1] A denied applicant deserves recourse. It does not follow that courts should invent a freestanding right to explanation, or that an explanation is the recourse that works.
[C1.2] First, this is a job courts are institutionally unfit to do. “Significant effect,” “principal reasons,” adequate specificity, timing, remedies, and trade-secret protection — none of that is in the proposition, and none can be deduced from due-process principles, especially against private landlords, employers, and hospitals. One judge in one screening case cannot set standards for credit models, hiring tools, and clinical triage at once. The result will be patchwork duties firms cannot predict and plaintiffs must litigate case by case.
[C1.3] The credit notices PRO invokes prove the opposite point: Congress legislated them, with defined triggers and standardized reason codes. That is how such a regime should be built, if at all — not improvised by courts.
[C1.4] Second, for many automated systems, “the principal reasons” do not exist in the form the right imagines. A complex model does not decide like a loan officer citing income and debts; it weighs hundreds of interacting variables. Post-hoc explanations are simplified reconstructions, and different accepted methods can rank factors differently for the same decision. A court-ordered explanation therefore risks becoming a plausible story rather than the true cause — worse than silence, because it invites the applicant to “fix” a factor that was not decisive and gives discrimination a clean paper trail.
[C1.5] Third, predictable compliance will be boilerplate, gaming, and retreat. Firms will adopt lawyer-approved generic reasons — “insufficient rental history,” “qualifications mismatch” — that satisfy counsel while revealing little. Where reasons are specific enough to game, fraud screening and clinical models become easier to manipulate. Small landlords and clinics, unable to fund explanation litigation, will abandon useful tools or revert to informal human judgment, which is less auditable and often more biased.
[C1.6] Existing anti-discrimination, consumer-reporting, and data-accuracy laws, plus targeted audits and human reconsideration, attack real harms without this fiction. Courts should enforce those democratically enacted duties, not create a vague explanatory ritual.
