[P1.1] When a taxpayer funds a study, they do not pay to be locked out of the results. Right now, publicly funded papers sit behind $40 paywalls, raw data vanishes when a grad student leaves, and code that produced critical results is never shared even when other researchers beg for it. This is not an edge case. This is the default system today.
[P1.2] Mandating open licenses by default fixes this, and it is the only fair and effective policy for public science funders.
[P1.3] First, this returns value to the people who paid. A high school biology teacher explaining viral evolution should not pay out of pocket to read the study their taxes funded. A small town engineer testing flood models should not be blocked from the public climate research they need. This is not abstract.
[P1.4] During the first year of COVID, closed research slowed vaccine development, slowed public health guidance, and cost lives. We have already seen the cost of the status quo.
[P1.5] Second, this makes science actually work. Replication, the foundation of real science, is impossible without open data and code. Most published claims today cannot be independently checked, because the underlying materials are not available. This mandate is not a burden on researchers: it is the minimum guardrail to ensure public money produces reliable, usable knowledge.
[P1.6] This is a default rule, not an absolute ban. Researchers can apply for carveouts for sensitive medical data, patentable work, or safety risks. All this policy does is stop closed access from being the automatic, unexamined choice that nobody ever has to justify.

[C1.1] Mandating open licenses by default for publicly funded papers, data, and code confuses access with ownership and convenience with progress. Public funding does not strip researchers of incentives or convert every output into free raw material. It backs risk-taking work that still requires intellectual property, selective control, and pathways into usable products.
[C1.2] This rule attacks translation. University patents and exclusive licenses turn basic discoveries into medicines, energy systems, and engineered tools that deliver public value. Default open licenses chill industry partnerships, drain tech-transfer revenue that recycles into further research, and leave findings stranded without development capital. An open paper helps readers; open code and data that erase proprietary edges simply invite free-riding before the work can scale.
[C1.3] The replication claim is oversold. Failures of science stem more from statistical weak designs, publication pressure, and incomplete methods than from paywalls or missing repositories. Forcing early dumps of raw data and scripts often yields noisy, uncurated artifacts or privacy exposures rather than reliable checks. Teachers and engineers already draw on large open collections; the bottlenecks are quality and interpretation, which mandates do not supply.
[C1.4] Defaults-with-carveouts are not pragmatic escapes. Sensitive medical records, dual-use algorithms, security constraints, and early-stage patent windows will spawn endless exemption battles. The “default” collapses into bureaucracy that careful researchers game or sidestep by chasing private funds. Stronger incentives for selective openness, case-by-case funder review, and journals that reward verified sharing protect integrity without flattening legitimate differences.
[C1.5] The status quo has frictions, yet the proposed mandate trades working innovation mechanisms for a symbolic open label. Public money should purchase durable advances, not the forced redistribution of unfinished or commercially strategic materials.
