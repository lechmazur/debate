[P1.1] Picture a communal conservancy in northern Namibia. An old bull elephant, past breeding, has been trampling millet fields. A foreign hunter pays a large fee to shoot him. That money hires game guards, compensates farmers for lost crops, and funds a water point.
[P1.2] The meat feeds the village. The next elephant that wanders in is an asset, not a pest. Now remove the hunter. The elephant is still shot — by a farmer with a snare or poison — and nobody gets paid, no guard is hired, and the land quietly converts to cattle.
[P1.3] That is the real choice, and the motion has already stripped out the hard cases. It does not defend canned hunts, corrupt ministries, or money vanishing into offshore accounts. It asks a narrower question: when the revenue verifiably reaches conservation and local people, should we still ban it because it offends us? Say no, and you are choosing your own comfort over the survival of the animals you claim to care about.
[P1.4] The binding constraint on African wildlife is not the death of individuals; it is habitat. Wildlife survives where it pays more than cows and maize. Photographic tourism does this in a handful of scenic, accessible places. It cannot do it in the vast remote, malarial, tsetse-ridden country where most large mammals actually live.
[P1.5] Hunting can, because a few clients paying tens of thousands each need no roads, lodges, or views. The history is instructive: Kenya banned hunting in the 1970s and lost a large share of its wildlife outside protected areas over the following decades. Botswana banned it, watched human-elephant conflict and poaching rise, and reversed course. Namibia and Zimbabwe kept it, and their community programmes are among the few places wildlife expanded on communal land.
[P1.6] Moral distaste is a feeling, not a policy. We do not ban abattoirs because they upset us. A conservation policy is judged by what happens to habitat, populations, and people when it exists versus when it does not. On that test, transparent, community-benefiting hunting wins.

[C1.2] Consider what transparency actually means in practice. In country after country, audits have shown that trophy revenues reach communities as a fraction of what was promised — sometimes under ten percent.
[C1.3] The condition the motion relies on is precisely the one most often violated.
[C1.4] Even granting the best case, the proposition concedes that we kill animals so other animals can live. [C1.5] The PRO case hides a substitution. It assumes banning produces the current baseline plus poaching. But bans change political economy. [C1.6] Legal markets for charismatic dead wildlife also sustain the global trade in animal parts, laundering demand through "sport."
[C1.7] The revenue claim is fragile. A photograph of a living elephant generates income year after year. A trophy is a one-time payment. Building conservation on a model that requires killing to continue is to build on sand.
[C1.8] Moral distaste is not a distraction from policy. It is the question of what relationship we want with the natural world. We should not outsource conservation to those who pay for the privilege of killing.
