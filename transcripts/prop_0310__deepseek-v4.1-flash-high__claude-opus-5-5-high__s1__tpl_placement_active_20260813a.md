[P1.1] Every adult will eat, live somewhere, and handle money. None of those are niche interests. That is why cooking, basic home repair, and personal finance belong in the core curriculum, not in the elective catalog for students who already know to look.
[P1.2] The current optional model is not neutral; it is a filter. Students whose parents teach them to cook, fix a running toilet, or read a loan agreement arrive with a hidden curriculum. Students without that at home are left to learn by expensive mistake—or never. Making these subjects optional therefore widens the exact gaps schools exist to close.
[P1.3] A required baseline—food safety and cheap meals; shutting off water, patching a wall, understanding a lease; interest, taxes, credit, and contracts—gives every student the same starting kit for adulthood.
[P1.4] This is not vocational training or a demand for expertise. Core means a floor, not a ceiling. We require algebra without making everyone an engineer; we require health without making everyone a doctor. Basic life skills fit the same logic: they are widely used, high-consequence, and hard to acquire safely by trial and error.
[P1.5] A young person who cannot cook is more vulnerable to poor nutrition and dependency. One who cannot recognize a predatory loan can lose years to debt. One who cannot handle a minor household problem may pay for a small repair as if it were a crisis.
[P1.6] The decisive question is not whether some motivated students benefit from electives. It is whether a society can afford to leave these competencies to chance and family background. It cannot. Schools are the one institution that reaches nearly everyone before the consequences arrive.
[P1.7] If these skills matter every day, they should be taught as core.
