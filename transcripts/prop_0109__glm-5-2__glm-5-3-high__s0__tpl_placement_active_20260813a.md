[P1.1] When institutional investors buy single-family homes in tight markets, they aren't adding supply. They're converting existing owner-occupied stock into rental inventory using advantages no family can match: all-cash offers, waiving contingencies, closing in days. A household making a financed offer with an inspection contingency will lose to a private equity fund every time, even at the same price. The proposition targets exactly this distortion—tight markets where local buyers are being crowded out—and leaves room for investment where competition isn't the problem.
[P1.2] The deeper issue is what gets lost. Homeownership remains the primary vehicle for household wealth-building in most American communities. When a family buys a home, they gain equity, stability, and a stake in neighborhood outcomes over decades. When an institutional landlord buys that same house, the tenant pays rent that builds someone else's balance sheet, gets less control over their living situation, and faces rent increases driven by fiduciary duty to distant shareholders.
[P1.3] The conversion isn't neutral—it redirects wealth upward and erodes the financial on-ramp that middle-class stability depends on.
[P1.4] This isn't speculative. After the last foreclosure crisis, large firms entered single-family rental markets at scale, and the pattern repeated in the post-2020 boom: concentrated purchases in Sun Belt metros, rents rising faster than wages, local families priced out of neighborhoods they'd lived in for years. The mechanism is simple math—capital that can borrow cheaply and hold patiently will always outbid a household constrained by income, down payment, and loan approval.
[P1.5] Cities already regulate who can own what through zoning, short-term rental rules, and landlord licensing. Restricting institutional purchases of single-family homes in demonstrably tight markets is a proportional response to a documented problem, not an radical intervention. The proposition doesn't ban renting or investment broadly. It says: when the market is failing the people it's supposed to house, protect the path to ownership before it's gone.
