[P1.1] Street disorder and drunk driving are not products of the hour on the clock; they are the predictable consequences of stranding thousands of intoxicated people in the street with no safe way home.
[P1.2] Earlier venue closing times rely on a blunt, coercive mechanism that actively worsens street conditions: the simultaneous ejection bottleneck. When every bar in an entertainment district is forced to shut at 1:00 or 2:00 AM, thousands of patrons are dumped onto the pavement at the exact same moment. This creates an immediate flashpoint. Crowds compete for scarce late-night food and dwindling rideshare vehicles, surge pricing skyrockets, and tempers flare on congested sidewalks.
[P1.3] Far from reducing disorder, synchronized closing concentrates it in time and space.
[P1.4] Moreover, an earlier curfew does not sober patrons up; it simply strands them earlier. When people are ejected into a transit desert, the barrier to getting home safely becomes nearly insurmountable. Faced with steep surge fares, endless wait times, or cold sidewalks, impaired individuals make catastrophic calculations to get behind the wheel. Earlier curfews also fuel rapid "pre-gaming" before last call and displace drinking to unregulated house parties in car-dependent suburbs, expanding the geographical footprint of impaired driving.
[P1.5] Weekend late-night transit extensions solve the logistical root of both problems. Transit functions as a continuous pressure-release valve. Instead of trapping an agitated mass of people in commercial corridors, reliable late-night subways and buses provide a continuous, high-capacity exit route. Patrons disperse fluidly as they choose to leave, preventing the friction of crowded taxi ranks and sidewalk bottlenecks that trigger brawls.
[P1.6] Crucially, transit offers a visible, affordable, and immediate alternative to driving at the precise moments when judgment is lowest.
[P1.7] You cannot legislate away human nightlife with an arbitrary curfew, but you can build the infrastructure that drains crowded streets and clears roads safely.

[C1.2] It reduces the number of impaired people created; transit mostly relocates the lucky subset who live on a usable line.
[C1.3] The “simultaneous ejection bottleneck” [P1.2] is not inherent to earlier closing. An earlier last call can be paired with staggered departures and food service; even where crowds still bunch, they are smaller, less drunk, and easier to police. Fewer rounds mean fewer escalating arguments, less fatigue, and fewer people out when judgment is worst. A queue at 1 a.m. is not self-evidently worse than a thinner, more intoxicated, harder-to-monitor crowd at 3 or 4 a.m.
[C1.4] Transit is also a fragile drunk-driving answer. The driving decision is often made before visible intoxication: someone drives in because “I’ll figure it out later,” leaves keys nearby, then cannot reliably navigate a timetable, transfer, or walk to a stop. Many trips end in suburbs, industrial edges, or last-mile neighborhoods that late-night rail barely touches. A downtown train does not solve a parked car, a missing feeder bus, or the house party after bars shut.
[C1.5] It can simply shift disorder to platforms, stops, and interchange queues.
[C1.6] So the “pressure-release valve” [P1.5] is conditional: right city, right line, right destination, right patron, right sobriety. Earlier closing is blunter, but its causal reach is broader: less alcohol sold, less time for conflict to ripen, fewer impaired journeys attempted, and more of the night handled while normal transport and enforcement still work. Extensions can complement that policy; they do not beat it on either harm in the motion.
