[P1.1] When a publisher flips the switch on an online game, it can do more than end a service; it can delete a cultural object and a community’s shared history. The proposition does not demand that publishers run servers forever, subsidize hobbyists, or surrender their intellectual property. It asks only that shutdown not be synonymous with total erasure: allow private servers, an archival mode, or mod support under reasonable terms. That is the central comparison—control versus annihilation.
[P1.2] A publisher can retain ownership while permitting noncommercial preservation.
[P1.3] Online games are especially fragile because the client often becomes useless without the server. Players may have paid for access, built characters, made friends, and produced communities around a world that vanishes overnight. Other media do not require rightsholders to keep every theater open, but libraries and archives preserve books and films after their commercial life ends. Games should not be uniquely disposable simply because their cultural value is wrapped in code and network dependencies.
[P1.4] Community servers and mod support are concrete mechanisms: they can keep abandoned multiplayer playable, document patches and events, and let players experience works that would otherwise exist only in memory.
[P1.5] The predictable objections are piracy, security, and brand control. But permission can be narrow: noncommercial licenses, no use of official trademarks, no access to customer data, community operation only after official shutdown, and release of server software or protocol documentation where feasible. These are governance choices, not impossibilities. Publishers can retain enforcement against bad actors.
[P1.6] The alternative—blank legal erasure—guarantees loss and pushes preservation underground, where it is less accountable and less accessible.
[P1.7] The decisive question is who bears the burden of irreversible loss. Publishers have already captured the commercial value; after shutdown, the marginal cost of allowing preservation is low, while the cultural cost of refusal is permanent. A game preserved through an archival mode or a licensed community server still belongs to its creators in law and to its players in memory. Shutdown should end support, not existence.

Release either breaches contracts or exposes live infrastructure.
[C1.3] The compliant path is a purpose-built archival build: real engineering spent on a product that just failed, often by a team being laid off. The motion turns every shutdown into an unfunded preservation project.
[C1.4] Second, permission is not a one-time act; it is permanent exposure. A fan server carries the publisher's name regardless of trademark disclaimers. When one becomes a vector for malware, scams, or abuse — and some will — the reputational damage and the lawsuit both find the deep pocket that "allowed" it. Enforcement "against bad actors" [P1.5] is an eternal legal vigil over a product the company abandoned.
[C1.5] Rational counsel answers that risk exactly as the motion forbids: clean erasure.
[C1.6] Third, incentives. If launching an online world means underwriting its afterlife forever, studios build fewer persistent games, or build them cloud-only so nothing preservable exists. The obligation does not save games; it teaches publishers to design games that cannot be saved.
[C1.7] The library analogy fails the same way. Libraries shelve finished objects; an online game is an ongoing service. Its culture genuinely survives shutdown — in video archives, wikis, streams, fan work. What the motion demands is not archiving but resurrection, underwritten by the creator indefinitely.
[C1.8] Shutdown ends a service, not a culture. Publishers should be free to end the cost and the liability when they end the game.
