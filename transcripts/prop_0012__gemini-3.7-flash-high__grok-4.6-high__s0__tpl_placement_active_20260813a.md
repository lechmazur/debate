[P1.1] Human remains are not specimens, curatorial property, or research assets; they are ancestors and the subjects of fundamental human dignity. When museums and universities delay the return of Indigenous and colonized peoples under the pretext of scientific value, they actively extend the colonial subjugation that acquired them.
[P1.2] The core ethical baseline of modern science is consent. In medical ethics and law, we strictly prohibit holding or experimenting on human bodies without consent, regardless of potential utility. Yet during imperial expansion, the remains of colonized peoples were systematically looted from battlefields, burial mounds, and massacre sites to fill Western collections. To argue that researchers may hold these remains hostage for study is to claim that historic plunder confers legitimate scientific title.
[P1.3] It enforces a glaring double standard: institutions would never retain stolen remains of their own majority populations merely because researchers found them informative.
[P1.4] Moreover, the appeal to "continued scientific value" is an infinite delay tactic. Scientific inquiry is limitless. There will always be a newer imaging technique, a more sensitive genetic tool, or a fresh hypothesis on the horizon. If potential future insight can block return, prompt repatriation will never happen.
[P1.5] It turns an excuse for further research into permanent institutional ownership.
[P1.6] Science does not exist in an ethical vacuum, and no research agenda outweighs basic human rights. Prompt repatriation restores bodily sovereignty and corrects a centuries-old injustice. Descendant communities—not the institutions that inherited the spoils of empire—hold the exclusive moral right to determine how their ancestors are treated.
