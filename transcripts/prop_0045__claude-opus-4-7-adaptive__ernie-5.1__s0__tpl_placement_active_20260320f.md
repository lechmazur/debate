[P1.1] Hiring decisions change lives. They decide who pays rent, who gets promoted, who gets sorted onto the "leadership track" and who gets quietly shelved. When an employer puts that weight on a test, the test should be able to carry it. MBTI and DISC cannot.
[P1.2] Start with the core problem: predictive validity. The whole point of using a test in hiring is that scores today should predict performance tomorrow. MBTI famously fails its own retest — a sizable share of takers, by the publisher's own historical admission, get a different type weeks later. A "trait" that flips when you take it on a Tuesday instead of a Thursday cannot predict job performance a year out.
[P1.3] DISC is built on similarly thin psychometric ground: forced-choice quadrants with weak evidence linking scores to actual outcomes like productivity, retention, or team effectiveness.
[P1.4] Compare that to what industrial-organizational psychology has been telling us for decades. Structured interviews, work-sample tests, cognitive ability assessments, and the Big Five conscientiousness measure consistently outperform MBTI/DISC at predicting job performance. The tools exist. Employers are choosing the weaker ones because they're fun, color-coded, and easy to sell in a workshop.
[P1.5] That choice isn't neutral — it's harmful. Three concrete harms:
[P1.6] First, bad hires. If a typology has near-zero incremental validity over a coin flip on the dimensions that matter, you are paying consulting fees to add noise to your decision.
[P1.7] Second, stereotyping and discrimination risk. Once "Sarah is an ISTJ" or "a high-D" enters her file, managers anchor on it. Stretch assignments and promotions get rationed by personality caricature rather than demonstrated skill.
[P1.8] Third, false confidence. Teams "explained" by quadrants stop doing the harder diagnostic work — unclear roles, weak feedback, bad incentives — because the poster on the wall has already told them why Bob clashes with Priya.
[P1.9] The proposition isn't anti-assessment. It's anti-snake-oil. Keep measuring; just use instruments that actually predict.

[C1.1] Employers should keep using MBTI, DISC, and similar tools because the claim that they lack predictive validity is both exaggerated and ignores the broader value these instruments deliver. The first thing to dismantle is the PRO’s myth of zero validity. Meta‑analyses in industrial‑organizational psychology consistently find that personality dimensions measured by the Big Five, and by extension by well‑constructed typologies, add incremental prediction to cognitive tests for outcomes such as teamwork, turnover, and safety compliance. While MBTI’s retest reliability is lower than ideal, the underlying dimensions it captures—especially extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness—correlate with real performance metrics at levels that matter for hiring decisions.
[C1.2] DISC’s four‑quadrant model, though simplified, reliably predicts role fit: high‑D individuals gravitate toward sales and leadership, high‑S toward support functions, and mismatches are a proven source of attrition. In practice, companies that added DISC to their interview workflow reported a measurable drop in early‑stage turnover, a result that no amount of structured‑interview data alone explains.
[C1.3] Beyond raw prediction, these tests serve a function that no cognitive assessment can: they create a shared language. When a manager says “Bob is a high‑C,” the team instantly knows to expect detail‑orientation and deadline‑driven behavior, reducing miscommunication faster than any work‑sample trial. This shared vocabulary cuts onboarding time, improves conflict resolution, and, contrary to PRO’s stereotyping worry, actually lowers bias by giving interviewers a concrete, observable anchor rather than vague “cultural fit” impressions.
[C1.4] Finally, cost and scalability matter. A DISC profile costs a few dollars per candidate and can be administered to hundreds in minutes, whereas structured interviews and work‑sample tests demand trained assessors and hours of time. For most mid‑size firms, the marginal gain from dropping a cheap, quick tool is outweighed by the loss of speed, consistency, and the diagnostic shorthand that keeps teams aligned. Stopping these tests would not eliminate snake‑oil; it would replace a modestly valid, highly efficient instrument with a heavier, slower, and not dramatically more accurate alternative.
