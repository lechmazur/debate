[P1.1] The motion turns on one promise: when a private citizen deletes a digital trace—or never made it public at all—that trace should be allowed to die.
[P1.2] Data brokers break that promise. They scrape, cache, archive, buy data exhaust, and assemble shadow dossiers, then resell stale screenshots, old addresses, location inferences, and browsing profiles long after the person has moved on. This is not neutral record-keeping; it is resurrection for rent.
[P1.3] That power destroys consent. Digital life depends on the ability to revise, retract, and escape one's past. If a domestic-violence survivor scrubs an old phone number and address, the entire point is that those facts should not follow. If a teenager deletes an impulsive post, the deletion is a boundary, not an invitation to preserve it forever.
[P1.4] A broker selling a cached copy or a prior address is not informing the public; it is overriding the author's own decision and converting personal autonomy into inventory. No one has a commercial right to profit from undoing someone's deliberate erasure.
[P1.5] The likely defense confuses public visibility with public permission. A post that was briefly public was not consent to indefinite resale inside a background-check bundle. And non-public traces—location pings, app activity, purchase inferences, data pulled from breaches or tracking SDKs—were never public in any meaningful sense. Selling them is not transparency; it is surveillance arbitrage.
[P1.6] Brokers are not remembering; they are mining.
[P1.7] Nor can the market correct this, because the subject is not the customer. You cannot bargain with a broker you have never met, and the harm is diffuse: stalking, fraud, denied jobs or insurance, and the chilling of speech because people fear a permanent, searchable past. Law already seals juvenile records; deleted and non-public digital traces are the modern residue of a life and deserve the same protection. Prohibition is not censorship—it is the only rule that makes deletion real.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to ban a category of business so wide it would swallow legitimate record-keeping along with the abuses. That alone should give us pause.
[C1.2] Three problems with prohibition as written.
[C1.3] First, the motion is dangerously overbroad. It covers "deleted or otherwise non-public digital traces" — breach data compilations, address-change services, fraud databases, and the headers credit bureaus use to confirm identity. A skip-tracer locating a civil witness, a vendor vetting a counterparty, a journalist confirming a source — all would be criminals under this rule. Prohibition does not draw lines around harm; it draws lines around information.
[C1.4] Second, deletion is not the clean boundary the proposition pretends. When a person posts, then removes, that post was already observed, archived by users, and indexed. The motion asks us to pretend "delete" gives the original author veto power over every downstream copy. We have not wanted that.
[C1.5] Reporters quote sources; scholars archive pages; courts admit prior publications. Prohibition would be the first rule in our law to make mere possession of a digital copy of publicly-originated information a crime.
[C1.6] Third, less restrictive tools already work better. Access rights, correction duties, purpose-limit statutes like the FCRA, and opt-out regimes target the actual harms — stalking, housing denial, identity fraud — without destroying the record flow that lenders, employers, and law enforcement rely on. Prohibition throws out the baby with the bathwater.
[C1.7] The juvenile-records analogy cuts the other way. Juvenile sealing is narrow, time-limited, and pierced by specific public-safety needs; it is not a blanket industry ban. If anything, it shows that granular rules — not flat prohibition — are how a free society handles sensitive records.
[C1.8] I take the harms seriously. But the answer is targeted regulation, not a ban that criminalizes every investigator, archive, and risk-screening service in the country.
