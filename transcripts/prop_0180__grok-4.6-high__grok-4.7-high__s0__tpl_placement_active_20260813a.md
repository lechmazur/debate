[P1.1] Abandoned farmland in Europe and North America is not empty space waiting for a nursery. It already has soils, seed banks, and nearby forests. Rewilding it—stop tilling, remove a few barriers, let succession run—locks more carbon and recovers more species per dollar than programs that grow seedlings, plant them, and replace the ones that die.
[P1.2] The cost gap decides this. Active reforestation pays for every stem: collection, nurseries, trucks, crews, weeding, tubes, and often a second planting after drought or deer. Rewilding costs almost nothing per hectare. A fence, a blocked drain, or a cheap grazer reintroduction is trivial beside industrial planting.
[P1.3] The same budget therefore recovers far more land. Carbon and wildlife scale with area, not with how many saplings you invoice.
[P1.4] Carbon follows that arithmetic. Abandoned temperate fields rebuild woody biomass and soil carbon on their own. Natural regeneration skips nursery energy, diesel, and the soil carbon lost when planters disturb ground. Successional mosaics—scrub, wet corners, young woodland—store carbon in several pools and do not fail as one even-aged crop.
[P1.5] Plantations look efficient per hectare until mortality, pests, and operational emissions are counted. Speed is not the metric. A fast plantation on twenty hectares can lose to slow recovery on two hundred hectares bought with the same money. Most planting programs never beat that ratio once failure and overhead are included.
[P1.6] Biodiversity is worse for planting. Even “native” programs flatten structure: few species, even ages, little dead wood, thin understory. Rewilding rebuilds habitat complexity and the food webs that keep carbon in place. New England’s post-farm woods and Europe’s quietly abandoned fields already show what happens when money is not spent fighting succession.
[P1.7] A row of tubes is not a community. A recovering farm is. Per dollar, most active reforestation buys a smaller, more fragile, less living result.
