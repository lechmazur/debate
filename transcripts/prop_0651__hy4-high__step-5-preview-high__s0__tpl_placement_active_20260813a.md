[P1.1] English is defended as a bridge; dominance has made it a border. The test is not speed, but whether non-anglophone cultures and knowledge systems can create, circulate and renew knowledge on their own terms. Under monopoly, science, business and web visibility require Anglophone passage.
[P1.2] Science shows the filter. Publication, peer review, citations, conferences and grants tend to reward English. Work on local soils, fisheries, customary law or medicinal plants becomes “parochial” until recast in English keywords and journals. That recasting is not neutral: terms carry classifications and authority; local concepts survive as footnotes to Anglophone frameworks.
[P1.3] The result is not one global conversation but a hierarchy. English-language work travels; Arabic, Tamil or Swahili work must explain why it should.
[P1.4] The same filter shapes aspiration. Families pay for English because elite credentials, promotion and contracts often require it. Students who might expand technical manuals, legal commentary, literature or science textbooks in their own languages are trained to leave those domains undeveloped. Once a language is absent from labs, boardrooms and syllabi, parents invest less in it, children treat it as home speech, and “choice” becomes domain loss.
[P1.5] The gain is individual mobility for a minority; the harm is collective capacity across generations.
[P1.6] Online, the feedback is faster. Search, moderation, advertising, autocomplete and text-heavy tools are disproportionately built around English. Non-English writing is often harder to find, less monetizable, worse served by software, and pressured into English tags, hooks and memes. Culture is not censored; it is made less scalable.
[P1.7] The reply will be that English enables collaboration and markets. That is a benefit of a lingua franca, not of dominance. Multilingual journals, serious translation, local-language technical education and platforms without an English default keep the bridge without the toll. As things stand, the toll is paid in prestige, vocabulary, attention, and finally in what a culture can imagine saying in its own voice.
[P1.8] Those cumulative losses outweigh short-run convenience.

[C1.1] The motion treats English as a cage. I see a ladder. A shared second language does not erase a culture; it plugs that culture into a network that would otherwise be unreachable.
[C1.2] Consider the counterfactual. Before English became the default, global science and commerce ran on Latin, French, or the language of whichever empire held the ledger. Each of those carried a church, a court, and a colonial administration. English today is a utilitarian tool, detached from any single throne.
[C1.3] A Kenyan epidemiologist and a Brazilian agronomist can argue over dataset methods without one colonizing the other. That is not hegemony; that is infrastructure.
[C1.4] The claim that knowledge is a fixed pie—publish in English and your local taxonomy starves—mistakes bilingualism for replacement. But the Swahili poet who also codes in Python is not a victim of domain loss; she is expanding what her language can reach. Local knowledge suffers when communities are poor and isolated, not because they read English. English is the escape hatch from that isolation, not the cause of it.
[C1.5] Online, the assertion that non-English culture is made less scalable ignores how fast the web has localized. The very tools said to be English-built—search, machine translation, voice input—are the engines that now let Tamil and Arabic content find global audiences. English was the bootstrapping code, not a wall.
[C1.6] Yes, dominance imposes costs: translation work, accent bias, the fatigue of constant second-language labor. But the benefit is planetary. Outbreak data, climate models, and supply-chain standards move in English. Dismantling that lingua franca would not liberate the margins; it would strand them.
[C1.7] The answer to linguistic inequality is more languages online and better translation, not tearing down the only bridge everyone already crosses.
