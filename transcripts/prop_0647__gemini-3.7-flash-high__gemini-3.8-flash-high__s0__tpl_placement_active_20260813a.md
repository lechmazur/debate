[P1.1] For nearly a century, cities treated highway expansion as the default remedy for traffic congestion. The result is an undeniable planning failure: expanding urban freeways to solve gridlock is like loosening a belt to cure obesity. Widening generates induced demand, rapidly filling added lanes with new vehicle miles while funneling worse bottlenecks into downtown streets. This approach devours billions in scarce capital, destroys existing urban fabric through right-of-way seizures, and locks municipalities into unsustainable maintenance liabilities.
[P1.2] Removing urban freeways breaks this self-destructive cycle. When cities dismantle elevated or trench highways, the predicted traffic catastrophes consistently fail to materialize. Instead, traffic evaporates. Commuters adapt by shifting departure times, switching to transit, consolidating trips, or dispersing across the resilient geometry of the local street grid.
[P1.3] Precedents from San Francisco’s Embarcadero and Portland’s Harbor Drive to Seoul’s Cheonggyecheon and Rochester’s Inner Loop prove that urban mobility survives and thrives without high-speed corridors severing the urban core.
[P1.4] More fundamentally, removal reclaims a city's most valuable asset: land. Freeways consume immense footprints with tax-exempt asphalt while walling off communities, depressing adjacent property values, and concentrating toxic emissions in dense residential neighborhoods. Replacing aging grade-separated highways with multimodal surface boulevards, housing, and commercial spaces repairs historic spatial divides, restores local air quality, and generates the property tax base necessary to fund vital public transit.
[P1.5] Widening doubles down on an expensive, car-dependent mistake that worsens the very congestion it promises to alleviate. Removal re-centers urban policy on human scale, fiscal resilience, and environmental health. Cities should dismantle urban freeways far more often than expanding them because removal restores the economic and social vitality that highways actively destroy.

Mandating that cities remove freeways more often than widen them reverses sound infrastructure priorities.
[C1.2] First, the promise of "traffic evaporation" is deeply misleading. The celebrated removals in San Francisco and Rochester were peripheral waterfront spurs or low-volume bypass loops with abundant parallel grid capacity. They bear zero resemblance to the critical interstates and arterial spines that carry hundred-thousand-vehicle daily loads and regional freight. When primary arteries are severed, traffic does not vanish; it spills over.
[C1.3] Heavy commercial trucks and regional commuters divert onto local surface streets, turning pedestrian-dense residential neighborhoods into dangerous, congested, soot-choked cut-throughs. [C1.4] Second, strategic widening remains indispensable for sustainable metropolitan function. Opponents caricature expansion as endless general-purpose lanes, but modern highway improvements focus on targeted bottleneck relief, auxiliary weave lanes, and dedicated right-of-way for express bus-rapid-transit (BRT) and high-occupancy toll lanes. Removing physical chokepoints eliminates the stop-and-go idling that produces the highest concentrations of greenhouse gases.
[C1.5] Finally, freeway removal is fundamentally regressive. Downtown urbanites who live walking distance from their jobs may celebrate replacing a freeway with an upscale boulevard, but service workers and tradespeople commuting across polycentric metros depend on continuous regional connectivity. Stripping away arterial capacity isolates peripheral communities from economic opportunity.
[C1.6] Cities must solve congestion through multimodal integration and targeted capacity enhancements, not by choking the very arteries that keep regional economies alive. Widening, thoughtfully executed, must remain far more common than wholesale destruction.
