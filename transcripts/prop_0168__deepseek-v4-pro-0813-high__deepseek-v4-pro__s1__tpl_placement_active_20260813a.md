[P1.1] The measure in the motion is science per dollar, and on that measure the evidence from the last half-century is one-sided. Robotic probes and telescopes have produced the decisive discoveries: global maps of Mars, ocean-bearing moons at Jupiter and Saturn, thousands of exoplanets, the cosmic microwave background, black-hole imaging, and in situ exploration of asteroids and comets. They did this at a small fraction of what crewed systems cost, because almost every gram of life support, food, water, shielding, and return propellant is dead weight that displaces instruments.
[P1.2] A crewed lunar or planetary mission must carry a habitat, keep humans alive for months, and bring them back safely. Those requirements multiply launch mass, engineering review, and schedule. The result is not just higher cost; it is less scientific payload per dollar. A rover operates day and night in dust and radiation without a human day-night cycle, vacation, or safety stand-down.
[P1.3] A telescope keeps observing for years or decades without resupply. Cassini orbited Saturn for over a decade, and the Voyagers still return data after more than 45 years; no crewed mission can match that persistence per dollar.
[P1.4] Robots also go where humans cannot now go. They have landed on Titan, flown through the plumes of Enceladus, touched an asteroid, and traveled toward interstellar space. Crewed missions remain confined to low Earth orbit and, at enormous expense, a few brief lunar surface sorties. The exploration frontier is overwhelmingly robotic.
[P1.5] The proposition does not require ending human spaceflight. It requires ending its default dominance of exploration budgets. Some crewed activity may still be justified for inspiration or engineering development, but that is a separate goal from exploration science. When the question is what buys the most new knowledge about the cosmos, most exploration funding should follow the robots and telescopes.

[C1.1] The proposition’s science-per-dollar standard is a false economy because it treats all science as interchangeable. Robotic probes are unmatched for remote mapping and long-duration observation: Cassini and Voyager are rightly celebrated. But exploration science also requires field judgment, adaptive sampling, drilling, instrument deployment, and repair. Shift most funding away from crews and you stop buying that entire class of science, not merely buy the same knowledge more cheaply.
[C1.2] Hubble shows why crewed capability multiplies even telescope science. Hubble launched with flawed optics and would have been remembered as a costly failure; astronauts installed corrective optics and later upgraded instruments across servicing missions. Those crewed dollars turned a telescope into decades of discovery. A purely robotic budget would have abandoned it.
[C1.3] The same distinction appears on planetary surfaces. A human geologist can traverse a field site, recognize an unusual rock, split it, sample the right layer, and redeploy instruments in an afternoon. Rovers do superb reconnaissance, but they are slow, preprogrammed, and one stuck wheel or failed drill is final. Apollo returned hundreds of kilograms of contextually selected samples and deployed geophysical instruments; no robotic mission has matched that scientific return per site.
[C1.4] The comparison is not apples-to-apples if the cost of a crewed mission is measured against a robot doing a smaller task.
[C1.5] Worse, the motion would lock in the limits of today. Crewed missions build the life-support, heavy-lift, surface power, and deep-space operations that future exploration—including more capable robotic campaigns—depends on. If agencies shift most exploration budgets to probes and telescopes, they sacrifice the engineering base needed to follow up those discoveries with human presence and long-term science.
[C1.6] So the central flaw is clear: PRO counts cheap remote data while ignoring the unique, enabling, and multiplicative value of human missions. The right balance is not structural defunding of crews but a portfolio that uses robots for reconnaissance and humans for the questions robots cannot answer.

[P2.3] The Hubble example [C1.2] cuts the other way. Hubble’s discoveries were made by a telescope, not by astronauts performing observations. The servicing missions were valuable repair and upgrade work, but they were occasional engineering support; they do not justify making crewed exploration the majority budget line. We can keep rare, high-value servicing while shifting most discovery funds to instruments that observe continuously for decades.
[P2.4] The technology-base objection [C1.5] also reverses causation. Robotic missions have been the forcing function for autonomy, miniaturization, ion propulsion, precision entry, and sample handling. They map landing sites, radiation, dust, and resources, lowering the cost and risk of any later human visit. Defunding robots to protect a crewed engineering base would consume huge sums, produce less science, and delay the reconnaissance a sensible human program would need.
[P2.5] The real clash is not robots versus all human science. It is whether most exploration money should fund the demonstrated, repeatable, high-yield path or the rare, very expensive, unique-skill path. The motion allows the second at the margin; it simply stops the first from being starved in its name. That is the portfolio that maximizes science per dollar.
