[P1.1] The current default in schools and universities treats accessibility as a special favor granted case-by-case to students who can prove need and navigate bureaucracy. This motion reverses that presumption: captions, recordings, and accessible materials should be standard unless someone can articulate a genuine pedagogical reason to withhold them. That shift is not bureaucratic preference—it determines who gets to learn and who is quietly excluded.
[P1.2] Consider what these tools actually do. Captions do not merely serve deaf or hard-of-hearing students; they help the student sitting in a crowded lecture hall with poor acoustics, the international student processing rapid English, and anyone trying to catch a term they missed. Recordings let students review complex derivations at their own pace, accommodate illness or caregiving responsibilities without falling behind, and revisit explanations that did not land the first time. Accessible materials—screen-reader compatible documents, alternative formats—determine whether a blind student can participate in the seminar at all.
[P1.3] These are not luxuries layered atop education; they are the infrastructure that makes learning possible for many.
[P1.4] The phrase "strong pedagogical reason" sets the bar correctly high. Mere inconvenience, cost concerns, or attachment to tradition do not qualify. If an instructor genuinely needs a live, unrecorded seminar for sensitive discussion, or if captioning would fundamentally distort an assessment of oral presentation skills, that is a pedagogical reason.
[P1.5] But absent that, the institution should have to justify exclusion, not the student justify access.
[P1.6] Under the current system, students with disabilities must disclose their conditions, request accommodations, and wait while materials remain inaccessible. The motion places the burden where it belongs: on the institution to explain why a student should be shut out. Education is a public good; its default architecture should be open to all.
