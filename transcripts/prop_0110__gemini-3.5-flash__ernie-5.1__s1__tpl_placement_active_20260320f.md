[P1.1] Public healthcare systems inherently deliver superior population health outcomes per dollar because they align economic incentives with long-term wellness and eliminate the massive friction costs of private market structures. To evaluate this proposition fairly, we must focus on efficiency: how effectively a system translates financial inputs into actual public health.
[P1.2] First, public systems leverage monopsony power to control costs. When a single public entity or coordinated public framework purchases pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and hospital services, it commands immense bargaining power. Private insurance systems, by contrast, are highly fragmented. This fragmentation strips away purchasing leverage, leading to artificially inflated prices for the exact same treatments and prescription drugs.
[P1.3] The result is a massive inflation of the dollars spent without any corresponding benefit to population health.
[P1.4] Second, public systems radically reduce administrative waste. A predominantly private insurance system requires a vast, expensive apparatus dedicated to billing, complex underwriting, marketing, and generating corporate profit margins. In contrast, public systems operate with streamlined administration, ensuring that a far higher percentage of every healthcare dollar is directed straight to clinical care rather than bureaucratic paper-pushing.
[P1.5] Third, public systems align financial incentives toward preventative care. Because a public system is responsible for a citizen’s health over their entire lifetime, it is economically rational to invest heavily in early detection, vaccination, and chronic disease management. Private insurers, operating on yearly contract cycles, expect high customer turnover. They have little financial incentive to fund interventions that only pay off decades down the road, as those benefits will likely accrue to a competitor.
[P1.6] This market failure leaves preventable illnesses to escalate into expensive, late-stage crises.
[P1.7] By minimizing administrative overhead, exercising purchasing power to lower prices, and prioritizing preventative health, public systems ensure that every dollar spent translates directly into longer, healthier lives.
