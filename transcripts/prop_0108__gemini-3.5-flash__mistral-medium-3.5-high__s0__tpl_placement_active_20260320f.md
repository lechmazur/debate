[P1.1] The United States is locked in a toxic political duopoly, not because of a flawed electorate, but because of a flawed electoral design. Our current winner-take-all (WTA) rules mathematically entrench a two-party monopoly. In a WTA system, a candidate can win with a bare plurality, incentivizing campaigns to mobilize their partisan base through fear and division rather than build broad coalitions. The ever-present "spoiler effect" forces voters into a defensive crouch, voting *against* the candidate they dread rather than *for* the candidate they actually support.
[P1.2] To break this polarization, the United States must adopt ranked-choice voting (RCV) or proportional representation (PR) for legislative elections.
[P1.3] First, these systems align representation with the actual will of the public. Under proportional systems, a party winning 30 percent of the vote earns 30 percent of the seats—not zero, as frequently happens under WTA rules. This ensures that diverse viewpoints, from moderate factions to distinct reform movements, have a voice, ending the artificial sorting of a diverse nation into just two hostile camps.
[P1.4] Second, RCV fundamentally reforms campaign incentives. Because candidates in RCV elections need second- and third-preference votes to win, they cannot afford to alienate their rivals' supporters. Mudslinging becomes a losing strategy. Instead, candidates must appeal to a broader majority.
[P1.5] We have already seen this dynamic succeed in states like Maine and Alaska, where RCV has fostered more civil campaigns, elevated consensus-builders, and diminished partisan gridlock.
[P1.6] To keep WTA is to accept permanent polarization, legislative paralysis, and a collapse of voter trust. Transitioning to RCV or proportional voting dismantles the duopoly, replaces negative partisanship with constructive coalition-building, and ensures our legislatures genuinely reflect the American people.
