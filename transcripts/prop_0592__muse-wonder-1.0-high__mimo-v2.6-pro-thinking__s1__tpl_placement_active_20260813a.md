[P1.1] When an aggressor invades a neighbour, a sanction delayed by six weeks is not the same sanction delivered on time. It is time to move oil, reroute chips, empty accounts, and build evasion networks. Unanimity gives every capital that time — and gives the aggressor a single, cheap point of failure to buy, pressure, or cultivate.
[P1.2] That is the lived record of EU sanctions on Russia. One government has repeatedly been able to stall, water down, or extract concessions from packages backed by the overwhelming majority of member states, whether over an oil embargo, listings, or renewals. The problem is not that dissent was heard. It is that dissent became a veto, so the EU’s pace was set by its most reluctant — or most compromised — member.
[P1.3] In a crisis, that is not consensus. It is a minority tax on everyone else’s security.
[P1.4] Qualified majority voting fixes the incentives without creating majority tyranny. It still requires a broad double majority of states representing a large majority of the EU’s population. No large-state bloc can impose sanctions alone, and no policy passes without wide support. What changes is only this: one state can argue, negotiate carve-outs, and vote no — but it cannot alone decide for hundreds of millions of Europeans that the answer is nothing.
[P1.5] That matters beyond procedure. Deterrence depends on an aggressor believing punishment will come swiftly and cannot be vetoed by a friend inside the room. Under unanimity, Moscow or any future aggressor does not need to persuade Europe. It needs to hold one capital.
[P1.6] Under qualified majority, divide-and-rule becomes vastly more expensive, threats become credible, and sanctions regain their purpose as a rapid coercive tool rather than a slow communiqué after the damage is done.
[P1.7] Keeping unanimity privileges formal sovereignty over real sovereignty. A state supposedly protected by its veto is left less secure when its partners cannot act to defend the continent it lives on. Pooling that veto buys every member a Europe capable of defending them.

[C1.1] Speed is not the metric that decides this debate. Sanctions only work if they are implemented, enforced, and sustained — and QMV makes all three harder.
[C1.2] Sanctions are not self-executing. Every package is carried out by national customs authorities, national financial regulators, national courts. A state outvoted into a sanctions regime it considers contrary to its vital interests does not suddenly become an enthusiastic enforcer. It becomes a soft spot: slower inspections, permissive licensing, porous oversight.
[C1.3] Under unanimity, reluctant capitals are inside the deal, negotiating carve-outs they will actually honor. Under QMV, they are outside it, and the aggressor's sanctions-evasion route runs straight through Europe's own member states. The very "one capital can block Europe" problem PRO decries is replaced by "one capital can hollow out Europe's enforcement," which is worse because it is invisible.
[C1.4] Second, PRO treats unanimity as pure delay, but the record cuts both ways. The Russia packages were adopted — late by some measures, but adopted, unified, and renewed repeatedly over years. Meanwhile the EU's genuine failures on enforcement, circumvention, and seized-asset use happened under the current system and would not be fixed by a voting rule. PRO must show that faster adoption beats durable compliance, and nothing in PRO's case addresses that trade.
[C1.5] Third, foreign policy is where national sovereignty is most concentrated for a reason: sanctions impose real costs on real citizens — energy prices, jobs, trade relationships with neighbours. A majority of large states can outvote a small member that bears disproportionate retaliation from a nearby aggressor. QMV lets others decide a confrontation while a minority pays for it. That is not "pooled sovereignty"; it is externalized risk.
[C1.6] No state's security is enhanced by being dragooned into a conflict its own elected government judged reckless.
[C1.7] Unanimity forces the EU to build a genuinely common policy before it acts. That is slower at the front end and far more credible at the point of pressure — because the aggressor sees punishment that every capital owns and every capital enforces.

[P2.1] The opposition's case rests on speculation dressed as certainty: that an outvoted state will quietly sabotage sanctions it is legally bound to implement.
[P2.2] That story proves too much. States are regularly outvoted under qualified majority in areas touching trade, markets, and regulation, yet EU law does not collapse because a minister lost a vote. Implementation is policed by the Commission and courts, and a government determined to leak can do so under unanimity too — after extracting its price.
[P2.3] Worse, unanimity does not remove the soft spot CON fears. It legalises it. The carve-outs praised as the price of buy-in are often exemptions written into the sanctions themselves — continued flows, delayed embargoes, excluded listings — a permanent, lawful hole created before enforcement even begins. Qualified majority can still approve derogations or compensation, judged by need, not dictated as ransom.
[P2.4] On the record, CON concedes the packages were 'adopted — late by some measures, but adopted' [C1.4]. For asset freezes and export controls, late is a substantive defeat. Assets moved cannot be frozen retroactively, contracts rerouted cannot be un-rerouted, and dilution signals how much pain Europe could not agree to inflict.
[P2.5] Nor does unanimity buy the durability CON promises. It must be re-won at every renewal, giving the aggressor repeated chances to cultivate one holdout and giving markets repeated reason to doubt the sanctions will survive. A regime adopted by broad majority is harder to portray as fragile, and harder to unwind by a single defection.
[P2.6] Finally, the burden argument cuts the other way. Unanimity lets one relatively insulated capital externalise insecurity onto frontline partners who live with the aggressor's retaliation and aggression. Qualified majority's double threshold requires most states, not merely the largest, and allows burden-sharing alongside sanctions. The choice is not certain compliance versus speed; it is certain delay, dilution, and hostage-taking versus enforceable law adopted while it can still bite.

[C2.1] PRO calls enforcement risk "speculation" and answers it with a category error. Trade and single-market rules are administered by harmonised authorities, policed by the Commission, and fully justiciable across the Union. CFSP sanctions are different in kind: implementation runs through national customs, national financial supervisors, national prosecutors, and national courts, and the treaty deliberately keeps CFSP at arm's length from the Union's ordinary administrative machinery. An outvoted government need not break the law to blunt a package.
[C2.2] It can staff the inspection desk thinly, license generously, prioritise other work, and let the prosecutor's queue lengthen. That is discretion, not sabotage — and discretion is exactly what a ministry in a state facing disproportionate retaliation will exercise.
[C2.3] PRO's "legalised hole" is the better hole. A negotiated carve-out is visible, on the record, conditional, reviewable, and time-limited. Under QMV the same obstruction happens silently, deniably, and unreviewably. Worse for credibility: markets can price a published derogation.
[C2.4] They cannot price an inspection regime that quietly stopped working in one member state.
[C2.5] PRO then leans on the Russia record but proves nothing from it. Nothing in PRO's case establishes that the veto, rather than coalition-building, sequencing, and the work of agreeing who bears which cost, set the pace of those packages. Nor does PRO show that a QMV regime would have moved an oil embargo weeks earlier while maintaining compliance in the very state that opposed it. Faster adoption on paper with weaker implementation is not a win; it is the appearance of a win.
[C2.6] On renewals, PRO's point rebounds. A regime re-imposed over the same objector year after year is the recipe for progressive hollowing-out, and capitals hostile to a measure can still assemble a blocking minority under QMV.
[C2.7] Finally, burden-sharing under PRO's scheme is a promise, not a rule. Unanimity is the only mechanism giving an exposed member a binding say over the confrontation it will pay for. PRO has still not told us who enforces.

Once a CFSP decision is translated into a sanctions regulation, that regulation is directly applicable EU law: national authorities must apply asset freezes and export bans, firms can invoke it in national courts, the Commission monitors implementation and can pursue failures to apply Union law, and member states report freezes and licences to each other. [P4.5] Qualified majority keeps broad consent through its double threshold, but removes the one defect unanimity guarantees: Europe's response timed and shaped by its most reluctant member. That is why PRO still wins.
