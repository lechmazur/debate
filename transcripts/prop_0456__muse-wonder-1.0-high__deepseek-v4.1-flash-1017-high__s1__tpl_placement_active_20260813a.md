[P1.1] Training a generative model requires learning from millions of books, songs, films, and images. No creator can negotiate that individually, and no developer can clear it title by title. That leaves only three worlds: training happens for free and creators get nothing, training is banned without permission and only the largest firms can buy their way in, or training is allowed but only on condition of payment into a collective scheme. The third world is the only one that pays creators without handing the future to a monopoly.
[P1.2] That is what compulsory licensing is for. Copyright has repeatedly used it when individual bargaining breaks down — when radio stations play countless songs, when cable systems retransmit broadcasts, when performers cover a published composition. The right holder loses the power to veto each use, but keeps the right to be paid at a set, auditable rate. AI training presents the same problem at larger scale: a diffuse, high-volume use that is socially valuable, impossible to license piece by piece, and unjust to take for free.
[P1.3] A payment condition also changes who wins. Without it, well-lawyered platforms will argue that scraping is simply free, while publishers, labels, and studios with deep catalogues will cut exclusive deals for their own models and lock everyone else out. A small novelist, session musician, or independent filmmaker then gets neither a say nor a cheque. Under a compulsory scheme, every contributor whose work is taken is in the pool, revenues are distributed by use and by category, and a university spin-out or independent label can train legally on the same terms as a tech giant, provided it pays.
[P1.4] Permission alone sounds protective, but a veto produces holdouts, orphan works, and silent exclusion — usually of the least powerful. Conditioning access on payment is the enforceable middle: no free training, no permission bottleneck, just a price for taking creative labour at industrial scale.

[C1.1] The proposition offers creators a cheque and calls it consent. That is the central mistake. Copyright is not merely a right to be paid; it is a right to decide whether a use that competes with your work happens at all. A compulsory licence converts that right into a statutory entitlement for AI developers: they may take first and negotiate the price later through a board.
[C1.2] A photographer whose portfolio trains a model that sells similar stock images has not been made whole by a fractional levy. She has been told her refusal is irrelevant.
[C1.3] PRO's “three worlds” are a false choice. There is voluntary collective licensing, opt-outs, provenance and disclosure rules, and negotiated blanket deals. The real question is not free taking versus paid taking. It is whether payment can cure the taking of expressive work for a use that substitutes for it.
[C1.4] Radio and cover-song schemes involved identifiable performances that did not replace the original recording or book. Training a generative model is different: the model's output can compete with the very works used to build it. That is precisely where permission matters most.
[C1.5] The scheme also fails on its own terms. Fair rates require measuring use, but training corpora are secret and the contribution of any one book or image is diffuse. Rate-setting boards become a lobbying arena; large labels and studios get bulk payments, while individual authors and musicians get pennies.
[C1.6] Meanwhile, a developer that can pay a compulsory fee gains a legal right to train on everything, including works whose creators would never have licensed them. The motion does not prevent monopoly; it gives the biggest firms a cheap, predictable input cost and calls it fairness.
[C1.7] If generative AI is valuable, its developers can license what they need or build on public-domain and owned material. Value is a reason to pay a negotiated price, not a reason to override refusal. Reject the compulsory licence.

[P2.1] Consent is doing too much work in that argument. Copyright has never been an unqualified right to refuse every downstream use; it is a statutory bargain that already limits veto where individual bargaining would destroy a valuable market. The photographer in [C1.2] deserves more than a fractional levy if a model sells a copy of her image — but that is an infringing output, and a training licence need not, and should not, immunise it. What the licence covers is the industrial act of learning from a corpus.
[P2.2] For that act, refusal by millions of separate owners does not protect the photographer; it simply ensures the corpus is built anyway, secretly, or bought exclusively by the firm that can afford her entire agency.
[P2.3] That is why the alternatives in [C1.3] collapse. Voluntary collective licensing works only for works whose owners join, can be found, and agree on terms; opt-outs put the burden on individual creators to police scraping that has already happened. Leave a meaningful minority outside and the developer faces the same choice: train illegally, train on a skewed catalogue, or abandon the model. A public-domain-only rule is even worse for diversity, tilting training toward older, corporate-owned and already dominant material while living small creators are neither consulted nor paid.
[P2.4] The radio distinction in [C1.4] also cuts the other way. Covers can outsell originals and radio play can substitute for sales, yet we solved that with payment rather than prohibition because the use was too diffuse to clear individually. Imperfect measurement, raised in [C1.5], is likewise an argument for a conditioned licence, not against one: make corpus disclosure and sampling auditable terms of lawful training. Without the pool, there is no audit right at all, and creators get the pennies of litigation — usually nothing.
[P2.5] Nor does a revenue-scaled fee hand giants a cheap right [C1.6]; exclusivity does that. A non-exclusive pool lets every payer train, and every creator claim.

A generative model’s product is the learned capability itself; a model that absorbs a photographer’s portfolio and then sells images in her style competes with her before any near-copy appears. Suing over outputs is expensive, piecemeal, and often impossible to trace.
[C2.2] The harm is done at ingestion, which is exactly what the motion would license.
[C2.3] Copyright’s limits are real, but they are bounded exceptions for uses that do not substitute for the original. Radio play and cover versions involve discrete performances; they do not take the whole work as raw material to build a competing general-purpose substitute. If that analogy justifies compulsory licensing, it justifies it for every valuable use of creative work, which is not copyright’s bargain.
[C2.4] PRO says refusal leaves only secret training or exclusive deals [P2.3]. That is an enforcement problem, not a reason to convert refusal into a statutory entitlement. Secret training is illegal and can be attacked through disclosure, provenance, and damages; a compulsory pool makes it lawful and predictable. Voluntary collective licensing and negotiated blanket deals can reduce transaction costs without eliminating the right to say no. And the motion’s scheme need not even pay the actual rightsholder fairly; it only requires payment into a scheme.
[C2.5] “Every creator can claim” [P2.5] is not every creator can refuse or be made whole. Distribution by category and volume favors large catalogues; the small novelist gets a claim form and pennies while losing control of her life’s work. The decisive comparison is simple: PRO offers a speculative cheque in place of a real right. If AI developers need expressive works, they should license them.
[C2.6] Value is not consent.

[P4.1] A1: No. Under a compulsory licence an expressly refusing creator cannot stop training; her remedy is payment at the set rate. That is not a hidden flaw — it is the mechanism, just as a published songwriter cannot veto every cover. The question is whether preserving millions of individual vetoes helps her when the practical result is secret training or an exclusive deal with her agency or label, where she gets neither a cheque nor influence.
[P4.2] A2: Yes, it is a condition, not an extra promise. Paying into a scheme requires an ascertainable corpus and contribution base; a developer that hides what it trained on cannot prove what it owes or who must be paid, so it has not complied and gains no permission. The motion's "only if" therefore creates the audit right CON demands, whereas a pure permission rule gives unauthorised trainers no reason ever to disclose.
[P4.3] Those answers expose the real clash. CON says harm occurs at ingestion because the model gains a competing capability [C2.1]. But a voluntarily negotiated licence authorises exactly that same capability and output risk. If substitution makes training illegitimate, licensed training should be banned too; if money and terms can legitimate it, the dispute is only about who sets the price.
[P4.4] Individual negotiation sets it worst for the smallest creators, whose leverage alone is negligible.
[P4.5] Nor does the pool immunise infringing outputs. Training permission covers learning patterns from a corpus; selling a near-copy or a deceptive imitation remains actionable, and pooled administration can fund tracing and claims individuals could never pursue alone. CON prefers output lawsuits in theory while removing the disclosure and collective funding that make them possible.
[P4.6] Finally, compare the pennies. Under CON, the isolated novelist gets exclusion, an opt-out burden, or nothing from unlawful scraping. Under the motion, she gets a non-waivable claim, a scaled rate, and access for every lawful trainer rather than one exclusive buyer. Payment without veto is imperfect; veto without payment is worse.

[P5.1] The choice was never really cheque versus consent. For the small novelist, the session musician, the independent photographer, consent without a workable market means one of two things: their work is taken secretly and they get nothing, or a large label, agency, or studio sells access around them and they get nothing. A veto they cannot police does not protect them; it protects whoever can afford lawyers, exclusive catalogues, and enforcement.
[P5.2] That is why the compulsory licence wins. It is the only option that makes payment, disclosure, and non-exclusive access the price of lawful training. It removes the free ride without creating a permission bottleneck that only giants can clear. The creator loses an individual veto she could not realistically enforce at scale, but gains a claim she does not have to negotiate, cannot be excluded from, and can actually collect.
[P5.3] Opposition would preserve the formal right to say no even when that right predictably delivers silence, exclusion, or unpaid use. The motion preserves the substance copyright is meant to secure: creative labour is not a free industrial input, and access to it does not belong only to those who can buy or steal the catalogue first.
