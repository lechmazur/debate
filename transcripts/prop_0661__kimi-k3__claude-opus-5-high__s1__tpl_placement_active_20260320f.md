[P1.1] A warehouse worker is fired because a system clocked her packing two seconds under quota. A driver wakes up deactivated — no human to call, no reason given. Neither can contest the decision, because neither was told what was being measured. That is the world the CON side defends.
[P1.2] The proposition is deliberately modest: not a ban, not prior approval — disclosure. Employers must reveal what monitoring and scoring systems exist, what they measure, and how scores feed decisions, to workers and to regulators. Three reasons follow.
[P1.3] First, contestation. Every workplace right — appealing a dismissal, challenging a review, negotiating conditions — presupposes knowing the basis of the decision. You cannot appeal a metric you have never seen or correct data you never knew was collected. Gig deactivations show the endpoint: livelihoods ended by a score the worker is structurally unable to question.
[P1.4] Disclosure turns workers from objects of measurement into parties who can answer back.
[P1.5] Second, accuracy. These systems err: keystroke counters cannot distinguish thinking from idling; productivity trackers penalize the employee mentoring a colleague; break-timing systems punish medical conditions. Errors persist precisely because the scored person never sees the score. Credit reports already come with a legal right to inspect and dispute them — a worker's livelihood deserves at least the protection we give a loan application.
[P1.6] Third, enforcement. Anti-discrimination law, wage rules, and organizing protections are unenforceable against systems nobody must reveal. Screeners that filter out older applicants, hours shaved off records, monitoring aimed at union activity — none of it is auditable in the dark. Disclosure is the precondition for every other labor protection.
[P1.7] The objections answer themselves. Trade secrets? We require disclosure of chemicals, financials, and ingredients without publishing formulas; a system's logic is not its source code. Burden?
[P1.8] A firm sophisticated enough to log every keystroke can afford to say so.
[P1.9] The question this motion reduces to: may systems that can end a career operate invisibly? Every CON argument must ultimately defend that invisibility.

Disclosure regimes select for harsher management, not gentler.

[P4.2] A2: One step: knowing what to subpoena. Entry powers locate the door, not what is behind it; no inspector can demand records of a system whose existence need never be revealed, and no pattern claim survives without naming the practice. And their tribunal has the same drafting problem they pin on disclosure: the employer writes the cause justification too — but post-firing, for one worker who can afford to sue, usually sealed by settlement.
