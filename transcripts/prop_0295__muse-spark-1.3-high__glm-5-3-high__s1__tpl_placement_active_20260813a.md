[P1.1] Returning an object is not the same as returning what was taken.
[P1.2] Colonial taking stole more than wood, bronze, and gold. It stole the revenue, institutions, and generations of expertise that would have protected those objects had they stayed. European museums then spent a century building climate labs, training programs, and tourist income around looted collections while source communities were denied the same investment. To ship a crate home and declare the debt paid freezes that inequality in place.
[P1.3] True restitution must repair the conditions for survival, not just change the address on the box.
[P1.4] That is practical, not sentimental. A carved mask or manuscript that survived decades in a London store needs stable humidity, pest control, secure storage, and trained conservators to survive in Lagos, Accra, or Addis Ababa — especially as heat and flooding intensify. Without funded conservation, return becomes a slow-motion loss: cracking ivory, corroding metal, insect-eaten textiles. A former power that returns an artifact knowing it may deteriorate has not done justice; it has outsourced neglect.
[P1.5] Provenance research and museum capacity complete the same logic. Most colonial records are incomplete, mislabeled, or silent about which shrine, family, or town was looted. Uncovering that history requires archivists, oral historians, language skills, and lab time on both continents. Likewise, strong local museums — storage, documentation, digitization, curatorial jobs — let returned heritage be studied and seen at home rather than locked away for safety.
[P1.6] This is obligation, not charity, and it need not mean control. Funds can be endowments directed by receiving institutions, with joint research on equal terms. Justice does not end when the plane lands; it ends when the homecoming can last.
