[P1.1] A four-day workweek pilot delivers predictable, universal structural gains that unlimited paid-time-off (PTO) cannot match, because the latter is a culture-dependent perk that fails to address the root causes of burnout and disengagement. The core flaw of unlimited PTO is that it does not reduce baseline weekly work hours or eliminate implicit pressure to prioritize work over rest. Multiple industry surveys find that employees at firms with unlimited PTO take 10-15% less vacation time than those with standard accrued PTO, as workers fear being perceived as less committed or leaving their teams short-staffed.
[P1.2] This gap is starker for junior employees, caregivers, and workers from marginalized groups, who face disproportionate professional penalties for taking time off. Unlimited PTO also does nothing to lighten actual workloads: an employee can take three weeks off, but will return to a full five-day backlog, often forcing them to cram more work into their remaining days or check emails while on leave to stay afloat. In contrast, a properly structured four-day workweek pilot—offering 100% pay for 80% of hours, condensed into four days—delivers a guaranteed, recurring reduced work week with no ambiguity.
[P1.3] Large-scale 2022 UK trials of more than 60 firms found that 71% of participating employees reported reduced burnout, 60% reported higher productivity, and 9 out of 10 participating firms said they would retain the policy long-term, with staff turnover falling or remaining stable. Unlike unlimited PTO, the four-day pilot does not require employees to navigate office politics to access rest: the day off is automatic, universal, and eliminates the constant context switching of a five-day grind that erodes well-being over time.
[P1.4] For retention, the four-day pilot is a clear, unambiguous benefit that signals an employer’s commitment to work-life balance in a way unlimited PTO never can. When a company rolls out a four-day pilot, it makes a concrete, collective investment in its workforce that applies to every employee regardless of role or tenure, eliminating the fear of backlash for taking time off that plagues unlimited PTO policies.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to compare a pilot — a temporary experiment — against a standing policy, and that asymmetry is fatal. A pilot is by definition provisional and revocable. When a company dangles a four-day week for six months and then quietly retreats to five, it doesn't merely return to baseline; it inflicts loss aversion at full force. Employees who reorganized childcare, commutes, and lives around the gift now experience its withdrawal as a betrayal.
[C1.2] Pilots that fail or stall are retention poison, while unlimited PTO is a durable commitment employees can plan around for years.
[C1.3] Second, the four-day week doesn't shrink workloads — it compresses them. Clients still call five days a week. Deadlines don't observe Fridays off. In practice, employees cram five days of output into four longer, denser days: fewer breaks, back-to-back meetings, evenings bleeding into "off" days.
[C1.4] That intensification is its own burnout engine. Worse, the model is rigid: it hands everyone the same day off whether or not it matches their lives. Unlimited PTO delivers rest where well-being is actually made or broken — the sick child, the dying parent, the mental health crisis, the three-week trip that genuinely restores someone. A standing Friday off cannot cover a Tuesday emergency.
[C1.5] Third, four-day pilots are structurally inequitable. Hospitals, support desks, retail floors, and client-facing teams often cannot participate, so the pilot creates a visible two-tier workforce — a guaranteed way to drive resentment and turnover among precisely the frontline workers most at risk of leaving. Unlimited PTO scales across every function.
[C1.6] Finally, the cited trial results come from self-selected firms that volunteered, expected success, and reported their own outcomes. That tells us little about the median employer. Unlimited PTO's known flaw — under-use — is an implementation problem, fixable with minimum-leave mandates and leadership modeling. The four-day pilot's flaws — impermanence, intensification, rigidity, and inequity — are baked into its structure.
[C1.7] Fixable beats fundamental.
