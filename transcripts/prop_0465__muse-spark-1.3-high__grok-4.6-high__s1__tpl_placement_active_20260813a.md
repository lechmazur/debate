[P1.1] A river cannot walk into court to stop its own poisoning. A mining corporation, a legal fiction, can. That tells you everything about why our law fails nature. Granting legal personhood to major rivers and ecosystems simply corrects that rigged standing: it gives the living system a voice, a guardian, and the right not to be treated as mere plumbing for whoever holds the permit.
[P1.2] Without it, protection depends on underfunded agencies trading fines for permission to destroy.
[P1.3] This matters most when Indigenous or local communities ask for it. They are not seeking a poetic metaphor. They are asserting a relationship of care and responsibility stretching back generations, and asking the state to stop licensing destruction over their heads. Their knowledge holds what distant regulators miss: when spawning fails, when floodplains need to breathe.
[P1.4] To deny that request is to repeat colonial theft in environmental language: we took the land, now we will take the right to protect it.
[P1.5] Critics warn this will complicate infrastructure. Good. Dams that dry deltas, diversions that kill fisheries, mines that leave toxins for a century should be complicated. Personhood does not ban development; it forces it to negotiate rather than take.
[P1.6] Instead of fragmented permits where pollution is just a fee, a guardian must answer a simple question: can the river survive this intact? That friction prevents the collapses cheap approvals create: dead fisheries, undrinkable cities, worsened floods.
[P1.7] This is neither radical nor unworkable. Law already grants personhood to corporations, trusts, and municipalities. New Zealand recognized the Whanganui River at Maori initiative, Colombia's courts did the same for the Atrato. Projects continued, but on terms that keep the system alive.
[P1.8] Infrastructure delayed is regrettable; a river killed is irreversible. When those closest to the water ask for that shield, justice and survival both demand we grant it.

[C1.1] Legal personhood for a river does not give nature a voice. It gives a handful of guardians a veto and pretends the river asked for it. Governments should refuse that grant even when Indigenous or local communities request it. The request does not answer who speaks, what the river's "interest" is, or who pays when infrastructure stalls.
[C1.2] A company is a legal person with owners, officers, and assets that can be seized. A river has none of that. "Can the river survive this intact?" is a political question dressed as ecology. Intact compared with what year?
[C1.3] For salmon or for flood storage? For the village upstream or the city that drinks from it? Personhood does not resolve those clashes. It moves them into court, where a guardian sues in the river's name while remaining unaccountable to voters, ratepayers, and neighboring peoples on the same waterway.
[C1.4] The motion's kicker—"even if it complicates infrastructure"—is fatal. Infrastructure is how we keep toxins out of taps, hold back floods, store water through drought, and build grids that replace coal. Delay is not virtuous friction. It raises costs, kills projects by process, and leaves people with the same pollution plus less capacity to adapt.
[C1.5] The losers are often the same communities: fishers needing roads, farmers needing irrigation, towns needing sewage plants instead of raw discharge.
[C1.6] Indigenous care deserves real legal weight: title, co-management, consultation with teeth, tighter pollution rules. Those tools name the people owed a duty. Personhood names a fiction, and it can pit one community's request against another's need for power or flood control. A one-river political settlement is not a standing duty to grant personhood on demand.
[C1.7] Weak agencies, not missing birth certificates, let rivers die. Strengthen the standards. Do not invent an immortal plaintiff that can complicate every public work until a metaphor outranks the public.

[P2.1] The claim that personhood hands “a handful of guardians a veto” pretends the river asked for it misses how law works. Every legal person speaks through appointed humans. A corporation’s CEO was never elected by voters or ratepayers either. We accept that fiction because it organizes responsibility.
[P2.2] A river guardian is more accountable, not less: nominated with the requesting community and the state, bound to ecological health, answerable and removable in court.
[P2.3] What is the river’s interest? “Intact compared with what year?” Law answers open questions every day: a child’s best interests, reasonable care, fiduciary duty. Scientists and elders can testify whether a diversion starves a delta or a mine poisons spawning beds. That judgment is difficult, but the present answer — whatever the permit says — is not neutral. It is a license to take dressed up as expertise.
[P2.4] Nor does this move conflict into unaccountable courts. It moves conflict out of backroom permit trades where weak agencies sell destruction cheaply. Co-management and consultation sound protective until a minister overrides them. Without standing, communities get a seat at the table but no power to stop the table being sawed apart.
[P2.5] Personhood turns advice into an enforceable duty not to kill the system that sustains everyone on it.
[P2.6] The infrastructure panic inverts reality. A sewage plant that ends raw discharge, a flood defense that works with a floodplain, a grid that replaces coal serves the river’s health. Guardians have no reason to block it. What gets complicated is the dam that dries downstream villages, the mine that leaves toxins for a century.
[P2.7] And who pays for stalled megaprojects? Compare who pays when they proceed unchecked: fishers with dead fisheries, cities with undrinkable taps, farmers with salted fields.
[P2.8] Polluters love standards without plaintiffs. When those closest to the water ask for this shield, refusing it in their name is paternalism. Complication is negotiation. Killing a river to save paperwork is not development.

[C2.1] The corporation analogy collapses on contact. A company has shareholders who lose money, officers who can be fired, and assets a court can seize. That is why the fiction organizes responsibility. A river has none of those.
[C2.2] Saying a guardian is "more accountable" because they are nominated with the requesting community simply collapses the river into that community's preference. Neighboring towns, downstream cities, and Indigenous groups who never asked for personhood still get bound by a plaintiff nobody elected and nobody can vote out.
[C2.3] "Bound to ecological health" does not answer intact-compared-with-what. A child's best interests concern one human life. A river's "survival intact" is a choice among salmon, flood storage, irrigation, hydropower, and drinking water. Scientists and elders can testify on both sides of every diversion.
[C2.4] Personhood does not resolve that clash; it lets one appointed team sue in a name that sounds like nature itself.
[C2.5] Moving conflict from permits to court is not a virtue. Courts issue injunctions and years of discovery; they do not build sewage plants. Co-management can be overridden. The fix is statutory standards with teeth and rights for affected people, not an immortal plaintiff.
[C2.6] Personhood litigates "the system" even when a treatment plant, levee, or grid must alter the channel to stop raw discharge or replace coal. They say guardians will not block works that serve river health. That holds only if the guardian's "intact" matches the public's need for pipes and floodworks. Delay is not negotiation with a river.
[C2.7] It is an immortal docket while towns wait for clean pipes.
[C2.8] Refusing a request is not paternalism. The state already owes those communities title, consultation with teeth, and pollution limits they can enforce in their own names. Granting personhood on request hands them a fiction that binds every other user of the same water. Polluters should face named plaintiffs.
[C2.9] The public should not lose adaptive infrastructure to a metaphor.
