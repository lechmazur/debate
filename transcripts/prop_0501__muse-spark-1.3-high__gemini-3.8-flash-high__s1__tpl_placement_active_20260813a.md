[P1.1] Price stability that ignores house prices is no stability at all. For most households the dominant price in life is not coffee or televisions but a home, yet conventional inflation targeting looks away. Owner-occupied housing is largely excluded from consumer indices or smoothed into slow-moving rents, so central banks could declare victory on inflation while house prices soared dangerously. That blindness is a design flaw, not a technical quirk.
[P1.2] The flaw is destabilizing because housing is not just another asset. It is collateral for the banking system, the main store of household wealth, and the engine of debt cycles. When cheap money inflates homes, borrowing expands against rising collateral, consumption surges on paper wealth, construction overextends. When the turn comes, balance sheets collapse, banks retrench, and recessions become deeper and longer.
[P1.3] The late-2000s crash and the pandemic-era surge followed by painful rate shocks both showed the same pattern: consumer inflation was quiet until it was not, while housing was screaming.
[P1.4] An explicit housing stability mandate would force honesty. Central banks already drive asset prices through interest rates and bond and mortgage purchases. Pretending those effects are side-effects lets policymakers inflate bubbles without accountability. A published sensitivity to sustained, broad house-price misalignment — lean modestly against overheating, avoid crushing needed corrections — would anchor expectations, discourage speculative leverage, and make tightening earlier and gentler.
[P1.5] It is also a matter of fairness. When asset inflation enriches owners while wages stagnate and first-time buyers are priced out, claims of stable prices lose democratic legitimacy.
[P1.6] Critics warn this overloads monetary policy. The opposite is true: ignoring housing forces far harsher interventions later, with mass unemployment and bailouts. Including it protects the very goals inflation targeting claims to serve: sustainable growth, livable costs, and a financial system that serves families rather than betting against them.

[C1.2] The fatal flaw in PRO’s case is the violation of Tinbergen’s Rule: a policymaker cannot hit two divergent targets with one tool. Consider the paralysis this mandate creates. When consumer inflation is subdued but housing prices rise—often driven by demographic shifts, localized shortages, or supply bottlenecks—what is the central bank supposed to do? If it hikes interest rates to suppress house prices, it crushes manufacturing, suppresses wage growth, and throws people out of work just to deflate real estate.
[C1.3] Worse, higher interest rates choke off financing for new residential construction, directly exacerbating the housing shortage that drove prices up in the first place.
[C1.4] Furthermore, the diagnostic hurdle is insurmountable. PRO promises central banks can merely "lean modestly" [P1.4], but modest rate nudges do not deter speculative manias; only severe, punishing rate hikes pop asset bubbles, and those same hikes inevitably manufacture recessions.
[C1.5] PRO’s concerns regarding financial contagion and debt cycles [P1.2] are legitimate, but interest rates are the wrong cure. Regulators already possess targeted instruments: macroprudential policy. Loan-to-value caps, debt-to-income limits, and countercyclical capital buffers insulate the banking system and restrict speculative mortgage leverage directly, without putting millions of workers on the unemployment line.
[C1.6] Explicitly targeting asset prices overloads the central bank, muddies its mandate, and sacrifices broad employment to police property values. True stability comes from keeping monetary policy disciplined on consumer prices, leaving asset-market risks to targeted regulation. We must not sacrifice ordinary workers to manage real estate portfolios.
