[P1.1] Nobody signs up for a subscription to rent access to a library that burns books when you aren't looking. That is the fundamental broken promise at the heart of this issue. When a streaming service commissions an original show or film, they do not just produce content. They convince hundreds of thousands of people to sign up on the promise that work will be available.
[P1.2] They convince actors, writers, and crew to accept lower upfront pay on the understanding their work will exist and be seen. Then, often just 18 months later, they delete it entirely—never released on physical media, never licensed elsewhere, just erased forever, to write down the asset for a tax break. This is not normal market behaviour. No other media industry ever operated this way.
[P1.3] When a studio made a film in the DVD era, they could not unmake every copy of it to save on tax. They could not pull every theatre print and burn the negatives just as an accounting trick. This rule would not force services to run expensive advertising, or put old titles on the front page. It only requires that if you made the work, you leave it up, searchable, accessible to anyone paying for the service for a minimum ten year term.
[P1.4] That is the absolute minimum bar for the product they are selling. Do not accept claims this will raise prices. When companies erase content, they never pass those tax savings back to subscribers. They keep all the benefit.
[P1.5] All this rule changes is that subscribers get the thing they already paid for, creators get the audience they were promised, and our shared culture does not get quietly erased for spreadsheet tricks.

[C1.1] The promise PRO describes is one nobody actually made. When you subscribe to a streaming service, the offer is explicit: rotating access to a catalog that changes, not a permanent personal library. That's why it costs a few dollars a month instead of the price of a box set. PRO wants to rewrite that bargain by force—and that has consequences they're not counting.
[C1.2] Here is the core problem with the motion: it confuses preservation with forced commercial hosting. Leaving a film "searchable and accessible" on a particular paid platform does nothing to protect our shared culture. The day that company restructures or folds, the title vanishes anyway. If you genuinely care about works being "erased forever," the answer is a deposit requirement—a copy lodged with a national archive or library, or a physical-media and licensing pathway.
[C1.3] That actually saves the work. PRO's mandate saves nothing; it just chains one commercial entity to indefinitely warehousing a title on its own dime.
[C1.4] And that dime is real. Keeping a title "up" is not free storage. Music synchronization licenses expire and must be renewed. Residuals and certain guild payments can accrue with continued availability.
[C1.5] Legal and compliance liability runs the whole term. PRO asserts costs won't reach subscribers, but you cannot legislate away an expense—it either raises prices or, more likely, kills projects before they exist.
[C1.6] That's the decisive harm. Faced with a ten-year mandatory liability on every original, a service rationally greenlights fewer originals, especially the risky, niche, culturally valuable ones PRO claims to champion. The safe franchise gets made; the strange debut film does not. PRO's rule protects works that already exist by strangling the works that don't yet.
[C1.7] So the comparison is simple. PRO offers a costly mandate that fails at preservation and chills new creation. The better path—archival deposit and licensing freedom—achieves the actual goal without forcing companies to host commercial inventory forever. Reject the motion.
