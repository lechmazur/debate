[P1.1] Social media competition fails when the product is not merely an app, but access to everyone already using it. [P1.2] Interoperability breaks this lock-in without requiring anyone to abandon existing contacts. A user could adopt a privacy-focused messenger while still reaching friends on a dominant platform, or choose a service with different accessibility tools and moderation while carrying over authorized connections. [C1.1] Mandating interoperability sounds like freedom, but it forces the most sensitive part of social media — who knows whom, and who may reach whom — out of a controlled system and into every approved stranger's system. That is not portability for a user. It is compulsory exposure for everyone in that user's graph.
[C1.2] A social graph is not personal property like photos. It reveals other people: who friended whom, blocked whom, joined a closed support group, or kept a contact private to escape an abuser or stalker. One user's authorization cannot authorize a third party to reconstruct and message through those relationships. Revocation is illusory once copies proliferate, and data minimization is unverifiable against a service whose business is analyzing connections.
[C1.3] Interoperable messaging also breaks protections closed systems use to contain harm. End-to-end encryption, spam filtering, child-safety checks, and blocking assume a platform can control clients and cut off bad actors. Open the gates and those controls fragment: a harasser banned on one service messages through another, spam floods across bridges, and encrypted content must be translated at gateways. Qualification certifies a service on one day; it cannot police every update, employee, acquisition, or vulnerability afterward.
[C1.4] Worse, the mandate entrenches what it claims to attack. Only large firms will afford certification, audits, legal teams, and constant protocol compliance. Governments and incumbents together will decide who is qualified, freezing today's feature set into law and punishing experimental designs. Small alternative communities built around closed membership and distinct norms will be forced to interconnect or drown in compliance.
[C1.5] Users deserve exit tools: export of their own content and contacts they provided, and voluntary standards. But a legal duty to open live messaging and graphs trades irreversible privacy and safety losses for speculative competition. A rival needing mandated access to everyone else's relationships has not earned users; the state has redistributed trust people placed in one service to services they never chose.
