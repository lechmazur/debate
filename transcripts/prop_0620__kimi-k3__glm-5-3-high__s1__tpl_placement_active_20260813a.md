[P1.1] Every legal system already knows the answer to this motion in a simpler context. If a company hired people to follow you through your day — recording what you read, who you meet, what you buy, where you sleep — and sold the file to anyone who paid, no court would ask whether you had "opted out." The question would be where they got permission. Large-scale data harvesting is that following, done invisibly and at industrial scale.
[P1.2] The motion applies the same default: taking from people requires their yes, not their failure to find an opt-out buried in a settings menu.
[P1.3] The current regime rests on a consent fiction. "Notice and choice" means tens of thousands of words of privacy policy no human reads, bundled as a condition of using services that function as essential infrastructure — email, search, maps, social connection. Consent extracted on take-it-or-leave-it terms for the tools of modern life is coerced consent, and coerced consent is not consent. Presumptive unlawfulness restores the only version that matters: an informed, affirmative opt-in.
[P1.4] The harms justify the strong default. This is not about seeing a relevant shoe ad. Behavioral profiles enable manipulation of the vulnerable — gambling ads steered to addicts, payday loans to the desperate — and discriminatory filtering of housing and job opportunities by proxy traits. Harvested data flows to brokers and leaks in breaches; once collected, it cannot be un-collected.
[P1.5] These harms are diffuse, hidden, and individually small, which is exactly why private lawsuits and market discipline have failed for two decades and why regulators need a bright-line presumption.
[P1.6] Note what the motion does not do: it bans nothing. If targeted advertising truly delivers the value its defenders claim, companies can ask, and users can say yes. Everything people genuinely want survives an opt-in. What dies is only what people would never choose.
[P1.7] That is not a flaw in the presumption; it is the reason for it.

[C1.1] The strongest line in the opposing case is also its weakest link: "everything people genuinely want survives an opt-in." Defaults are not neutral add-ons to choice; they largely determine outcomes. When GDPR-style consent banners and cookie opt-ins appear, the share of users who affirmatively opt in is typically tiny — not because people are tricked, but because most would rather keep free services than manage a consent inbox. So a regime where nothing is banned but everything requires opt-in is a de facto ban wearing a costume.
[C1.2] Call that consumer choice if you like, but be honest about what it destroys: the ad-funded internet that made search, email, maps, and independent journalism free at the point of use.
[C1.3] Second, the presumptive-unlawful framing inverts a sensible legal order. Law normally presumes conduct lawful until shown harmful, especially when the "harm" is a relevant advertisement. If profiling enables discrimination in housing or preys on gambling addicts, those are real wrongs with existing tools — anti-discrimination statutes, consumer-protection law, sector-specific rules. A blanket presumption is a blunt instrument that bans the benign majority to reach the malicious minority, punishing a local bakery reaching nearby customers the same way it punishes the worst broker.
[C1.4] Third, note who bears the cost. Compliance with opt-in infrastructure — consent management, audits, litigation risk — is fixed-cost overhead. Large platforms can absorb it. Small publishers and challengers cannot.
[C1.5] A presumption of illegality entrenches exactly the giants whose data practices motivated the motion, while accelerating the consolidation of the open web into walled gardens.
[C1.6] Finally, the "coerced consent" argument proves too much. Bundling is how nearly all valuable services work — supermarkets bundle parking with groceries. The answer to unread policies is better notice and real opt-outs, not criminalizing the default. Presumption of guilt is a poor foundation for a twenty-first-century economy.
