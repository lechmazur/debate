[P1.1] A rating system is not neutral when the customer holding the star button can punish a worker’s accent, race, gender presentation, disability, or refusal of a sexual advance. In customer-facing gig work, ratings are not merely feedback; they are the condition of continued access. Falling below a cutoff can mean lost shifts, lower visibility, and eventual deactivation. That turns individual bias into a structural threat.
[P1.2] The core problem is that the worker cannot refuse the corrupted evaluation without refusing the job. A woman who rejects a passenger’s advances can be one-starred in retaliation, and the platform treats that as a performance failure. A Black or visibly Muslim driver can receive systematically lower scores for the same route, timeliness, and courtesy. Workers with nonstandard speech are rated on customer comfort, not service.
[P1.3] When access is tied to those scores, the platform is not measuring quality; it is laundering discrimination and harassment into “customer satisfaction.”
[P1.4] That is why the remedy is narrow: workers should be able to refuse a rating system strongly shaped by abuse, bias, or harassment without losing platform access. This does not ban all ratings or eliminate quality control. Platforms can use completion rates, objective timeliness data, safety reports, dispute review, or filtered multi-source ratings. The question is whether they may make livelihood conditional on a system that reliably converts prejudice into punishment.
[P1.5] Quality control cannot justify that condition. A rating system heavily distorted by bias is not a reliable consumer signal; it is a discriminatory chokepoint. Consumers gain little information from scores that reflect retaliation or identity. The main effect is to discipline the worker into tolerating abuse, smiling through harassment, and accepting lower earnings rather than pushing back.
[P1.6] No worker should have to choose between enduring a discriminatory rating regime and losing the platform that provides their income. The ability to refuse is the minimum protection against making private prejudice the gatekeeper of gig work.
