[P1.1] A corporate “net-zero” claim should mean a company has stopped driving global warming. Today, it mostly means they hired a clever marketer. By buying cheap carbon offsets, airlines, oil giants, and retailers label themselves climate-neutral while their operational emissions climb. The law must cap offsets at a small fraction of any net-zero claim—strictly for irreducible residual emissions like cement chemistry—and mandate that most cuts happen directly inside the value chain.
[P1.2] First, offsets are physically unequal to direct reductions. Burning fossil fuels moves carbon locked underground for millions of years permanently into the active atmosphere. Most offsets, however, rely on temporary biological storage or speculative "avoided" emissions. Forests burn down, succumb to disease, or simply shift logging across a border.
[P1.3] Worse, offset developers routinely inflate baselines, claiming credit for protecting trees that were never actually in danger. Allowing companies to trade guaranteed, permanent industrial emissions for fragile, unverifiable paper credits violates basic atmospheric reality.
[P1.4] Second, uncapped offsetting stalls the industrial transition. Overhauling a supply chain—switching to electric heat, green steel, or clean logistics—requires serious upfront capital. Voluntary offsets often cost just a few dollars per ton. When a five-dollar credit buys the exact same "net-zero" badge as a hundred-dollar engineering upgrade, executives facing quarterly earnings pressure will choose the offset every time.
[P1.5] That starves clean technology of commercial scale and locks in fossil-fuel infrastructure for decades.
[P1.6] Finally, this rule restores market honesty without banning external climate finance. Corporations remain entirely free to fund rainforest conservation abroad and advertise those financial contributions. They simply cannot use outside charity to disguise a dirty supply chain as "net-zero." Today, honest innovators spending billions to genuinely clean up their factories are competitively punished by greenwashing rivals spending pennies on credits for the exact same label.
[P1.7] A legal limit ensures "net-zero" demands real decarbonization.
