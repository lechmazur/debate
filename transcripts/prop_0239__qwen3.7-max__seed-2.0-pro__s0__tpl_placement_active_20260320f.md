[P1.1] Consent is a mechanism for establishing legal boundaries, but ethics demands more than mere agreement. To claim consent alone makes a transaction ethical ignores the conditions under which that consent is given and the broader impact of the exchange.
[P1.2] First, consent cannot sanitize exploitation. When a person is starving, their agreement to work in a hazardous sweatshop for pennies is technically voluntary, but it is profoundly unethical. The employer is exploiting a baseline of desperation. Consent in this context does not reflect a fair exchange; it merely reflects a lack of better options.
[P1.3] If consent alone were sufficient, we would have to label the sale of vital organs by the deeply impoverished, or extreme predatory lending, as entirely ethical simply because the desperate party signed a contract. Ethics requires us to look at the power dynamics and vulnerabilities being leveraged, not just the final signature.
[P1.4] Second, transactions do not occur in a vacuum. Two parties can freely and enthusiastically consent to a deal that devastates others. A corporate executive and a corrupt official might consent to a bribe that allows toxic dumping in a community’s water supply. The mutual consent of the transacting parties does not erase the ethical violation inflicted on the unconsenting public.
[P1.5] A transaction that generates severe negative externalities remains unethical regardless of how willingly the primary actors participated.
[P1.6] Finally, some transactions violate baseline human dignity in ways that mutual agreement cannot cure. We universally ban the buying and selling of votes, or the voluntary selling of oneself into permanent slavery. We do not ban these because the consent is fake; we ban them because treating fundamental human rights and autonomy as alienable property is inherently degrading.
[P1.7] Consent is merely the starting line. For a transaction to be truly ethical, it must also be free from predatory exploitation, avoid harming third parties, and respect human dignity.
