[C1.1] Tunneling sounds clean: hide infrastructure, spare the street. That cleanliness is an illusion paid for at extraordinary opportunity cost.
[C1.2] First, a tunnel does not eliminate surface disruption or land need; it concentrates and prolongs them. Every underground line still needs stations, portals, ventilation shafts, substations and construction yards in exactly the dense places it claims to save. Years of shaft excavation, spoil removal and utility diversions happen before a boring machine moves. When geology surprises — groundwater, contaminated soil, buried foundations — there is nowhere to reroute.
[C1.3] The result is the familiar pattern of megaprojects arriving late and multiples over budget, while that money is unavailable for anything else.
[C1.4] Second, necessity is asserted, not proven. Land is scarce in a handful of urban cores, not across countries, and even there the cheapest new capacity is usually reallocating space already paved: bus lanes, surface or elevated rail, protected corridors, and road pricing that makes existing streets work. For utilities, relining, directional drilling, targeted bores and better mapping can replace failed pipes without walk-in tunnel networks that force water, gas, electricity and data into shared space with new fire, security and access conflicts.
[C1.5] A heavy national commitment treats the most expensive tool as the default.
[C1.6] Third, the century-life defense locks in century-long liabilities. Deep stations are slower to reach, harder to expand and costlier to ventilate, pump, waterproof and evacuate. A flooded, overheated or obsolete tunnel cannot be moved, widened cheaply, or repurposed easily. Its concrete and excavation also carry a large upfront carbon cost when budgets and emissions need quick returns from projects that open in years, not decades.
[C1.7] Finally, promised cost declines from building more are speculative. Cheaper tunneling abroad reflects geology, labor, planning law and state capacity, not merely volume. Spending heavily before fixing those causes rewards the dysfunction. Countries should improve surface capacity, selectively tunnel where no alternative survives scrutiny, and refuse a blanket heavy bet on the slowest, least flexible option.
