[P1.1] The proposition fixes a growing injustice: firms that capture the benefit of someone's labour while paying none of the social contribution price.
[P1.2] Under the standard employment model, social contributions buy pensions, health cover, unemployment protection, and accident insurance. Platforms and subcontracting chains let the visible employer escape that price: a platform calls a driver or warehouse picker "self-employed"; a subcontractor inserts a one-person agency between client and worker. The state still carries the residual risk, but the contribution base shrinks. And free-riding cannot be corrected by appeals to fairness: compliant firms that pay full contributions are undercut by firms that strip them.
[P1.3] That is labour dumping inside the single market — a distortion the EU can correct where member states cannot.
[P1.4] The motion is deliberately narrow: large companies that use platforms and subcontracting chains, nothing more. A hotel chain outsourcing cleaners through an agency is employment with a paper shield, not self-employment. Genuine small-scale self-employment is untouched, and platforms are not abolished; the rider still rides. What changes is that a large firm profiting from a workforce cannot declare someone else's job to be nobody's business.
[P1.5] "Enforcement is too complex" is the obvious objection, so answer it now. The EU has already legislated in this area, including a presumption of employment relationship in platform work and a push toward administrative enforcement. We extend that: group-level accounting, a look-through to the firm that actually directs the work, joint liability upstream, and a threshold rule for large users. The chain is documented in contracts, invoices, and payroll records; this is an administrative task, not an impossibility.
[P1.6] The alternative to a rule is not the status quo. It is social insurance quietly hollowed out while the rhetoric of entrepreneurship grows. If the workplace leaves the contribution base, the contribution must follow the workplace.

[C1.1] The injustice PRO describes is misclassification: a worker who is really an employee being labelled something else. The remedy for misclassification is classification. Reclassify the rider, and the platform pays the ordinary contribution like any other employer. The EU has already built that tool — the presumption of employment in platform work, joint liability in construction and posting chains.
[C1.2] PRO even concedes this [P1.5]. The motion does something different and worse: it attaches a surcharge to a legal form, charging large firms a "higher share" whenever they use a platform or subcontract, regardless of whether anyone is misclassified at all.
[C1.3] Consider what that actually taxes. A manufacturer hiring a specialist electrical contractor. A bank engaging a cybersecurity firm. A hospital buying catering from a regional caterer whose cooks are fully employed, with every contribution paid.
[C1.4] Under the motion, the client pays more for using them. That is not closing a loophole; it is a penalty on buying services from other companies — and the companies on the other side of those contracts are overwhelmingly small and medium-sized firms whose business model is winning work from large clients. Punish the large client for subcontracting and the SME supplier loses the contract.
[C1.5] Then ask who really bears the cost. Platforms respond to levies by trimming per-task fees; large firms respond by insourcing or by routing work through intermediaries sitting just below the threshold. The worker PRO wants to protect sees lower pay or a longer chain, not a pension.
[C1.6] Finally, "higher share" is a double charge where the subcontractor already pays full contributions on its employees — the same labour funding social insurance twice. And contribution rates are the heart of national social systems, where the EU acts by unanimity for good reason.
[C1.7] Fix the abuse by naming it. Do not tax the structure and call it fairness.

[P2.1] CON's central move is to say the motion taxes a legal form. But that is what a differential contribution rate is: the price of labour differs with the route to the worker, direct employment or a chain. Employ cleaners directly and you pay full contributions; reach them through an agency and pay a fraction. That gap is an incentive: while it exists, every firm has reason to prefer the chain.
[P2.2] Classification fixes one worker at a time; only the differential removes the arbitrage that created the chain.
[P2.3] CON's examples expose the problem rather than solve it. If a complying caterer and a chain-hiding client compete for the same contract, the client wins by paying less in contributions, and the difference lands on the cook's pension and on the state. Compliance undercut by non-compliance is the race to the bottom.
[P2.4] Now take the two ways a platform responds to a surcharge. It trims the rider's fee, so the worker bears more of their own insurance, or it folds the work into direct employment and pays properly. A firm that insources carries the contribution instead. Both responses repair the insurance base.
[P2.5] Does the alternative leave more workers covered? CON's does not.
[P2.6] Routing work through an intermediary to escape a threshold is not a rebuttal; it is a confession. Group-level thresholds and a look-through to the firm that directs the work defeat it.
[P2.7] The "double charge" objection concedes too much: the surcharge is a differential, not a duplicate into the same account. Competence is no obstacle: the EU has legislated on platform employment relationships and coordinates which member state's social security applies across borders. This is an internal-market distortion.
[P2.8] The choice is not fairness versus competitiveness. It is whether the firm that captures the profit from a workplace decides what that workplace's insurance is worth.

[P4.1] A1: I concede that a lawful agency paying full contributions creates no hidden “fraction” for its client to save. Uniform enforcement matters. But CON mistakes the evader for the full target. Misclassification is often hidden inside ordinary invoices, while worker-by-worker enforcement lets large clients switch suppliers whenever a chain’s liability becomes visible.
[P4.2] A contribution on large users closes that option: whether audited later or not, the chain no longer obtains a cheaper route to labour. Reclassification should still occur; the surcharge is not its substitute, but a backstop against arbitrage.
[P4.3] A2: No, money paid into an undifferentiated treasury would not protect a rider, and CON’s false choice between reclassification and money ignores this combination. The payment must follow the worker’s status: where employment presumptions are met, the platform pays ordinary employer contributions and the rider receives employment rights. Where genuine self-employment remains, the platform’s payment must be credited to portable pension, sickness, and accident coverage, not vanish into general revenue. If neither condition is satisfied, the large user remains liable.
[P4.4] Payment cannot purchase immunity from classification.
[P4.5] Those safeguards answer double charging: the chain-specific payment is not a second claim for identical benefits. It is an account-level top-up that prices residual risk and creates portable coverage for independent workers. In a compliant catering chain, ordinary contributions continue and the additional amount strengthens the workers’ protection rather than merely entering general revenue.
[P4.6] The decisive comparison is not contributions versus none, but evasion that is detected yet remains profitable. Reclassification alone can punish an exposed chain while a client selects another. The higher share makes large companies bear responsibility for the labour architecture they benefit from, while status and earmarking rules ensure money reaches workers. CON’s lawful-chain examples therefore demand defeating an unnecessary penalty; PRO removes its purpose.
