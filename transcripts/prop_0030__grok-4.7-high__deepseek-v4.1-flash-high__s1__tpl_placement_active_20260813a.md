[P1.1] Britain’s economy would be stronger today had it remained in the European Union. Brexit replaced deep, low-friction integration with permanent barriers to its largest market, and nothing since has closed the gap.
[P1.2] The EU accounted for roughly half of British trade. Membership meant goods, and much of services, moved without tariffs, customs declarations, or divergent product rules. Leaving the single market and customs union imposed checks, paperwork, and regulatory risk on every cross-border transaction. Those costs do not fade; they compound.
[P1.3] Exporters lose competitiveness, importers pass higher prices into shops and factories, and supply chains that once treated the Channel as a minor inconvenience now treat it as a border. Less trade means less specialization and weaker productivity than the path of continued membership.
[P1.4] Investment follows the same logic. Firms place capital where market access is secure. Years of uncertainty, then a settled but inferior deal, discouraged inward investment and shifted some activity to the continent simply to keep EU customers. A thinner capital stock and slower diffusion of know-how leave output lower today.
[P1.5] Services—finance, law, consulting—lost the passport that let British firms sell across the bloc from a single base. Ending free movement created shortages in health, logistics, and food production, raising costs without a productivity surge to offset them.
[P1.6] New trade deals and regulatory autonomy have not compensated. Agreements with distant partners cover a fraction of the commerce the single market once did, and “sovereignty” has mostly meant copying old rules or accepting EU standards in order to keep selling there. The foregone growth is not a one-off shock. It is a lower trajectory: weaker public finances, lower real incomes, and less room to invest in infrastructure or skills.
[P1.7] Remove that large, asymmetric cost, and the economy that remains is larger, more open, and more resilient. That is the Britain that would exist today without Brexit.

[C1.1] The proposition sells a simple story: remove Brexit, get a bigger economy. That is not an argument; it is a counterfactual with one variable changed and every other hard truth frozen in place. The real question is not “Brexit versus frictionless trade.” It is “a Britain still inside the EU, with the same productivity stagnation, the same regional divide, the same housing and skills shortages, and the same political fracture that produced the referendum.”
[C1.2] Start with the comparator. The EU is not a high-growth machine. The UK’s trade with the EU was already falling as a share of its total trade before 2016; the fastest-growing markets were outside Europe. Staying in would not have reversed that.
[C1.3] It would have kept the UK inside a slow-growing bloc, still paying into its budget, still bound by rules made elsewhere, and still unable to sign independent trade deals. The trade friction of Brexit is real, but it is a tax on a subset of commerce, not the master variable of British productivity. Brexit is a headwind, not the engine failure.
[C1.4] Then the domestic drags. Britain’s weak investment, low productivity growth, expensive housing, and skills gaps long predate the referendum. They are not Brexit effects, and no-Brexit would not have cured them. If anything, a permanently unresolved Brexit debate—ignored referendum, repeated votes, or a second referendum—would have produced more uncertainty, not less.
[C1.5] Firms invest in stable rules; a country that voted to leave and then stayed would have had unstable rules.
[C1.6] PRO’s case depends on an invisible assumption: that the only changed variable is EU membership and everything else improves automatically. That is not economics; it is wishful thinking. A no-Brexit Britain might have slightly smoother trade with Europe. It would still be a low-growth, high-inequality, underinvesting economy, and it might be more constrained and more politically volatile.
[C1.7] The proposition needs to prove a stronger economy, not just fewer customs forms. It does not.
