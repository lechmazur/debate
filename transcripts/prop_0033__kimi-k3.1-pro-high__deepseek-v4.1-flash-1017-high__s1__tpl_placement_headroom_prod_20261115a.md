[P1.1] Democratic persuasion ends where campaign communication becomes an experiment performed on the voter. Behavioral micro-targeting based on inferred psychological or emotional profiles crosses that line: it uses a person’s clicks, pauses, purchases, and associations to guess at fear, insecurity, impulsivity, or distress, then selects the message most likely to exploit that guess. The voter cannot see the diagnosis, correct it, or know why a particular appeal arrived. Campaigns gain a private advantage not over rival arguments, but over citizens’ defenses.
[P1.2] First, this threatens self-government even when every advertisement is factual. A message can be true and still be delivered because someone is inferred to be unusually susceptible to panic, humiliation, or resentment. The wrong is the covert optimization of vulnerability, not merely offensive content. Consent is hollow if the conditions of persuasion are engineered invisibly and individually.
[P1.3] Second, micro-targeting defeats democratic accountability. Opponents, journalists, and regulators cannot effectively scrutinize claims shown only to small, psychologically selected audiences. A campaign can reassure the anxious, inflame the resentful, and demobilize the despondent while presenting a different public face. Ordinary advertising can be answered in a common political arena; this practice systematically removes the audience most able to answer it.
[P1.4] The prohibition is therefore about a method, not a viewpoint. Campaigns remain free to publish, broadcast, canvass, organize, and address any issue in any language. They may still make the same arguments publicly. What they lose is the power to convert hidden psychological inference into individualized political pressure.
[P1.5] A rule limited to that method is far more administrable than judging whether each message was manipulative after the election.
[P1.6] CON will likely call this efficient persuasion. But campaign convenience cannot outweigh the precondition of legitimate elections: citizens choosing under conditions they can understand and contest. Protecting that choice requires prohibition, not another privacy notice.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to outlaw a method so elastic that it swallows ordinary politics. A campaign reads a public housing report and sends a message about rent anxiety; a canvasser notices a voter’s worry about a parent’s care and adjusts the pitch; a candidate speaks differently to farmers and to students. Each involves inferring emotion and tailoring persuasion. PRO calls this an experiment on the voter, but persuasion has always begun with attention to what people fear, hope, and resent.
[C1.2] The proposition cannot separate that human practice from algorithmic targeting without giving regulators an impossible line-drawing task: which inferred trait is 'psychological' enough, which model is 'behavioral' enough, which appeal is legitimate responsiveness and which is forbidden optimization? A get-out-the-vote text aimed at people inferred to feel politically powerless is emotional targeting—and also participation support.
[C1.3] That vagueness is not a technical footnote. It chills routine campaign speech and invites selective enforcement against outsiders and challengers. Enforcement would require regulators to inspect internal models and message variants—a power ripe for real partisan abuse. And the ban is overbroad relative to any real harm.
[C1.4] Covert manipulation can be met with disclosure of targeting criteria, ad archives, audits, and limits on sensitive data. Those tools preserve accountability while allowing campaigns to reach people who tune out broadcast politics—young voters, language minorities, the politically disengaged. A blanket prohibition instead rewards the loudest, best-funded megaphone and makes politics less responsive.
[C1.5] PRO’s own precondition—citizens choosing under conditions they can understand and contest—points toward transparency, not prohibition. A voter can contest a message she can see; she cannot contest a message never sent because the law banned the campaign from identifying her concern. That is the democratic choice. Reject the motion.
