[C1.1] Mandating negotiation sounds protective. It isn't. The proposition would create a rigid legal framework that fails workers, distorts innovation, and solves the wrong problem—all while leaving the real harms of workplace AI largely untouched.
[C1.2] Start with the mechanism itself. "Elected worker councils" sounds democratic, but the moment you legally require negotiation with them, they become procedural gatekeepers whose representativeness is always contestable. Which workers vote? On what schedule?
[C1.3] What happens when a council blocks a deployment, then loses an election six months later? The proposition offers no answers because it can't—these questions require legislative specificity that varies enormously across industries, firm sizes, and national labor traditions. What gets built in practice is an administrative thicket that large employers with legal departments navigate easily while smaller firms near the threshold struggle. The gap between companies that can afford compliance theater and those that cannot is a feature, not a bug, of poorly designed mandates.
[C1.4] More fundamentally, the proposition misidentifies the mechanism of harm. The problem isn't that workers lack a negotiating body—it's that specific AI applications enable specific abuses: opaque algorithmic performance targets, biometric surveillance, discriminatory hiring filters. Those are concrete, addressable harms. Targeted legislation against discriminatory algorithmic outputs, mandatory human review of AI-driven discipline, and data transparency requirements would address each abuse directly.
[C1.5] A generic negotiation mandate addresses none of them directly. A worker council can negotiate all day and still sign off on a biased hiring system because neither side has the technical capacity to identify the flaw.
[C1.6] Finally, there is a dynamic cost the PRO side obscures. Legal negotiation requirements create a credible threat that slows deployment timelines. Firms respond by front-loading automation before the mandate takes effect, or by restructuring employment to stay below the threshold. Workers in those jobs—the most vulnerable ones—receive no protection.
[C1.7] The mandate accelerates exactly the disruption it claims to prevent.
[C1.8] The central question isn't whether workers deserve a voice. They do. It's whether this particular legal mechanism actually delivers one.
