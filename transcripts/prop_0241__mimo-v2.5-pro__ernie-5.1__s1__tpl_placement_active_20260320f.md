[P1.1] The question before us is not whether offenders deserve consequences—they do. It is which aim of punishment should take priority. Rehabilitation should be prioritized over retribution because it better serves the state's deepest obligation: protecting its citizens from future harm.
[P1.2] Consider two societies. One treats prison as a warehouse of suffering, where offenders pay a debt through punishment alone, emerging no better equipped to live lawfully. The other treats incarceration as an opportunity to address the root causes of criminal behavior: addiction, lack of education, untreated mental illness. The first society will see those same offenders cycle back through its courts.
[P1.3] The second will not.
[P1.4] This is not speculation. Systems that invest in rehabilitation—cognitive behavioral therapy, substance abuse treatment, educational programs—consistently show lower recidivism than those that prioritize punishment alone. Countries that emphasize rehabilitation over retribution report reoffending rates substantially below those of heavily punitive systems. Every prevented re-offense is a victim who never suffers.
[P1.5] Retribution's appeal is visceral: someone caused harm, so they should suffer in return. But satisfying a desire for vengeance is not the same as making society safer. A retributive system can lock someone away for decades, release them without treatment or training, and then act surprised when they reoffend. It has done nothing to reduce the stock of crime—it has merely delayed it.
[P1.6] The strongest retributive argument rests on moral desert: wrongdoers deserve punishment regardless of consequences. But even granting moral weight to desert, that does not establish retribution should be *more important* than rehabilitation. Only rehabilitation produces a dividend—fewer future crimes, fewer future victims, people capable of rejoining their communities. Retribution produces nothing but suffering returned for suffering.
[P1.7] The purpose of criminal justice is not catharsis. It is safety. Rehabilitation serves that purpose; retribution alone does not.

[C1.1] Retribution is the essential foundation of criminal justice because it affirms the moral order that makes any society possible. When a person harms another, the community’s first obligation is to acknowledge that harm and to impose a proportionate response. Without that acknowledgment, the law loses legitimacy, victims lose trust, and the deterrent effect of punishment collapses. Rehabilitation, however useful, cannot substitute for this core function.
[C1.2] Consider the victim. A mother whose child is murdered does not find solace in a report that the killer will receive “cognitive‑behavioral therapy.” She needs to know that the state recognizes the wrong and that the offender pays a price commensurate with the loss. Retribution delivers that moral accounting; rehabilitation offers no guarantee of it and may even be perceived as the state softening justice for the offender’s benefit.
[C1.3] Deterrence is another pillar that retribution secures more reliably than rehabilitation. The certainty and severity of punishment discourage potential offenders far more effectively than speculative treatment programs that succeed only for a fraction of participants. Empirical evidence shows that harsh, consistent sentences reduce crime rates even when rehabilitative services are minimal, while many rehabilitation initiatives show modest or negligible impact on recidivism.
[C1.4] Moreover, placing rehabilitation above retribution risks a utilitarian calculus that treats offenders as means to an end, ignoring their moral desert. If the state’s primary aim is to “produce fewer future crimes,” it may justify indefinite confinement of those deemed unlikely to be rehabilitated, eroding the principle that punishment must be proportionate. Retribution imposes a clear limit: the sentence fits the crime, not the projected risk.
[C1.5] Thus the proper hierarchy is clear: retribution secures legitimacy, satisfies victims, and deters crime; rehabilitation is a valuable supplement but cannot be the more important aim. The motion fails because it elevates a secondary, uncertain tool above the indispensable moral core of punishment.
