[P1.1] Europe’s security problem is not simply a shortage of expensive equipment. It is a shortage of people who can operate, sustain, and replenish defense systems during a prolonged crisis. Professional forces remain essential, but a small professional army cannot by itself provide territorial defense, replace casualties, protect infrastructure, and sustain civil resilience at scale. Mandatory national service closes that gap by creating a large, periodically refreshed pool of trained reservists, while giving states a reliable way to identify and develop specialists in logistics, medicine, engineering, cyber defense, and communications.
[P1.2] This is deterrence through credible depth, not nostalgia for mass conscript armies. A potential aggressor must consider not only the troops available on day one, but whether a country can mobilize trained citizens, disperse essential capabilities, and continue functioning months later. Countries such as Finland show that conscription can coexist with advanced professional forces and liberal democracy; Sweden’s decision to restore conscription after relying on voluntary recruitment reflects the same basic lesson: when the security environment deteriorates, hoping enough volunteers appear is not a strategy.
[P1.3] National service also addresses a quieter vulnerability. Europeans increasingly live in separate occupational, regional, and digital worlds, while defense burdens can fall disproportionately on those with fewer alternatives. Properly designed service puts young citizens from different backgrounds into shared, purposeful work with a visible public mission. That does not magically erase prejudice, but repeated cooperation toward a common goal is a far stronger foundation for civic trust than speeches about solidarity.
[P1.4] Civil-defense and disaster-response components can make that solidarity tangible beyond the barracks.
[P1.5] The obvious objection is coercion. But the answer is not to abandon a necessary collective duty; it is to constrain it. Those safeguards distinguish civic service from militarization.

[C1.1] Mandatory national service is a twentieth-century answer to a twenty-first-century problem, and it fails on both of the motion's own terms.
[C1.2] Defense readiness first. Modern war rewards trained specialists and stockpiled equipment, not short-service conscripts. A conscript trained for six to twelve months becomes useful just as he or she leaves; the skills then decay unless the state funds regular refreshers, which states rarely do. Ukraine shows what actually matters: drones, air defense, artillery shells, seasoned professional units.
[C1.3] Mass conscription there has produced casualties more than capability. Europe's real bottlenecks are procurement, ammunition production, and retention of professionals — every euro spent housing, feeding, and administering hundreds of thousands of conscripts is a euro not spent on those. Germany suspended conscription in 2011 not because the threat had vanished but because the system was costly and militarily thin.
[C1.4] The exemplars cut the other way. Finland's model rests on a long Russian border and a century of territorial-defense culture; it is not exportable to Portugal or Belgium. And Sweden undermines the motion outright: it restored conscription on a small, selective basis — a few thousand a year to cover volunteer shortfalls. That is a targeted fix, the alternative to universal service, not an instance of it.
[C1.5] Then the cost. Europe faces labor shortages and stagnant productivity, yet conscription pulls young people out of education and work at the most productive moment of their lives — lost earnings, delayed degrees, forgone output, multiplied by hundreds of thousands.
[C1.6] Finally, cohesion. Solidarity compelled by the state is compliance, and young people know the difference. The promised safeguards — pay, deferrals, civilian alternatives — either hollow out the military rationale or confirm the coercion. If you want cohesion, fund voluntary civic service people actually choose.
[C1.7] A draft teaches one civic lesson above all: the state owns a year of your life. That breeds resentment, not trust.

[P2.1] The opposition keeps defeating a policy the motion does not require: a universal, short-course conscript army replacing professionals. “Reintroduce or expand” lets states calibrate selection, duration and reserve training to their needs. Sweden’s selective compulsory system is therefore not a counterexample; it demonstrates that mandated service can fill defined gaps without drafting an entire generation. Finland’s circumstances shape its design, not whether trained depth matters. And Germany’s suspension in a different security environment cannot settle today’s choice.
[P2.2] Ukraine does not show that manpower is obsolete. It shows the demands of sustaining a long war: drones, air defenses and artillery still need operators, maintainers, medics, logisticians and replacements. Procurement and personnel are complements, not rival currencies; ammunition without trained units is inventory, not readiness. The claim that conscripts become useful only as they leave ignores the point: the relevant output is a trained reserve, not permanent conscript employment.
[P2.3] Skills decay without refreshers, so build refreshers into the system. That is a design task, not a case for having no reserve.
[P2.4] Nor is CON’s accounting neutral. Expanding professional forces also requires pay, recruitment, housing and retention in tight labor markets. A limited, paid obligation with deferrals distributes that burden instead of pretending readiness is costless. Civilian alternatives do not “hollow out” defense when they strengthen medical, infrastructure and disaster response—the same societal resilience a prolonged crisis demands.
[P2.5] Finally, compelled solidarity is not automatically mere compliance. Democratic states already rely on bounded civic duties; legitimacy comes from fairness, purpose and limits, not from every duty being optional. Voluntary civic schemes tend to attract the already engaged and cannot guarantee mixing across class and region. Service creates repeated cooperation under a shared mission.
[P2.6] CON offers equipment without durable depth and cohesion without common obligation. Well-designed national service supplies both; that combined resilience is the decisive advantage.
