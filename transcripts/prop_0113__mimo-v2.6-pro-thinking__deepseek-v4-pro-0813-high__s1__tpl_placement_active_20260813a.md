[P1.1] Right now the economics of beverage and packaging waste are backwards. A company chooses the bottle, the cap, the film, the multi-layer pouch — then ships millions of units into a market, collects the revenue, and walks away. Local government and taxpayers then pay to sort, haul, landfill, and clean up whatever comes back. That is a subsidy from the public to the producer's balance sheet, and it is the central injustice this motion asks us to fix.
[P1.2] The first principle is simple: the poller creates the problem and captures the profit, so the polluter should pay for the mess. Producers don't just incidentally generate packaging waste — they engineer it. Every resin choice, every bonded label, every pigmented PET bottle that a sorting facility can't handle is a decision made in a design meeting where end-of-life cost was nobody's line item, because it never showed up on their invoice. Move that cost to them and it shows up immediately.
[P1.3] This is the incentive argument, and it's stronger than the fairness one. When producers must fund collection and recycling — through extended producer responsibility fees modulated by how recyclable their packaging actually is — they stop selling packaging that can't be recycled. They lightweight, they switch to mono-materials, they invest in refill and deposit systems because returns and reusability cut their own bills. A tax-funded municipal recycling program has no such lever: it can only process whatever the market throws at it.
[P1.4] Producer responsibility turns waste reduction from a public relations slogan into a line of production cost.

Producers who use cheap, unrecyclable composite packaging currently pay exactly the same as producers using clean, easily recycled aluminum or glass — nothing. Taxpayers eat the difference. That is not neutrality; it is a tax on the public to finance the worst packaging choices.

[C1.1] The real question is not who writes the check, but whose budget is actually lightened. The producer-pays story misses a basic economic fact: fees imposed on producers are passed forward through prices. Beverage and packaged-goods companies operate on thin margins and face intense retail competition, so a packaging or collection fee becomes an ingredient cost and shows up on the shelf. The taxpayer has not been rescued from the bill; the bill has simply been moved from a visible municipal waste charge into the hidden price of soda, juice, milk, and packaged staples.
[C1.2] Households still pay, with less transparency and less democratic control.
[C1.3] That hidden shift is also more regressive. Property-tax and general-fund waste collection draw from a broad base. Per-bottle and per-package fees, by contrast, fall most heavily on people who buy single-use, small-format, convenience products: shift workers, renters without storage or transport, disabled and elderly people who cannot easily haul containers to a redemption site. If returns require driving, sorting, and reclaiming deposits, the people least able to comply end up paying the deposit as a penalty.
[C1.4] That is not polluter-pays. It is an uneven consumption penalty dressed as policy.
[C1.5] Municipal collection also has a structural advantage the proposal weakens. One integrated route serves garbage, recycling, litter reduction, and public sanitation in every district. Producer-financed programs create pressure to define what counts as packaging narrowly, cut services to high-cost or low-volume areas, and shift attention toward materials easy to process, not places or services. Those who pay much more will quite reasonably demand control—and that control warps toward cost recovery, not community need.
[C1.6] The design problem does not require making producers pay "much more" of collection. Modest design standards, material restrictions, and focused fees can improve recyclability without replacing the visible local tax base. As written, the proposal simply relabels a household consumer tax and makes the system less accountable. Reject it.
