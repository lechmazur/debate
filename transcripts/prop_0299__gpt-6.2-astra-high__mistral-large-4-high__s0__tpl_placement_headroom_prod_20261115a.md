[C1.1] A standing public fund does not simply add money to preservation; it changes who controls memory. When a community must apply to a government panel to record an elder, it trades dependence on donors for dependence on bureaucrats. The arm's-length grant model still requires communities to persuade officials that their language is 'endangered' enough and their archive 'worthy' enough. That is not neutrality; it is gatekeeping with tax dollars.
[C1.2] Donors can be fickle, but they are numerous and diverse. A standing fund concentrates approval in a single politically appointed gatekeeper whose priorities shift with elections and austerity.
[C1.3] Governments must also decide what counts as a minority language or legitimate oral history, and those categories are never neutral. Bureaucratic definitions favor groups with the legal status and organizational capacity to file paperwork, while smaller or marginalized communities get filtered out. When the state funds preservation, it standardizes what gets archived—digitizing and cataloging according to official schemas. The result can be a sanitized, state-curated version of culture rather than the living, community-controlled memory that transmits a language.
[C1.4] There is also the matter of priorities. Every coin in a standing fund is a coin not spent on housing or schools in the communities whose languages are at risk. Language dies when speakers are dispersed by poverty; a recording grant cannot offset the economic forces that scatter a community. Standing funds also create dependency: once preservation relies on annual appropriations, it becomes vulnerable to the next budget crisis, and communities lose the habit of mobilizing their own diaspora.
[C1.5] Charitable and private models are not perfect, but plural and resilient. Tax incentives, project-based matching grants, and support for community-run institutions like tribal colleges keep memory in the hands of those who live it, without a standing bureaucracy deciding whose past deserves a line item.
