[P1.1] Who actually pays these fees? Incarcerated people earn little or nothing, so the bill lands on mothers, grandmothers, and children outside — families who committed no crime and often just lost a breadwinner. Vendor-priced calls are a regressive tax on the innocent, collected by monopoly. A family cannot switch providers: the facility signs one exclusive contract, and vendors have competed not on lower prices but on who pays the jail the bigger commission on every minute.
[P1.2] It is a market designed to fail the people it serves.
[P1.3] Family contact is not a perk; it is correctional infrastructure. Family ties are among the most reliable predictors of successful reentry — housing, work, and stability usually run through relatives — and regular contact improves behavior inside facilities as well. Every dollar a vendor extracts is a tax on the exact mechanism that prevents the next crime. A state serious about public safety does not put a paywall between prisoners and the one support network that keeps them from coming back.
[P1.4] The cost objection collapses under scrutiny. The marginal cost of a call is pennies; video platforms are cheaper still. Yet families have been charged dollars per minute — staggering markups on a captive market — and some facilities have even replaced free in-person visits with paid video calls, charging families for what used to cost nothing. Making communication free is a rounding error beside the tens of thousands of dollars spent each year warehousing a single prisoner, and jurisdictions like New York City and Connecticut already do it without disaster.
[P1.5] Imprisonment is the sentence. Isolation from one's children is not — and it certainly should not be sold back to those children at a profit. The question in this debate is not whether taxpayers can afford free calls; it is whether we can defend a system whose business model runs on a grandmother choosing between groceries and a phone call with her grandson. End it.

That is the wrong remedy, and it will deliver worse contact, not better.
[C1.2] First, "free" does not mean unlimited; it means rationed some other way. A housing unit has a handful of phones and a limited number of video terminals. When price disappears, demand surges and queues form. Inside a prison, queues are not polite.
[C1.3] The strongest prisoners, or organized groups, control phone time and sell or trade slots. A small per-minute charge at cost is a transparent rationing device. Take it away and access is allocated by intimidation, and the timid prisoner calling his daughter loses out.
[C1.4] Second, PRO says the marginal cost is "pennies." The dial tone is. Correctional calls are not. They must be recorded, screened for three-way connections, and monitored for witness intimidation and drug coordination.
[C1.5] That costs staff time and infrastructure, and more calls mean more of it. Someone pays. If legislatures do not appropriate new money, and they rarely prioritize prisoners, the cost comes out of education, treatment, and reentry programs, which do the very recidivism work PRO cares about.
[C1.6] Third, free state video turns PRO's own warning [P1.4] against them. PRO worries that facilities replaced in-person visits with video. Make video free and state-run, and administrators get an ideal justification: "Families have unlimited free screen time, so why staff a visiting room?" In-person contact, the most valuable kind, is what gets cut.
[C1.7] So compare the two paths. Cost-based caps with no commissions cure the grandmother's dilemma. Families pay a few cents a minute, capacity is managed, and security is funded. Mandatory free provision promises more and delivers queues, cuts to programs, and emptier visiting rooms.
[C1.8] Fix the monopoly. Don't abolish the price.
