[P1.5] Finally, we must protect the essential raw material of AI. Foundation models do not create from nothing; they compress and recombine human expression. [C1.1] The proposition sounds tidy, but a compulsory license is the wrong instrument for this problem, and adopting it would lock in harms that exceed the harms it claims to fix.
[C1.2] Start with what "compulsory" actually means. It strips rightsholders of the right to say no. A novelist who does not want her work feeding a model that will imitate her voice loses that choice by statute. A photographer who objects to his images training a system that generates deepfakes is forced into the pool and handed a check. PRO frames this as protecting creators; it actually overrides them.
[C1.3] Any creator who currently has a viable infringement claim — and many do under existing law — has that claim extinguished and replaced with a government-set rate. That is a transfer from creators to AI labs, dressed up as compensation.
[C1.4] Second, the ASCAP analogy collapses on contact. Music licensing covers discrete, identifiable performances of registered works with metered uses. Training ingests trillions of tokens from billions of works, most unregistered, many orphan, scraped in tangled provenance. There is no plausible mechanism to attribute royalties fairly.
[C1.5] The realistic outcome is a slush fund captured by large publishers and stock-image conglomerates, with individual writers and artists receiving pennies — exactly the pathology that plagues existing collective licensing.
[C1.6] Third, compulsory licensing entrenches incumbents. Only Google, Microsoft, Meta, and OpenAI can write the statutory checks at web scale. Open-source projects, academic researchers, and startups get crushed. We would be using copyright law to hand the foundation-model market to four companies, permanently.
[C1.7] Fourth, it preempts better answers. Courts are already sorting fair use case by case; markets are producing voluntary deals (news archives, stock libraries); opt-out registries are emerging. A statutory license freezes this evolving landscape into one rigid scheme designed for today's architectures, which will be obsolete in five years.
[C1.8] The right verdict is to reject a clumsy, creator-disempowering, incumbent-entrenching mandate.
