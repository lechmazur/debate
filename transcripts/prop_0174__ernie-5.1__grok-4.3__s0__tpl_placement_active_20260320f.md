[C1.1] Aging infrastructure carries real risks, yet the claim that it constitutes an unrecognized crisis demanding a rigid tripling of maintenance spending before any new construction rests on a false scarcity. Wealthy countries already direct substantial portions of transport and utility budgets toward upkeep; the visible gaps stem more from inefficient allocation, labor shortages, and regulatory delays than from outright neglect. Blanket increases would simply amplify waste without guaranteeing safer outcomes.
[C1.2] The proposed sequencing rule is the deeper flaw. New megaprojects frequently replace or modernize the oldest, highest-risk assets rather than merely adding parallel capacity. A replacement bridge or upgraded water system can embed current standards for durability, seismic resistance, and lower lifetime maintenance while serving growing demand that older structures were never designed to meet. Halting such work until an arbitrary maintenance multiple is reached would freeze precisely the investments that reduce long-term risk most effectively.
[C1.3] Tripling maintenance budgets also ignores diminishing returns. Once core safety thresholds are satisfied, additional spending on existing concrete yields smaller safety gains than equivalent resources applied to network resilience, traffic management technology, or selective replacement. Wealthy societies face competing safety pressures—flood defenses, hospital capacity, energy grid hardening—that do not pause during an infrastructure moratorium.
[C1.4] The motion therefore imposes a mechanical priority that severs the connection between spending and actual risk reduction. Targeted maintenance paired with selective new construction that retires obsolete assets produces faster safety improvements than any across-the-board funding rule.
