[P1.1] Noncompetes are not simply private contracts; they are state-enforced restrictions on a worker’s ability to sell the one asset most people have: their labor. The proposition asks whether governments should restrict them for nearly all workers instead of writing broad employer exceptions. It should pass if broad exceptions predictably expand into routine jobs and if narrower rules can protect genuine business interests without freezing labor markets.
[P1.2] First, broad exceptions are an invitation to abuse. When the standard is whatever an employer calls “confidential information,” “training investment,” or “customer relationships,” almost any job can be covered. A warehouse supervisor knows routing; a sales associate knows clients; a nurse knows patients. The employer drafts the clause, the worker often sees it after accepting the job, and the mere threat of litigation deters movement even when the clause would not survive in court.
[P1.3] That chilling effect is the point: it suppresses outside offers, weakens bargaining power, and keeps wages pinned.
[P1.4] Second, the economic stakes are not just individual fairness; they are dynamism. Labor markets work when skills move. When workers cannot take better jobs, startups cannot hire experienced staff, incumbents face less pressure to raise pay or improve conditions, and regional clusters thin out. Restrictions are especially important for workers without rare trade secrets: technicians, retail managers, caregivers, franchise employees, and early-career professionals.
[P1.5] For them, a noncompete rarely protects innovation; it often just protects a firm from competition for labor.
[P1.6] CON will say some workers truly hold secret formulas or sensitive strategic plans. But that is why governments should restrict noncompetes for nearly all workers and keep only tight, role-specific exceptions. Trade-secret law, confidentiality agreements, nonsolicitation clauses, and garden leave for genuinely senior executives can address real harms. Broad employer exceptions are a blunt instrument that sacrifices ordinary workers’ mobility to avoid drafting precision.
[P1.7] The better rule is simple: if a firm wants to stop competition, it should compete on wages and conditions, not on legal locks.

A near-universal restriction would not simply free workers; it would make employers stingier with training, client exposure, pricing authority, and operational know-how. The workers harmed most are often the ones PRO claims to champion: technicians, sales staff, account managers, and early-career employees who need access and development to become more valuable.
[C1.2] A small company may not have a secret formula, but it has training investments, client lists, and soft intelligence about local customers. If a new firm spends a year teaching an account manager its methods and introducing them to clients, that manager’s exit should not become the firm’s punishment. PRO’s fallback—trade-secret law and confidentiality agreements—is too narrow. Client goodwill, curated relationships, and nonpublic but not formally “secret” business methods can be legitimate interests that workers carry with them.
[C1.3] Broad exceptions do not require unreviewable clauses. Governments can keep reasonableness limits, duration limits, consideration, and judicial blue-pencilling. That preserves flexibility and deters abusive scope better than a categorical near-ban. PRO caricatures broad exceptions as “whatever an employer calls confidential,” but a well-designed statute can tie enforceability to factual showings: genuine customer relationships, specialized training, or proprietary pricing information.
[C1.4] It is false that only senior executives have protectable interests.
[C1.5] Finally, mobility without investment is not dynamism. If firms cannot secure some return from developing workers, they will hoard knowledge, hire less, and atomize work. The better rule is calibrated enforcement against abusive clauses, not a blunt restriction that treats nearly all workers as if no legitimate employer interest can exist.

[P2.2] The hold-up problem has narrower solutions. If a firm shares real secrets, confidentiality and trade-secret law apply. If it fears client poaching, a time-limited nonsolicitation clause can protect relationships without prohibiting employment. If it pays for documented, expensive training, it can use repayment or retention bonuses that do not ban future work.
[P2.3] Those tools let firms recover investment while leaving the labor market open. Small firms can use these tools too, and they cannot afford litigation over every departure. A noncompete does something harsher: it makes the worker’s general skill and local network unusable.

Reasonableness is decided after the worker has already received the outside offer, often with no ability to pay a lawyer. Blue-pencilling makes overdrafting safe: employers can write broad clauses and rely on courts to trim them, while workers assume the worst.
[P2.5] Broad exceptions therefore do not merely police genuine harms; they distribute bargaining power to the party that drafts.
[P2.6] Finally, CON’s “mobility without investment is not dynamism” [C1.5] sets up a false choice. The clash is whether the default remedy for ordinary training and client relationships should be a work ban. It should not. [C2.2] The proposition’s answer is to erase the line entirely, sacrificing real investments in ordinary jobs because some employers draft clauses badly.
[C2.3] The substitutes PRO offers [P2.2] protect information, not relationships. Trade-secret law misses negotiated service habits, pricing tolerances, and years of company-funded introductions. Repayment clauses merely make exit a cost; they do not prevent the rival firm from buying the worker and harvesting the investment.
[C2.4] The account manager, field technician, or home-care coordinator PRO dismisses as “ordinary” is often the exact person whose trained relationships carry the business.
[C2.5] On enforcement risk [P2.4], the chilling effect is a reason to regulate clauses sharply, not ban their subject matter. Fee-shifting, safe harbors, notice requirements, and voiding overbroad clauses can curb overdrafting far more precisely than a near-universal restriction. PRO’s preferred tools create the same after-the-fact litigation threat, so that problem does not uniquely discredit noncompetes.
[C2.6] The decisive point is incentive. If firms cannot secure any enforceable return from developing workers, they will not suddenly invest anyway; they will hoard client contact, fragment jobs, limit training, and hire from outside rather than promote from within. A calibrated regime with broad but bounded employer exceptions protects mobility where no genuine investment exists and protects development where it does. That is a better default than treating nearly every worker as if no legitimate relationship investment can ever justify a limited work ban.
