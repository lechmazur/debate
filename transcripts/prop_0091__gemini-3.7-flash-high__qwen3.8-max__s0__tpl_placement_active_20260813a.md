[P1.1] When someone purchases a car, they buy a physical machine, not a revocable software license. Locking existing, onboard hardware—such as heated seats, full battery capacity, or performance steering—behind recurring subscription paywalls fundamentally distorts property rights and turns owners into perpetual tenants of their own vehicles.
[P1.2] First, gating physical components behind software paywalls is an extractive double-dip. Automakers do not install hardware out of charity; the cost of manufacturing, wiring, and assembling these components is inevitably baked into the vehicle’s baseline cost. Furthermore, the driver carries the ongoing burdens of that hardware: they haul its extra weight, absorb the efficiency penalties, and bear the long-term repair liabilities. Demanding a monthly fee simply to flip a digital switch on physical parts already bolted into the chassis is not an ongoing service—it is rent-seeking on dormant metal and silicon.
[P1.3] Second, subscription-gated hardware undermines vehicle longevity and the secondary market. A car is a durable asset built to last decades. When hardware functionality depends on recurring authorization, vehicle utility becomes hostage to corporate servers and proprietary billing systems. If a manufacturer alters terms, raises prices, or sunsets its digital infrastructure, functional physical equipment becomes dead weight.
[P1.4] This degrades resale value, complicates used-car markets, and accelerates artificial obsolescence.
[P1.5] There is a vital distinction between genuine ongoing services that generate recurring cloud costs—such as live satellite navigation or cellular streaming—and local, onboard hardware that costs the manufacturer nothing to operate once installed. Hardware already present in the vehicle requires no continuous labor or bandwidth from the automaker. Once a car leaves the dealership, the owner who purchased it must retain permanent, unencumbered access to every physical capability built into the machine.
