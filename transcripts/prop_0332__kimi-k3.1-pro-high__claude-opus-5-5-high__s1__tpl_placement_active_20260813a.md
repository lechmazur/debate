[P1.1] A fifteen-year-old watching a favourite creator does not experience a casino promotion as a sober lesson in probability. They experience it as a trusted person turning risk into entertainment: a bonus code, a celebratory win, a link one tap away. When commercial speech uses that trust to recruit likely underage audiences into high-risk gambling, the law should stop the promotion.
[P1.2] The harm justifies intervention. High-risk products—such as fast, continuously available online casino games—combine repeated play, rapid losses, and incentives to keep staking. Minors are especially poorly placed to assess manipulated odds, disguised advertising, and losses that feel temporary during play. The relevant exposure occurs before any gambling-site age check: the creator has already made the product attractive, normalised participation, and supplied the route to it.
[P1.3] An “18+” label on the destination cannot undo that recruitment.
[P1.4] Influencer promotion is not interchangeable with an ordinary advertisement. Its force comes from apparent authenticity and an ongoing relationship. A creator can say, “I use this,” celebrate a jackpot, or offer an exclusive code while commercial intent fades into ordinary content. Disclosures may identify sponsorship; they do not remove the endorsement or protect children from its persuasive design.
[P1.5] Expecting parents to monitor every embedded promotion across videos, streams, and disappearing posts simply privatises an enforcement problem created by professional marketers.
[P1.6] CON will invoke adult choice and free expression. But this motion does not ban gambling, prohibit discussion of it, or silence criticism and reporting. It prohibits promotion of a narrowly defined dangerous product where the audience is likely to include minors. Adults retain access to gambling information through channels not built around youth reach; creators retain their voices, but not a legal entitlement to monetise them through underage recruitment.
[P1.7] Self-regulation is inadequate because platforms and promoters profit from reach, while harm falls on families later. A clear legal rule changes that incentive before exposure. The decisive comparison is therefore modest speech restriction against preventable addictive and financial harm to children. On that comparison, prohibition wins.
