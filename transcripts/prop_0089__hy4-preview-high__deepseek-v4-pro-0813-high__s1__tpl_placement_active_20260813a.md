[P1.1] Debates get decided by the wrong scoreboard. If the test is whether a third-grader's math score is still a few points higher than a control group's, the case looks shaky. But that is not the motion. Universal pre-K must be judged on gains that endure: higher graduation, reduced special-education placement, less crime, higher earnings, civic stability.
[P1.2] Test-score "fadeout" is compatible with real persistence, because control children catch up on narrow basics while treated children keep quieter advantages—self-control, persistence, ability to function in a group. Those reappear when school gets harder and employers reward reliability.
[P1.3] The mechanism is straightforward. Ages three and four are a sensitive period for language, executive function, and social understanding. In a good classroom, a child never read to hears thousands of new words; a child who cannot wait or share is coached, not expelled. Long-run follow-ups of intensive early-childhood programs—smaller than universal proposals, but built on the same ingredients—found attendees more likely to finish school, hold jobs, and avoid arrest.

[P1.5] Picture a kindergarten where half the children are ready to listen, line up, and recognize letters, and half are not. [P1.6] "Targeted instead" is a trap. Means-tested programs erode politically, miss struggling children just above the cutoff, and lose the middle-class peers that improve classrooms. Universal design buys steadier quality and broader support.
[P1.7] Critics will lean on fadeout and sticker price. But sticker price ignores offsets: less grade repetition, less special education, less crime, higher earnings and tax contributions. Scale those into a universal entitlement and the treatment changes: thinner teacher pipelines, lower average instructional quality, less individualized attention, and classrooms filled with children who would otherwise be in adequate private or family care.
[C1.3] A program that works at a model site does not automatically work statewide.

[C1.6] The promised offsets—less crime, less remediation, higher taxes—are uncertain, distant, and sensitive to assumptions. They do not provide a reliable fiscal return.
[C1.7] The better comparator is not doing nothing. It is directing intensive services to children who need them most, or improving K-2 instruction. Universal pre-K spreads resources thinly to buy political breadth, but political popularity is not educational effectiveness. A universal slot can be mediocre, and a mediocre slot is not worth its public cost.
