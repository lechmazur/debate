[P1.1] Self-custody cryptocurrency wallets—where users control their own private keys rather than entrusting assets to third parties—represent a fundamental choice about financial autonomy. The proposition is straightforward: these wallets should remain legal by default, with restrictions only when tied to specific criminal conduct. This is not radical; it is the application of established legal principles to new technology.
[P1.2] The presumption of innocence is foundational. We do not criminalize tools because they *might* be misused. Encrypted messaging apps protect dissidents and journalists while also shielding criminals. Cash enables private transactions and financial inclusion while facilitating tax evasion.
[P1.3] Cars enable mobility while enabling getaways. In each case, we prosecute the underlying crime—money laundering, fraud, trafficking—not mere possession of the tool. Self-custody wallets deserve identical treatment.
[P1.4] Consider the legitimate interests at stake. Millions use these wallets to protect savings from hyperinflation in Argentina and Turkey, to preserve wealth across borders as refugees, or to transact without corporate surveillance. When FTX collapsed, self-custody users kept their assets while those trusting the exchange lost billions. Financial self-sovereignty has real value for real people.
[P1.5] The alternative—restricting self-custody by default—punishes lawful users preemptively while failing to stop determined criminals. Wallet software is open-source and global; prohibition cannot eliminate it. What restrictions would do is drive ordinary users toward less secure options, expose them to exchange failures and seizures, and undermine the privacy rights of law-abiding citizens who have done nothing wrong.
[P1.6] The burden belongs on the state to demonstrate specific wrongdoing, not on citizens to justify their tools. This principle separates free societies from authoritarian ones. A wallet is a container for value—no more inherently criminal than a safe or a lockbox. The question is not whether criminals use these tools, but whether we abandon the presumption of innocence to pursue them.

[C1.1] The motion sounds like common sense until you notice what it actually forbids: any preventive regulation at all. "Legal by default unless directly tied to specific criminal conduct" means the state may act only after the harm—after the ransom is paid, after the sanctions are evaded, after the pensioner's savings vanish into a wallet no one can identify, freeze, or reverse. That prosecution-only model is exactly the framework we rejected for the rest of finance a century ago, and for good reason.
[C1.2] Notice that PRO's own analogies cut against the motion. Cars are not legal by default unless tied to a crime—we require registration, licensing, insurance, and inspection before you touch the road, precisely because the harm is fast and irreversible. Banks must verify customers and report suspicious flows before money laundering happens, not after. The entire architecture of anti-money-laundering law is preventive, and it works only because there are gatekeepers.
[C1.3] Self-custody wallets are engineered to have none. Granting them blanket default legality carves a permanent, unmonitorable hole in that architecture.
[C1.4] And the harms flowing through that hole are not hypothetical. North Korea finances its weapons program substantially through stolen crypto moved into self-hosted wallets and mixers. Ransomware exists as an industry because attackers can demand payment to addresses no institution controls. Pig-butchering scams drain life savings into wallets that, by the motion's standard, remain presumptively legal until investigators can "directly tie" them to crime—a near-impossible evidentiary bar when keys are anonymous and funds hop chains in minutes.
[C1.5] The alternative is not prohibition, and PRO should not be allowed to pretend it is. It is conditional legality: registration thresholds, identity attestation for large transfers, travel-rule compliance at on-ramps—the same neutral rules every other channel for moving serious money accepts. The motion forecloses all of that by fiat. A society can honor financial autonomy without granting any tool a constitutional exemption from prevention.
[C1.6] This motion grants exactly that, and that is why it fails.
