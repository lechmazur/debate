[P1.1] Long-term care is a risk, not a certainty, and that single fact decides this debate. Some of us will die after a short illness needing almost nothing. Others will spend eight years with dementia needing round-the-clock help that costs more than a house. Nobody knows at fifty which they will be.
[P1.2] When a cost is unpredictable, catastrophic, and unevenly distributed, the rational response is to pool it. We do exactly this for fires, car crashes, and hospital bills. The status quo asks each household to self-insure against a lottery it cannot see.
[P1.3] Every alternative to pooling fails on its own terms. Private savings cannot work, because saving for the worst case means most people oversave while the unlucky still run out. Private insurance has been tried and has largely withered: insurers cannot price a risk thirty years away, premiums spike, carriers leave, and the people who most need coverage are refused. Means testing is not a safety net but a trapdoor: it pays only after you are ruined, which punishes the thrift critics claim to prize and rewards hiding assets.
[P1.4] And "family" is a euphemism for unpaid daughters and wives leaving jobs, losing pensions, and wrecking their own health, a cost no budget records.
[P1.5] So the question is never whether society pays for elder care. It already does, through bankrupted savers, shrunken workforces, and hospital beds blocked by people with nowhere safe to go. The question is whether we pay in an organised, predictable way or a random, cruel one. Germany and Japan chose social insurance for long-term care decades ago, and neither has abandoned it; both adjust it, as you adjust anything worth keeping.
[P1.6] Hold the other side to one test: name a mechanism other than broad pooling that protects the ordinary family from the dementia lottery.

[C1.1] Pooling is not the issue. The issue is what you pool, for whom, and who pays. The motion demands universal or near-universal systems, which means paying for the expected, routine costs of ageing for everyone, including millionaires, out of the wages of a shrinking working generation. That is not insurance against a lottery.
[C1.2] It is a subsidy to inheritance.
[C1.3] Take the dementia lottery test head-on. The answer is a catastrophic backstop: a hard cap on lifetime out-of-pocket care costs, with public money stepping in above it, means-tested below it, and family and savings carrying the ordinary first years. Fire insurance works because fires are rare and the payout is bounded. Elder care is the opposite: most people need some help, and once care is free at the point of use, demand expands without limit.
[C1.4] Economists call it the woodwork effect. Families who would have managed a parent at home convert to formal, paid care because the state now pays. The result is not organised risk-sharing but a bill that doubles.
[C1.5] Look honestly at the examples offered. Japan's premiums have climbed steadily since launch, eligibility was tightened and co-payments raised for better-off users. Germany's benefit has lagged real costs for years, so families still cover large gaps, and contribution rates keep rising. The Netherlands, once the model of universal coverage, pushed much of it back to municipalities and families because it became unaffordable.
[C1.6] "Adjusting" in practice means retrenchment toward targeting. The universal promise is made at the start and quietly broken later, which is crueller than never making it.
[C1.7] Meanwhile every euro pooled for elder care is a euro taken from young workers, child care, and housing, transferred upward to the wealthiest age cohort in history. Means testing is not a trapdoor. It is the only way to spend scarce money where need actually exists.
