[P1.1] The home is not an office with worse lighting. When a company points an AI camera into a remote worker’s room and records every keystroke, it is not checking attendance. It is collecting faces of children, fragments of private conversation, banking passwords, unsent emails, and the pauses an algorithm will later brand as “disengagement.” That monitoring should be allowed only with opt-in consent and strict limits, because without those constraints it is not management. It is a power grab into private life.
[P1.2] Employment is a bargain under pressure. Most people cannot walk away from a paycheck. If video and keystroke surveillance is bundled into the job, calling it consent is a legal fiction. Real opt-in means the same work, the same pay, and the same promotions available without the feed.
[P1.3] Otherwise the “choice” is unemployment versus a camera in the kitchen.
[P1.4] Limits are not red tape. They are the difference between a tool and a dragnet. Use must be for a stated, narrow purpose. Data must be minimized, kept briefly, and barred from sale, from emotion scoring, and from secret scoring of character.
[P1.5] Adverse decisions need a human who can be challenged. Always-on home video is almost never necessary when deliverables, version history, and scheduled presence already show whether work happened.
[P1.6] Drop those conditions and the costs land on the worker and, eventually, the firm. People perform busyness for software. Trust dies. Talent flees.
[P1.7] The company learns less about output and more about who will live under a lens. Measuring work by results is older, cheaper, and more honest than filming a living room.
[P1.8] Companies do not need a ban. They need a rule: the privilege of filming a home and logging every key happens only if workers actually agree, and only inside hard rails. That is the minimum for treating remote employees as people rather than data sources.
