[P1.1] The central question is simple: when a downturn hits and debt is already high, what actually stabilizes the debt ratio — letting automatic stabilizers work, or choking them off? The answer is that the debt ratio is a fraction. Fiscal consolidation that deepens a slump shrinks the denominator faster than it trims the numerator. Greece-style austerity under elevated debt repeatedly produced exactly this perverse outcome: primary surpluses pursued while output collapsed, and debt ratios rose anyway.
[P1.2] Letting stabilizers expand is not recklessness about debt; it is arithmetic about debt.
[P1.3] Automatic stabilizers are uniquely well-suited to this job precisely because they are temporary and self-reversing. Unemployment insurance claims rise when the economy falls and fall when it recovers. Tax receipts do the mirror image. No legislator needs to pass a bill mid-crisis, no one needs to forecast the size of the shock in real time, and the support shuts itself off as the economy heals.
[P1.4] Compare that to discretionary austerity, which is timed by politics, not by the output gap.
[P1.5] Third, downturn multipliers are large. When the private sector is retrenching — households deleveraging, firms cutting investment — a dollar of government support withdrawn from an economy with idle resources does not vanish into neutral; it multiplies downward through lost incomes and second-round spending cuts. And when monetary policy is pinned near the lower bound, fiscal policy loses its offsetting headwind and gets more, not less, effective.
[P1.6] Fourth, hysteresis is the long-run fiscal risk my opponent must confront. Extended slumps destroy worker attachment, firm capabilities, and investment. Those output losses persist after the recession ends, permanently shrinking the tax base. The stabilizers that cost money in year one are the cheapest insurance against a permanently smaller economy and a permanently higher debt ratio.
[P1.7] PRO wins if the metric is the debt ratio over the medium term plus economic stability. On both, expansion wins.
