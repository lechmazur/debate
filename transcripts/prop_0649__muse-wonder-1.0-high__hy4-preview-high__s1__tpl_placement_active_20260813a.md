[P1.1] At noon, a rooftop can generate far more electricity than its owner can use. Today that surplus usually has two bad options: sell it to the utility for a pittance, or let it be wasted. The neighbor meters away meanwhile buys expensive power generated far away. Peer-to-peer rooftop solar trading legalizes the obvious: let the first household sell directly to the second at a price both prefer.
[P1.2] That price changes behavior. When households can earn a fair local return instead of a low feed-in rate, solar stops being a subsidized gesture and becomes a rational investment. Owners install larger arrays, pair them with batteries, and shift flexible demand like water heating and EV charging to sunny hours because nearby buyers value that energy. Rooftops are the fastest clean-energy land available — no new transmission corridor, no contested land use, just generation where demand already lives.
[P1.3] Banning peer trade depresses returns and slows that deployment.
[P1.4] Local trades also make the grid cheaper and tougher. Power consumed on the same street avoids long-distance transmission losses and reduces strain on overloaded feeders at peak solar hours. A neighborhood that can balance itself is less blackout-prone when a distant plant or line fails, and dynamic local prices reveal where storage and efficiency are worth most. Utilities still move and balance power, but they compete and coordinate rather than dictate the only legal buyer.
[P1.5] This is also democratizing. Renters, apartment residents, and families without a suitable roof cannot install panels, but they can buy a neighbor's surplus — often cheaper than retail — and keep spending in the community. Instead of value flowing only to a distant monopoly, ordinary people become producers, buyers, and investors.
[P1.6] Allowing is not deregulating recklessly. Countries can require licensed platforms, certified smart meters, transparent grid-use fees, and safety standards, as pilots have shown workable. The choice is not order versus chaos. It is wasting local abundance versus letting citizens trade it.

Peer-to-peer trading sells an illusion of disintermediation. The physical reality is a collectively funded network that both parties still depend on.
[C1.2] That illusion creates the central flaw: cost-shifting. Sellers and buyers want to capture the retail price gap while minimizing "grid-use fees" [P1.6]. Yet poles, transformers, and firm capacity are fixed costs. When prosumers trade locally and underpay for network services, the shortfall is recovered through higher fixed charges or volumetric rates on everyone else—above all renters and low-income households without suitable roofs.
[C1.3] PRO's democratization [P1.5] is inverted; P2P lets asset-owners extract more value from a system that non-owners still fund.
[C1.4] The grid needs balancing, not just local matching. Output is intermittent; a cloud or evening peak reverses flows. Someone must provide firm power and frequency control. Peer trades ignore that unless regulators impose fees so heavy the local premium vanishes—the outcome whenever the utility remains buyer of last resort.
[C1.5] If fees are light, the grid is underfunded; if accurate, "peer-to-peer" is just a billing layer with no autonomy. That dilemma defeats the motion.
[C1.6] There is also administrative cost. Licensed platforms, certified meters, and grid-use fees [P1.6] require data systems, consumer protection, and dispute resolution. Complexity favors the wealthy, who can game dynamic prices [P1.4] and batteries, while ordinary households face confusing contracts. And the cheapest clean energy comes from scale—utility solar, community solar, storage—not millions of bespoke rooftop deals.
[C1.7] Countries should reject this motion. Rooftop surplus is real, but the fix is regulated compensation, cost-reflective network charges, and inclusive community solar—not bilateral trading. P2P trading mistakes a billing preference for market reform and makes everyone else pay.
