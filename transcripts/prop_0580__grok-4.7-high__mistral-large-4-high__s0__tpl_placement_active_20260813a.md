[P1.1] Expanding the child tax credit and paying it monthly attacks child poverty where it is measured: too little cash in the home. Equivalent spending on childcare subsidies does not. Poverty lines track income, not services. A refundable monthly credit lifts nearly every poor family with children, whether parents work, whether they use formal care, and whether this month’s crisis is rent, food, or a sitter.
[P1.2] A subsidy reaches only those who find an eligible provider, clear the paperwork, and buy covered care.
[P1.3] That gap in reach is the cost-effectiveness case. The deepest child poverty sits in households with weak labor-market attachment, irregular hours, disability, or infants cared for by relatives. Those children gain little from a childcare expansion and a great deal from cash. Even among the working poor, a subsidy is worth only the share of the budget that would otherwise buy formal care.
[P1.4] The same dollar as a credit can pay for care if that is the priority, or for the bill that actually keeps the child above the line. Cash covers the poverty gap; the subsidy covers one input.
[P1.5] Monthly delivery closes the rest of the comparison. Hardship is a cash-flow problem, not an annual accounting problem. Regular payments cut food and housing shortfalls in the months they occur. Subsidy systems more often mean waiting lists, provider shortages, and reimbursement rules that delay or shrink the benefit.
[P1.6] Leakage runs the other way as well. Childcare dollars partly bid up provider prices, partly pay for care that would have been bought anyway, and partly flow to higher-income families who use formal care at high rates. An expanded credit can be phased so the marginal dollar lands on low-income children. Same budget, broader coverage of the poverty gap, less waste on families who were never poor.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a poverty line for a poverty solution. The poverty measure counts cash, so a monthly child tax credit can lift a family over the line in the month it arrives. But that is static. The more cost-effective use of the same dollars is to remove the expense that keeps low-income parents out of the labor market: childcare.
[C1.2] For a parent with an infant, formal care can cost more than the wage from a low-paying job. A subsidy does not merely supplement income; it makes work possible. The resulting earnings recur after the subsidy ends and build the employment history that raises future wages. A monthly credit leaves the underlying constraint in place.
[C1.3] This is also a targeting argument. An expanded monthly credit reaches a broad swath of families above the poverty line and pays the same per child whether or not the family faces a binding care barrier. Childcare subsidies can be directed at low-income families who use care, and because the payment is tied to a real service, it is less likely to leak to higher-income households. The proposition calls this limited reach a weakness; it is the source of cost-effectiveness.
[C1.4] We are spending on families whose poverty is driven by the cost of working, not making every budget a little easier.
[C1.5] Finally, cash treats poverty as a monthly shortfall. The deepest child poverty is often a duration problem: parents who cannot work enough hours because care is unaffordable. Subsidized care attacks that directly, lowers a required expense, and raises market income at the same time. Those gains persist after the subsidy is no longer needed.
[C1.6] A monthly credit can be spent and gone; a parent who enters work because care is affordable keeps the income. The better question is not which payment shows up in this month's poverty statistics, but which spending changes the household's position next year. On that test, childcare subsidies win.
