[P1.1] Predictability is not profitability, and chasing short-term box-office safety is hollowing out the film industry’s future. Even when franchise sequels offer a more predictable ticket-sales floor, their astronomical costs make them fragile investments. Shifting more capital toward mid-budget originals is not art-house charity; it is disciplined portfolio management essential for studio survival.
[P1.2] First, mid-budget originals offer superior risk-reward asymmetry. A modern franchise sequel routinely requires massive production and marketing budgets, pushing its breakeven point into the stratosphere. When a bloated sequel slightly underperforms due to franchise fatigue, it triggers a catastrophic write-down. For the cost of one $200 million tentpole, a studio can fund five $40 million originals.
[P1.3] Because mid-budget films reliably recoup much of their cost through post-theatrical licensing and streaming windows, their downside is capped. Meanwhile, their upside is massive: one breakout like *Get Out*, *A Quiet Place*, or *Everything Everywhere All at Once* delivers return-on-investment multiples a bloated fifth sequel can rarely touch.
[P1.4] Second, mid-budget originals are the industry's research-and-development pipeline. Studios cannot harvest intellectual property forever without planting new seeds. Every lucrative recent franchise—from *John Wick* to *Knives Out*—began as a modestly budgeted original bet. Starving the mid-budget tier forces studios to strip-mine decaying IP until audiences walk away.
[P1.5] It also destroys the talent pipeline: without a mid-budget proving ground, studios pluck directors from tiny indie films and hand them massive blockbusters, inviting creative mismanagement and costly reshoots.
[P1.6] Finally, mid-budget films sustain the broader theatrical and streaming ecosystem. Relying solely on mega-sequels trains audiences to visit cinemas only a few times a year, leaving theaters barren in between. Mid-budget thrillers, comedies, and dramas keep diverse demographics engaged year-round while supplying studio streaming libraries with fresh, culturally vibrant titles that prevent subscriber churn.
[P1.7] Predictable box office means nothing when runaway costs and creative exhaustion break the model. Studios must fund mid-budget originals to secure their future.

[C1.1] The motion asks studios to move marginal dollars from the category that reliably returns capital toward the category that most often does not. That is not disciplined portfolio management; it is buying lottery tickets and calling it a strategy.
[C1.2] PRO's case is built entirely on survivorship bias. *Get Out*, *A Quiet Place*, *Everything Everywhere All at Once*, *Knives Out* — every example is cited precisely because it is an exception. The invisible denominator is the far larger number of mid-budget originals each year that open soft, fail to find an audience, and quietly become write-downs or straight-to-streaming losses. You cannot justify a category by pointing only at its winners. The relevant question is expected value across the whole slate, and on that measure the modestly budgeted original is the riskiest bet in the building: no pre-awareness, no built-in audience, no merchandise, no global brand pulling in opening weekend.
[C1.3] The "one $200 million sequel versus five $40 million originals" math smuggles in an assumption that five unbranded films are safer than one. They are not. Each original still carries a marketing spend that can rival its negative cost, and each must fight for attention in a market where most originals simply do not break through. Five coin flips are not a diversified portfolio when most of them resolve to zero.
[C1.4] Worse, PRO's proposal undercuts the very engine that funds risk-taking. Franchise sequels generate the reliable cash flow, the global pre-awareness, and the downstream licensing and merchandising that underwrite everything else a studio does. Shrink that to subsidize unproven bets and studios will not produce more daring work — they will produce less of everything, because the dependable floor disappears.
[C1.5] The R&D and talent-pipeline arguments do not rescue the motion either. Studios can plant seeds cheaply without spending more on production: acquiring finished films, signing first-look deals, optioning scripts, and greenlighting lower-cost bets. The motion says *spend more*, and PRO has not shown the marginal dollar beats these alternatives.
[C1.6] Predictability is not the enemy of creativity. It is what pays for it.
