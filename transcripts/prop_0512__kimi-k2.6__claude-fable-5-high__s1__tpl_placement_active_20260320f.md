[P1.1] Tax incentives for hyperscale data centers are not neutral economic development; they are a wealth transfer from local ratepayers and residents to global technology giants. Governments should cap these subsidies unless the beneficiary fully internalizes the grid and water costs it imposes. That condition is not anti-growth; it is the minimum standard for a fair deal.
[P1.2] A single hyperscale campus can consume as much electricity as a mid-sized city. When states offer decade-long tax abatements, they do not simply forgo revenue; they trigger a cascade of public infrastructure costs. Utilities must build new substations, transmission lines, and generation capacity to meet surging demand. Those capital costs are routinely socialized through rate hikes on households and small businesses, while the subsidized firm locks in discounted power.
[P1.3] The community gets the grid strain; the firm gets the tax holiday.
[P1.4] The same asymmetry appears in water. Cooling hyperscale loads requires enormous volumes, often in regions already facing scarcity. Local aquifers, treatment plants, and pipeline networks bear the strain, yet the tenant contributes little to the municipal budget that maintains them. Residents face usage restrictions and rising water bills while the incentivized center continues to draw at industrial scale.
[P1.5] Capping incentives unless costs are fully internalized is not a ban; it is a hard budget constraint that forces honest accounting. It prevents jurisdictions from hiding infrastructure subsidies inside utility rate bases or deferred maintenance. If a data center truly generates net value, it can afford to pay for the electrons and gallons it consumes. If it cannot, the tax break was never a sound investment; it was simply a giveaway funded by the local public’s hidden overdraft.
[P1.6] No blank checks, no hidden overdrafts. Pay for the grid you load and the water you draw, or the public cap stays in place.

Grid and water costs are governed by utility tariffs, rate cases, interconnection agreements, and water permits. Tax incentives are governed by economic development law. If households are subsidizing data center power, the fix is a large-load tariff class that charges hyperscalers the full marginal cost of their connection—something regulators in several states are already implementing.
[C1.2] Capping a tax abatement does nothing to change who pays for a substation. PRO's remedy simply doesn't touch PRO's harm.
[C1.3] Worse, the condition "fully internalized" is unworkable as written. No regulator can certify that a facility's grid costs are "fully" internalized, because cost allocation on a shared network is inherently contested—transmission built for a data center also serves growing residential load; a hyperscaler's flat, predictable demand actually spreads fixed grid costs across more kilowatt-hours, which can lower average rates. A standard nobody can ever satisfy is not a cap; it is a ban dressed in accounting language, and it invites endless litigation over counterfactuals.
[C1.4] Then consider what jurisdictions actually lose. Data centers are among the largest property-tax payers many counties ever see, often even after abatements—rural Virginia and Oregon counties fund schools off them. They anchor fiber buildout, construction employment, and increasingly the renewable power purchase agreements and even new nuclear contracts that finance clean generation for everyone. Capacity demand is global and mobile: cap incentives in one state and the facility lands in the next one over—or overseas—taking the tax base with it while your residents still pay national-level costs of constrained AI and cloud infrastructure.
[C1.5] The honest framework is simple: price utilities correctly through utility regulation, negotiate water at industrial rates with reuse requirements, and let elected governments weigh tax incentives case by case. A blanket cap conditioned on an impossible standard delivers neither fairness nor growth—only forgone deals.
