[C1.1] A person sterilized against her will deserves recognition, care, and access to her own records. But the motion does not stop there. It also demands compensation for "descendants," and that is where its architecture fails.
[C1.2] Sterilization is an individual bodily wrong, not an inherited estate. The survivor’s injury is her own loss of reproductive capacity; it is not passed to grandchildren like an unpaid debt. Once descendants can claim, no limiting principle remains. Great-grandchildren, collateral relatives, and remote heirs all become moral claimants on the same public budget, and every payment diverted to a descendant is a payment not used for an aging survivor who actually needs nursing, housing, or medical care.
[C1.3] The concern in [P1.4]—that victims age without children to care for them—cuts in favor of concentrating funds on living victims, not multiplying claimants.
[C1.4] Archival access is equally overbroad. Records of forced sterilization contain more than the victim’s name. They hold diagnoses, family histories, and private details about parents, siblings, spouses, and staff. Opening them to descendants and researchers is not neutral truth-telling; it exposes third parties who never consented and may still be alive.
[C1.5] The actual survivor already has a narrower route: a claim process can compel production of her own file. The motion turns that personal remedy into a general archive right with real privacy costs for others.
[C1.6] Nor is closure concealment. A state can acknowledge the program, preserve records under controlled access, fund direct medical and psychological care, issue a public accounting—and then close the claims window. Closure is what allows a society to budget honestly and weigh past wrongs against current reproductive, disability, and elder-care needs. The opposite of a closed chapter is not justice; it is an endless document-and-descendant entitlement that drains the resources living survivors need most.
[C1.7] The motion bundles one defensible idea—living survivors matter—with two dangerous ones: hereditary cash and open files. Reject it as written.
