[P1.1] A rideshare driver with thousands of five-star trips has built something real: trust. Yet if she tries to switch apps, that trust vanishes. She starts over as a zero, indistinguishable from a beginner with no record. That erasure is not natural.
[P1.2] It is enforced lock-in.
[P1.3] Ratings and verified work history are not the platform's invention. The worker earned them trip by trip, delivery by delivery, client by client, through punctuality, skill, and care. The platform merely recorded them. Allowing the platform to keep that record hostage turns reputation into a leash.
[P1.4] Stay where you are exploited, or forfeit years of proof that you are good at your job. No free labor market should work that way.
[P1.5] Mandatory portability breaks that leash. With a standardized transfer — completed jobs, tenure, average rating, cancellation rate, verified badges — a worker can arrive on a rival platform as the experienced professional she is. That does three things at once. It gives workers bargaining power to reject bad pay and unsafe conditions because exit becomes real.
[P1.6] It forces incumbents to compete on pay and treatment instead of coasting on captive labor. And it lowers barriers for challenger platforms, which today must overcome both network effects and a cold-start reputation trap.
[P1.7] We already accept this principle elsewhere. Phone numbers move with customers, bank histories support credit, medical records follow patients. Reputation is a worker's professional identity, and portability respects that ownership.
[P1.8] Standardization does not mean blind equivalence. Receiving platforms can still weigh, discount, or display transferred data as they choose. It only means they cannot pretend the history does not exist, and dominant platforms cannot delete a worker's past to keep her future.

[C1.1] A five-star rating is not a portable deed to trust. It is a score produced inside one platform’s rules, customers, prices, and matching logic. Forcing rivals to ingest that score through a mandated pipe treats unlike judgments as interchangeable facts and shifts safety risk onto companies that never controlled the conditions that produced it.
[C1.2] Platforms do not merely notarize virtue. They design the job, define a cancellation, choose which customers rate whom, and decide what “good” means. Speed praised on a rideshare app can be a liability where careful handling matters. A cancellation rate that is ordinary in one city or business model can be disqualifying in another.
[C1.3] Standardization flattens those differences into a false common language. Even if a receiving platform may “weigh” the import, it still inherits a number it did not define and cannot audit.
[C1.4] The harm is concrete. Gamed, bought, or grievance-driven histories travel with the worker. A company that paid for background checks, vehicle inspections, and review integrity watches a competitor free-ride on that trust and then wear the liability when the imported reputation fails. The rational reply is not higher pay.
[C1.5] It is thinner verification, stingier ratings, and less reason to build the reputation systems workers want to export.
[C1.6] The analogies collapse. A phone number is an identifier, not a performance grade. A credit file is a regulated financial record with dispute rights and uniform metrics. Gig ratings are proprietary judgments under proprietary conditions.
[C1.7] Workers already multi-app; skill shows up on the next platform within weeks. What should not be confiscated is one company’s private assessment of performance on its own terms.
[C1.8] Mandatory portability does not cut a leash. It nationalizes a quality signal, invites gaming, and punishes the platforms that invested in earning customer trust. Competition should reward better treatment, not seize reputation infrastructure.
