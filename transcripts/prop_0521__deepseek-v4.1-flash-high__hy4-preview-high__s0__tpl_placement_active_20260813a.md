[P1.1] The employer-funded training account is not a company tool; it is deferred compensation with a worker’s name on it. A nurse who earns a certification, a machinist who completes advanced CNC training, a software engineer who takes a cloud-security course while her employer chips in—these are investments the worker helped finance through labor, study time, and often lower current wages. When she moves to another hospital, shop, or firm in the same sector, that credential, account, and accrued benefit should travel with her.
[P1.2] Forfeiture is not just unfair; it is economically dumb. It turns training into a golden handcuff. Workers stay in mismatched jobs, hostile workplaces, or dead-end roles because leaving means losing thousands in accrued benefits. That traps talent and reduces productivity.
[P1.3] The same sector loses the return on training it already paid for. Portability keeps the skill active in the sector, not wiped out at the exit interview.
[P1.4] Employers’ legitimate concern is free-riding. But the proposition doesn’t abolish sensible vesting: contributions can vest over time, unvested balances can remain, and portability can be limited to same-sector moves. That still protects the employer’s short-term investment while preventing permanent confiscation of earned benefits. And if an employer truly needs retention, it should compete on pay and conditions—not by making the exit costly.
[P1.5] Portability also changes bargaining for the better. If workers can carry accounts, they demand training and choose employers who fund it. Firms get a larger, better-skilled sectoral talent pool and less duplicated retraining. The alternative—each employer hoarding and forfeiting training—underinvests in exactly the skills the sector needs.
[P1.6] The decisive principle is simple: employer-funded does not mean employer-owned forever. Workers should keep what they earned when they move within the sector. That is fair, efficient, and better for the economy.

The original hospital paid for a skill its rival now uses, and the portable account sweetens the rival’s offer.

[C1.6] Sector-wide, the result is underinvestment. If any firm can be forced to fund a portable entitlement that a poaching rival captures, rivals will let others train and then bid workers away. The talent pool shrinks because the funder cannot capture the return. Workers already gain from general training through higher wages and outside offers.
