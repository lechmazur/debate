[P1.1] A carbon price with an equal per-capita dividend beats green subsidies on the two things that actually decide whether decarbonization happens: coverage and durability.
[P1.2] Start with coverage. A subsidy only reaches the emissions a legislature thought to name. There is no plausible subsidy for the marginal ton in a cement kiln in one country, a diesel freight route in another, a poorly insulated apartment block, a farmer's fertilizer choice, or the decision to fly instead of taking the train. A price reaches all of them at once, and it reaches the conservation and efficiency margins that subsidies structurally cannot — because subsidies make clean things cheaper without ever making waste expensive.
[P1.3] Subsidize heat pumps and you get heat pumps. Price carbon and you get heat pumps, retrofits, smaller commutes, shorter supply chains, and a thousand substitutions no ministry could enumerate.
[P1.4] Then durability, which is where the dividend earns its keep. The honest objection to carbon pricing has always been political: fuel costs are visible and voters revolt. The dividend answers it directly. Because emissions rise steeply with income, a flat per-capita cheque makes most households net beneficiaries — the bottom half of the income distribution typically comes out ahead.
[P1.5] That converts a tax into something closer to a shared property right in the atmosphere, and property rights are politically sticky. Alaska's oil dividend is the clearest illustration: no politician touches it, because voters see the cheque with their name on it. Subsidies, by contrast, create narrow constituencies of incumbent firms who lobby to keep receiving money whether or not they are still cutting carbon.
[P1.6] And subsidies cost real money — borrowed or raised from other taxes — while paying many recipients for investments they would have made anyway. Dividends cost nothing net; the revenue returns to citizens rather than to shareholders of whichever industry lobbied best.
[P1.7] Governments are bad at guessing which technology wins and good at sending cheques. Build the policy around what the state does well.

[C1.1] The PRO case mistakes a textbook for a power plant.
[C1.2] Decarbonization is not about making the dirty life marginally more expensive and hoping people find alternatives. It is about building alternatives that do not yet exist at scale. A carbon price, even with a cheque attached, does not build a grid, a battery factory, a green steel plant, or a bus network. It punishes consumption while waiting for the market to supply what the market has not supplied.
[C1.3] That is why high prices fail the very coverage test PRO claims to win [P1.2][P1.3]. When a renter heats with gas because the landlord won't retrofit, when a truck driver has no electric charging corridor, when a city has no transit, demand is inelastic. You get higher bills, not lower emissions. A dividend does not fix inelasticity; it just recycles pain, and often back into the same fossil fuel, because the low-income household still has to heat and commute.
[C1.4] Subsidies solve the missing market. Solar and batteries did not get dramatically cheaper because we priced carbon. They collapsed in cost because Germany, China, and the US subsidized deployment and drove learning curves, scale, and supply chains. One subsidized gigafactory cuts more future emissions than a decade of modest carbon prices.
[C1.5] PRO's durability claim gets Alaska exactly backwards [P1.5]. Alaska's dividend makes oil drilling politically untouchable because voters love the oil cheque. A climate dividend that rises with emissions gives voters a material interest in high carbon prices staying high enough to fund cheques, but also in the status quo persisting. Green subsidies build a different coalition: workers who build turbines, communities with new plants, firms whose profits depend on clean tech winning.
[C1.6] If you want less carbon, you need to build more clean capital, not mail more cheques.
