[P1.1] Price discrimination has always existed, but algorithmic pricing changes its nature, and that change is what this debate turns on. A vendor at a bazaar haggling over a rug is visible: you can walk away, compare, haggle back. Algorithmic price discrimination is invisible. It uses what you pay for the rug—your browsing history, your device model, your location, your purchase timing—to compute a price calibrated to your maximum willingness to pay, in milliseconds, with no way for you to see the computation or contest it.
[P1.2] Three arguments, and the third is decisive.
[P1.3] First, informed consent is the foundation of legitimate market exchange. Markets allocate resources well when prices are signals that everyone can read. When the price signal is personalized and hidden, comparison shopping—the mechanism that disciplines sellers—breaks. [P1.4] The seller has abandoned the market game and is running an extraction game instead. Disclosure restores the market; prohibition restores the fairness. Those are the only two options consistent with legitimate commerce.

"Different customers pay different prices" sounds neutral until you notice which customers reliably pay more.
[P1.6] Third, the asymmetry is unfixable by consumer effort. The defense of hidden algorithmic pricing always reduces to "buyers can be smarter." [C1.1] The proposition treats individualized prices as a regulatory default problem. That gets the economics backwards. Price differences are how markets clear, expand access, and allocate scarce capacity. The question isn't whether two customers pay different amounts; it's whether the practice deceives, excludes, or harms competition.
[C1.2] On that test, algorithmic pricing is usually neither disclosure- nor ban-worthy.
[C1.3] Start with what PRO calls "maximum willingness to pay." That is a scary phrase for ordinary demand. Airlines charge less to Saturday-stay travelers, hotels discount unsold rooms, software offers student rates, insurers price risk, ride-hailing raises prices when drivers are scarce. Algorithms can do this faster and more precisely.
[C1.4] Often the beneficiaries are price-sensitive buyers: the coupon-clipper, the flexible traveler, the late-night shopper offered a discount to close a sale. A blanket disclose-or-ban rule would flatten those discounts or expose them to retaliation, raising average prices and shrinking output. That is a consumer-welfare loss, not fairness.
[C1.5] PRO's bazaar contrast [P1.1] proves too much. Posted prices are also opaque where it matters: you don't know the seller's cost or reserve price. Markets discipline sellers through entry, substitution, and reputation, not through every buyer seeing every price computation. Comparison shopping fails because of deception, fake reference prices, hidden fees, and collusion—already illegal or regulable.
[C1.6] Target those, not price differences.
[C1.7] PRO's regressivity examples [P1.5] conflate price discrimination with cost or risk differences: emergency surge pays drivers to show up; delivery fees reflect distance and labor; insurance reflects expected loss. Where protected traits are used to exploit, antidiscrimination law applies. A general rule would also capture benign personalization: loyalty discounts, hardship rates, targeted promotions. And disclosure is not costless: publishing individualized prices can help competitors coordinate and train customers to hold out, destroying discounts that help the vulnerable.
[C1.8] "Effectively the same product" is not stable either; timing, convenience, risk, and contract terms differ. Police deception and invidious discrimination, not differentiated pricing.
