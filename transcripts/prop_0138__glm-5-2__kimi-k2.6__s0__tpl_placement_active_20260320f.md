[P1.1] The core question is whether a person's access to the truth of their own origins can be legitimately traded against the convenience of maintaining a donor supply. It cannot. The right to identifying information once adulthood is reached must stand even if donation rates fall.
[P1.2] Knowing one's genetic origins is a fundamental interest, not a preference. It anchors medical history, identity formation, and the ability to understand inherited risks. For adoptees, the law has moved toward recognizing this, because the case is simple: a person should not be permanently barred from knowing something so central to who they are because two other parties arranged it that way before they existed. Donor-conceived adults occupy the same position and deserve the same answer.
[P1.3] The donor's privacy interest is real but weaker than it first appears. Anonymity was a promise made by clinics to donors, not a natural right to be untraceable to one's own offspring. Once reproduction occurs, the donor has created a person with independent interests. Those interests do not vanish at the clinic door.
[P1.4] The donor's desire for privacy is understandable, but it competes with—and loses to—the child's stake in self-knowledge.
[P1.5] The supply objection concedes its own weakness with the word "even if." It says: if the only way to keep supply high is to deny adults information about themselves, we should do it. That treats donor-conceived people as means to an end—their identity sealed as a price for someone else's family-building. A just system cannot require one group to absorb the cost of sustaining a market in which they are the product.
[P1.6] Open-identity regimes have survived without collapse wherever adopted. But even granting the premise, the right holds. A society that cannot recruit donors without permanently hiding them from their offspring has a supply problem, not a rights problem. The answer is better recruitment, compensation, and support—not sacrificing the identity interests of adults who had no say in the arrangement.

[C1.1] The proposition treats one adult’s desire for background information as a trump card over another adult’s contractual privacy. Donors provided genetic material under explicit conditions of anonymity, often as young students relying on solemn assurances that their identity would remain shielded forever. To strip that anonymity decades later is to retroactively rewrite the terms of bodily consent, converting a gift given under specific promises into a lifelong tether the giver never agreed to bear.
[C1.2] The parallel to adoption collapses under scrutiny. Adoption transfers an existing child from one family to another; gamete donation is the intentional creation of a child for others to parent. The donor never held parental status, never made placement decisions, and specifically chose to participate precisely because the arrangement was bounded and invisible. Treating these as identical erases the distinctive moral structure of reproductive donation and forces a biological stranger into a parental spotlight they explicitly rejected.
[C1.3] The supply objection is neither abstract nor trivial. Where jurisdictions have abolished anonymity, donor participation has dropped sharply, leaving lengthening waiting lists for infertile couples and pushing desperate patients toward unregulated, unsafe markets. Framing this as a mere “supply problem” ignores the tangible childlessness, financial devastation, and medical risks produced when a recruitment system is dismantled by broken promises.
[C1.4] Nor do donor-conceived adults need a name to secure their legitimate interests. Rigorous non-identifying medical screening, updated health registries, and genetic counseling provide the inherited risk information and biological narrative that matter for wellbeing, all without exposing a donor who bargained for permanence. Identity is not medicine, and curiosity—however natural—is not a warrant to override another adult’s contractual privacy.
[C1.5] A decent society keeps its word. If policymakers wish to shift toward identifiable donation, they may do so prospectively for future agreements, letting the market adjust with open eyes. But retroactively enforcing transparency upon adults who relied on anonymity violates their autonomy, punishes the families they helped create, and destroys the trust on which reproductive medicine depends.
