[P1.1] The proposition turns on a simple principle: parks, beaches, and waterfronts are not ordinary real estate; they are civic infrastructure. Their value is not only scenic but social: they are among the few places where people of different incomes, ages, and neighborhoods can occupy the same city on equal terms. When access is broadly free, a child in a dense apartment can reach grass, water, and play without paying a toll; an elderly resident can walk safely; families can gather without turning leisure into a transaction.
[P1.2] The danger is not that private operators can improve amenities. It is that “improvement” becomes a wedge for enclosure. Once a waterfront is packaged as a paid experience—ticketed zones, VIP beaches, privatized piers, event lawns leased to sponsors—the best parts of public land are quietly reclassified as consumer products. Access then follows ability to pay, not citizenship or need.
[P1.3] That entrenches inequality in the most visible way: the same city, divided by who can enter the shore.
[P1.4] This matters because exclusion feeds itself. If the most attractive public spaces become fee-gated, everyday residents lose contact with them, and political support for maintaining them weakens. Governments become more likely to hand them to private managers, who recover costs through charges and exclusivity. The result is a downward spiral: less public access, less shared ownership, and less willingness to fund ordinary upkeep.
[P1.5] Free access does not mean unmanaged access. Cities can fund maintenance through general budgets, modest charges for optional extras like deck chairs or guided tours, timed entry where fragile habitats need protection, and public rules against litter, noise, and private monopolization. If private money is invited in, it should add optional convenience—not fence off the commons.
[P1.6] But the baseline must remain: these spaces belong to everyone. The central choice is whether waterfronts stay common ground or become paywalled privileges.

[C1.5] Third, the "downward spiral" [P1.4] runs backwards. Underfunded free spaces become unsafe; the middle class abandons them for private clubs and suburbs; political support evaporates. Paid experiences bring back the people whose presence and money hold the coalition for public land together.

A working parent excluded by a clumsy queue is owed a better queue; a working parent excluded by a fee is told the shore is not hers unless she pays.
[P4.4] That is the difference between managing scarcity and selling common ground.
[P4.5] This is why PRO still wins. CON’s case has no limiting rule.
