[P1.1] Start with the arithmetic that subsidy defenders never want to face. A fuel subsidy pays out in proportion to fuel burned. So a wealthy Cairo or Riyadh household with two large cars, a private generator, air conditioning running year-round and a domestic worker driving errands captures many times the benefit of a poor family that walks, shares a minibus, and owns no vehicle at all. Every study of the incidence of these programs across the region has found the same shape: the richest fifth captures far more of the money than the poorest fifth.
[P1.2] If you set out to design a welfare instrument that transferred public wealth upward, you would design a universal fuel subsidy.
[P1.3] So the clash here is not "help the poor" versus "austerity." It is which instrument helps them. For every dollar spent, a targeted transfer reaches a poor household with something close to a dollar; a fuel subsidy reaches them with pennies, while the rest leaks to the affluent, to smugglers moving cheap diesel across borders, and to energy-wasteful industry. Egypt, Jordan, Morocco and Saudi Arabia have all moved down this road since roughly 2012, and Saudi Arabia's Citizen's Account showed the region can pair price reform with direct compensation.
[P1.4] The fiscal stakes make delay indefensible. These subsidies routinely swallow shares of the budget comparable to health and education spending combined. That is money not spent on clinics, schools, water networks, or the diversification these economies need as oil demand plateaus. And artificially cheap fuel actively entrenches the problem: it kills the business case for solar in some of the sunniest countries on earth, and it locks in traffic and air pollution that poison poor neighborhoods first.
[P1.5] Five years is precisely the right horizon: long enough to build registries and digital payment rails most of these states already largely possess, and short enough to beat the alternative, which is a sudden, unplanned, socially violent cut forced by the next oil price crash.
