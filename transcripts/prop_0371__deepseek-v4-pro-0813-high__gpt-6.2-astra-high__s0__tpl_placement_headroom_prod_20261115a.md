[P1.1] An airline ticket is a promise of transportation under normal conditions, not merely a seat while carrying nothing. Normal conditions include a reasonable carry-on: one cabin bag that fits the overhead bin and a small personal item. Requiring that baseline in the fare is therefore not an attack on optional extras; it is a floor against hidden costs.
[P1.2] The strongest reason is price transparency. Today two advertised fares are not comparable if one airline charges for a carry-on and another includes it. The traveler discovers the real price only after beginning checkout or at the gate. Mandating a baseline makes the headline price honest and restores actual competition.
[P1.3] Second, these upcharges are not genuine cost recovery. The overhead bin space already exists once the aircraft is flying; the marginal cost of a standard wheeled bag is negligible. Airlines use the carry-on fee as revenue extraction from passengers who cannot reasonably avoid traveling with medication, work equipment, valuables, or supplies for a multi-day trip. A mandatory baseline prevents the basic service from becoming a penalty.
[P1.4] Third, fragmented charges create operational harm. When passengers are penalized for a cabin bag, many overload personal items, crowd gate areas, and argue with agents over arbitrary enforcement. That slows boarding and makes flying worse for everyone. A clear included baseline reduces last-minute surprise charges and boarding conflict.
[P1.5] Airlines would remain free to charge for checked bags, seats, priority boarding, and larger bags. The proposition is narrow: the standard carry-on should be part of the ticket. If an airline wants to offer a lower fare for no carry-on, it can—but it should not be allowed to advertise that stripped-down price as if it were the ordinary fare. That is the central question, and it favors requiring the baseline.
