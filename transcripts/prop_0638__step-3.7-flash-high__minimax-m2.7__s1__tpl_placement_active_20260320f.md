[P1.1] The fundamental flaw of short-cycle competitive grant systems is that they are tailored to incremental, applied research with predictable short-term outcomes, not the high-risk, long-horizon work that drives paradigm-shifting fundamental science—the kind that creates entire new fields, solves unforeseen crises, and delivers returns no 2-year progress report could ever anticipate. Fundamental science, by definition, has no guaranteed endpoint, no clear milestones to hit in a 12 to 36 month funding cycle, and no preliminary data to prove its worth before work begins.
[P1.2] Forcing this work into the competitive grant framework means public funds flow almost exclusively to safe, low-risk projects that pad CVs rather than push the boundaries of human knowledge.
[P1.3] The Howard Hughes Medical Institute’s unrestricted, long-term investigator grants—averaging 5 to 7 years of funding with no requirement for annual progress reports or pre-specified deliverables—offer a clear proof of concept for block grant success. This program has produced 27 Nobel laureates, including foundational work on CRISPR gene editing, a technology that emerged from decades of research on bacterial immune systems with no clear applied path for most of its history. That work would have been defunded repeatedly under standard 3- to 5-year NIH R01 grants, which prioritize proven preliminary data over bold, untested ideas.
[P1.4] The opportunity cost of the current system is far steeper than any risk of misused block grant funds: billions in public money are already wasted annually on low-impact, incremental research that scientists pursue only to secure their next round of funding. Further, the current system’s administrative bloat is staggering: researchers spend 30 to 40% of their time writing grant proposals, reviewing peer applications, and filing compliance paperwork, rather than conducting actual research. That wasted labor alone far outweighs any marginal accountability lost to block grants.
[P1.5] If we want public funds to support the science that will define the next century—from climate resilience to next-generation medicine to quantum infrastructure—we have to stop forcing that work into a short-term, results-obsessed box it was never built to fit.

[C1.1] Block grants sound liberating until you ask who decides which scientists get them, on what basis, and what happens when they produce nothing. The motion frames accountability as a tolerable cost of better science. That framing is backwards. Accountability is not bureaucratic friction—it is the mechanism that ensures public money serves public purposes.
[C1.2] Without it, block grants become institutional welfare for researchers regardless of performance, entrenched at the expense of the next generation of scientists.
[C1.3] Competitive peer review, whatever its flaws, is the best mechanism humanity has invented for distinguishing genuine scientific merit from comfortable mediocrity. Switching to block grants would concentrate funding decisions in the hands of program managers, advisory panels, or institutional gatekeepers—removing the scientific community's own quality filter and replacing it with political and administrative judgment. History shows what happens when accountability disappears: agricultural subsidies flow to farms regardless of yield, defense contracts sustain vendors regardless of capability. There is no reason to believe publicly funded science would be immune.
[C1.4] The PRO side's flagship example is telling. The Howard Hughes Medical Institute is a private foundation, not a government agency. It answers to its own trustees, not to Congress or the public. When a democracy funds science, it answers to every taxpayer—including citizens who want cures for childhood cancers, solutions for antibiotic resistance, or climate research that serves national interests.
[C1.5] That accountability is not a burden to be minimized. It is the democratic contract that makes the funding possible at all.
[C1.6] Long grant cycles already exist within the competitive system. The NIH's R37 mechanism extends R01 grants to seven years for strong performers. The European Research Council's Consolidator and Advanced grants run five years. The current framework is not as rigid as opponents claim.
[C1.7] Finally, competitive grants protect scientific diversity. Block grants would favor established institutions with lobbying power and name recognition, starving emerging researchers and new fields of oxygen. Peer review, imperfect as it is, at least opens doors based on ideas rather than pedigree. That openness is worth preserving.
