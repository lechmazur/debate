[P1.1] Granting work authorization within sixty days transforms asylum seekers from public dependents into self-supporting residents. Under current backlogs, applicants routinely wait many months or even years before they may legally earn income. Removing the work ban after a brief initial security window breaks that dependency cycle.
[P1.2] An asylum seeker who can work pays rent, covers groceries, and contributes payroll taxes instead of drawing down scarce public resources meant for the truly unemployable. Every month of delayed authorization leaves cities paying for rent and food that a legal job would naturally cover.
[P1.3] The sixty-day standard is deliberately narrow. It confers only the right to work, not asylum itself. Fraudulent applicants seek permanent residency and eventual citizenship; they do not file bogus claims merely to secure a temporary work permit tied to a case they know will likely be denied in the end. The financial and legal payoff of fraud is the final protected status, not a few months of legal employment in restaurants, construction, or home health care.
[P1.4] Expedited work authorization therefore does not materially increase the incentive to file false claims.
[P1.5] Opponents will argue that faster work rights invite abuse. Yet the real abuse is a system that warehouses capable adults in publicly funded limbo. Fraud is already deterred by criminal penalties, biometric screening, and the rigorous asylum interview process; adding economic desperation to that deterrent is both cruel and fiscally foolish.
[P1.6] By contrast, allowing work quickly means fewer families in emergency shelters, fewer meals provided by government programs, and more immediate tax revenue. The trade is straightforward: replace public assistance with private wages, and do it without opening any meaningful new avenue for fraud. That is a bargain worth taking.
