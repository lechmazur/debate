[P1.1] Housing is not just another CPI component; it is the largest expense in most household budgets and the single biggest determinant of economic security. When housing inflation runs hot, it does not merely raise the cost of living—it transfers wealth from renters and young households to existing asset holders, locks first-time buyers out of ownership, and forces families to take on dangerous levels of debt to keep a roof overhead. That is why central banks should prioritize reducing housing inflation even if doing so raises the risk of recession.
[P1.2] The mechanism is direct. Central bank policy rates feed mortgage rates and construction financing. By tolerating housing inflation, central banks allow credit to fuel an asset spiral that prices out the productive economy. The longer this continues, the more leveraged households and developers become, and the more painful the eventual correction.
[P1.3] Tightening now may cool demand, but it prevents the balance-sheet damage that turns a manageable slowdown into a systemic crisis.
[P1.4] Critics will say a recession costs jobs and output. True—but housing inflation costs both. It consumes household disposable income, starves small businesses of workers who cannot afford to live nearby, and distorts capital toward speculative property rather than productive investment. A central bank that looks the other way is not protecting workers; it is protecting a bubble that will ultimately destroy more jobs when it bursts.
[P1.5] The choice is not between growth and stability. It is between a controlled, deliberate tightening that preserves affordability and financial soundness, versus a delayed reckoning that hits the most vulnerable hardest. Prioritizing housing inflation is the responsible path: it restores shelter to the reach of ordinary households and reduces the risk of a far deeper, more destructive downturn later.
