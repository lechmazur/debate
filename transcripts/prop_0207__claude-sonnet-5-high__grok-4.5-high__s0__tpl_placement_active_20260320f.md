[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are one of the most expensive ways governments have ever devised to help the poor. When a government caps the price of gasoline or diesel for everyone, the person who benefits most in absolute terms is the owner of the SUV or the factory running diesel generators, not the family riding a bus or cooking with kerosene. That regressive distribution is not an accident of implementation; it is baked into the mechanism itself. If you subsidize a liter of fuel, richer households simply consume more liters, so more money flows to people who need it least.
[P1.2] That single fact should decide this debate. The comparison is not "help versus no help." It is "expensive, leaky help versus cheaper, targeted help." Targeted cash transfers can be aimed at income brackets, verified through existing tax or social-registry systems, and delivered directly, so a larger share of every dollar spent actually reaches the households the policy claims to protect.
[P1.3] Fuel subsidies, by contrast, spray money across an entire economy, including tourists, large agribusiness, and industrial users, none of whom need the state's protection.
[P1.4] Broad price controls compound the problem by distorting the market signal itself. When fuel is artificially cheap, there is no incentive to conserve it, smuggling across borders becomes profitable, black markets emerge, and state budgets bleed money that could otherwise fund schools, clinics, or the very transfers we are proposing. Several countries that tried to unwind subsidies gradually found that fiscal pressure from subsidy costs eventually forced abrupt, chaotic price hikes anyway, precisely because the subsidy became unsustainable. A planned phase-out paired with direct transfers avoids that whiplash by giving vulnerable households cash before prices adjust, rather than freezing prices for everyone until the system breaks.
[P1.5] Cash transfers also preserve price signals, which price controls destroy. Households and firms still see the true cost of fuel, which means they still have reason to conserve, switch to alternatives, or invest in efficiency. That matters for long-run resource use as well as short-run fairness.

[C1.1] Universal fuel price protection should stay. The proposition swaps a blunt, universal shield for a narrow and brittle substitute that fails the very households it claims to rescue. Fuel is not an ordinary consumer good. When its price jumps, transport costs, food logistics, and household cooking fuel all move together.
[C1.2] A cash transfer can compensate only the people who make the list and only for a modeled fuel budget; it cannot stop the wider cascade that hits markets before the next payment cycle. Price stability at the source prevents that cascade. Cash chases it afterward and usually loses.
[C1.3] Targeting is sold as precision. In practice it is exclusion. Informal workers, mobile households, people without papers or digital access, and communities outside incomplete social registries drop out first. They still ride buses and buy kerosene.
[C1.4] Universal price controls reach them the moment they buy. A registry does not. The absolute-consumption argument that “the rich get more liters” ignores the relative damage: the poor spend a larger share of income on energy-linked prices and have no cushion when those prices spike. Removing the universal mechanism therefore taxes the poor more harshly even if their absolute liters are fewer.
[C1.5] Administrative reality makes the trade worse, not better. Building clean, frequent, fraud-resistant cash systems requires capacity many of the same governments lack. Leakage simply migrates from middle-class consumption into ghost beneficiaries, delayed payouts, political capture of lists, and inflation that erodes the transfer’s value. Fiscal savings on paper become social costs in the street: protest, emergency reversals, and emergency subsidy restorations after the damage is done.
[C1.6] Broad price management is imperfect and fiscally heavy. It is still more reliable insurance for mass livelihoods than a phase-out that banks on perfect targeting and perfect timing. Governments should reject the swap.
