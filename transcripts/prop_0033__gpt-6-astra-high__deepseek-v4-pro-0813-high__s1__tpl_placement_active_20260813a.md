[P1.1] Political campaigns should win support by persuading voters, not by secretly estimating their psychological vulnerabilities and tailoring pressure accordingly. The distinction is not between rational and emotional speech. Campaigns may tell moving stories, argue forcefully, and address people interested in particular policies. They should not use inferred psychological or emotional profiles to decide who receives which appeal.
[P1.2] Consider a campaign that infers anxiety from someone’s browsing behavior, then repeatedly serves that person threatening messages. The concern does not require mind control, perfect predictions, or even false statements. It is the conversion of private behavioral traces into a lever over political choice, without the voter understanding the basis for that treatment. Errors do not make this benign: a mistaken profile can still expose someone to manipulative pressure.
[P1.3] This practice also weakens democratic accountability. Public claims can be challenged by journalists, opponents, and fellow citizens. Profile-based targeting lets campaigns distribute different emotional realities to different audiences while obscuring the strategy connecting them. Publishing an advertisement afterward does not reveal why particular people received it, which inferred vulnerabilities were used, or how their exposure was optimized.
[P1.4] Transparency about content cannot substitute for limits on hidden profiling.
[P1.5] A workable prohibition would cover campaigns’ use of these profiles directly or through advertising vendors. Enforcement should examine targeting criteria, model inputs, and delivery instructions, with recordkeeping requirements and meaningful penalties. It need not prohibit geographic outreach, contextual advertising, or appeals based on voluntarily stated policy interests. Regulate the profiling mechanism, not the political viewpoint or emotional tone.
[P1.6] The cost is losing one tool for increasing campaign efficiency; the benefit is preserving a boundary against covert psychological sorting in elections. Smaller campaigns still have plentiful ways to find supporters. No campaign is entitled to every technology that might improve persuasion. When the tool’s distinctive advantage is exploiting what voters have not knowingly disclosed, prohibition is justified.
