[P1.1] Restitution is not a courier service. The motion’s core insight is that shipping an object back can complete a legal transfer while leaving the deeper injury untouched. Colonial extraction did not merely remove things; it stripped communities of the institutions, records, and expertise needed to care for them. Former colonial powers should therefore fund conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity—not as charity, but as part of the remedy.
[P1.2] Consider a returned sacred object or ancestral remain. In a recipient museum without trained conservators, stable storage, pest control, or documentation, it may deteriorate, be stolen, or become inaccessible to the community that fought for it. The object is home geographically but not restored relationally. Funding capacity changes that: it lets the receiving country decide how to conserve, display, or rebury, while building skills that outlast any single return.
[P1.3] Provenance research is equally inseparable. Colonial archives—shipping manifests, missionary records, military inventories, collectors’ correspondence—often remain in former imperial capitals. Returned objects frequently arrive with gaps: Which community? Which ceremony?
[P1.4] Was it sold, seized, or looted? Without funded research and shared records, source communities cannot reconstruct meaning, pursue further claims, or resist false ownership narratives. The former power holds the paper trail; it should help pay to reconnect it.
[P1.5] Museum capacity also protects restitution from its enemies. Weak documentation and security can feed black markets, giving opponents of return a pretext: “They cannot care for it.” That argument is patronizing, but it becomes politically potent when colonial inequities are left unfunded. Capacity support defuses it.
[P1.6] Crucially, this funding must be unconditional and recipient-led. It must not become a new leash, a management fee, or a condition on return. But the alternative—declaring victory when the crate is opened—is hollow. Restitution is complete only when return plus repair makes sovereignty real.

[C1.1] The proposition turns restitution into probation. It says the former colonial power must keep paying for conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity after the object arrives. That preserves the old metropole as the supervisor of repair, not the defendant that has discharged a duty. We should reject it.
[C1.2] Restitution’s purpose is to transfer authority, not to create a new oversight relationship. Once title, possession, and decision-making power have moved, the receiving community has the right to decide what happens next: display, storage, reburial, restricted access, or even letting an object follow a ceremonial life that professionals would call deterioration. A mandated funding stream inevitably brings conditions: audits, preservation standards, reporting, expert exchanges, and expectations of public access. Those conditions can clash with secrecy protocols, ritual obligations, or local governance.
[C1.3] A sacred mask or ancestral object may be harmed not by humidity alone but by being transformed into a professionally catalogued exhibit. Provenance funding carries the same risk: projects answerable to former imperial institutions can privilege colonial paperwork over oral memory and decide which claims count.
[C1.4] The motion also strengthens the very forces it claims to disarm. If funding and capacity are part of proper restitution, opponents can argue that every return must wait until museums are “ready,” and every conservation problem proves outside management is still needed. That is paternalism with a chequebook. It also lets wealthy former powers buy absolution: a conservation grant becomes a substitute for broader reparations, fuller returns, or unbundling the colonial archives they still control.
[C1.5] Where capacity is genuinely needed, it can be provided on request, by whichever partners the recipient chooses, without making the former colonizer a permanent co-manager. The decisive test is not whether the old power keeps writing checks; it is whether the receiving community has free control over the object. The proposition confuses repair with continued custody, and it should fail.
