[P1.1] Picture a certified nursing assistant at one nursing home whose employer funds a training account to become a licensed practical nurse. After two years, she takes a modestly better job at another care facility in the same health sector. Under the status quo, the account is lost. The skills she already learned are not; they stay in her head and in the sector.
[P1.2] What disappears is the earned funding she was promised. That is the wrong rule.
[P1.3] Employer-funded training is deferred compensation, not a tool of captivity. The employer paid for training during employment; the learning is now embodied in the worker and cannot be repossessed or unlearned. The training account is the earned benefit attached to that human capital, like a contributed retirement match or accrued leave. When a worker changes jobs within the same sector, forfeiture is not a firm reclaiming property; it is a penalty on mobility and a transfer of earned value to the employer.
[P1.4] The sector-wide payoff is decisive. Moving within the same sector keeps the skill in the industry. A worker trained in EV repair who moves from one dealership to another, or a logistics worker moving between warehouses, does not export the training to a competing industry; they raise productivity and innovation exactly where the sector needs it. Portability ensures the training is used where it has the highest return, instead of trapping a worker in a mismatched role to protect an account.
[P1.5] The objection is that employers will stop funding training if workers carry accounts to a rival. That fear proves too much. It would condemn every portable earned benefit and ignore the recruiting side: portable accounts attract talent, and every firm also hires workers with previously funded balances. The original employer already benefited from a better-trained worker during employment.
[P1.6] The fair and efficient rule is simple: when the skill stays in the sector, the funding follows the worker.

[C1.1] Training accounts are not vacation days or a retirement match. Vacation and retirement are compensation for labor already performed; the value is banked because the work is done. A training account is the opposite: it is an employer's forward bet on labor not yet performed. The employer pays now, hoping to recoup the investment through years of the trained worker's output.
[C1.2] That distinction is the whole case, and PRO's opening never confronts it.
[C1.3] Once you understand training as risk capital rather than earned wages, the mandate collapses into a simple economic prediction: force full portability and employers stop making the bet. Right now, firms that fund a CNA's path to LPN status routinely attach retention clauses or reimbursement clawbacks precisely because they need some assurance of return. That is not captivity; it is the market-tested mechanism that makes the training affordable to offer in the first place. Strip employers of any ability to structure that trade, and the rational response is not "pay anyway"—it's "stop paying."
[C1.4] The workers hurt worst are exactly the ones PRO's nursing assistant represents: lower-wage employees who cannot self-fund a credential and depend on the employer being willing to front the cost.
[C1.5] There's also a collective-action problem PRO's "sector payoff" ignores. If every firm knows a rival can costlessly poach a worker the moment training finishes, no single firm wants to be the one who pays for it. Everyone waits for someone else to train the workforce. The sector doesn't get more skilled labor moving efficiently—it gets less training funded overall, because the first-mover always loses to the free-rider next door.
[C1.6] Finally, "same sector" is not a clean line, it's a legal minefield. Is a staffing agency the same sector as the hospital it places workers into? Is a private equity–owned nursing chain the same sector as an independent facility? Every transfer becomes a dispute, which is its own quiet tax on the very mobility PRO claims to protect.
