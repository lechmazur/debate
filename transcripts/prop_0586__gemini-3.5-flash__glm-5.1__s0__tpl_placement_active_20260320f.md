[P1.1] A single, blunt minimum wage increase treats a multi-state retail conglomerate, a local diner, and a specialized agricultural enterprise as if they operate on the exact same margins. This structural blindness is why large, across-the-board minimum wage hikes trigger employment losses. Sectoral bargaining and wage boards offer a targeted, economically precise alternative that raises pay for low-wage workers while safeguarding their jobs.
[P1.2] First, sectoral bargaining aligns wage floors with industry-specific capacity. Different sectors have vastly different labor-to-revenue ratios and profit margins. A wage board bringing together workers, employers, and public representatives can set a floor that maximizes worker pay without exceeding the breaking point of that specific industry. Under a blunt minimum wage hike, vulnerable sectors like childcare or hospitality face immediate pressure to cut hours or lay off staff to survive.
[P1.3] Sectoral boards prevent this by tailoring wage schedules to what each specific market can bear.
[P1.4] Second, sectoral standards level the playing field, neutralizing the "low-road" competitive advantage. When an entire sector must comply with the same tailored wage structure, individual employers do not face a unilateral cost disadvantage. They are spared the choice between slashing staff or going under, as all competitors must meet the same standard. This structural uniformity reduces the pressure to downsize, stabilizing employment across the industry.
[P1.5] Finally, wage boards negotiate holistic compensation packages, not just a single hourly rate. When a blunt minimum wage mandate spikes labor costs overnight, employers often claw back compensation by cutting benefits, training, or predictable scheduling. Sectoral bargaining allows for mutually beneficial trade-offs—such as balancing wage increases with scheduling stability—ensuring that gains in pay do not come at the expense of job quality or headcount. For low-wage workers, sectoral bargaining delivers targeted prosperity without the pink slips.

[C1.1] Sectoral bargaining sounds precise until you look at how it would actually work in the United States. The proposition rests on a false premise: that large minimum wage increases cause significant employment losses that sectoral bargaining would avoid. Decades of empirical research—from Card and Krueger forward—show that moderate minimum wage hikes produce minimal job losses because employers adjust through reduced turnover, small price increases, and productivity gains. The "employment catastrophe" narrative is the very thing being challenged in current labor economics, so building an alternative on top of it is building on sand.
[C1.2] Even if you accept the premise, sectoral bargaining in the US faces three fatal problems.
[C1.3] First, regulatory capture. Wage boards would be dominated by industry incumbents with every incentive and resource toslow-walk, water down, or exempt their way out of meaningful increases. Minimum wage is a simple, enforceable rule—harder to erode through procedural obstruction. The history of occupational licensing and industry self-regulation shows who wins when complex sectoral negotiations replace clear standards.
[C1.4] Second, coverage gaps. Sectoral bargaining leaves workers stranded in the cracks—gig workers, domestic workers, employees of firms that resist classification. A minimum wage covers everyone in one stroke. Under sectoral bargaining, you need a board for every sector, and workers outside defined categories get nothing until someone builds them a table to sit at.
[C1.5] Third, delay. Each wage board requires study, negotiation, and rulemaking. Meanwhile, low-wage workers need relief now. The federal minimum has been stuck at $7.25 for fifteen years precisely because complex legislative processes favor inaction.
[C1.6] Sectoral bargaining multiplies those veto points.
[C1.7] The proposition asks workers to trade a proven tool for an untested bureaucracy that will be slower, more porous, and more vulnerable to industry capture. Minimum wage increases have a demonstrated track record of raising pay with minimal job loss. Sectoral bargaining offers theoretical elegance but practical paralysis.
