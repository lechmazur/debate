[P1.1] Europe cannot price carbon at home and give a free pass at the border. That is not free trade; it is subsidized pollution.
[P1.2] Inside Europe, steel, cement, aluminium and chemicals pay for every ton they emit. Without an equivalent charge on imports, every ton made cleanly and expensively in Europe is undercut by the same ton made dirtily and cheaply abroad. Production does not get cleaner, it moves. Emissions do not fall, they relocate.
[P1.3] A climate policy that exports jobs and imports carbon fails on both economics and environment.
[P1.4] That is why coverage must be broad. A narrow tax on raw steel invites evasion: foreign producers simply ship slightly processed goods — ingots become parts, slabs become machines — and leakage continues downstream. Covering most industrial goods closes that back door and tells investors plainly: if you sell into one of the world's largest markets, you play by its carbon rules. Half measures create paperwork without protection.
[P1.5] Retaliation does not overturn that logic. Washington or Beijing may threaten tariffs, but they would be taxing their own exporters' access to European consumers to defend their right to pollute — a costly bluff that fades when their lobbies count the losses. More importantly, that pressure is the mechanism working. When exporters must pay Brussels for their carbon, their governments gain every incentive to price carbon at home and keep the revenue, turning a trade fight into a race upward.
[P1.6] The real choice is not smooth trade versus a spat. It is a border charge with friction versus slow surrender: either Europe guts its carbon price to save its factories, or it keeps the price and watches its green industry die while global emissions rise. Only a broad adjustment lets Europe stay ambitious, keep firms investing in decarbonization at home, and force global producers to follow. Pay a short-term tariff cost to avoid long-term climate defeat.
