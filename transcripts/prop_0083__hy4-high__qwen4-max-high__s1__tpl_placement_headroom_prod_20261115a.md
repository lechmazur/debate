[P1.1] A dating profile is evidence, not decoration. Users rely on the face as evidence of a real person they might meet, and on chat as evidence of that person’s interest and character. AI-generated photos and AI-written conversation assistance break that chain. The recipient is not merely tolerating a flattering angle; they are being asked to trust a synthetic face and synthetic social performance as the account holder’s own.
[P1.2] That is why undisclosed use is deceptive: it changes the facts on which attraction and consent to continue depend.
[P1.3] The relevant line is augmentation versus substitution. Makeup, lighting, retouching, spellcheck, or advice from a friend leave the person as the source. A model that creates the face or writes the substantive reply changes the thing being evaluated. Spellcheck fixes how I spell; an AI wingman can manufacture the wit, vulnerability, and availability I seem to have.
[P1.4] If platforms normalize that as ordinary self-presentation, they let one user harvest attraction under a false signal while others stake time, trust, and safety on a false test.
[P1.5] This is not a ban. The remedy is disclosure: a visible label for AI-generated images and a clear notice such as “some replies are AI-written.” Some users will welcome it; others will opt out. Informed choice belongs to both sides, not only to the person receiving the boost.
[P1.6] Platforms must set the default. Their interface effectively says “this member’s face, this member’s words”; they can label, require confirmation, limit undisclosed generative use in recommendations, and punish repeat violations. CON is left defending hidden synthetic faces and hidden synthetic conversation as ordinary profile management. That default rewards optimization over honesty, scales catfishing and romance-scam tactics, and makes ordinary users absorb the uncertainty.
[P1.7] Disclosure is a small burden to prevent a large distortion of judgment.
