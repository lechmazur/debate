[P1.1] The motion asks for a narrow, well-defined legal duty: platforms must mitigate *foreseeable* offline harm from *coordinated* campaigns. Not all speech, not all hurt feelings, not every disagreement. The distinction matters because it answers the major objections before they are raised.
[P1.2] Consider a real pattern. A journalist publishes an article. Within hours, accounts surface her home address. The next day, someone shows up at her door.
[P1.3] Local police cannot trace the network; the journalist does not even know the accounts exist; but the platform sees all of it—the amplification, the cross-account coordination, the timeline that made the offline harm foreseeable. Today, when that company is notified, its obligation is largely undefined. Under this duty, it would have a clear, actionable standard: act on what you can already see.
[P1.4] The case rests on three truths. First, *information asymmetry*. Platforms have unique visibility into coordinated abuse that victims, journalists, and even law enforcement cannot replicate. Second, *foreseeability makes the duty workable*.
[P1.5] The motion does not ask platforms to predict every consequence of every post; it asks them to act on patterns their own systems already detect in their own logs. Third, *the offline harm is the point*. A pile-on that drives a family from their home at three in the morning, a swatting call traceable to a coordinated thread, a workplace invasion enabled by a posted schedule—these are not metaphors. They are foreseeable downstream effects of identifiable online behavior.
[P1.6] Opponents will reach for First Amendment language. But the duty I defend does not regulate speech—it requires platforms to use tools they already possess against patterns their own data already identifies. We already impose analogous duties on employers, venues, and schools: when serious harm is foreseeable, action is not optional. Social media platforms should not be the last industry exempt from that basic obligation of care.

That defensive posture always resolves the same way: mass over-removal, pre-emptive suspension of accounts, and suppression of exactly the counter-speech that helps victims. When a platform faces liability for downstream violence it cannot fully predict, the rational business response is to silence anyone who *looks* like part of a pile-on—including the harassed person's own defenders, satirists, and journalists reporting on the harassment itself.
[C1.2] The victims PRO wants to protect are often the first casualties of the moderation regime PRO wants to create.
[C1.3] The employer and venue analogies fail. A workplace or stadium has a bounded population, physical control over entry, and clear custodial responsibility. A platform hosts billions of unrelated speech acts by strangers with no relationship to each other; "coordination" among them is inferred, not observed, and inference is exactly where legal duties become unworkable—either toothless because courts can't establish foreseeability after the fact, or ruinous because platforms preempt any risk by over-blocking.
[C1.4] There is already a body of law suited to this harm: criminal statutes on doxxing, stalking, threats, and swatting; civil tort remedies against identifiable harassers; and law enforcement power to compel platform cooperation through subpoena. The real gap PRO identifies isn't a missing legal duty on platforms—it's under-resourced policing and under-enforced existing law. Layering a new vague duty onto platforms doesn't fix that; it just shifts blame from the actual wrongdoers onto the intermediary easiest to sue, while doing nothing to stop the next coordinated campaign from simply moving to encrypted channels beyond any platform's visibility at all.
[C1.5] This duty will punish speech platforms can see and miss harm they can't, while entrenching whichever giant company can afford the compliance apparatus—crushing smaller, more speech-friendly competitors. That is not protection. That is capture dressed up as care.
