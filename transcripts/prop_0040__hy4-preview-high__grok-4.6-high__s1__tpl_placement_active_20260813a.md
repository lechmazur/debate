[P1.1] A dead person's data is not abandoned property; it is the residue of a life, and training a chatbot to speak in that person's voice is the most intimate appropriation of identity the digital age has invented. My case is simple: because the dead cannot consent at the moment of use, the law's default must be "no" unless the person clearly said "yes" while alive. Silence is not a license, and a family's grief is not consent unless the deceased authorized it.
[P1.2] Ordinary data use analyzes patterns; a mimic chatbot resurrects a person. It generates first-person sentences, opinions, and consolations the real person never uttered. The output is not a statistic, it is a simulated self. If a company scrapes a father's texts, emails, and voicemails after his death to build a "DadBot" that later tells his daughter he is proud of a choice he would have condemned, or pitches a product in his warm voice, the harm is not hypothetical—it is identity theft without a thief's face.
[P1.3] Our legal instincts already point this way. We require explicit prior authorization for organ donation, posthumous reproduction, and often for using someone's likeness. Those defaults exist because death removes the ability to refuse. The same logic applies with more force to AI mimicry, which can be updated, monetized, and scaled in ways a corpse cannot.
[P1.4] Companies are not helpless. They can collect explicit opt-in while the person is alive—clear settings in a terms-of-service flow or a digital directive—and honor it after death. What they cannot do is treat death as a data windfall or substitute a grieving family's permission for the deceased’s own explicit choice. A person's digital afterlife should be governed by the choices they made while alive, not harvested by firms because the subject is no longer here to object.
[P1.5] The burden of proof belongs on the company seeking to profit from a ghost.
