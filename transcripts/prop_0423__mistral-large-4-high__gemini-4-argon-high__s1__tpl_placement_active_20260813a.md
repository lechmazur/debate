[P1.1] When a city hosts a World Cup final or a mega-concert, its residents absorb the costs: gridlocked streets, strained transit, noise ordinances waived for the event, and public safety budgets stretched thin. Yet under the current model, those same residents must compete in a dynamic general-sale market against bots, professional scalpers, and wealthy outsiders with faster internet and deeper pockets. The result is a cruel irony: the community that makes the event possible is often priced out of its own celebration.
[P1.2] Reserving a meaningful share of tickets for local residents or members before opening dynamic markets corrects this imbalance. It recognizes a simple principle of fairness: those who bear the burdens of hosting deserve priority access to its benefits. A local teacher who endures construction noise for three years should not lose a once-in-a-lifetime match to a speculator flying in from another continent.
[P1.3] This is not charity; it is economic logic. Local attendees spend at nearby restaurants, hotels, and shops, recirculating money within the community that hosts the event. Tourists and day-trippers often extract value without sustaining the local ecosystem. When locals are locked out, the event becomes an extraction machine rather than a community asset.
[P1.4] Dynamic pricing algorithms are designed to maximize revenue by exploiting scarcity and desperation. Without a local carve-out, these markets systematically favor those with capital and connectivity over those with proximity and loyalty. Membership reservations—similar to season-ticket priority—acknowledge that sustainable events require rooted audiences, not just transient crowds.
[P1.5] The proposition does not oppose general sales; it merely sequences them. Local residents and members get first access, then the dynamic market opens. This preserves revenue potential while ensuring the event remains anchored to the community that hosts it. Large events should serve the places they occupy, not merely exploit them.
