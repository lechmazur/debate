[P1.1] When a paycheck is tied to a number, the person earning it must be able to see the number. That principle is the whole proposition. Streaming platforms increasingly pay residuals, bonuses, or profit participation based on audience reach. Yet the platform is simultaneously the scorekeeper, the payer, and the only party with the underlying data.
[P1.2] No honest accounting system can work that way, and no serious market leaves it that way.
[P1.3] Standardized viewing data—aggregated plays, hours, completion, territory, and the allocation rules attached to them—would let filmmakers and performers verify what they are owed. It need not be public. It need not expose individual users. It can be confidential, audited, and privacy-protective.
[P1.4] The point is not to turn Netflix or Spotify into open books; it is to give the people whose compensation depends on the metric the same ability to check the metric that any supplier has when a buyer controls the ledger.
[P1.5] Without that ability, a contractual right becomes a discretionary gift. A performer with a residual triggered by a streaming threshold cannot know whether the threshold was crossed. A filmmaker with backend points cannot test whether the platform's internal accounting shifted revenue, bundled content, or defined a "view" to minimize payouts. The old media economy was far from perfect, but residuals and royalties were at least subject to statements, audits, and shared industry definitions.
[P1.6] Streaming replaced that with a black box.
[P1.7] Opponents will call this burdensome or dangerous. But standardized data sharing is a narrow condition: if you demand the right to pay by reach, you must let the other side see reach. That reduces disputes, exposes neither passwords nor trade secrets, and makes informed negotiation possible. A compensation system without verifiable reach is not performance-based pay.
[P1.8] It is a promise the payer alone gets to interpret.

[C1.1] The proposition says that when pay is tied to reach, creators must see the number. But “reach” is not a raw fact like box-office receipts; it is a constructed metric. Streaming measurement is a bundle of judgment calls: what counts as a view, how many seconds matter, whether restarted episodes count, how profiles, downloads, free trials, ad-supported streams, and bundled territories are allocated. A mandated standardized dataset freezes those choices into one official number and attaches paychecks to it.
[C1.2] That is not transparency; it is false precision.
[C1.3] The better rule is contract, not compulsion. If compensation turns on reach, the parties can and should negotiate the metric, reporting frequency, audit rights, and an independent verifier. A performer’s union or a filmmaker’s counsel can demand summarized statements and a third-party audit, just as royalty accounting has long allowed. That solves the real problem—unverifiable payouts—without forcing every service, from a global platform to a niche documentary app, into the same reporting architecture.
[C1.4] A mandate also hurts the people it promises to help. Compliance costs fall hardest on smaller streamers and experimental distribution models. If standardized disclosures become legally risky, platforms will respond by buying out rights outright, lowering participation offers, or moving more deals into flat fees. Creators lose upside.
[C1.5] Worse, standardized metrics can be gamed: once a “qualified view” is defined, editing, packaging, and release strategies can be tuned to hit the metric rather than serve audiences.
[C1.6] Finally, “confidential” does not mean harmless. Detailed completion rates, territory-level engagement, and drop-off points are competitive intelligence. Requiring them to circulate among producers, performers, agents, lawyers, and auditors expands the risk of leaks and strategic misuse.
[C1.7] The right answer is enforceable audit rights, clear contractual definitions, and independent verification where compensation depends on reach. The proposition replaces that flexible toolkit with rigid standardization, more litigation, and weaker bargaining positions for creators.
