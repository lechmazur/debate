[P1.1] For most patients, the decisive fact about organ donation is not that people are selfish; it is that dying is chaotic. Opt-in asks a grieving family to recall a decision most healthy people never made, then treats silence as refusal. Presumed consent changes that default: adults are donors unless they record an objection. That switch increases transplants because it captures the many people who support donation but never register—an intention-action gap defaults reliably shrink.
[P1.2] This is not expropriation. A legitimate presumed-consent system has safeguards: robust public information, a simple opt-out, and consultation with the family. If the family says no, donation stops. That destroys the family-distress objection: bereaved relatives who might have been asked “did your loved one want to donate?” are instead asked “do you object?” No family is forced to endure retrieval against its stated wishes.
[P1.3] Distress can fall, because families are not left guessing about an unregistered wish.
[P1.4] Ethically, the policy respects autonomy because objection is easy, informed, and revocable. It does not claim to read minds; it sets a reversible default that saves lives. Compare the two errors. Under opt-in, a willing donor’s organs are buried because they never completed a form.
[P1.5] Under presumed consent, a refuser must take one simple step. The first error is irreversible death; the second is paperwork.
[P1.6] Opponents will point to hard cases: mistrust, minorities, the disabled. Those are implementation challenges, not reasons to preserve a default that suppresses donation. A system can have opt-outs, family vetoes, and equity audits. What it cannot do is pretend silence means refusal when silence often means inertia.
[P1.7] If even a fraction of unregistered supporters become donors, transplant rates rise measurably. And because refusal remains available, family veto remains respected, and the default is transparent, that rise comes without adding ethical violations or distress. The motion is not radical; it corrects a policy that turns inertia into lost lives.
