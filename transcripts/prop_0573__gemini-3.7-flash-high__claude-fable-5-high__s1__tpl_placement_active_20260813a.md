[P1.1] In any functioning market, trade requires both parties to understand the value of what is being bought and sold. Yet today’s streaming economy operates behind a proprietary black box. Platforms act simultaneously as the distributor, the buyer, and the sole, unverified accountant of performance. Mandating standardized viewing and payout data for creators, artists, and rights holders is essential to restore fair bargaining, ensure contract enforcement, and protect cultural production.
[P1.2] First, opaque data makes meaningful contract enforcement impossible. Rights holders and talent sign revenue-sharing, royalty, or backend agreements that depend entirely on audience metrics. When platforms conceal raw numbers or arbitrarily define what constitutes a "stream"—shifting between two seconds, thirty seconds, or full completion—creators cannot verify whether they are being paid accurately. Requiring standardized, auditable metrics ensures that contracts reflect economic reality rather than a platform’s self-serving accounting.
[P1.3] Second, information asymmetry suppresses fair compensation. In traditional entertainment, independent metrics like box office receipts, verified ratings, and record sales gave creators leverage to negotiate their true market value. Today, streaming platforms exploit secrecy to underpay talent. A show or album can become a global hit, driving massive subscriber acquisition and retention, while the artists receive flat fees or fractions of a cent because the platform alone controls the data.
[P1.4] Standardized transparency gives labor and independent producers the information needed to negotiate fair residuals, advances, and licensing terms.
[P1.5] Third, standardized transparency fosters a healthier creative ecosystem. Independent studios, labels, and individual creators make significant financial and career investments based on audience demand. When viewing habits and revenue generation are locked inside algorithmic walled gardens, only tech conglomerates can optimize their investments, crowding out independent talent.
[P1.6] Forcing platforms to open their books does not threaten legitimate innovation; it simply ends the unilateral exploitation of creative labor. Standardized transparency must be required.
