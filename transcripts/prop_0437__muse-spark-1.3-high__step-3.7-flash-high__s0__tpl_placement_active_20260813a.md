[P1.1] Pre-pandemic sidewalk rules treated public space as something to keep empty. A narrow walking lane, a curb full of parked cars, and storefronts forbidden from spilling life outward. Neighborhoods that felt dead came alive.
[P1.2] Flexible outdoor dining should stay because it puts scarce street space to its highest public use.
[P1.3] First, it keeps local restaurants and jobs alive. Restaurants run on razor-thin margins and small footprints. A dozen outdoor tables can be the difference between closing and hiring another cook or server. Those businesses then pay more sales tax, fill vacant storefronts, and draw foot traffic that helps the bookstore and bakery next door.
[P1.4] Protecting a parking space or an extra few feet of bare concrete instead is a terrible trade.
[P1.5] Second, well-used sidewalks are better sidewalks. A lively block with diners, lights, and staff present feels safer and more welcoming than a dark, windswept corridor, especially in the evening. Flexible rules let cities reclaim asphalt from car storage and give it back to people — a more sociable, walkable, human-scale street that most residents actually prefer to navigate.
[P1.6] Flexibility does not mean anarchy. Cities can require a clear accessible path, set hours and noise limits, charge fair permit fees, and remove bad actors. Complaints about change deserve a hearing and clear standards, not a rollback that kills what plainly works. What cities should not do is hand a veto to a few voices nostalgic for empty pavement.
[P1.7] Public space belongs to the whole public, including diners, workers, and small owners, not just those who want everything exactly as it was in 2019. Streets are for living, not just passing through.
