[P1.1] The modern sports fan is trapped in a museum of channels they never asked to visit. If your loyalty begins and ends with one club, one league, or a single summer tournament, the industry still expects you to purchase the entire sports buffet—hundreds of channels, sports you will never watch, and a monthly bill that rivals a car payment. That model is not merely inconvenient; it is economically backwards.
[P1.2] Consider the tennis viewer who waits all year for Wimbledon, or the basketball fan whose devotion belongs to a single NBA franchise. For them, the broad subscription is not a product—it is a tax. Many simply refuse to pay. Others turn to unreliable streams or illegal feeds.
[P1.3] The result is lost revenue for broadcasters, a fractured fan experience, and a growing wall between leagues and the next generation of viewers.
[P1.4] Cheaper team-specific or competition-specific passes fix this by aligning price with actual demand. They lower the barrier to entry so that casual and younger fans can legally follow the sport they love without committing to hundreds of dollars a year. The supporter who pays a modest monthly fee to watch their club is a revenue stream the bundle never captured. The viewer who buys a one-weekend pass for a single cup final is a customer the all-or-nothing model excludes entirely.
[P1.5] The claim that bundling protects broadcaster revenue confuses sticker price with profit. When most fans decline to buy because the cost is detached from their interest, the bundle shrinks the market. Unbundling grows it: it converts fence-sitters into subscribers, reduces churn, and undercuts piracy by offering a legal, affordable alternative.
[P1.6] Fans should not have to buy a supermarket to get a meal. Sports broadcasters should sell the dish the fans actually want.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes a risk-sharing system for a preference. A broadcaster paying for league rights, cameras, streaming capacity, and studio production must recover that fixed cost from some subscriber base. Bundles are what make that possible. Spread those costs only across fans who self-select into one club or one tournament, and each must pay far more—or the product must cut cameras, commentary, angles, and reliability.
[C1.2] The “cheap pass” is usually a worse product or a hidden premium.
[C1.3] Take the Wimbledon-only fan. A two-week tournament still requires full tournament rights, courtside crews, streaming infrastructure, and studio coverage concentrated into days. Those costs do not shrink because that viewer skips football in the autumn. A one-tournament pass must either carry a heavy price or become a thin production.
[C1.4] The same arithmetic applies to a one-club NBA pass: the league’s national rights and production apparatus remain, and the broadcaster cannot simply hand over the local fan’s share.
[C1.5] The claim that bundling shrinks the market [P1.5] is backwards. Broad bundles pool high-demand and low-demand programming. That pool subsidizes women’s leagues, lower-division games, shoulder programming, and smaller competitions. Unbundle, and those offerings become separate fees or disappear.
[C1.6] The fan who says they only want their team still benefits from that ecosystem. The result is not a fair à-la-carte menu; it is a few expensive winners and many unsupported losers.
[C1.7] Most importantly, narrow passes do not solve the broadcaster’s revenue problem. The likeliest buyer is already a bundle subscriber who downgrades, cannibalizing the payments that support other sports. Marginal new fans rarely replace that loss. So the rational business response is restrictions, blackouts, delayed matches, or prices high enough to push consumers back to the bundle—which is exactly why truly cheaper standalone passes are far less likely than the proposal assumes.
