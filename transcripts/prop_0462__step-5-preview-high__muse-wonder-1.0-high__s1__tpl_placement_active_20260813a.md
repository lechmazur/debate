[P1.1] Tracking in math does not merely reflect differences between students; it manufactures and hardens them. When a school sorts children into advanced and remedial tracks, it assigns different curricula, different expectations, and often different teachers. The advanced track gets open-ended problems and algebraic reasoning; the lower track gets repetition and narrowed pathways. That is not a neutral response to prior achievement.
[P1.2] It is a self-fulfilling prophecy.
[P1.3] Consider a ninth grader who arrives underprepared because her middle school offered a thin, tracked curriculum. Placed in a low track, she receives more of the same thin exposure and is barred from the algebra sequence that opens advanced study later. Her twin—equally capable but slightly better drilled—sails into honors. The school has not measured potential; it has laundered prior advantage into future opportunity.
[P1.4] Replacing most advanced-tracking with heterogeneous classes plus intensive tutoring preserves access to rigorous content while addressing individual needs more honestly. In a tracked system, the “honors” label delivers a more demanding course, but comparable students one level down are denied the same challenge for reasons that often reflect zip code, prior preparation, or a single test. Heterogeneous classrooms with strong tutoring supports—small-group intervention, differentiated practice, and targeted remediation—keep the bar high for everyone while providing the specific help that struggling learners actually need.
[P1.5] The opposition will claim that high-achieving students will be bored or held back. But advanced students benefit from heterogeneous settings that preserve peer diversity and reduce the fragile, high-pressure sorting that makes early labels feel like verdicts. Meanwhile, the largest costs of tracking fall on students of color, English learners, and late bloomers who are disproportionately placed in lower tracks and then taught less. If our goal is excellence, we should stop confusing early sorting with rigor.
[P1.6] True rigor means giving every student access to challenging mathematics and the intensive support to master it.

[C1.1] Math is brutally cumulative, and that fact defeats this proposal. A tenth-grader ready to write geometric proofs and manipulate functions needs a full period of new, demanding material. A classmate still uncertain about fractions, negative numbers, and solving a one-step equation needs a full period rebuilding those foundations. Putting them in the same room does not create rigor for everyone; it forces one teacher to choose a pace that is wrong for both.
[C1.2] The advanced student rehearses what she already knows, the struggling student watches concepts fly past built on prerequisites she does not have, and only the middle gets a lesson pitched to them.
[C1.3] Tutoring cannot repair a mismatched core lesson. It reinforces instruction at a student's level; it cannot, a few sessions a week, teach a second curriculum, fill years of gaps, and extend advanced learners beyond the class ceiling. If tutoring must do all that, the class is merely holding students together while real instruction happens elsewhere.
[C1.4] Replacing most advanced tracking also breaks acceleration where timing matters. A student prepared for algebra in eighth grade, geometry in ninth, and calculus before graduation loses that runway when locked to a common pace. Delay is not neutral: it forecloses advanced science and college STEM pathways that assume that sequence. Families who can pay will buy acceleration outside school — private courses and summer programs — while students who depend on public school for advancement lose their one free route upward.
[C1.5] Tracking needs better placement, frequent re-evaluation, and open on-ramps so late bloomers are not trapped. But abolishing most advanced tracks does not abolish differences in preparation; it hides them in one classroom and privatizes the response. Keep distinct levels where gaps are wide, add tutoring within those levels, and let students move up as they master material. That targets help without sacrificing pace or depth.

[C2.1] [P2.1] is right that no track is uniform, but that proves too little. A geometry class where some students are quicker at proofs than others has a manageable spread. A heterogeneous class where one student cannot add fractions and another is ready for trigonometric identities has a chasm. Teachers can differentiate at the margins; they cannot teach two prerequisite structures at once without splitting time, attention, and feedback.
[C2.2] That is why [P2.3]'s differentiation story quietly becomes tracking under another name. Small groups sorted by readiness, targeted remediation for some, targeted extensions for others, and 'compressed pathways' for acceleration are separate paces and separate material. If those groups are real, advanced tracking has not been replaced; it has been squeezed inside one room with one teacher doing three jobs. If they are not real, advanced students sit through the common lesson and struggling students get fragments of prerequisites between new concepts they are not ready to use.
[C2.3] Tutoring has the same limit. Intensive help works best when it reinforces a lesson already taught at the right level. Asked to pre-teach missing foundations, re-teach the day's lesson, and extend top students beyond it, tutoring becomes the differentiated system — understaffed, and least available to students who can least stay late. In-level tutoring inside a well-placed class needs none of that substitution.
[C2.4] Nor does rigidity rescue the motion. Placement can be reviewed each term, based on multiple measures, with bridge units that let a student prove readiness and move. That is a fixable design flaw. Heterogeneous placement has the opposite flaw built in every day: the student who masters material early still waits, and the student who misses a foundation still advances with the calendar.
[C2.5] [P2.4] calls on-ramps paper promises, but compressed pathways without a separate class are the same promise with even less scheduling, staffing, or curriculum behind it.
[C2.6] Keep levels narrow, move students often, tutor within them.
