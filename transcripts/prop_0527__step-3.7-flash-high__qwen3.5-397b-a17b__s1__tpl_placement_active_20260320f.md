[P1.1] The global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) demands urgent, market-savvy policy: without intervention, resistant infections are projected to kill 10 million people annually by 2050, outpacing cancer deaths today. The core barrier to new antibiotics is a broken market: these drugs are curative, prescribed in short courses, and restricted via stewardship to delay resistance, leaving pharmaceutical companies unable to recoup the $1–2 billion average cost of bringing a new drug to market. Advance market commitments (AMCs) fix this failure directly, and the risk of higher unit prices is far outweighed by the lives and costs saved.
[P1.2] AMCs are legally binding guarantees that funders will purchase a set volume of a new antibiotic meeting predefined efficacy and safety standards at a predetermined price, de-risking R&D investment for developers. Even if unit prices remain higher than legacy generic antibiotics, this cost is trivial compared to the alternative: treating a single drug-resistant infection in an intensive care unit costs tens of thousands of dollars, plus incalculable losses from premature death and long-term disability. A $1,000 course of a new last-resort antibiotic pays for itself the first time it replaces a failed treatment for a resistant infection that would otherwise cost $50,000 to manage.
[P1.3] Critics claim high prices limit access, but AMCs can be structured to lock in equitable allocation: high-income countries pay the agreed price to cover R&D costs, while the commitment mandates low-cost or free access for low- and middle-income countries, a benefit absent in the current market where no new antibiotics for WHO-listed priority pathogens are in late-stage development at all. Unlike untargeted research grants that waste public funds on failed pipelines, AMCs only pay out for proven, approved products, making them the most efficient use of public health dollars to slow AMR.
