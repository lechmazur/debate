[P1.1] A hungry child cannot learn, and a means test is a bad way to find hungry children. It asks the families least able to navigate paperwork to prove poverty before their child can eat, then brands the child who succeeds. Universal free meals solve the problem schools actually have: feed every student, during the school day, as part of education itself.
[P1.2] First, targeting misses the need it claims to prioritize. Eligibility lines are blunt: a parent earns slightly too much on paper, works irregular hours, gains overtime one month and loses it the next, or misses a form, deadline, or proof of address. Some families avoid applying from fear, confusion, or shame. The result is not precision.
[P1.3] It is eligible children going unfed, food-insecure children just above the line excluded, and schools policing applications instead of teaching. If the goal is that no poor student goes hungry, universal provision is the more reliable mechanism because it has no gate to fail at.
[P1.4] Second, means testing divides the cafeteria. When some pay, some receive an identified free meal, and some accrue lunch debt, children read the hierarchy instantly. Students skip lunch rather than be marked as poor, trade dignity for calories, or receive a substitute meal for unpaid balances. Universal meals remove that signal.
[P1.5] Like textbooks, libraries, and the school bus, lunch becomes a shared part of the school day, not a welfare transaction performed in front of peers.
[P1.6] Third, universality is simpler and pedagogically stronger. Schools no longer verify incomes, maintain separate accounts, chase debts, or enforce meal codes. Provision speeds lunch lines and raises participation, including among poor students who previously opted out. Better-fed students are more likely to attend, concentrate, and behave — benefits that do not stop neatly at an income cutoff.
[P1.7] An affluent family receiving a free meal is not failure; it is the modest price of never asking a hungry seven-year-old to prove she belongs in the line.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to treat school lunch the way we treat the school bus. That is the wrong analogy. The school bus moves a child to school. A universal lunch program delivers a daily public subsidy to every household in the district - including those that pay for private tutors, family vacations, and never glance at grocery prices.
[C1.2] Public schools do not have an unlimited mandate; they have a finite budget and a defined mission.
[C1.3] That is the heart of this debate. Education dollars are not infinite, and every dollar spent feeding an affluent child is a dollar not spent on the reading specialist, the smaller algebra class, or the after-school program in the neighborhood that actually needs one. PRO concedes the point in plain language when it admits "an affluent family receiving a free meal is not failure." On the contrary, it is the daily price of the motion - paid by the children the system was built to serve.
[C1.4] PRO also paints targeting as a clumsy ritual of paperwork and shame. That is not the only version that exists. Community eligibility, already used in many U.S. districts, extends free meals to every student in a school where a threshold share of families qualify through existing programs like SNAP. No family application, no income form, no cafeteria line that reads as a welfare transaction, and full universal participation within the schools that need it.
[C1.5] Smart targeting can deliver most of the participation gains PRO celebrates while preserving public money for the rest of the school day.
[C1.6] PRO also treats means testing as the only alternative to universality. It is not. The honest comparison is universal free meals versus the best version of targeted support - high take-up, low friction, no public stigma, and the school budget still able to fund everything else a child needs. On that comparison, the motion loses.
[C1.7] Feeding every child, wealthy or not, with the same public check is generosity to the wrong people at the wrong scale.

[P2.5] And pedagogically the comparison reverses: a reading specialist cannot teach a hungry child who is absent or distracted. Feeding does not compete with learning; it makes the rest of the budget work.

[P5.1] The choice is not between feeding poor children and feeding rich children. It is between a system that sometimes feeds a child who could have paid, and a system that sometimes fails to feed the child it was built to help.
[P5.2] That failure is not incidental to targeting; it follows from its design. Every version must draw a line — an income cutoff, a school threshold, enrollment in another program — and identify the child on the right side of it. Income changes, applications fail, poverty does not cluster neatly, and the child outside the line stays hungry. Expanding the threshold moves the line; it does not remove it.
[P5.3] Universal meals accept the opposite error, and it is the safer one. The extra tray for an affluent classmate is visible, predictable, and shared by all. The missed meal is invisible, concentrated on the poorest, and paid for in absence, shame, and lost concentration — precisely the harms school exists to prevent.
[P5.4] If public schools must err, they should err toward feeding the child in front of them. That reliability is why lunch belongs with textbooks and buses: not as a reward for proving need, but as a condition for learning.
