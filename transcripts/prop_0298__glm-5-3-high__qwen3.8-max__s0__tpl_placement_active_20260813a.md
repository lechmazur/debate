[C1.1] The strongest case against this motion is not that recovery should be left to unchecked markets, but that making governments the primary funder and gatekeeper of deeply personal family records risks repeating the harms under a kinder name. Slavery, adoption, dispossession, and displacement records are not merely missing; many were sealed, altered, or restricted to protect people from the state, hostile relatives, or later persecution. The proposition glides past the hardest question: who has the right to see a name, and when?
[C1.2] When the state caused the paperwork, it does not automatically gain legitimacy to publish it.
[C1.3] Private recovery is imperfect, but it is plural. Competing archives, nonprofits, churches, universities, and community projects can offer tiered access, consent-based disclosure, and culturally specific controls. An adoptee may trace a parent without broadcasting a birth parent’s identity; an Indigenous community may keep sacred materials restricted; a displaced family may search without creating a centralized list of vulnerable people. Markets can be disciplined by law: mandatory access duties, open metadata, descendant subsidies, and penalties for exploitative paywalls.
[C1.4] Government custody looks safer until states behave like states. Archives are underfunded, politicized, and slow. A centralized database is a tempting target for breach, surveillance, or nationalist myth-making. Records once released cannot be un-released.
[C1.5] The same bureaucracy promising dignity can classify, redact, or delay files that embarrass officials. For communities already harmed by state recordkeeping, trust is easier to place in distributed, community-controlled recovery than in another national database.
[C1.6] Most importantly, the motion flattens reality. Many records are already available free through libraries, nonprofits, and local archives; private digitization has made obscure collections searchable across borders. The better standard is targeted public support: fund preservation, require open access where rights are clear, protect privacy where they are not. That delivers recovery without handing the state a monopoly over memory.
