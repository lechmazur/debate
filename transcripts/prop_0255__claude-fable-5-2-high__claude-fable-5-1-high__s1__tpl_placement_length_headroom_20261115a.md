[P1.1] When you delete something, you are giving the clearest instruction a person can give online: this is no longer yours to keep. When something was never public — your location history, your private messages, your browsing — you never offered it at all. The motion targets one business practice: third parties who warehouse that material anyway and sell it to whoever pays. That practice should be illegal.
[P1.2] Three reasons.
[P1.3] First, consent. Every legitimate market rests on someone having the right to sell. Brokers have no relationship with the people they catalogue, no agreement, no accountability. Selling a deleted post or a harvested location trail is commerce in something the subject withdrew or never released.
[P1.4] A market built on overriding refusal is not a market worth protecting.
[P1.5] Second, harm that cannot be undone. Picture a woman who flees an abusive partner, deletes her accounts, changes her number. A broker's archive makes her deletion meaningless; a few dollars buys her old addresses, her gym, her routine. The same archives let buyers infer clinic visits, sexuality, debts, or religious attendance, and every archive is a breach waiting to happen.
[P1.6] Deletion that doesn't delete is a lie we tell users.
[P1.7] Third, narrowness. This prohibition leaves public records, journalism, court-ordered access, and companies' own first-party records untouched. Fraud prevention and background checks run on public filings and data people knowingly provide. Police who need more can get a warrant — which is precisely the safeguard broker sales let them skip.
[P1.8] So the question for the other side is simple: what legitimate purpose requires a stranger to stockpile and sell what a private citizen tried to erase or never shared? If the purpose is legitimate, consent or a court order can supply the data. If it can't survive asking permission, that tells you what it is.

[C1.1] Prohibition is the bluntest tool in the regulatory drawer, and this motion swings it at a category so wide it hits almost everything. "Non-public digital traces" is not a narrow carve-out. It is your email address, your phone number, the device ID your weather app shares under terms you accepted, the address history a lender checks before approving your mortgage. The motion does not say "regulate," "require consent," or "limit purposes."
[C1.2] It says prohibit, archive and sale alike. Vote for that and you don't end a business practice; you abolish an infrastructure that private citizens quietly depend on.
[C1.3] Start with fraud. Identity verification works precisely because someone holds a non-public record of where you have lived and which numbers you have used. When a stranger applies for credit in your name, the mismatch between their story and your trace history is what flags it. Synthetic-identity rings exploit exactly the gaps this motion would create.
[C1.4] The scammer who deletes his profile and reopens a fresh one is the person who benefits most from mandatory forgetting.
[C1.5] Next, deletion is not a magic word. The harasser who sends threats and then deletes them, the landlord who scrubs a discriminatory listing, the executive who removes a damning post before a lawsuit: the motion protects every one of them, because retained deleted traces are evidence, and evidence must be held by someone outside the deleter's control.
[C1.6] Then the asymmetry. Platforms keep their first-party records forever under this motion. Prohibiting independent brokers hands Google and Meta a monopoly on memory, with no competitive check and no outside audit trail.
[C1.7] The abuse victim deserves protection. She gets it from access controls, purpose limits, and audit duties, which existing privacy regimes already impose on brokers. What she does not get from a ban is her identity back when a thief, now untraceable, empties her account.
