[P1.1] Every day, roughly ten trillion dollars in financial transactions moves through a few hundred fiber-optic cables lying unarmored on the ocean floor. Those cables carry about 97 percent of intercontinental data: banking, logistics, government communications, and the intelligence and targeting feeds modern militaries run on. They are the single most load-bearing infrastructure on earth—and the least defended.
[P1.2] The proposition is simple: harden and duplicate that backbone, even at the price of more photogenic weapons. Three reasons.
[P1.3] First, dependence. A squadron of fighters that cannot receive satellite tasking, drone feeds, or secure logistics data because its country's cables were cut is an expensive sculpture. Modern defense runs through cables; protecting cables *is* defense procurement, just without a ribbon-cutting.
[P1.4] Second, vulnerability is not hypothetical—it is cheap, deniable, and already exploited. Cables are severed routinely by accident: dragging anchors and fishing trawls cause well over a hundred faults a year, and repairs take weeks because the global repair-ship fleet is small and aging. Hostile actors have noticed. Russian survey ships loiter over NATO cable routes; in the Baltic and around Taiwan, vessels have dragged anchors across cables in incidents governments treat as deliberate hybrid warfare.
[P1.5] An adversary can impose weeks of economic and military disruption for the cost of a fishing boat. That asymmetry is an engraved invitation.
[P1.6] Third, the economics crush the trade-off objection. A resilient national cable network—diverse landings, spare capacity, monitoring, faster repair—costs a small fraction of one stealth bomber program or a single aircraft carrier. We are not choosing between security and cables; we are choosing between the marginal weapon in an already large arsenal and closing a catastrophic single point of failure. Redundancy also deters by denial: an attacker who cannot count on a knockout blow rarely attempts one.
[P1.7] You cannot sanction a severed cable back to life.
[P1.8] The expected objection is that cutting visible procurements weakens deterrence. But deterrence rests on capability that survives contact with the enemy. A military whose nervous system an adversary can snip in an afternoon projects weakness, however impressive its parade. [clipped]

[C1.1] The proposition sounds prudent until you ask what disappears to pay for it. Shifting "much more" funding from visible defense to submarine fiber means fewer fighter squadrons, thinner carrier groups, and hollowed-out expeditionary forces. That is not a budget tweak; it is a green light for exactly the kind of regional aggression that makes cable-cutting a live question in the first place. Deterrence is not a line item hidden in a ledger.
[C1.2] It is a story told by deployable, observable capability. When an adversary sees a navy that cannot sustain presence or an air force missing wings, the calculation of risk changes—and the threshold for testing borders drops—long before anyone touches a seabed cable.
[C1.3] The private sector already builds redundancy into global networks because outages cost them billions. Internet traffic reroutes automatically; that was the original design logic of packet switching. While a single cut inconveniences, the traffic does not simply vanish. Low-earth-orbit satellite constellations are expanding capacity that can shoulder critical government and financial traffic during cable outages.
[C1.4] Treating undersea fiber as a catastrophic single point of failure ignores both the resilience of existing networks and the speed at which orbital alternatives are maturing, while asking taxpayers to subsidize infrastructure that commercial operators should finance themselves.
[C1.5] Most importantly, the proposition inverts the hierarchy of warfare. You cannot patrol cable corridors in contested waters without surface ships, attack submarines, and maritime patrol aircraft—the very visible systems the motion would starve. A military that lacks the hulls and wings to deny enemy action at sea will not keep its data safe by laying backup fiber. Secure communications matter, but they matter because they serve armed forces that can actually fight and impose costs.
[C1.6] Strip those forces to armor the ocean floor, and you protect the wires while losing the nation they were meant to serve.
