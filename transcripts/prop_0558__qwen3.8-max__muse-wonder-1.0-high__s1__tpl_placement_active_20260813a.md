[P1.1] Indefinite immigration detention turns administrative processing into open-ended imprisonment. A person arriving without status may have violated a civil rule, yet can be locked up for months or years while paperwork, identity checks, or removal negotiations stall—often through no fault of their own. That is the core wrong: detention becomes the default, with no meaningful limit, no individualized necessity finding, and no realistic horizon.
[P1.2] The proposition replaces that with presumptive release plus case management. Release is the starting point; detention remains possible only when the state shows a specific, reviewable risk. Case-management programs assign officers to help people understand hearings, update addresses, access legal advice, and meet reporting conditions. Where needed, supervision can include phone check-ins, community sponsors, or electronic monitoring.
[P1.3] This is not open borders; it is a system that keeps people in the process without making them disappear into prisons.
[P1.4] The case rests on three grounds. First, liberty. Democratic states should not imprison people indefinitely for civil status disputes. If criminal detention requires proportionality and review, immigration detention cannot be exempt from time, necessity, and judicial scrutiny.
[P1.5] Second, effectiveness. The central problem in immigration systems is not that released people vanish; it is that cases drag because people lack notice, counsel, and stable contact points. Case management increases compliance by making obligations understandable and achievable. A person with a caseworker, a court date, and a realistic path to resolution is far more likely to appear than someone isolated in a detention center or driven underground by fear.
[P1.6] Third, harm reduction. Detention worsens mental health, separates families, and burdens taxpayers with the most expensive form of supervision. Community-based management preserves dignity while still allowing states to enforce decisions.
[P1.7] The decisive issue is burden: when detention is indefinite, the state avoids justifying each day of imprisonment, while detainees carry an impossible proof burden. If some migrants are dangerous, the answer is individualized, time-limited detention—not indefinite detention as a blanket tool. The state can enforce immigration law without using custody as its first and final instrument.

[C1.1] Should a person with no lawful right to remain be released automatically because removal took too long, even when he caused the delay and is likely to vanish? The proposition answers yes. That is its fatal flaw.
[C1.2] A prohibition on prolonged detention creates a playbook for defeating removal. Destroy documents, give a false name, refuse a repatriation interview, or benefit when a home country stalls on travel papers, and time becomes an ally. Once the limit passes, the state must release someone it may still not have identified, with no enforceable departure date. Non-cooperation is rewarded with the very presence the person was ordered to lose.
[C1.3] Presumptive release compounds this by reversing a burden the state often cannot meet at the start. New arrivals may have unverified identities, no fixed address, no local ties, and sometimes prior removals or serious convictions abroad that take time to check. Demanding proof of a specific risk before custody, about a person whose history is unknown, makes the safeguard illusory. A caseworker cannot manage someone never reliably known or located.
[C1.4] Case management is weakest exactly when it matters most. Check-ins, sponsors, and electronic monitoring may work for cooperative families with plausible claims. They do not compel attendance from someone who has just learned his claim failed and removal is next. Then disappearing is rational, a monitor can be cut, and an alert tells officials only that he is gone.
[C1.5] Detention is the only reliable way to ensure hearings matter and final orders can be carried out.
[C1.6] The better answer is reviewed detention: regular judicial scrutiny, humane conditions, and a duty to pursue removal diligently, with custody available while risk or obstruction persists. That preserves enforcement without treating detention lightly. The proposition instead makes custody expire by calendar, regardless of danger, flight, or sabotage — and a system whose final orders are optional will lose both control and public trust.

A deadline cannot distinguish the cooperative claimant with a pending appeal from the unidentified repeat remover who burned his passport.
