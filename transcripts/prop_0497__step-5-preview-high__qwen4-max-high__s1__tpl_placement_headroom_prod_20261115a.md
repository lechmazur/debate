[P1.1] Software is culture. A game is not merely a program that outputs images; it is a designed experience whose code, assets, interface, and runtime behavior are inseparable from meaning. Films and books have long received legal deposit because a society that values memory cannot rely on commercial survival or private collectors to decide what endures. The same logic now covers software, especially games, apps, and digital art that embody language, humor, politics, and design values of their time.
[P1.2] The threat is concrete. Works disappear when studios close, app stores delist titles, digital rights locks fail, formats become obsolete, or creators lose access to distribution accounts. A game that once ran on an operating system may be unplayable within a decade. Without deposit, future historians, educators, and artists inherit only screenshots, reviews, and marketing claims—not the work itself.
[P1.3] Even when marketing materials survive, the artwork does not.
[P1.4] Mandates solve this by shifting responsibility from fragile creators and companies to durable public institutions. National libraries already manage copies, metadata, access rules, and preservation for publications. Legal deposit can require source or runnable packages where possible, while allowing archives to preserve software in emulated environments. Sensitive or personal-data-heavy works can be handled through redaction, embargo, or access-restricted preservation.
[P1.5] Opponents will emphasize cost, security, or copyright, but these are implementation questions, not reasons to abandon preservation. Copyright already has exceptions for archiving and research. Privacy-sensitive works can remain protected while society still preserves the culture software carries. The motion does not nationalize software; it records it.
[P1.6] Books and films are preserved because they explain who we were. Software increasingly does that too. A nation that archives novels but lets its defining digital works vanish has chosen amnesia.

[C1.1] Legal deposit works for books because a book is finished. Hand a library a novel and it holds the novel. Hand it the installer for an online game and it holds a login screen pointing at a server that no longer exists. The culture my opponent describes — the designed experience, the runtime behavior — lives in matchmaking, leaderboards, licensed soundtracks, third-party middleware and other people's machines.
[C1.2] A mandate can compel the slice a developer owns. It cannot compel the slice that made the work matter.
[C1.3] Second, "culturally significant" is not a workable trigger. Deposit's genius is that it is mechanical: publication happens, a copy goes. Nobody adjudicates whether a pamphlet matters. Insert significance and you create a state board deciding, in advance, which apps deserve memory — and significance is exactly what nobody knows yet.
[C1.4] The demoscene demo, the discarded chat client, the ugly early indie game become important decades later, often because they were marginal. Either the mandate covers everything, and drowns, or it covers the canon and misses the marginal works that justify the motion.
[C1.5] Third, the burden lands backwards. A publisher mails one copy. A two-person studio cannot deposit a licensed engine, an expiring audio contract, or a build that runs without its backend. So the mandate delivers what it can actually enforce: legal exposure for the smallest creators, and confidential source code and signing keys in government custody, where embargo is a promise, not a guarantee.
[C1.6] Preservation is bought with curators, rights clearance and emulation engineers, not compulsory shipping. Funded, negotiated acquisition — working with the communities who already keep dead platforms playable — saves runnable works. The proposition must show that compulsion adds preservation rather than paperwork. A shelf of unrunnable builds is not memory; it is amnesia with an accession number.
