[P1.1] Every foundation model today is built on the same unresolved question: who pays for the books, articles, photographs and illustrations that made it possible? Right now the answer is "whoever loses in court." That is the worst possible system, and a compulsory license is the fix.
[P1.2] Consider the two alternatives CON must choose between. The first is treating training as free fair use. Then a novelist whose life's work teaches a model to write in her style receives nothing, forever, while the company sells that capability back to her readers. That is not innovation policy; it is an uncompensated transfer from millions of creators to a handful of labs.
[P1.3] The second is full exclusive rights: every rightsholder can refuse or demand any price. Training at scale requires millions of works from millions of owners, many unreachable, dead, or unknown. Individual negotiation is impossible, so only the richest firms can strike bulk deals with big publishers and platforms. Startups, universities and open-source projects are locked out, and the individual photographer still gets nothing because nobody negotiates with her.
[P1.4] A compulsory license escapes both traps. Anyone may train on lawfully accessed text and images; in exchange, they pay a statutory rate into a collective fund, set by a neutral rate tribunal and distributed to registered rightsholders. Creators get paid. Developers get certainty.
[P1.5] Competition survives because access doesn't depend on who can afford a legal department.
[P1.6] This is not an experiment. American copyright has used compulsory licenses for over a century, starting with the mechanical license that lets anyone record a cover song for a set fee. Cable retransmission and webcasting work the same way. Each time, a new technology made individual bargaining unworkable, and the law responded with "you may use it, but you must pay."
[P1.7] Music did not collapse; it scaled.
[P1.8] Meanwhile, the status quo is already producing payment, just chaotically: major lawsuits from news organizations, stock-image companies and authors, and settlements reportedly running past a billion dollars.
