[P1.1] Indefinite immigration detention is imprisonment without a sentence. A person who has committed no crime can be locked up not for months tied to a hearing date, but for years with no date at all — because removal is impossible, their country will not take them back, or bureaucracy has stalled. That inverts the basic rule of a lawful state: liberty is the default, and the government must justify taking it away, person by person, for a specific and finite reason.
[P1.2] Presumptive release with case management restores that order without abandoning immigration enforcement. Release is the starting point. A case manager ensures the person has a stable address, understands their obligations in a language they speak, gets notice of hearings, access to legal advice, and support for health and family needs. Regular check-ins create a record of compliance.
[P1.3] If the state can show a concrete flight risk or danger, it can seek time-limited detention or stricter supervision — but it must prove that case, and keep proving it.
[P1.4] That model works better than a cell. People are far more likely to attend hearings and accept a final decision when they understand the process, trust it is fair, and are not broken by it. Detention does the opposite: it traumatizes asylum seekers and children, severs them from lawyers and evidence, and makes cooperation harder. It also does not speed removal when the obstacle is a missing travel document or an unresolved claim — a locked door cannot fix paperwork.
[P1.5] It is also indefensible waste. Keeping one person in secure detention costs many times more than supervising them in the community, while producing worse mental-health outcomes, self-harm, and legal challenges. That money should fund faster, better decisions — the real source of limbo.
[P1.6] Prohibit the indefinite, presume liberty, and manage cases in the open. That is more humane, cheaper, and more effective at delivering lawful outcomes.

[C1.1] The proposition treats immigration detention as if it were criminal punishment imposed for its own sake. It is not. Where removal or danger persists, detention may lawfully persist; duration alone does not make it arbitrary. Detention is an administrative backstop to make exclusion and removal possible when a person has no lawful right to remain, cannot be safely supervised, or will not cooperate.
[C1.2] A blanket prohibition on indefinite detention, plus presumptive release, removes the state’s last tool in the hardest cases.
[C1.3] The fatal weakness in the proposed model is that case management only works with people who can be managed. It assumes known identity, stable address, credible claim, and willingness to check in. But immigration systems must deal with people who destroy documents, give false identities, have absconded before, or present security concerns that cannot be assessed quickly. If a person cannot be removed because their country refuses travel documents, release does not solve the problem; it converts an enforceable refusal into uncontrolled residence.
[C1.4] Years later, when removal becomes possible, the person may be gone.
[C1.5] The proposition also ignores the difference between ordinary applicants and those whose release would endanger others. A detainee may have committed violent offenses, be suspected of terrorism, or be part of smuggling networks. Case managers are not police, and regular check-ins do not stop someone who intends harm. Requiring the state to release first and monitor later shifts the risk onto the public.
[C1.6] The better standard is not presumed liberty, but lawful, reviewable detention. Governments can and should be required to justify continued detention periodically, use alternatives where safe, and process claims quickly. But a categorical prohibition ties the state’s hands. Without a detention backstop, even a final deportation order can become meaningless.
[C1.7] It tells people with no legal right to remain that they can outlast the system by delay, noncooperation, or risk. Immigration enforcement depends on credible consequences; the proposition replaces them with paperwork and appointments.

[P4.1] A1: No. Presumptive release does not require freeing someone on credible evidence of danger. It requires the state to act on that evidence promptly: seek time-limited detention or the strictest supervision, disclose the basis for review, and renew custody only while danger and necessity are proven. What it forbids is converting “incomplete” into “indefinite” — holding a person while officials gather, without deadline, evidence they may never find. Demonstrated danger can justify custody; unresolved suspicion cannot justify custody without end.
[P4.2] A2: No mechanism short of custody guarantees it, and that demand proves too much. Case management can require a fixed residence, frequent in-person reporting, electronic monitoring, travel restrictions, a financial surety, and immediate re-detention for breach, making disappearance harder and detection faster. Custody guarantees removability only if the state will imprison for years — perhaps for life — because a foreign government delayed papers. That is not a proportionate guarantee; it is certainty bought with another person’s liberty.
[P4.3] These answers expose the dividing line. CON ask release to guarantee compliance, then defend detention that guarantees only confinement. It does not create a passport, establish an identity, or make a dangerous person safe on the day detention finally becomes untenable — and that day comes, through court orders, ill health, cost, or impossibility. Releasing someone then, without address, support, or reporting history, is the real disappearance risk.
[P4.4] Managed release from the start preserves contact, tests compliance, and creates the record that justifies tougher measures if trust is broken.
[P4.5] For the genuinely dangerous or proven flight risk, our model is not softer where it matters: the presumption is rebutted, conditions are intensive, and breach brings custody. What changes is that the state must keep earning that custody. A system confident in its evidence does not need infinity. It needs deadlines, proof, and supervision that works in the community.
