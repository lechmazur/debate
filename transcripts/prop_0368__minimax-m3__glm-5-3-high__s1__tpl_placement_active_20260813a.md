[P1.1] The motion asks a precise question: when travel and delivery apps default to countdown timers and scarcity prompts whose main purpose is pressuring impulsive spending, should they keep doing it? [P1.2] Consider what these cues actually do. A timer reading "12 minutes left to book" or a banner shouting "only 2 rooms left at this price" targets well-documented cognitive shortcuts: loss aversion, urgency bias, and the terror of missing out. Any honest product manager will tell you these features are built because they work on impulse, not because they inform. When the design goal is conversion under time pressure, the user is no longer being served.
[P1.3] They are being exploited.
[P1.4] The defender of these defaults will reach for a softer frame: maybe the timer reflects real inventory, maybe the scarcity is genuine. That is the wrong debate. Genuine information does not need theatrical packaging. If a flight is truly about to sell out, a clear factual notice does the job.
[P1.5] The countdown flourish exists precisely because raw facts do not move people fast enough. Its purpose is to short-circuit deliberation.
[P1.6] Defaults also carry moral weight the opponent cannot wave away. Companies know defaults stick. Choosing countdown timers as the default, knowing they exploit bias, is choosing manipulation as the baseline experience for every user, including those who never asked for it and would prefer a calmer interface.
[P1.7] So the central clash is this: PRO says the default should not be a manipulation lever aimed at impulsive spending. CON has to defend that default. That is a hard position to justify to anyone who has ever abandoned a cart because a timer hit zero.
