[P1.1] A child with Leigh syndrome is born looking healthy. Within months the muscles weaken, the seizures start, the feeding tube goes in, and the family learns that every cell's power supply is failing and nothing will fix it. Public health systems already pay for all of this: the intensive care, the ventilators, the years of specialist care that end in a small funeral. The only question on the table is whether the same systems should pay a fraction of that sum to stop it happening at all.
[P1.2] Mitochondrial replacement therapy does exactly one thing: it swaps a mother's faulty mitochondria for a donor's healthy ones before the embryo develops. The child's nuclear DNA, the roughly twenty thousand genes that shape appearance, intelligence, personality, comes entirely from the parents. Mitochondrial DNA carries around three dozen genes concerned with energy production. This is not designing a baby; it is replacing a broken battery.
[P1.3] Three points carry the case.
[P1.4] First, need. For women whose eggs carry a high or uniform mutation load, embryo screening offers no healthy embryo to select. Egg donation ends the genetic link. Adoption is not a treatment.
[P1.5] For these families, MRT is the only route to a genetically related child free of the disease.
[P1.6] Second, precedent. Public systems already fund IVF, preimplantation genetic diagnosis, and prenatal screening for inherited disease. Funding MRT extends an existing principle to the hardest cases, not a new one.
[P1.7] Third, equity. The UK legalised the procedure years ago under strict licensing, and a small number of children have now been born through it. Leaving it unfunded does not stop it; it reserves it for families who can pay privately while everyone else waits for the ventilator.
[P1.8] The regulatory guardrails already exist: licensed clinics, case-by-case approval, severe disease only. What CON must defend is a system that spends generously on a child's slow death but nothing on the intervention that would have spared it.

Early research also found that small amounts of the mother's faulty mitochondria can carry over, replicate, and in some cases return to dangerously high levels in the developing child.
[C1.3] The procedure meant to prevent disease may, in a subset of cases, merely delay its onset. A public system should not fund an intervention whose failure mode is discovered one generation too late.
[C1.4] Second, scale. Severe mitochondrial disease with a uniformly high mutation load — the only population embryo screening cannot help — is vanishingly rare, a handful of births per year even in large countries. Meanwhile the same systems have unmet need everywhere: unfunded fertility treatment, new gene therapies for sickle cell disease that affect vastly more people, ordinary pediatric care with waiting lists. Rationing is not a hypothetical; it is the daily reality of public health.
[C1.5] Funding MRT means telling a larger, sicker population to keep waiting so that a very few families can have a genetically related child rather than a healthy one via donor eggs.
[C1.6] Third, precedent cuts the other way. We fund IVF and screening to help many families avoid transmitting disease. MRT is different in kind: it creates a heritable genetic change, in a child who cannot consent, using a third parent's genome. Once a public system pays for one form of germline alteration, the argument against funding the next — edits for Huntington's, then for risk genes — loses its principled footing.
