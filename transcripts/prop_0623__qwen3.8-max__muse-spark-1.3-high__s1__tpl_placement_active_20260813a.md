[P1.1] The central question is whether labor standards should be set firm-by-firm, where low-road employers undercut high-road ones, or industry-wide, where minimum rules stop pay from becoming a race to the bottom. Governments should adopt sectoral bargaining because it creates enforceable floors that protect workers, honest employers, and public budgets.
[P1.2] Workplace-only bargaining fails where power is unequal and firms are small, precarious, or fissured. A café, warehouse contractor, or care agency can gain an edge by suppressing wages and shifting costs onto workers and taxpayers. Sectoral bargaining removes that lever. By setting minimum pay and conditions across an industry, it forces competition toward quality, training, and efficiency rather than who can pay least.
[P1.3] That is good for workers and for better-run firms that already meet decent standards but are undercut by bad actors.
[P1.4] Sectoral frameworks also reduce administrative and enforcement burdens. Instead of thousands of fragmented disputes and compliance gaps, inspectors and courts can rely on a transparent industry norm. This matters most in sectors with high turnover, subcontracting, and weak union density: hospitality, retail, social care, logistics, construction. A sectoral floor makes exploitation harder to hide and easier to penalize.
[P1.5] The proposition does not require rigid uniformity. A well-designed system can set minimums, allow regional or occupational differentiation, and preserve firm-level bargaining above the floor. That is why the strongest comparison is not perfect policy versus no policy, but a system with industry floors versus a system where standards depend on the bargaining power of each isolated workplace.
[P1.6] CON will warn about flexibility. But flexibility without a floor often means flexibility to undercut. Even where transition costs exist, they are outweighed by higher earnings, fairer competition, and less pressure on public assistance. If governments want decent work and durable industry standards, the better answer is sectoral bargaining.

[C1.1] Setting wages industry by industry sounds fair until you ask which industry, which firm, which town.
[C1.2] A downtown hotel chain and a family-run motel in a rural county are not the same business. A fast-growing tech-enabled logistics startup and a thin-margin warehouse contractor are not the same employer. Sectoral bargaining pretends they are. It imposes one pay scale and one set of work rules across profoundly different productivities, costs, and customers.
[C1.3] When the floor is set to what large, high-productivity firms can afford, smaller firms do not magically become more productive. They cut hours, stop hiring teenagers and less-skilled workers, automate, close, or go informal.
[C1.4] That is why the race-to-the-bottom story misleads. Pay competition is not only exploitation; it is also how diverse firms survive and how low prices reach working-class consumers. Force every café, care home, and building subcontractor to pay the same minimum package and you do not end low-road competition — you move it. Firms outsource more, reclassify workers, use temp agencies, or exit the formal sector entirely.
[C1.5] Consumers then pay more for food, retail, and care, with the heaviest burden on the poor.
[C1.6] Representation makes it worse. In exactly the sectors invoked — hospitality, retail, care, logistics — union membership and employer-organization coverage are thin. Sectoral bargaining would let unrepresentative insiders write binding law for thousands of workplaces that never sat at the table. That protects incumbents and squeezes entrants who compete precisely by organizing work differently.
[C1.7] We already have a better floor: a general statutory minimum wage plus health, safety, and anti-discrimination law, leaving firms and workers to bargain upward where conditions allow. That protects a baseline without outlawing diversity.
[C1.8] On balance, uniform industry standards trade jobs, entry, and lower prices for the illusion of order. Governments should not adopt them.

[P2.4] They warn that low-road competition will move into outsourcing, temp agencies, or informality [C1.4]. That is an argument for defining the sector by the work performed and extending the floor across contractors, not for leaving standards firm-by-firm. Workplace bargaining is easier to evade: a franchisor can shift risk to franchisees, a lead firm can subcontract to the weakest bidder, and isolated workers cannot inspect the books. An industry norm gives regulators a clearer benchmark and makes evasion easier to spot.
[P2.5] The claim about unrepresentative insiders [C1.6] is weakest. In hospitality, care, and logistics, workplace-level representation is often nonexistent. Sectoral systems can include broad associations, public-interest members, and formal consultation. The status quo already lets powerful employers write the rules by default.
[P2.6] Low prices matter, but not when they depend on poverty wages and turnover. If consumers pay somewhat more, that is more transparent than paying twice: once at the register and again through public assistance. Sectoral bargaining forces competition toward productivity and service, not toward who can externalize labor costs.
