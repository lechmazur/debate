[P1.1] Academic independence is not the absence of money. It is the absence of a funder's veto over what may be taught, researched, or said. Foreign government-linked money is uniquely able to install that veto, because a state carries leverage no private donor holds: visas, student flows, market access, and pressure on a researcher's relatives at home. Accepting it quietly means importing another government's political interests into your own governance.
[P1.2] The mechanism is not exotic. Confucius Institutes were routinely hosted under agreements giving a foreign state agency a hand in staffing and materials, sometimes with confidentiality terms; universities that already publish domestic donor conflicts signed contracts their own faculty could not read. American disclosure rules for foreign gifts existed for decades and were widely ignored until enforcement pressure exposed large unreported sums. The predictable result is anticipatory silence: an administrator declines a Tibet speaker, a student skips a Xinjiang question, a junior scholar chooses a safer topic.
[P1.3] Nobody issues a ban. The dependence does the work.
[P1.4] Notice what opposing this requires: defending opacity. Journals demand conflict-of-interest statements from authors; universities disclose trustees and domestic gifts. Why should a foreign ministry receive less scrutiny than a pharmaceutical company? Nothing here targets foreign students, foreign scholars, or private diaspora philanthropy.
[P1.5] The trigger is state control, not foreign birth.
[P1.6] And "limit" need not mean prohibition. Bar secret clauses and funder vetoes over hiring or curriculum; cap dependence where the security stakes are real; publish the rest. Money keeps flowing, on terms a scholar can defend in daylight. The cost CON weighs against this is paperwork and some forfeited revenue.
[P1.7] The cost we weigh is a campus that has learned, permanently and invisibly, which questions not to ask. Transparency is cheap; recovered independence is not.

[C1.1] The proposition protects universities from foreign political control by empowering domestic political authorities to police their income. That is not independence; it is a change in who can threaten it. A ministry that defines “government-linked,” demands disclosures, and requires limits can reward compliant institutions and isolate inconvenient ones. It creates the very funder veto PRO fears, closer to home.
[C1.2] Its category is radically overinclusive. Foreign public research agencies, state universities, sovereign funds, and scholarship programs can be government-linked without exercising editorial control. A national regime cannot reliably distinguish an authoritarian cultural contract from a joint telescope, public-health study, or engineering studentship. Disclosure may chill lawful partnerships; mandatory limits convert suspicion into lost research, especially for smaller institutions unable to afford specialist compliance.
[C1.3] PRO’s examples actually argue for governing contractual control, not financial nationality. If a Confucius Institute agreement gives a sponsor power over staffing, materials, or secrecy, faculty should reject those clauses whether the sponsor is foreign, domestic, governmental, or corporate. A domestic donor demanding a veto is not made acceptable by a passport, and a foreign research grant without one is not made corrupt by nationality.
[C1.4] Universities have better instruments: faculty senates, gift-acceptance rules, conflict-of-interest review, academic-freedom clauses, and refusal of sponsor vetoes over appointments, publication, or curriculum. Existing legal disclosure duties can be enforced without inventing a universal regime of caps and exposure. These safeguards preserve collaboration while attacking interference directly.
[C1.5] Nor is public disclosure costless. It can expose researchers and partners to harassment or retaliation, while lists of acceptable funders invite each government to redraw permissible inquiry. Dependence is a real risk, but the response is diversification and enforceable autonomy, not a political filter over academic finance. Reject the motion: prohibit control, wherever it comes from, rather than rationing knowledge by the passport of its funding.

A foreign state can withdraw visas, block student recruitment, restrict market access, or visit a co-author's family. [P2.3] Contract policing catches the clause; only funding rules catch the dependence. That is why the trigger is state control, not passport: a Danish research council and a ministry that runs censorship abroad are not the same animal, and a tiered rule can tell them apart — disclose broadly, restrict narrowly where control or security stakes are real. [P2.5] "Diversification" is not a limit: two dominant funders can each still hold a veto.
[P2.6] On harassment: redact individuals, disclose institutions, amounts, purposes, and terms. Nobody needs a graduate student's home address to see that a foreign ministry funds the center writing about its own borders.
