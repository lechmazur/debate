[P1.1] Every day in the United States, dozens of people die by firearm suicide, and a significant share of those deaths happen within minutes of a crisis. When a family member, a teacher, or a law‑enforcement officer sees a credible warning—a spoken threat, a pattern of violent behavior, or a documented mental‑health crisis—the only tool that can intervene before a tragedy unfolds is a temporary removal order. Red‑flag laws give courts that tool, and they do so with built‑in safeguards that preserve due process.
[P1.2] First, the statute is narrow: a petition must be supported by specific, articulable facts, and a judge must find probable cause that the person poses an imminent risk. The order is not a permanent confiscation; it typically lasts days to weeks, after which a full hearing determines whether any longer restriction is warranted. This mirrors the same procedural standard used for emergency protective orders in domestic‑violence cases, which courts have long upheld as constitutional.
[P1.3] Second, the cost of inaction is irreversible. A convicted felon may be barred from guns after trial, but a person who has not yet acted can still purchase, carry, or use a weapon today. Waiting for a criminal conviction means waiting for a victim to be shot, for a school to be attacked, for a family to lose a loved one to suicide. The temporary inconvenience of losing a firearm for a short period is a trivial price compared with a life that cannot be restored.
[P1.4] Finally, the empirical record supports the model. States that have enacted red‑flag statutes report measurable declines in firearm suicides, and law‑enforcement agencies cite them as a critical tool for averting potential mass‑shootings. The law does not erase rights; it pauses them when the evidence demands it, and it restores them promptly when the danger passes.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept that a court may strip someone of a constitutionally enumerated right—the same category of right protected by Heller and Bruen—based on nothing more than "credible evidence" of "imminent risk," before any adjudication of wrongdoing. That standard is not analogous to a domestic-violence emergency order, which restricts contact with a specific person; it is a full seizure of property tied to a fundamental right, triggered on an ex parte petition that the respondent never sees until after the guns are already gone.
[C1.2] That process inversion is the whole problem. In almost every red-flag regime, the initial order issues on one side's affidavit alone—often a family member, an ex-partner, or a neighbor with an ongoing dispute. Courts move fast precisely because no one is there to contest the facts. That is a recipe for erroneous deprivation: an angry ex-spouse, a landlord in a rent fight, or a relative contesting an inheritance has every incentive to describe ordinary anger or grief as "imminent risk."
[C1.3] Even a well-meaning judge, hearing only one side, cannot reliably tell the difference between a genuine threat and a exaggerated one. The people this harms most are exactly those already living under suspicion—minorities, the mentally ill who have never been violent, people mid-divorce.
[C1.4] And the claimed necessity dissolves under scrutiny. We already have tools for genuine imminent danger: involuntary psychiatric holds when someone is an acute danger to self or others, emergency criminal detention when a specific threat is made, and warrants when probable cause of a crime exists. Each of those requires a threshold tied to actual conduct or clinical judgment, not a vague "pattern of behavior." Red-flag orders don't fill a gap—they bypass the safeguards those existing tools built in.
[C1.5] Nor is the suicide-prevention data as clean as claimed; declines are correlational, drawn from states that adopted many policies simultaneously, and say nothing about the thousands of people flagged who were never actually dangerous. Sacrificing due process for a "trivial inconvenience" that isn't trivial, and isn't necessary, is a bad trade.
