[P1.1] Essential goods are not ordinary purchases. A major supermarket selling food, medicine, diapers, or soap is where people turn money into the means of getting through the week. At that point, payment is not a lifestyle choice; it is the door. A customer with cash in hand should not be told by a private checkout policy that their money is the wrong kind of money.
[P1.2] Cash matters because it is unconditional. It needs no bank account, charged phone, signal, card network, app login, approved identity record, cleared balance, or functioning bank. Card-only retail is smooth for the comfortable customer and brittle exactly where an essentials rule should be strongest: unbanked households, irregular earners, older and disabled customers, people fleeing abuse, and anyone hit by a lost card or outage. If the shelf is open but the payment gate is closed, exclusion is real.
[P1.3] Cash is also resilience. When broadband fails, card authorization goes down, or a storm disrupts mobile service, a cash-capable lane can still sell bread and medicine. A parent with baby formula and a ten-dollar note should not be sent away because a chain's digital ecosystem is temporarily unavailable. Essential purchases should not depend on private uptime.
[P1.4] The efficiency objection ignores the narrowness of the requirement. This is not a cash-only economy, not a ban on apps or self-checkout, and not a burden on small shops. It applies to major retailers and essential goods. Large chains already handle inventory, refrigeration, refunds, fraud, and staffing; one cash-capable lane, a safe, bank deposits, and training are manageable.
[P1.5] Theft risk is real, but safes and procedures address it; refusing cash shifts the cost onto people with the least choice.
[P1.6] Voluntary policies are unreliable because vulnerable shoppers need a guarantee, not a manager's discretion. Card-only is a retailer convenience. Cash acceptance is a public floor: people with cash can buy necessities. For essential goods, governments should make that floor mandatory.

[C1.1] Requiring major retailers to take cash sounds compassionate until you ask why any store stops taking it. Major chains want revenue; they do not reject customers on a whim. When an urban pharmacy or grocery format goes cashless, it is because physical cash is a magnet for violent robbery and heavy handling costs. [P1.5] claims "safes and procedures address it," but a backroom safe does not protect a late-shift cashier facing a weapon at an open till.
[C1.2] In low-margin, high-crime neighborhoods, a cash mandate outlaws the exact security adaptation that keeps struggling stores open. A shuttered pharmacy or grocer in an underserved neighborhood is far worse for vulnerable residents than a card-only checkout.
[C1.3] Moreover, the mandate as written is operationally absurd. Requiring cash specifically "for essential goods" forces minimum-wage cashiers to police arbitrary lines at checkout. If a shopper brings milk, aspirin, socks, and a frying pan to the single cash lane [P1.4] proposes, must the cashier split the basket and reject cash for the pan? In practice, stores must maintain full cash infrastructure—armored transport, cash rooms, manual reconciliation—driving up food prices across razor-thin grocery margins.
[C1.4] Nor does cash deliver outage resilience [P1.3]. Modern chain supermarkets cannot scan barcodes, verify prices, or open electronic tills when networks or power fail; a ten-dollar bill cannot operate a dead point-of-sale system.
[C1.5] Finally, this mandate uses the wrong tool. Most major retailers already accept cash wherever it is safe. Where security or store design precludes it, stores can use cash-to-card kiosks that convert bills into free, anonymous prepaid cards on the spot. That solves [P1.2]'s concern about bank accounts, apps, and identity checks without putting cash in registers.
[C1.6] Governments should expand public banking and permit safe alternatives, not force retail workers to bear the risks of physical currency.
