[P1.1] A textbook is the only history most citizens will ever formally read. The question is not whether children receive a national story — they will — but who holds the pen when the facts are inconvenient: people who can be voted out, or people who can be proven wrong.
[P1.2] Political control of textbooks produces two predictable failures. First, whiplash. When the curriculum follows electoral cycles, each government rewrites the past to fit its present: Turkey's curriculum has swung sharply with administrations; Russia moved toward a single state-approved history text; India's textbooks are rewritten as parties change power, with Mughal-era chapters expanded or deleted depending on who rules. Students stop getting an education and start getting the memoir of the ruling party.
[P1.3] Second, error correction. A politician's incentive is to make the nation look virtuous; a scholar's incentive, in an open and plural review process, is to survive criticism from peers. That asymmetry is everything. It is why postwar Germany could co-write textbooks with French and Polish historians through international commissions, while politically controlled systems keep relitigating settled atrocities: Japan's government screening of textbooks has repeatedly softened descriptions of Nanjing and wartime forced labor, corroding both diplomacy and education at once.
[P1.4] The objection that this trades democracy for technocracy fails. Elected boards can still set goals — which eras, which skills, which civic values. What leaves their reach is only the power to overrule evidence: to declare by vote what happened. That power belongs to no one.
[P1.5] Independent review panels — historians, teachers, archivists, affected communities — with published critiques and named authors are more accountable than a minister's quiet edit, because their claims can be checked.
[P1.6] So weigh the motion by one question: which process is more likely to tell a student something true that the government of the day would rather she not know? Only one side passes.

[C1.1] History education is not merely a sterile data-delivery system; it is the vital process by which a self-governing community defines its shared values and civic identity. The proposition assumes that narratives of war, colonialism, and national identity can be objectively resolved by a neutral, scholarly priesthood. This is a dangerous illusion.
[C1.2] First, narratives are inherently normative, not purely empirical. While historical facts are stubborn, deciding which facts to highlight, how to frame a nation’s triumphs and tragedies, and what moral lessons to draw are value judgments. There is no "objective" scholarly formula to weigh the legacy of a founding father or a colonial era. Academics possess expertise in historical evidence, but they have no special moral authority to dictate a community's collective memory.
[C1.3] In a democracy, that authority belongs to the public, exercised through elected representatives.
[C1.4] Second, replacing political oversight with "independent scholarly review" simply trades democratic accountability for elite capture. Academic institutions are not neutral archives; they are insulated socio-political ecosystems with their own pronounced ideological biases. Delegating the framing of national identity to an unelected, self-policing guild of specialists disenfranchises the very families public schools are built to serve. When parents and local communities are locked out of the curriculum, public trust in education collapses, driving deeper societal polarization.
[C1.5] Finally, the political friction of curriculum debates is not a bug; it is a democratic feature. It forces a society to actively negotiate its past and present values. Leaving history to democratic control ensures that textbooks remain responsive to the evolving consensus of the living public, rather than fossilized under the preferences of an unaccountable academic elite. To preserve democratic legitimacy, we must keep the pen in the hands of the people.
