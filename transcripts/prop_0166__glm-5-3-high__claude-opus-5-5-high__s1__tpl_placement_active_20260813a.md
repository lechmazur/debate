[P1.1] The premise underlying most objections to de-extinction is that conservation is a zero-sum budget, and that's false twice over. First, money for ambitious, headline-grabbing projects rarely comes from the same pool that funds ranger patrols and habitat acquisition. De-extinction attracts venture capital, philanthropists, and biotech investment that would otherwise go to other tech ventures, not to park rangers. Colossal's mammoth effort is privately funded; the thylacine work in Melbourne sits inside a research institute that trains geneticists who will spend careers on threatened species.
[P1.2] The "diversion" is largely imaginary, and where public money is involved, governments routinely fund long-horizon science alongside, not instead of, urgent programs. Nobody argues we should cancel astronomy because emergency medicine exists.
[P1.3] Second, and more important: de-extinction builds tools that conservation desperately needs. The genetic engineering techniques developed to revive a mammoth—editing cold-tolerance traits into elephants, gene synthesis at scale, artificial wombs—are the same techniques needed to inject genetic diversity into the dwindling Florida panther, to engineer resistance into amphibians being wiped out by chytrid fungus, or to rescue the northern white rhino, down to two infertile females. The black-footed ferret was rescued from near-extinction through cloning of frozen cell lines—a de-extinction-adjacent technique already paying off for a living endangered species.
[P1.4] Treating these technologies as a luxury misses that they are infrastructure. The choice is not mammoth versus rhino; it's building the genetic toolkit now or discovering in twenty years that we need it for a hundred rhinos.
[P1.5] Third, de-extinction restores ecological function, not just individuals. Mammoths could help convert boreal forest back to grassland, preserving permafrost; returning the thylacine could rebalance Tasmanian ecosystems where mesopredators now run unchecked. The conservation model of "protect what remains" is losing—species keep sliding toward extinction while we triage. A movement with a plan that cannot even hold the line needs more arrows in its quiver, not fewer.
[P1.6] Finally, attention is not a subtraction either. A living mammoth calf would be the greatest conservation recruitment event in history, and governments should want to own that moment.
