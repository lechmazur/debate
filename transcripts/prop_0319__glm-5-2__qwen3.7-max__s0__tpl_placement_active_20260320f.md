[P1.1] Small live-music venues are not merely businesses. They are the incubators, training grounds, and gathering points that sustain a city's musical ecosystem—the places where artists form, audiences develop, and scenes coalesce before anything reaches arenas or recording contracts. When a city loses its small venues, it doesn't just lose entertainment options; it loses the productive capacity that feeds its entire cultural economy. That is the definition of infrastructure: the underlying system that makes other activity possible.
[P1.2] The threat is real and asymmetric. A single noise complaint from a new condo conversion can trigger licensing reviews that drain a 150-capacity room's reserves. Policing decisions that treat live music crowds as default security risks can make operation untenable. Zoning changes that allow residential encroachment into formerly industrial or commercial corridors systematically punish venues that existed first.
[P1.3] Each individual closure looks like routine market churn. The aggregate effect is the destruction of a city's cultural reproductive system.
[P1.4] Cities already recognize this logic for libraries, museums, community theaters, and public art. These receive protective treatment in planning decisions because their value exceeds what user fees or foot traffic can capture. Small venues generate the same kind of spillover—talent development, neighborhood identity, tourism appeal, creative-industry employment—yet they are treated as discretionary commercial enterprises fully exposed to whoever moves in next door. That asymmetry is not principled.
[P1.5] It is an artifact of category inertia.
[P1.6] Treating small venues as cultural infrastructure does not mean immunity from regulation. It means a default presumption of preservation: burden-shifting so that late-arriving residents know what they are moving near, licensing frameworks calibrated to venue size rather than blanket enforcement, and zoning that recognizes cultural use as a legitimate land function worth protecting. The proposition asks for nothing radical—only that cities apply to small venues the same logic they already apply to institutions whose value cannot be measured by quarterly revenue alone.

When a restaurant fails due to market churn, we do not call it a failure of civic infrastructure. We should not do so for a music venue.
[C1.3] PRO’s proposed solution—burden-shifting and presumptive preservation—actively harms urban livability and exacerbates the housing crisis. By demanding that late-arriving residents absorb the noise, crowds, and late-night disruptions of a venue, the proposition prioritizes the leisure of concertgoers over the basic right to quiet enjoyment of one's home. Cities are currently struggling to build dense, mixed-use housing to solve affordability crises. If zoning and licensing decisions must default to protecting the acoustic footprint of a 150-capacity club, developers will simply build elsewhere.
[C1.4] Shielding venues from standard nuisance laws effectively weaponizes cultural nostalgia to block residential development and freeze neighborhoods in an artificial state of arrested decay.
[C1.5] Finally, treating these venues as infrastructure creates regulatory capture. A vibrant music scene relies on new spaces opening, not just old ones being artificially kept on life support through zoning exemptions. If a venue’s cultural value truly exceeds its commercial viability, the market has already spoken.
[C1.6] The appropriate response is not to distort municipal zoning and policing to prop up failing business models, but to rely on direct arts grants or non-profit models for the rare venues that genuinely serve a public, rather than purely commercial, function.
