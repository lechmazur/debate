[P1.1] The decisive issue is not whether every grant dollar can be tracked to a quarterly deliverable, but whether public funding is structured to produce the unpredictable discoveries that drive long-run prosperity. Fundamental science is not a construction project. Its most valuable results are often unrecognizable when they begin. A grant system organized around short competitive cycles forces scientists to promise near-term outputs, measurable milestones, and safe hypotheses.
[P1.2] That bias toward the predictable is exactly wrong for a domain where breakthroughs come from following anomalies, building instruments, and testing ideas that may fail for years.
[P1.3] Long-term block grants correct that mismatch. By giving laboratories, institutes, or universities stable multi-year support, governments let researchers pursue questions rather than proposals. They reduce the enormous hidden cost of constant application writing and review churn. They allow teams to keep a promising but unpromising-looking line alive through early failures.
[P1.4] They make space for cross-disciplinary work that does not fit neatly into a call for applications. And they preserve scarce capabilities—trained technicians, shared datasets, experimental platforms—through funding gaps that project grants routinely destroy.
[P1.5] History supports this. Much of modern computing, communications, and medicine rests on basic research that no short-cycle manager would have funded as a line item: quantum mechanics before transistors, bacterial immune systems before gene editing, pure mathematics before cryptography. The public cannot know in advance which threads will matter; pretending otherwise substitutes bureaucratic legibility for scientific judgment.
[P1.6] Yes, block grants loosen dollar-by-dollar control. But accountability should be judged at the level of mission stewardship, not micromanagement. Institutions can still report progress, undergo audits, and lose funding for sustained underperformance. The relevant question is accountability for what: spending categories, or civilizational learning.
[P1.7] When the goal is frontier knowledge, the cheaper oversight model is not the better one. The better model is patient funding, then evaluate institutions by their cumulative contribution to discovery.

[P4.3] A2: The enforcement mechanism is not necessarily closing a famous institute tomorrow. [P4.4] CON assumes block grants mean no consequence; that is false. It means consequences are tied to cumulative scientific stewardship rather than quarterly receipts.
[P4.5] That is why PRO still wins. CON’s strongest point is that project grants make failure visible. But visibility is not truth. A system that annually surfaces mediocre compliance while starving slow, unpromising-looking breakthroughs is not accountable in the way that matters.
