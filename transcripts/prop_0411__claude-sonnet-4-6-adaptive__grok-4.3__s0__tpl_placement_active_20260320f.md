[P1.1] Mobility is not a luxury. It is the mechanism through which people access work, healthcare, education, and social life. When transit systems deliver that mechanism to some riders in ten minutes and make others wait an hour for the same trip, they are not running different services—they are running a two-tiered society sorted by disability status.
[P1.2] That is the current reality. Paratransit systems, where they exist, routinely require advance booking of twenty-four hours or more. Ride-hail companies launched accessible vehicle programs years after their standard services and still cover only a fraction of markets. A wheelchair user who misses a medical appointment because the accessible van was unavailable has not experienced an inconvenience—she has experienced a civil rights failure.
[P1.3] The proposition asks for something reasonable: wait times and convenience *close to* standard service. Not identical, not instantaneous—close. That language acknowledges operational complexity while refusing to accept a gap so wide it effectively excludes disabled riders from spontaneous travel altogether. The current gap is not close; it is categorical.
[P1.4] The practical case is also strong. Ride-hail companies already manage dynamic dispatch across enormous fleets. Requiring a minimum accessible-vehicle ratio and algorithmic parity is an engineering problem they are equipped to solve. Public transit agencies in cities that have prioritized low-floor bus fleets show that accessibility and operational efficiency can coexist.
[P1.5] The cost is real but manageable, especially spread across large systems and phased over time.
[P1.6] The CON side will argue cost and operational complexity. But notice what that argument concedes: that the barrier to equal access is money and inconvenience—for the operator. Disabled riders bear those costs every single day in lost time, lost opportunity, and lost dignity. The question is who should bear the burden of solving this problem: the system or the person the system was built to serve.
[P1.7] The answer is obvious, and the law should reflect it.

[C1.1] Requiring near-parity wait times for accessible vehicles forces operators to hold large numbers of specialized assets in constant readiness rather than allowing flexible matching. Ride-hail fleets already show that wheelchair-accessible vehicles represent a small share of total supply; spreading them to match standard dispatch speeds means many sit idle or travel empty between calls, raising the cost per completed trip for every rider.
[C1.2] Public transit faces a parallel constraint. Low-floor buses improve access on fixed routes, yet door-to-door paratransit remains slower because it must handle variable origins, medical equipment, and securement time. Mandating equivalent headways across both modes requires either abandoning efficient high-frequency trunk service to free vehicles for on-demand accessible trips or expanding the entire fleet dramatically. Either path increases taxpayer or rider costs without adding new capacity.
[C1.3] The mandate also distorts incentives. When regulators set binding wait-time targets, operators prioritize compliance over responsiveness to actual demand. Cities that have tried strict accessible dispatch ratios report longer average waits for non-disabled users during peak periods and occasional outright withdrawal of service from lower-density areas where the economics no longer cover the required accessible fleet. Those outcomes shrink overall mobility rather than expand it.
[C1.4] A concrete illustration is a medium-sized metro area with 4,000 ride-hail vehicles. Achieving a 90-percent match rate for accessible trips within ten minutes would require roughly doubling the accessible subset and subsidizing their repositioning. The resulting fare increase or subsidy draw falls on all users, including low-income disabled riders who already face higher relative costs from reduced choice.
[C1.5] The proposition therefore converts an access problem into a capacity and pricing problem whose burdens spread across the entire system.
