[P1.1] The choice is not standards versus speed. It is competence verification versus vacancy paperwork. Faster recognition asks: can this person safely do the job? Tighter labor-market testing asks: has an employer documented that no local worker was available?
[P1.2] The first checks the real risk in professional work. The second guesses demand through forms. Scarce administrative capacity should go to the tool that measures skill, not the one that mostly slows hiring.
[P1.3] Recognition need not mean rubber-stamping. A fast regime can verify documents, require exams, language checks, bridge courses, supervised practice, and provisional licenses. What it removes is duplicated assessment, opaque equivalency rulings, and years-long queues that leave a trained engineer driving deliveries or a foreign-trained nurse restarting basic training. That waste lowers tax revenue, deepens clinic and school shortages, and pushes skilled migrants into jobs below their level.
[P1.4] It is state-created deskilling, not worker protection.
[P1.5] Tighter testing is also a weak protector of domestic labor. Shortages rarely appear as a neat failed recruitment file; they appear as cancelled appointments, delayed projects, and overworked staff. An employer can advertise at the wrong wage, in the wrong region, or under a label that misses transferable skills, then still pass. A real vacancy can die while files sit.
[P1.6] More testing rewards firms with HR lawyers, not communities with unmet needs, and does not itself test whether the migrant is competent.
[P1.7] Pay floors, anti-exploitation enforcement, and licensing thresholds protect wages directly; making a hospital wait longer to confirm a diploma does not. Credential recognition is the stricter standard where it matters: competence, safety, and equivalence. Labor-market testing tightens only the gate around job offers, and a rejection for a paperwork defect is a shortage by administrative choice. If policy must prioritize, it should verify skill quickly, license conditionally where gaps exist, and let proven competence—not bureaucratic prophecy about demand—decide access to the profession.
