[C1.1] PRO’s case rests on a category error: treating corporate wellness apps as if they were only meditation timers. Modern platforms commonly include confidential counseling, structured behavioral coaching, sleep and financial-stress support, benefits navigation, and clinician referral. For a warehouse worker with panic symptoms, an exhausted single parent, or an employee in grief, timely access to treatment may cut burnout more than another scheduled social ritual. Burnout has many causes; isolation is only one.
[C1.2] The rituals are not uniformly effective either. Predictable shutdown weeks are impossible in hospitals, emergency services, and continuous manufacturing; where possible, coverage costs are high and can fall on lower-paid staff. Gatherings can also make isolation worse. Mandatory offsites can intensify anxiety, exclude caregivers, disabled employees, night-shift teams, and remote workers, and leave someone remembering why they never fit in.
[C1.3] In-person contact helps some trust, but it is episodic, expensive, and does nothing about workload, discrimination, illness, or bad management.
[C1.4] Apps are also a diagnostic layer, not merely coping content. Aggregated feedback can flag a team where sleep is collapsing, show that people are not taking leave, and trigger workload review before attrition. A single app dollar can triage the employee who would never attend an offsite or ask a manager for help. Low uptake is not proof of low value when stigma is the barrier; a confidential pathway changes the calculus.
[C1.5] The real comparison is therefore not “recovery versus a breathing exercise.” It is whether employers should defund confidential, scalable, evidence-based care to fund two visible interventions with real but partial benefits. They should not. Buy the leave and gatherings where they fit; keep the tools that reach the people the rituals miss.
