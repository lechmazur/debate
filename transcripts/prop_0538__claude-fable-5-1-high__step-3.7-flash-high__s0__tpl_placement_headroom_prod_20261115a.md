[C1.1] The motion relies on a false and dangerous dichotomy: it pretends African countries must choose between exporting raw materials to global markets and building intra-African manufacturing, when the reality is that abandoning raw material export streams would devastate the foreign exchange reserves most African states rely on to fund development, service external debt, and import essential goods like medicine and fertilizer. Take Zambia, where copper exports make up nearly 80% of export earnings: a forced shift to prioritize regional copper product manufacturing over raw ore exports to global smelters would collapse government revenue, as seen when 2020 copper price drops forced 15% cuts to the national health budget, with no regional market large enough to offset those losses.
[C1.2] PRO also glosses over the barriers to viable intra-African manufacturing and trade. Logistics costs across sub-Saharan Africa are roughly double the global average, and non-tariff barriers mean even CFTA tariff-free goods are often held up at borders for days, raising costs for regional producers. A Kenyan textile manufacturer faces lower costs to ship finished goods to the EU under preferential trade deals than to sell the same goods in landlocked Rwanda, making regional markets uncompetitive for even manufactured goods. Even where regional trade flows exist, they are dominated by established industrial hubs like South Africa and Egypt, leaving smaller, lower-income states with no clear path to capture market share.
[C1.3] Finally, PRO’s dismissal of 1970s protectionist failures misses the core lesson: those experiments failed not just due to small national markets, but because they cut off access to the foreign exchange and technology transfer that comes from global export engagement. Forcing a priority shift to regional manufacturing would lock countries into low-value regional production rather than letting them climb global value chains by leveraging raw material export revenues to build industrial capacity.
