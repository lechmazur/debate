[P1.1] Default always-on surveillance treats normal adolescence as a pre-crime investigation. The teenage task is to learn to navigate time, space, and relationships without a manager hovering. If every location ping is watched and every DM is searchable, you do not teach judgment; you replace it. And judgment, unlike a dashboard, has to be practiced.
[P1.2] That surveillance does not even buy safety. It spends trust, which is the actual safety infrastructure in a family. When a parent's default is 'I will see everything,' a teenager's rational response is 'I will show you less.' Second accounts, deleted threads, location spoofing, a friend's phone to chat.
[P1.3] More importantly, they stop telling you things voluntarily. The teen who would have said 'I'm uncomfortable at this party, can you get me?' now hesitates because admitting where they are will trigger a lecture about three weeks of location history. What we know about adolescent disclosure is consistent: control without cause reduces disclosure. Less disclosure means you know less when it really counts.
[P1.4] Private messages deserve special protection. They are where teenagers try out identities, process anxiety, argue with friends, and talk about sex and mental health. That space needs privacy to function. A kid questioning their sexuality or dealing with depression needs peer confidants who can be spoken to uncensored.
[P1.5] A mother reading the inbox literalizes surveillance and chills help-seeking.
[P1.6] This proposition is restrained. It leaves the door wide open for monitoring when there is a specific, articulable safety concern - a pattern of risky use, contact with a predatory adult, severe mood change. What it rejects is suspicion as a household rule. Absent cause, you start from trust, with check-ins, shared expectations, and location sharing by request or agreement, not by panopticon.
