[C1.1] The motion’s conditions are a receipt, not a pedagogy. Disclosure tells an instructor that a machine touched the work; it does not tell them what the student can do. “Accountability for accuracy and reasoning” is an after-the-fact audit of a product, not evidence that the student produced the reasoning. That distinction is decisive, because a university grade certifies a person’s competence, not the quality of a document.
[C1.2] Writing is not merely the packaging of thought; it is where thought is built. A student who struggles to frame a thesis, organize evidence, and revise a clumsy paragraph is learning to reason. Hand them a fluent draft and ask them to “verify” it, and you have reversed the cognitive order: novices are now auditing prose they could not have generated, using exactly the judgment they came to acquire. The professional analogy fails for that reason.
[C1.3] Lawyers and analysts can responsibly supervise AI because they already have domain expertise. Students are in the business of acquiring it.
[C1.4] Nor do the safeguards bite. “AI-assisted drafting” stretches from a spellcheck to a full outline to a generated essay, and disclosure of that label gives no meaningful measure of substitution. Accountability is equally porous: an oral defense can be rehearsed, and a student can often explain a polished argument without being able to construct one.
[C1.5] Meanwhile the student who follows the rules and writes unaided competes against someone who discloses a light edit but used the model heavily. The proposition says bans are unenforceable; permission is too. The difference is that permission makes the evasion legitimate and universal.
[C1.6] Some courses can and should permit AI, but the motion makes it the default for most coursework—precisely the essays, reports, and problem sets where the learning happens. Universities would be certifying outcomes they can no longer observe. That is not accountability. It is abdication.
