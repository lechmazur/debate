[P1.1] Learning to read is not natural. Children acquire spoken language by immersion, but written language is a code that must be explicitly taught—phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension, built systematically over years. Decades of cognitive science converge on this point. When policy leaves curriculum design to thousands of individual classrooms, it guarantees that a child's chance of learning to decode depends on whether their particular teacher happens to have absorbed that science.
[P1.2] That is not autonomy; that is a lottery.
[P1.3] The case for structured instruction rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, the evidence asymmetry. Approaches built on teacher discretion—balanced literacy, three-cueing, guessing words from pictures—dominated American classrooms for decades while roughly a third of fourth graders read below basic levels. States that mandated structured, phonics-based instruction with aligned teacher training, most famously Mississippi, moved from the bottom of national rankings toward the middle within a decade. When a poor state with high poverty outperforms wealthier states on early reading, the variable that changed was policy prioritizing structure.
[P1.5] Second, equity. Affluent children with collapsing classroom instruction get tutors; their parents fill gaps. Poor children, English learners, and dyslexic students depend entirely on school. Classroom autonomy in curriculum design is a luxury good—its costs fall hardest on those with the least cushion.
[P1.6] A structured floor is the only mechanism that protects the most vulnerable readers.
[P1.7] Third, autonomy survives where it belongs. Prioritizing structure in curriculum design does not turn teachers into robots. It specifies what gets taught and in what sequence; teachers retain judgment over pacing, grouping, examples, texts, and how to reach the struggling child in row three. Surgeons follow protocols and remain professionals.
[P1.8] Structure channels expertise; it does not erase it.
[P1.9] The question is not whether teachers are skilled. It is whether a six-year-old's literacy should hinge on local improvisation when we know what reliably works. Policy exists precisely to close that gap. Prioritize the science; let craft flourish within it.
