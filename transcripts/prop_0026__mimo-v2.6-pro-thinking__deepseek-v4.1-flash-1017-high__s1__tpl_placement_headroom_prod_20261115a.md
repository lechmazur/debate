[P1.1] Bodily autonomy is the moral principle that actually governs medical decisions; "dignity" is the principle other people invoke to overrule them. That asymmetry should decide the case.
[P1.2] Consider what each term does. Autonomy is first-person authority: the patient who consents, refuses, or chooses among treatment options is the one who lives with the consequences, and the one whose body is at stake. Dignity, by contrast, is almost always asserted by third parties — clinicians, families, legislators — about what kind of choice is "befitting" the person. When a Jehovah's Witness refuses a blood transfusion, or a patient declines life-prolonging ventilation, or a woman chooses or refuses an abortion, the dignity argument reliably says the choice is beneath the person's worth.
[P1.3] That is not a description of the patient's good; it is a judgment that the patient's own ranking of values is illegitimate. In personal medical choices, that judgment should lose.
[P1.4] Notice the structural problem with dignity claims: they are unfalsifiable. Show that the patient is informed, uncoerced, and settled in their decision, and the dignity objection still stands, because it was never about competence or pressure. It was about content. A principle that cannot be defeated by the patient's own considered will is not a safeguard of the person; it is a veto over them.
[P1.5] Autonomy at least has operational teeth — informed consent, capacity assessment, the right of refusal — and those procedures can distinguish genuine choice from coercion.
[P1.6] The decisive comparison is whose judgment prevails when the two conflict. Dignity discourse is strongest where it blocks commodification or coercion, but even there the work is done by harm and consent, not by dignity itself. In the personal medical sphere — reproductive care, end-of-life refusal, gender-affirming treatment, amputation or body modification — the dignity label is the vehicle by which strangers convert their discomfort into authority over a patient's flesh.
[P1.7] Where the person is informed and free, the body is theirs. [clipped]

[C1.1] Autonomy is not a self-justifying trump; it is a permission that matters because persons have dignity. Strip dignity away and autonomy becomes mere preference-management. The proposition gets the priority backwards.
[C1.2] Its central move is to contrast first-person autonomy with third-party dignity. But autonomy in medicine is not self-executing. Someone defines capacity, frames options, withholds or presents information, and decides whether a refusal is “informed.” Those gatekeepers already shape the choice. Dignity is not always an external veto either: patients themselves say they want to die with dignity, avoid indignity, or not be reduced to a medical project.
[C1.3] When a frail patient agrees to aggressive treatment because she fears becoming a burden, the consent form may be signed; dignity asks whether she is being treated as an equal person or as a cost.
[C1.4] That is the decisive point: autonomy tells us who decides, but dignity tells us why the decision deserves respect and when the conditions of respect are missing. A competent adult can be humiliated, commodified, or pressured into a choice that is formally autonomous. Dignity catches what consent alone cannot: adaptive preference, family coercion, institutional neglect, and the reduction of a body to a site of consumption or experiment.
[C1.5] The proposition’s absolute priority would make medicine incoherent. If autonomy outweighed dignity always, a patient could demand a pointless amputation or a degrading procedure and the ethics committee would have nothing to say except “your body, your choice.” But clinicians are not vending machines, and patients are not consumers of their own flesh. Dignity sets the baseline: the body is not property, and the person is not merely a preference-holder.
[C1.6] Autonomy deserves great weight. But it is weight borrowed from dignity. In personal medical choices, dignity is the deeper principle, and when the two conflict, dignity should not automatically lose.

[C2.6] So the real clash is not committee versus patient. It is whether the patient’s choice is authoritative because it is hers, or because persons deserve not to be used, humiliated, or reduced. The limiting principle is non-maleficence plus medicine’s duty not to inflict injury without therapeutic benefit, not third-party disgust. That standard is public and content-neutral: it asks what the procedure does to a body, not whether observers dislike the choice. The same applies to a demand for active killing with no medical indication.
[C4.2] If that sounds like a veto, the burden runs the other way: the objector must show harm, not distaste, and the patient keeps the right to refuse treatment and seek another clinician. This is not a committee veto over private preference; it marks the boundary of what medicine may be asked to do.
[C4.3] A2: The separating procedure is burden-shifting public justification. A patient’s own “die with dignity” claim is first-person and presumptive: she says what standing or experience is at stake, and her reasons are tested through capacity and voluntariness inquiry. A clinician’s dignity objection must be specified as a concrete harm—coercion, humiliation, commodification—and defended before independent review with the patient’s advocate. Bare “undignified” fails.
[C4.4] That distinguishes the two without pretending autonomy is self-validating.
[C4.5] This is why CON still wins. PRO treats autonomy as a neutral machine that can sort settled from coerced choices. It cannot. The voluntariness test already presupposes a person with standing whose treatment matters; dignity supplies that baseline, and autonomy executes it.
[C4.6] The amputation case shows the alternative: choice becomes consumption. The review procedure shows dignity need not be a veto by disgust. So when they conflict, dignity carries more moral weight because it explains why the patient’s authority deserves deference and where its limits lie. Autonomy alone cannot answer its own hardest question.
