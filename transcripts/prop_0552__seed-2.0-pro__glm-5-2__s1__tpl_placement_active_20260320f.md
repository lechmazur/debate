[P1.1] Electronic waste is not inert garbage. Old phone batteries, burned out circuit boards, cracked flat screens carry lead, cadmium, and persistent toxic flame retardants that do not disappear. When wealthy nations ship this waste to lower income countries, it is almost never processed under safety standards that would be legal at home.
[P1.2] Instead, men, women and children burn cables over open fires to extract copper, sift through unlined dumps with bare hands, and wash scrap metal in local rivers. These communities suffer elevated childhood lead poisoning, permanent lung damage, and contaminated drinking water that harms generations.
[P1.3] Opponents will argue these scrap jobs provide needed income. That argument only holds if you count the wages but ignore the cost of the permanent disability and early death those jobs produce. No worker accepts permanent brain damage for their child in exchange for two dollars a day by free choice. They take this work because rich nations dump waste so cheaply it puts any formal, safe regional recycler out of business before it can scale.
[P1.4] This ban ends that unfair subsidy for poison. It does not destroy opportunity. It forces producing countries to internalize the full real cost of the electronics they consume. It drives investment in safe domestic recycling, creates formal jobs instead of death trap work, and enforces the most basic moral rule: if you make the mess, you are responsible for cleaning it up.
[P1.5] There is no just reason the world's poor should absorb the toxic cost of wealthy countries' consumption.

[C1.5] The proposition also ignores that formal, certified recycling sectors in lower-income countries have been growing steadily. [C1.6] The real problem is unsafe processing, not trade itself.
