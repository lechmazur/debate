[P1.1] International shipping produces roughly 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions—more than every country except the top three. Left unregulated, it will cancel out every emission cut made by every major European nation by mid-century. This is not a marginal problem. Voluntary industry pledges have already failed.
[P1.2] Over the last fifteen years, carriers promised steady decarbonisation, and instead shipping emissions rose. This is not bad faith; it is market logic. Any single company that switches to low-carbon fuel will be immediately undercut by competitors that do not. Only a universal binding timetable from the IMO breaks that race to the bottom.
[P1.3] Yes, freight costs will rise. They will rise a little. Best available estimates put the average impact on finished consumer goods at less than one percent. A $20 t-shirt costs 15 cents more.
[P1.4] That is not a catastrophe. That is a trivial, widely distributed cost to avoid climate collapse that will disrupt global trade orders of magnitude more severely. This is also not just a long term climate bet. The same dirty bunker fuels that warm the planet kill roughly half a million people annually from particulate lung disease, overwhelmingly in low-income port communities.
[P1.5] Low carbon fuels eliminate almost all that harm the day they are adopted. Binding timetables do not block innovation—they cause it. No shipbuilder will invest in zero carbon designs, no fuel producer will scale green supply, unless they know everyone will be required to use them. Delay is not caution.
[P1.6] Delay is locking in another decade of irreversible damage, for no meaningful gain.

[C1.1] Nobody here defends bunker fuel. The question is whether the IMO should bind the world's fleet to a fuel timetable, and pass the bill to whoever happens to be downstream. On both counts, this is the wrong instrument.
[C1.2] Start with the mandate's core error: it regulates the fuel rather than the carbon. Green ammonia, e-methanol and hydrogen barely exist at commercial scale, and every litre of them competes for the same scarce renewable electricity that could instead be shutting down coal plants. A dollar spent making synthetic marine fuel buys a fraction of the emissions cut that dollar buys in the power sector. A binding timetable doesn't create electrons; it bids for them, raising the cost of decarbonising everything else.
[C1.3] That is not caution versus delay — that is a genuinely worse allocation of a limited clean-energy supply.
[C1.4] And the fuels aren't clean the day they arrive, as [P1.5] claims. Ammonia is acutely toxic and its combustion releases nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas hundreds of times more potent than CO2. LNG leaks methane. If the mandate is technology-forcing on a fixed date, ships will adopt whatever is available, not whatever is best — and much of it will be made from fossil hydrogen.
[C1.5] Then the incidence. The $20 t-shirt in [P1.3] is the one cargo that makes this look painless. Freight is a rounding error on sneakers and a major line item on grain, rice, fertiliser and cement. Small island and landlocked developing states pay several times the global average freight share on food and fuel imports.
[C1.6] "Even if freight costs rise" means higher food import bills for the countries with the least resilience — which is precisely why several of them have resisted blunt fuel mandates at the IMO.
[C1.7] Finally, enforcement. The IMO governs through flag states, and flags are chosen. A binding timetable without credible enforcement produces registry-shopping and paper compliance: costs for the honest, emissions unchanged.
[C1.8] Price the carbon. Fund the transition. Don't mandate a fuel we haven't built.
