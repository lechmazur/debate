[P1.1] The employer-based system has a built-in contradiction: it distributes a necessity of life according to the accident of employment. A diagnosis of cancer, a back injury, a pregnancy — these are health events. They become financial catastrophes only because the system insists that coverage travel with a paystub. Single-payer financing, by contrast, pools risk across the entire population and pays for basic medical needs as a public function.
[P1.2] That is the structural contrast, and it is decisive.
[P1.3] Consider three failures built into the employer model. First, coverage discontinuity. Roughly one in five Americans has been uninsured or underinsured in a given year, and the dominant pathway is job loss or job change. The insurance moves with the W-2; the illness does not wait.
[P1.4] Second, job lock. Workers stay in unsuitable jobs, refuse promotions, decline to start businesses, or remain with abusive employers because changing jobs means changing coverage — sometimes with new exclusions, new networks, new deductibles, or a gap that can last months. Third, inequity between workers. Two neighbors with the same illness, on the same block, receive wildly different care depending on whether they work for a large firm, a small firm, or themselves.
[P1.5] That is a lottery of employers, not a healthcare system.
[P1.6] Single-payer resolves these failures at the root. Coverage becomes a right of residence, not a benefit of employment. Risk pools grow to the whole population, lowering per-capita variance. Administrative overhead collapses because one payer replaces hundreds, eliminating duplicative billing, marketing, and prior-authorization machinery.
[P1.7] The bargaining leverage of a single public payer is unmatched.
[P1.8] The opposition will defend employer coverage as flexible and pluralistic. But the question is not whether it exists. It is whether, for basic medical needs, it is preferable. A system that withholds care from the unemployed, traps the employed in bad jobs, and varies quality by employer is not preferable to one that guarantees access simply because a person lives in the country.

[C1.1] Employer-based insurance aligns coverage with the production of resources that actually fund care. Workers generate the wages and tax base that pay for hospitals, doctors, and drugs; detaching insurance from employment severs that link and shifts the entire burden onto compulsory taxation that must still come from the same workforce.
[C1.2] Single-payer systems replace price signals and consumer choice with centralized allocation. When demand exceeds budgeted supply, the result is not expanded capacity but explicit or implicit rationing—longer waits for elective procedures, narrower formularies, and pressure to deny marginal treatments even for basic conditions. Countries that have adopted this model routinely experience queues for diagnostics and specialist access that employer plans in the United States largely avoid for working households.
[C1.3] The employer model also sustains competition among insurers and providers that drives incremental improvements in service delivery and technology adoption. A single government payer eliminates that rivalry, converting innovation decisions into political negotiations over annual budgets. Historical patterns show that concentrated public purchasing power tends to favor cost containment over rapid uptake of new therapies.
[C1.4] Finally, the claim that single-payer removes job lock ignores the new forms of lock-in it creates: political lock-in to whatever benefits the dominant coalition approves, geographic lock-in to approved facilities, and generational lock-in as unfunded liabilities grow. A system that conditions access on continuous employment still leaves open the option of changing jobs or starting a firm while carrying coverage; one financed entirely by taxation conditions access on the state’s willingness and ability to keep raising revenue without eroding the economy that produces it.

[C2.2] The claim that employer deductibles ration care the same way single-payer queues do ignores the difference in control. A household can select a lower-deductible plan during open enrollment or use health savings accounts to smooth costs. In contrast, single-payer systems impose uniform queues and formulary limits that no individual choice can override. For a working parent needing a routine specialist visit, the practical result is faster resolution under employer plans than under centralized capacity constraints.
[C2.3] Innovation pressure also reaches basic conditions. Competitive insurer networks and provider choice accelerate adoption of improved diagnostics, shorter recovery protocols, and outpatient alternatives that reduce both cost and disruption for routine problems. A single payer replaces those incentives with annual appropriations battles in which basic services compete against headline priorities rather than responding to patient demand.
[C2.4] Finally, the employment tie creates an explicit reciprocity that single-payer financing lacks. Workers who contribute through taxes and premiums maintain coverage tied to their participation in the economy. Removing that connection shifts access to residency status alone and invites political tests over who qualifies, how much is funded, and which regions receive facilities—precisely the new forms of lock-in that replace job mobility with dependence on state decisions. The employer model therefore preserves both access and accountability in ways the alternative severs.
