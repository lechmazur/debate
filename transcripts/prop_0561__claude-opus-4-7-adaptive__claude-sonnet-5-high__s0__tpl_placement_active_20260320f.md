[P1.1] The gig economy has quietly built a two-tier labor market. On one tier: workers with health insurance, sick leave, and a retirement match. On the other: a driver logging forty hours a week on an app, one illness away from financial collapse, told she is "her own boss" as a matter of legal fiction. Prorated, hours-based benefits are the cleanest fix to that gap, and here is why.
[P1.2] First, the equity argument. A nurse working thirty hours at a hospital gets partial benefits. A courier working thirty hours across DoorDash and Uber Eats gets none. The difference is not effort, skill, or economic contribution—it is a corporate classification choice.
[P1.3] When two workers do comparable hours of comparable work, the law should not let platform architecture erase basic protections. Prorating by hours is precisely the mechanism that neutralizes gaming: it doesn't force reclassification, it just attaches benefits to labor actually performed.
[P1.4] Second, the externality argument. When platforms don't fund health coverage or retirement, those costs don't vanish—they land on emergency rooms, Medicaid, and future public pension supplements. Taxpayers are currently subsidizing the business model. Requiring prorated contributions internalizes a cost the platforms already impose on the rest of us.
[P1.5] Third, feasibility. Platforms already track hours and earnings down to the minute; they already calculate surge pricing dynamically. Portable benefit accounts—where multiple platforms pay into one worker's pooled account based on hours—are administratively trivial compared to what these companies already do. Versions of this exist in parts of Europe and were piloted in Washington State for rideshare.
[P1.6] The technology is not the obstacle; the political will is.
[P1.7] The CON case will lean on two claims: it kills flexibility, and it kills the business model. Both are false. Prorating benefits does not require fixed schedules—benefits scale with whatever hours the worker chooses. And if a business is only viable because it offloads illness, aging, and injury onto workers and taxpayers, that is not efficiency.
[P1.8] That is a subsidy in disguise.

[C2.1] PRO's whole rebuttal hinges on "slopes don't get gamed." That's not how labor markets behave. A linear marginal cost still gives the platform a very simple profit-maximizing move: don't cut one driver off at a line—spread the same total hours across *more* drivers, each averaging fewer hours. If Uber needs 10,000 hours dispatched in a city tonight, prorated liability makes it cheaper, worker by worker, to source those hours from 500 people doing 20 hours each than 250 people doing 40.
[C2.2] No cliff required. That's not gaming an edge case; that's the ordinary logic of cost minimization applied to the entire workforce. The single-parent driver doesn't get "logged off at hour 20"—she gets fewer shifts offered to her from day one, because the algorithm is already optimizing dispatch to keep aggregate benefit exposure down. Proration doesn't eliminate the incentive to fragment hours; it generalizes it across the whole platform instead of concentrating it at one line.
[C2.3] On portable accounts: PRO's own opening cited exactly one example—a Washington State rideshare pilot. A single-state, single-industry pilot is not evidence that DoorDash, Instacart, Uber, and a dozen smaller apps can jointly build cross-platform hour-reporting, dispute resolution, and pooled contribution infrastructure. Who audits reported hours when platforms have opposing incentives to undercount? Who adjudicates when two platforms's records don't reconcile for the same worker?
[C2.4] PRO is asking the judge to grant the hardest, least-tested part of the proposal—the interoperability layer nobody has built at scale—as if it already exists.
[C2.5] And the deeper risk PRO never answers: if aggregate hours become expensive everywhere, platforms don't just ration—some contract total gig labor demand outright, pushing workers toward informal, unregulated work with zero benefits and zero transparency. That's a worse floor than today's status quo, not a better one.
