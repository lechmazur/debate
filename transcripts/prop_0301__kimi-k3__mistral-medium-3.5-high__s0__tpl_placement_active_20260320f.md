[P1.1] Every dollar a government spends carries conditions. Hospitals taking public funds must treat all patients. Universities taking research grants must follow oversight rules. Charter schools must meet standards or lose their charters.
[P1.2] The question before us is whether religious schools alone should be exempt from this ordinary logic of public accountability — and there is no good reason they should be.
[P1.3] This motion is deliberately moderate. It does not ban religious schools, does not secularize them, does not forbid prayer, theology, or religious identity. It says one thing: if you want the taxpayer's money, you accept the taxpayer's floor — sound curriculum, real civic preparation, and no discrimination. A Catholic school teaching catechism alongside rigorous biology keeps every cent.
[P1.4] A school teaching that evolution is fiction, or refusing admission to children of other faiths while cashing public checks, does not. That is not persecution; that is the price of the purse.
[P1.5] Notice the trap this sets for the opposition. They must defend one of two extremes. Either governments should fund religious schools unconditionally — meaning public money flowing to institutions free to discriminate in admissions and hiring, free to skip science, free to teach contempt for other citizens, with zero recourse for the people paying — or governments should never fund them at all, a far harsher position than ours that strips religious families of any path to support. We occupy the only defensible ground between blank checks and blanket exclusion.
[P1.6] Three principles carry the motion. First, accountability: public expenditure without conditions is not generosity, it is negligence. Second, the purpose of funding education at all is to form capable citizens; a state that funds schools indifferent to civic standards is paying to undermine its own reason for paying.
[P1.7] Third, genuine religious liberty: any school that finds the conditions intolerable may decline the money and operate with full independence. Choice is preserved on both sides — the state's and the school's.
[P1.8] Weigh this debate on who best reconciles pluralism, children's interests, and taxpayers' rights. Only conditional funding does all three.

[C1.5] Finally, the condition is a Trojan horse. “Comparable” civic and curricular standards will be set by secular officials, inevitably excluding religious viewpoints on origins, sexuality, and citizenship. This is not oversight; it is a purge of religion from publicly supported education, paid for by taxpayers who value faith. This is the opposite of pluralism; it is cultural hegemony in disguise.
