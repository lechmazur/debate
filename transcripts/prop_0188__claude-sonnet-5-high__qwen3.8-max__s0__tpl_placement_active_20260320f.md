[P1.1] The public pays for clinical trials and social-science research twice: once through taxes that fund the studies, and again through the distorted medical and policy decisions made when the results reaching journals aren't the results that actually came out of the lab. Preregistration and outcome-blind publication commitments fix a specific, well-documented failure mode: publication bias. This is not a hypothetical problem. Antidepressant trials are a textbook case — when regulatory filings were compared against published literature, the published record looked dramatically more favorable to the drugs than the full set of trials submitted to regulators actually showed.
[P1.2] Positive results get published; null and negative results get filed away. That asymmetry doesn't just embarrass researchers, it kills people, wastes money, and corrupts policy — because doctors prescribe, and legislators fund programs, based on a literature that has been quietly edited by outcome before anyone sees it.
[P1.3] Preregistration attacks this at the source. It requires researchers to state, before they see the data, what they are testing and how — so they can't quietly redefine "success" after the fact, a practice called p-hacking or HARKing (hypothesizing after results are known). Locking the analysis plan in advance doesn't prevent honest exploration; it just requires labeling it as exploratory rather than confirmatory, which is exactly the distinction a policymaker or physician needs to weigh evidence properly.
[P1.4] The publish-regardless-of-outcome commitment is the necessary second half. Preregistration without guaranteed publication just tells you which studies were run — it doesn't fix the bias unless the null results actually reach print. Registered Reports, where journals accept a study based on its design before results exist, have already demonstrated this works: they produce far higher rates of null-result publication than the standard literature, precisely because acceptance no longer depends on getting an exciting answer.
[P1.5] Since public money funds these studies, the public has a legitimate claim on seeing all of what that money bought — not a filtered, prettier subset. That is the case this debate should turn on.

[C1.1] The proposition identifies a real harm—publication bias—but responds with a mandate that is too broad, too rigid, and likely to make the evidence base worse. The central clash is simple: transparency is valuable only when it fits the kind of research being done.
[C1.2] Preregistration can work for confirmatory trials. It becomes counterproductive when imposed universally on publicly funded social science. Much of that work is exploratory: pilot studies, qualitative fieldwork, community-based design, early policy diagnostics. A researcher studying why a job-training program fails may need to revise interview guides, add comparison groups, or follow unexpected local factors.
[C1.3] Forcing a fixed hypothesis before seeing any data either freezes inquiry prematurely or produces registries full of vague, defensive language written to satisfy auditors rather than inform readers. The result is bureaucratic compliance, not genuine openness.
[C1.4] Clinical research raises the same problem in sharper form. Adaptive trials, safety monitoring, and emergency research often require protocol changes. Mandatory preregistration with a promise of publication regardless of outcome can slow revision, encourage defensive science, and reward paperwork over patient welfare. The proper answer is proportionate oversight, not a single procedural straitjacket for every funded study.
[C1.5] The second mandate is even more dangerous. Journals should not be required to publish preregistered studies regardless of outcome. Outcome-blind review can be useful in narrow settings, but guaranteed publication severs the link between publication and scientific value. A preregistered study can still be underpowered, badly measured, ethically compromised, or rendered obsolete by events.
[C1.6] Null findings from flawed designs do not clarify public policy; they clutter the record. Journals have limited space and editorial attention. Flooding them with low-value nulls buries the robust results clinicians and policymakers actually need.
[C1.7] The public deserves transparency, but not this blunt instrument. Require registration for clinical trials, encourage preregistration for confirmatory analyses, share data, and use registered reports where design is stable. Do not mandate universal preregistration and guarantee publication for every publicly funded study.

[P2.2] Take exploratory work. [P1.3] already answers this: preregistration doesn't ban exploration, it just requires labeling exploration as exploration. A researcher studying a failing job-training program can preregister the initial design and comparison groups, then openly report follow-up interviews or added variables as exploratory findings. That's not bureaucratic compliance — that's precisely the distinction a policymaker needs to know whether a finding was predicted in advance or discovered by rummaging through the data until something looked significant.
[P2.3] CON never explains why labeling honesty as "exploratory" is worse than the status quo, where exploratory findings get published dressed up as confirmatory ones.
[P2.4] On adaptive clinical trials: protocol amendments are already standard practice in registered trials. Preregistration doesn't forbid revision — it requires that revisions be logged and dated, so reviewers can see what was planned versus what was changed after seeing interim results. That's not a straitjacket, it's an audit trail, and it's the opposite of "rewarding paperwork over patient welfare" — it protects patients from trials quietly redefined after an unflattering interim look.
[P2.5] CON's strongest-sounding point is [C1.6]: that guaranteed publication floods journals with low-value nulls. But this concedes too much to a false premise — that outcome-blind means quality-blind. It doesn't. Registered Reports review happens at the design stage, before results exist, precisely so editors can reject underpowered or poorly measured studies on their merits, with no outcome to bias that judgment.
[P2.6] What gets guaranteed publication is good design, not good news. CON's own preferred alternative — "registered reports where design is stable" — is just PRO's mechanism with an arbitrary scope limit nobody has justified.
