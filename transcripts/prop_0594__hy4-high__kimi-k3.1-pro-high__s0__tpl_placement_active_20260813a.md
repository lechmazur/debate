[P1.1] A crisis does not make a border harmless; it makes it more dangerous. A staple-food export ban looks like a safety valve for the minister imposing it, but inside the AfCFTA it vents pressure into neighbouring markets. Import-dependent countries find signed market access vanishing exactly when maize, rice, or other staples are most needed. Traders stop quoting, millers hold stocks, consumers face panic prices, and other governments retaliate with bans.
[P1.2] A national measure becomes a continental run on trust.
[P1.3] Enforceable limits solve that coordination problem. Each capital has an incentive to close the border because much of the cost falls on foreigners. Yet every state is safer under a credible promise that others will not. AfCFTA rules should make staple bans presumptively unlawful, require notification, cap duration and scope, protect existing contracts, and expose violations to review and remedies.
[P1.4] Without consequences, restraint is only a press release.
[P1.5] The rule limits emergency action; it does not abolish it. A state facing genuine physical shortage could take a temporary, targeted measure and submit it to review. It should not retain discretion to ban staple exports merely to calm prices. That discretion rewards panic, invites retaliation, and tells farmers regional demand can disappear overnight.
[P1.6] The sovereignty objection confuses autonomy with resilience. Subsidies, public stocks, cash support, and shared stock information can protect hungry households without pushing scarcity across a border. A ban creates no extra sack of grain; it redirects fear. It can depress farm-gate returns, invite smuggling, and make deficit countries pay the highest price for another state's panic.
[P1.7] Continent-wide enforceability makes the AfCFTA credible when tested. Voluntary restraint fails precisely when panic is strongest; binding rules keep food moving and contracts honoured through the shock. The choice is between a scramble for the same short queue and governed exceptions that keep trade alive. In a food crisis, predictable access can keep a bad harvest from becoming a continental shortage.
