[P1.1] A lease is not just four walls; it is a promise of a home’s ordinary use. When a landlord markets a unit as a quiet residence while knowing the building generates recurring short-term-rental party complaints, severe noise, or chronic package theft, the landlord sells one thing and delivers another. Tenants cannot discover this before signing: visits are staged, neighbors are strangers, reviews rarely reveal a pattern. The landlord alone holds the complaint logs, police calls, and mailroom incident reports.
[P1.2] That asymmetry is the case: one party knows a fact that destroys the value of what the other is buying.
[P1.3] The harms are concrete. A night-shift nurse above a revolving door of short-term rental parties loses sleep, health, and use of her home. Chronic package theft means paying for a locker or replacing stolen medication and work equipment. Because moving is costly and leases bind, tenants absorb these losses while the landlord collects full rent.
[P1.4] Disclosure need not force silence; it forces truth into price and choice. The landlord can disclose and lower rent, restrict short-term rentals, secure the mailroom, or lose tenants to honest competitors.
[P1.5] The opponent will call this vague or unfair. It is not. The motion covers recurring, severe, chronic patterns, not one noisy weekend or an unproven grudge. These are documented, aggregate facts, not accusations against individuals.
[P1.6] Landlords must disclose bedbugs, mold, and lead paint in many places; extending that duty to known nuisance patterns is consistent, not radical. A landlord who truly does not know can say “no known recurring problem after reasonable inquiry.”
[P1.7] The real clash is not “guarantee silence” versus “tenant beware.” It is whether a seller of housing may conceal known, material defects. Consent to a lease is meaningful only when the tenant knows what they are signing. Because the landlord is the cheaper cost-avoider and the only party who can prevent or price these risks, disclosure is the minimal, decisive fix.

There is no threshold, no time window, no causation rule, and no distinction between a documented condition and an unverified complaint. That is not a drafting quibble; it is the entire problem. A landlord with two angry emails about a birthday party and one missing parcel must guess whether he now owes every applicant a warning.
[C1.2] Guess wrong and he faces liability. Guess defensively and tenants drown in meaningless boilerplate. Either way, the information gets worse.
[C1.3] The predictable response is not transparency. It is to stop creating the records. Managers will handle noise orally, tell tenants to call police directly, and avoid written complaint logs that could become disclosure exhibits. Package theft reports will be pushed to carriers.
[C1.4] The mandate therefore destroys the evidence that could actually help tenants, regulators, and good landlords.
[C1.5] These categories also mislead. A noise complaint can be retaliatory or reflect one tenant’s unusual sensitivity. A package theft can occur on a public sidewalk. A short-term-rental complaint can concern one bad weekend, not a building’s character.
[C1.6] Labeling such things “severe” or “chronic” without standards turns anecdotes into stigma, and a checkbox warning cannot tell a tenant whether the problem is a barking dog, a nightclub, or a personal feud. Tenants deserve a reliable picture, not a legally compelled rumor summary.
[C1.7] Existing law already provides remedies for quiet enjoyment, habitability, security, and fraudulent concealment. If a landlord lies about a material condition, that is actionable. The better fix is to enforce lease promises and punish misrepresentation, not to require unverifiable summaries that create litigation risk and encourage less honest record-keeping. The proposition promises precision but delivers guesswork, defensive silence, and warning labels that let a landlord say “you were told.” Vote CON.
