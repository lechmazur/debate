[P1.1] A personal phone is not a neutral tool. It holds bank logins, medical messages, photos of children, location history, dating apps—the most intimate record most people own. When a large employer makes installing its software on that device a routine condition of getting or keeping a job, it isn't asking for cooperation. It's leveraging a paycheck to claim a foothold inside an employee's private life.
[P1.2] That is the heart of this debate, and three things make it indefensible as a default practice.
[P1.3] First, consent under economic pressure isn't consent. "You can decline—and also be fired" is the logic of a shakedown, not an agreement. Large employers hold overwhelming bargaining power over rank-and-file workers; calling mandatory installation a "choice" launders coercion as voluntariness.
[P1.4] Second, the intrusion is real and asymmetric. Work apps frequently arrive bundled with mobile device management profiles that can read device inventories, track location, enforce remote wipes, and—through error or policy—erase personal data along with corporate files. Even a "mere" messaging app erodes the boundary between work and life: notifications at dinner, the expectation of off-hours responses, the quiet conversion of personal time into unpaid availability. Workers bear these costs while employers bear none.
[P1.5] They also subsidize the employer materially—data plans, battery, storage, and a device they bought themselves.
[P1.6] Third—and this is decisive—the alternative is trivially cheap. The motion specifies large employers. A company with thousands of employees can issue work phones, offer stipends for genuinely voluntary opt-in, or rely on web access from any browser. When a low-cost option exists that fully protects the employer's legitimate interests, insisting on colonizing personal devices anyway reveals the demand for what it is: convenience and control purchased with someone else's privacy.
[P1.7] The proposition doesn't ban voluntary arrangements or rare exceptional cases. It rejects one thing: making intrusion routine. To win, my opponent must defend coercion as a default when alternatives sit on the shelf. That's a losing brief.
