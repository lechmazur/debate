[P1.1] For decades, opponents of LGBTQ+ families have relied on intuition and ideology rather than data. But the empirical record is now clear: when researchers compare children raised by same-sex parents with those raised by different-sex parents, the differences in psychological wellbeing, social adjustment, academic performance, and identity development are negligible. What predicts a child's flourishing is not the gender of their parents but the stability, warmth, and economic security of their home.
[P1.2] Critics often cite studies showing children fare worse in single-parent households, then conflate that with same-sex parenting. This is a category error. Those studies overwhelmingly compare married different-sex couples against single or divorced parents—not against stable same-sex two-parent families. When researchers control for family stability, parental conflict, and socioeconomic resources, the apparent disadvantages attributed to having gay or lesbian parents vanish.
[P1.3] Major pediatric and psychological associations have reviewed this literature and concluded that children's adjustment depends on family processes and relationships, not the gender composition of their parents.
[P1.4] This evidence demands a specific policy response. Public policy—from marriage laws to adoption regulations to tax benefits—should not discriminate against families that produce identical outcomes for children. Denying same-sex couples the right to marry or adopt does not protect children; it deprives them of legal protections, inheritance rights, and stability while denying qualified, loving parents the ability to care for them. When the state restricts family formation based on sexual orientation, it actively harms the very children it claims to protect by reducing their pool of stable, supported homes.
[P1.5] We are not debating whether some children have it harder than others. We are debating whether the state should withhold rights from a class of parents when the evidence shows their children thrive just the same. The answer is no. Policy must follow the evidence, and the evidence says these families are equal.
