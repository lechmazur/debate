[P1.1] When a plant closes in a town of ten thousand, the laid-off machinist stops eating out, the diner cuts a waitress, and the waitress misses her car payment. That chain is not a moral failure; it is arithmetic. Automatic cash transfers break the chain at its first link, while the shock is still local and reversible.
[P1.2] The decisive word in the motion is *automatic*. Discretionary relief arrives late and arrives political: legislatures argue over eligibility while layoffs compound, and support often peaks after the trough has passed. A rule tied to the unemployment rate needs no vote, no lobbying, no heroic forecasting. It expands when the trigger fires and contracts when the trigger clears.
[P1.3] That is not a blank cheque; it is a thermostat. The alternative is waiting for a committee to agree that the room is cold.
[P1.4] On debt, notice what the opposition must claim: that borrowing is affordable in booms and intolerable in slumps. Deficits in a downturn happen regardless — revenues collapse, benefit rolls swell. The real choice is whether that borrowing buys households a floor or buys nothing at all. Cutting transfers to protect a debt ratio can be self-defeating: shrink incomes, shrink the tax base, shrink the denominator of the very ratio you were defending.
[P1.5] Post-2010 Europe ran that experiment, and the countries that tightened fastest spent years rediscovering demand they had legislated away.
[P1.6] And the costs of waiting are not temporary. Missed rent becomes eviction. A year out of work becomes a permanently lower wage. A child's disrupted schooling is not refunded when growth returns.
[P1.7] Debt raised in a slump is repaid over decades; a destroyed career is not repaid at all. We should be willing to borrow against the future we are trying to preserve.
