[C1.1] The demand to expose the "full fare split" assumes a fiction: that ride-hailing economics reduce to a simple two-way division between driver and platform. In reality, modern pricing is a dynamic bundle of temporary subsidies, demand forecasts, insurance reserves, and promotional discounts that shift second-by-second. Forcing platforms to display a static "split" would present drivers and riders with a misleading snapshot that obscures more than it reveals.
[C1.2] More dangerously, this transparency would weaponize information against service quality. When drivers see a large platform share during surge pricing, they face perverse incentives to cancel rides or cherry-pick trips based on resentment rather than efficiency. Riders, suddenly aware of driver compensation, would face awkward social pressure to tip or negotiate mid-ride, eroding the frictionless transaction that makes the service viable. The platform mediates risk and liquidity; turning every trip into a visible negotiation over surplus destroys the very coordination that allows strangers to share space without friction.
[C1.3] Privacy concerns compound the damage. Riders have no legitimate need to know a specific driver's exact earnings—a figure that varies wildly based on incentives, quests, and vehicle costs invisible to the passenger. Conversely, drivers seeing the rider's exact payment introduces resentment dynamics that currently don't exist in the simple exchange of service for agreed-upon compensation.
[C1.4] Markets function through final prices, not cost-plus accounting. We do not demand grocery stores itemize their markup on milk or force freelancers to reveal their hourly rate to clients. Ride-hailing succeeds because it abstracts complexity into a simple yes-or-no decision for both parties. Mandating fare-split transparency would replace this efficient coordination with confusion, conflict, and chronic second-guessing.
