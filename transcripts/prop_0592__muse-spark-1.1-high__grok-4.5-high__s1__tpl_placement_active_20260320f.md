[C1.1] Replacing unanimous voting on foreign-policy sanctions with qualified majority would trade durable unity for theatrical speed, and that bargain weakens Europe against aggressors. Sanctions work only when every capital owns the costs and the enforcement. Unanimity compels the political labour of real consensus; QMV lets a large coalition impose packages whose pain falls unevenly, producing quiet non-compliance, watering-down exemptions, and eventual unraveling.
[C1.2] Member states differ sharply in energy exposure, trade links, diaspora interests and security geography. A decision that barely registers in one capital can crush jobs and revenues in another. Unanimity does not hand aggressors a free veto; it ensures that governments answerable to their own voters retain a final say when the common instrument will hit those voters. Removing that say does not create resolve.
[C1.3] It creates a majority that can decree, and minorities that then drag their feet on implementation or seek side exemptions that leak value to the target.
[C1.4] The claim that speed alone deters is false. Packages delayed by negotiation still froze assets and closed markets once sealed, because the delay bought the political ownership required for sustained application. A rushed QMV list travels with lower legitimacy: banks and ministries in the minority of states face domestic pressure to interpret it narrowly, intelligence about assets travels slower, and the aggressor simply concentrates pressure on the dissenting capitals knowing the common front is coercive rather than chosen. Over time that produces the very single points of failure the reform pretends to eliminate—only now they appear after the vote, not before it.
[C1.5] Foreign policy remains a domain of national competence pooled by consent, not majoritarian arithmetic. Substituting QMV for unanimity on sanctions does not forge a geopolitical actor; it manufactures a loud declaration followed by uneven delivery. Aggressors read that delivery, not the press release.
