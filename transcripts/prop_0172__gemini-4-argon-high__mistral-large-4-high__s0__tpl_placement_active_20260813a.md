[P1.1] When human neural cells in a dish are wired to microelectrode arrays and learn to play a simple game like Pong, they are no longer passive tissue cultures. To learn, a biological network must process feedback, distinguish between states to seek and states to avoid, and reorganize its synaptic architecture in response. Yet under current rules, these learning brain organoids are governed like skin biopsies: subject to donor consent and basic biosafety, with zero oversight over what the living network undergoes during experimentation.
[P1.2] Crossing the threshold into rudimentary learning must trigger the ethical review frameworks applied to animal research, grounded in the Three Rs: Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement.
[P1.3] First, rudimentary learning marks the functional threshold where moral precaution becomes mandatory. In animal ethics, we do not wait for philosophical proof of consciousness before regulating research on rodents, zebrafish, or cephalopods. Instead, we trigger oversight when neurobiological organization supports adaptive, goal-directed behavior in response to feedback. Biological learning requires valenced processing, reinforcing certain pathways while penalizing others through disruptive stimulation.
[P1.4] Because this functional architecture is the precursor to nociception and distress, treating a learning neural network as inert tissue is an indefensible blind spot. It is perversely inconsistent to require strict ethical justification before testing on a mouse, while allowing unregulated, indefinite aversive stimulation of human cortical tissue demonstrating adaptive learning.
[P1.5] Second, animal review frameworks are uniquely built to govern without paralyzing science. Ethical review is not a ban; animal oversight committees are designed for proportionate, tiered evaluation. Triggering this framework simply requires researchers to justify why a living, learning organoid is necessary over a computer simulation (Replacement), minimize the number of organoids used (Reduction), and design stimulation protocols and endpoints that minimize potential neural distress (Refinement).
[P1.6] Waiting until organoids exhibit undeniable suffering means regulating too late. Triggering animal review frameworks at the proof-of-concept learning stage establishes workable accountability the moment inert tissue becomes an adaptive biological agent.

[C1.1] The PRO case rests on a fundamental category error: mistaking biological computation for moral status. When an organoid learns to play Pong, it is adjusting synaptic weights to minimize prediction error—not a creature pursuing goals, avoiding harm, or experiencing valenced states. Animal research frameworks exist because animals possess nociceptors, integrated nervous systems, and the capacity for suffering. A cortical organoid lacks pain receptors, peripheral innervation, and the thalamocortical loops necessary for conscious experience.
[C1.2] It cannot feel distress; it can only process electrical signals.
[C1.3] Applying IACUC-style review—built around anesthesia protocols, humane endpoints, and pain mitigation—to tissue that cannot experience pain is regulatory incoherence, not precaution. These frameworks ask committees to weigh animal suffering against scientific benefit. With organoids, there is no suffering to weigh, only computational complexity. Triggering animal oversight at the first sign of adaptive behavior imposes the wrong metric: regulating statistical pattern-matching in a dish as if it were a sentient mouse navigating a maze.
[C1.4] This overreach threatens genuine progress. Organoid research offers hope for Alzheimer's, ALS, and neurodevelopmental disorders. Subjecting every learning-capable organoid to full animal protocol review would bottleneck discovery, multiply costs, and delay therapies—without protecting a single sentient being. The Three Rs presuppose creatures with welfare interests capable of being reduced and refined.
[C1.5] The proportionate path is a specialized organoid ethics framework: rigorous donor consent, monitoring for neural integration markers, and chimerism safeguards. We should regulate by capacity for suffering, not by capacity for computation. Equating the two doesn't elevate organoids; it dilutes the ethical frameworks that actually protect animals.
