[P1.1] Ride-hail platforms made traffic worse because they increased the number of cars competing for the same scarce street space in the places and at the times congestion is worst. A ride-hail trip is not a one-car replacement for a private car. It is usually a car that arrives empty, cruises or idles while waiting, and then leaves empty to reposition. That empty running—deadheading—adds vehicle miles that a parked private car or a taxi at a stand never would.
[P1.2] In dense downtowns from San Francisco to New York, those empty miles are not harmless. They are cars stopping in travel lanes, blocking bus and bike lanes, and making U-turns at peak hour, so a single pickup can stall a busload of people and slow everyone behind it.
[P1.3] The second reason is who ride-hail pulls onto the road. Its biggest growth came not from converting solo drivers, but from replacing transit, walking, biking, and trips that would not have happened at all. Moving a rider from a subway car, a bus, or a sidewalk into a private sedan substitutes a low-capacity vehicle for a high-capacity or zero-car trip, which raises road space per passenger and total vehicle travel. Convenience and cheap early fares also induced new trips and longer trips, the same induced-demand effect that makes added road capacity fill up again.
[P1.4] The best case for ride-hail—that it replaces car trips and cuts parking search—cannot carry the overall verdict. In major cities, private car trips into the core were already limited by cost and congestion, and when a ride-hail car replaces a personal car it still adds pickup and deadhead mileage. Most importantly, the trips being converted are disproportionately the sustainable ones. Even if some ride-hail travel removes a car, the deadheading plus the shift from transit, bikes, and walking means more total vehicle travel and slower streets.
[P1.5] That is why congestion got worse overall.

[C1.2] Consider what disappears when ride-hail exists. Personal car trips into dense cores involve not just driving but parking—and parking search in cities like San Francisco or Boston consumes a significant share of downtown traffic. Ride-hail vehicles don't park. They drop and move.
[C1.3] A ride-hail trip replacing a personal car trip eliminates the parking search entirely, and that search is precisely the low-speed circling that clogs streets at peak.
[C1.4] PRO's deadheading argument counts the empty miles ride-hail adds but ignores the empty miles it removes. A personal car making a round trip parks at both ends; a ride-hail vehicle can serve multiple passengers across the same window. The relevant comparison is total vehicle-miles per passenger-trip, and ride-hail's pooling, high utilization, and elimination of parking search can push that figure below the personal-car baseline for many trips.
[C1.5] Then there's the attribution problem. Major cities grew denser, richer, and more active during the same period ride-hail expanded. Freight deliveries surged. Construction multiplied.
[C1.6] Tourism rebounded. Population rose. Isolating ride-hail as the cause of worsening congestion requires controlling for all of this, and the commonly cited studies don't—they identify correlations and assume causation. A city that added jobs and population for a decade would see worse congestion with or without Uber.
[C1.7] Finally, ride-hail is associated with reduced car ownership in dense cities. Fewer households buying and storing personal vehicles means fewer cars competing for street space over the long run. The proposition treats each ride-hail trip as additive, but the systemic effect—fewer personal cars, less parking demand, higher vehicle utilization—pushes the other way.
[C1.8] The claim isn't that ride-hail reduced congestion. It's that the overall case for *worse* doesn't survive the counterfactual.
