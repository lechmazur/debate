[P1.1] Public health is decided by defaults, not disclaimers. When an individual reaches for a loaf of bread, a frozen meal, or pasta sauce, the primary determinant of their cardiovascular and metabolic risk is what the manufacturer put inside the product, not the warnings printed on the packaging.
[P1.2] The fundamental flaw of labeling and education campaigns is that they treat a systemic supply problem as an individual information deficit. Ultra-processed foods are hyper-palatable formulations engineered to override human satiety mechanisms. Asking consumers to overcome these biological triggers through sheer willpower and nutritional literacy places an exhausting cognitive tax on daily life. Even aggressive front-of-package warnings suffer from decision fatigue and visual saturation; over time, consumers simply tune them out.
[P1.3] Mandates succeed precisely because they remove the burden of constant vigilance. A legal cap on sodium, added sugar, or industrial fats alters the baseline choice architecture. [P1.4] Furthermore, education and labeling systematically widen health disparities. Affluent consumers possess the financial and cognitive bandwidth to act on dietary guidance and purchase fresh, whole-food alternatives. Reformulation mandates ensure that the cheapest, most accessible calorie on the shelf is fundamentally less toxic.
[P1.5] We did not eliminate industrial trans fats by distributing pamphlets; we removed them from the supply chain. Changing the food supply itself will always outperform asking individuals to defend themselves against it.

[C1.1] The proposition gets the mechanism wrong. Ultra-processed food harms health through more than salt, sugar, and fat. The industrial processing itself—rapidly digested food matrices, emulsifiers, intense flavor engineering, and displacement of fiber and protein—contributes to overconsumption and metabolic harm. A bread with somewhat less sodium is still an ultra-processed bread.
[C1.2] Caps on three nutrients therefore leave the core problem in place while implying the product has been made safer.
[C1.3] Worse, reformulation is easily captured. When sugar is capped, manufacturers often replace it with non-nutritive sweeteners, starches, or bulking agents. When fat is reduced, they add refined carbohydrates or emulsifiers. When salt is lowered, they may add glutamate-like flavor enhancers.
[C1.4] The result can carry a “reduced” health halo and be consumed more confidently. That is not population health; that is marketing through regulation.
[C1.5] The trans fat analogy proves too little. Trans fats had a clear causal role and replaceable function; salt, sugar, and fat are pervasive, technically necessary across thousands of food matrices, and subject to substitution effects. Caps generate industry resistance, long phase-ins, and unpredictable downstream changes, while labeling and education can shift demand and create market pressure for genuinely less harmful foods.
[C1.6] Labeling is also not merely an information drip. Well-designed front-of-pack warnings and sustained education can change purchasing norms, reduce sales of the worst products, and push companies to reformulate voluntarily, without brittle one-size-fits-all caps. The motion claims mandates would beat “any” labeling or education campaign. That ignores how much consumer pressure drives real reformulation.
[C1.7] Because the mandate strategy treats ultra-processed food as a nutrient arithmetic problem, it can produce a slightly different product, not a different diet. Population health comes from replacing these products, not polishing them.
