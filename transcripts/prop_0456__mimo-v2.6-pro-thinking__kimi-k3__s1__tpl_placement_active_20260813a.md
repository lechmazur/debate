[P1.1] Two bad worlds sit on either side of a compulsory license, and CON has to defend one of them.
[P1.2] Take permission first. If training requires a deal with every rights holder, catalog giants sit at the chokepoint. The majors already own the film libraries, the sound recordings, the publishing backlists — and they have their own AI ambitions. A permission regime is not a victory for creators; it is a moat for incumbents, who will license to themselves and withhold from rivals, and a death sentence for the small studio or startup that cannot clear a hundred thousand contracts.
[P1.3] Holdouts and transaction costs do not protect authors. They protect whoever is big enough to afford lawyers.
[P1.4] Now take the free-use world, where training needs no permission and pays nothing. Every poem, every session recording, every back catalog then becomes unpaid input into a model that earns money downstream. That is a transfer from the people who made the works to whoever owns the compute. "Exposure" does not pay rent.
[P1.5] The compulsory license is the narrow middle, and we already know it works. Music's mechanical license has operated on this exact logic for generations: anyone may record and release a cover of a published song without the writer's consent, provided the statutory rate is paid. Nobody calls that a catastrophe. It produced a world with more covers, more distribution, and a functioning royalty stream.
[P1.6] Training is the same species of use — analytic, non-expressive, generative of new works — and it deserves the same treatment: access guaranteed, payment mandatory.
[P1.7] The word "only" is doing real work here. It converts permissionless training from a subsidy into a bargain: society keeps the innovation, creators keep a claim on the value their work adds. Collective schemes handle valuation administratively rather than contract by contract.
[P1.8] CON must therefore defend either a veto regime that entrenches incumbents or a free regime that confiscates labor. Neither survives contact with the interests of the people who actually make the works.

Copyright guards expression, not the unprotectable patterns — style, structure, statistical regularity — that any reader, human or machine, carries away from a work.
[C1.3] Training extracts those patterns; a trained model stores weights, not the catalog, and its outputs are new works, not copies. PRO's scheme therefore invents a right copyright never contained, then prices it. That is not a compromise between two bad worlds. It is an expansion of copyright dressed as one.
[C1.4] And the mechanism cannot function. Compulsory licenses work where uses are countable. PRO's own example proves the point: a cover recording uses one identifiable song in one identifiable record, so the statutory rate has a denominator. Training has none.
[C1.5] Billions of works go in; no output traces to any of them. [C1.6] Follow the money either way. Collective schemes distribute by catalog size, because they must — there is no other metric. The majors who own the backlists collect the pool; the working author receives a check that would not cover postage. PRO warns that a permission regime protects "whoever is big enough to afford lawyers."
[C1.7] A compulsory scheme protects whoever is big enough to own catalogs, with a royalty office stamped on top.
[C1.8] Finally, "only if" is the tell. The motion permits nothing; it bans permissionless training unless a fee no one can rationally set is paid. A lawful, productive practice becomes gated by bureaucracy. That is not a bargain for creators or for society.

The novelist reading a thousand novels is a private person; the firm renting a datacenter to ingest a million works and sell access to the resulting model is running a commercial operation at industrial scale. Copyright already draws that line: a shopkeeper may sing a song freely, but a shop playing it over speakers pays a performance license. Commercial scale and commercial purpose are exactly what trigger the obligation.
[P2.2] The mechanism objection collapses fastest. [C1.4] insists training has "no denominator," so rates cannot be set. But collective licensing has priced denominator-free uses for decades. Blanket performance licenses cover every song a station might play, with the pool allocated by airplay sampling and other proxies — not by tracing which song moved which listener to buy anything.
[P2.3] Reprographic and private-copying levies work the same way. "No per-use trace" is the normal condition of collective rights management, not a refutation of it. CON describes ordinary administrative difficulty and calls it impossibility.
[P2.4] On distribution, [C1.6] says the money reaches catalog owners while the working author gets a check that "would not cover postage." First, allocation is a design choice inside the scheme: songwriter shares are already paid directly to writers by collecting societies rather than funneled through publishers, and a compulsory license can reserve a statutory creator share the same way.
[P2.5] Second, weigh the worlds. Permissionless training pays the working author zero and leaves majors free to train their own models on the same catalog. Permission lets those majors set the terms. The compulsory pool is the only world in which payment to creators is legally owed at all.
[P2.6] A floor beats a zero.
[P2.7] Finally, [C1.8] misreads "only if." The motion bans nothing but *uncompensated* training. Direct licensing stays fully available for anyone who wants a bespoke deal, and permissioned access never required my scheme's permission. What disappears is the free lunch CON is defending and calling freedom.
