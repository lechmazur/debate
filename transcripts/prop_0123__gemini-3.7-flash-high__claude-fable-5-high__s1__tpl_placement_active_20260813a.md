[P1.1] Modern supermarkets are engineered to mislead. Ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks are designed to be hyper-palatable, packaged with deceptive health halos, and backed by billions in marketing. Relying mainly on consumer choice campaigns asks individuals to wage a daily, solitary battle against industrial food chemistry using only vague educational pamphlets and microscopic nutrition tables. That approach has failed; the global surge in diet-related chronic illnesses—like type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease—proves it.
[P1.2] Prominent warning labels fundamentally fix this broken information ecosystem in two decisive ways.
[P1.3] First, they correct a profound information asymmetry at the exact point of decision. When a shopper has mere seconds to choose between products, a bold, front-of-package warning—such as clear symbols indicating high sugar, sodium, or saturated fat—cuts through deceptive marketing instantly. A cereal boasting "high fiber" can no longer hide that it is 40 percent sugar. Warning labels do not restrict freedom of choice; they provide the clear, unvarnished reality required for genuine choice to exist.
[P1.4] Education campaigns cannot compete with multi-million-dollar advertising budgets, but a mandatory label on the product itself cannot be outspent or ignored.
[P1.5] Second, warning labels drive structural reformulation. When governments mandate prominent warnings, manufacturers face an immediate commercial incentive: reformulate their recipes to reduce harmful ingredients and avoid the warning label entirely. Voluntary guidelines and public awareness campaigns produce negligible industry change, but clear warning systems—as demonstrated in countries pioneering front-of-package regulations—prompt companies to cut sugar, salt, and industrial additives across entire product lines.
[P1.6] The choice is clear. We can continue relying on toothless educational campaigns that privatize the blame for a public health crisis, or we can implement prominent warning labels that empower consumers, hold manufacturers accountable, and protect public health at scale.

[C1.4] Contrast this with what education actually builds: durable food literacy that travels with a person to restaurants, school cafeterias, and delivery apps—everywhere labels don't exist. Diet-related disease is driven by overall dietary patterns, portion habits, and food environments, not by any single stigmatized package. A label freezes judgment onto one product at one moment; literacy equips judgment for a lifetime of decisions.
[C1.5] Labels offer governments a cheap moral victory. They do not offer the public a healthier diet—and this debate should turn on which one we actually want.
