[P1.1] A click-through consent form is not a waiver of mental privacy. Consumer neurotechnology does not merely collect clicks or location; it infers attention, emotion, fatigue, stress, and intent from signals users generate simply by thinking, sleeping, meditating, or wearing a device. Those inferences can be wrong, opaque, and far more intimate than anything a person chooses to disclose. Once they are fed into advertising, personalization, or pricing, the result is not neutral convenience: it is manipulation aimed at the mind itself.
[P1.2] The core problem is asymmetry. The company controls the sensors, models, and incentives; the user receives a long terms-of-service prompt at setup, often when the device is useless unless accepted. Standard consent assumes people can understand the bargain, compare alternatives, and withdraw without losing essential services. None of that holds for neural data.
[P1.3] A user may agree to “improve experience” without realizing the same signal can later predict emotional vulnerability, political attention, or purchase impulse. Prohibiting these uses creates a bright line that prevents consent from becoming a blank check for mental profiling.
[P1.4] This rule still leaves room for innovation. Companies may build neurotechnology for health, accessibility, safety, and user-controlled features, provided they are not turning brain signals into ad targeting or price discrimination. What we forbid is the most dangerous business model: selling access to a person’s inner state to advertisers or pricing algorithms. Imagine a sleep or focus device detecting fatigue and frustration, then serving a high-margin offer when resistance is lowest, or charging more because the user appears anxious and less likely to comparison shop.
[P1.5] That is not ordinary personalization; it is exploitation of cognitive weakness.
[P1.6] Some interests are too basic to be waived by a click. The proposition protects cognitive liberty where market pressure alone will not, and it preserves public trust without which neurotechnology will face needless backlash.
