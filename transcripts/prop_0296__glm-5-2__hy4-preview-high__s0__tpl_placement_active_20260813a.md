[P1.1] Public naming is an act of honor, not an act of memory. That distinction carries this debate. A street sign does not teach history—it confers recognition. A holiday does not preserve memory—it allocates collective celebration.
[P1.2] When a government names a school after a slaveholder, it is not recording that someone existed; it is telling children, including descendants of enslaved people, that this person deserves their admiration. That is a present-tense choice with present-tense consequences, not a passive inheritance.
[P1.3] Historical continuity, as the opposition frames it, conflates two different things. History lives in textbooks, museums, curricula, and public scholarship. Those are where we preserve the full record—including the ugly parts. But honor lives in names.
[P1.4] Removing a name from a building removes nothing from the historical record; it removes an endorsement. A dictator's crimes are fully documented in libraries. His name on a town square does something libraries cannot: it tells citizens "this community reveres you." Renaming does not erase; it stops praising.
[P1.5] Consider post-apartheid South Africa, which renamed streets honoring architects of racial domination. Nobody forgot apartheid because a road changed its name. But Black citizens stopped being forced to conduct daily life under the names of their oppressors. The benefit was real and immediate; the historical cost was zero.
[P1.6] The counterargument always retreats to a slippery slope: if we rename everything, where do we stop? But the proposition is already bounded. It targets names that "primarily honor colonizers, slaveholders, or dictators." These are not complicated judgment calls about flawed founders with mixed legacies.
[P1.7] They are people whose defining contribution was conquest, ownership of humans, or authoritarian rule. The criterion is clear, and so is the obligation.
[P1.8] Governments that keep such names force marginalized communities to pay tribute to their oppressors daily. Renaming is not erasure—it is the minimum threshold of civic respect.
