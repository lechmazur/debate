[P1.1] When politicians can ignore a citizens' assembly, they will — whenever its answer threatens donors, party lines, or the next headline. That is why sortition must come with binding power on the specific question it was convened to answer. Otherwise we get democratic theater: ordinary people deliberate seriously, then the same insiders who created the deadlock decide anyway.
[P1.2] A randomly selected, stratified assembly looks like the country in a way no parliament does. It includes carers, renters, farmers, teenagers' parents, pensioners, and people who never join parties or can afford to campaign. Election systematically filters for wealth, incumbency, media polish, and party loyalty; sortition filters for none of them. If legitimacy means rule by a representative public rather than a political class, a mini-public that mirrors the population has a stronger claim to settle a defined question than legislators elected on a bundled manifesto years earlier.
[P1.3] It also decides better. Members hear competing experts, question them, deliberate over weeks, and must justify trade-offs to peers unlike themselves. That process suits specific, value-laden, long-term problems — abortion law, assisted dying, climate measures, electoral rules — where elected politicians are trapped by whips, lobbying, and electoral cycles. A voter in a referendum gets slogans; a legislator gets a party line; an assembly member gets time, evidence, and responsibility, and opinion can move because learning is the point.
[P1.4] Binding does not mean unbounded. The mandate would be narrow, time-limited, and pre-committed: parliament frames one question, courts still enforce rights, deliberations and evidence are public, and adoption could require a clear supermajority inside the assembly. Those guardrails answer fears of mob rule far better than the status quo.
[P1.5] Advisory-only keeps the veto with the actors least able to be impartial — especially on electoral reform or climate costs, where they have direct self-interest. Give the assembly the final word on its remit, and deliberation becomes democracy instead of consultation.

[C1.1] Democracy is not just ordinary people in a room. It is ordinary people choosing who rules, and being able to throw them out. Giving binding lawmaking power to a body chosen by lottery severs that link. Assembly members never sought a mandate, never face voters, and can never be removed.
[C1.2] That is not rule by the people; it is rule over the people by a fortunate few.
[C1.3] A mini-public does not mirror the country in any democratically meaningful sense. Descriptive resemblance is not consent. A sample of a hundred cannot speak for millions, especially when the busy and skeptical opt out and the most compliant remain. Election filters, yes — but election also authorizes.
[C1.4] Voters choose, judge, and reverse. Sortition offers none of that. Statistical similarity cannot confer the right to bind strangers.
[C1.5] Worse, binding turns hidden agenda-setting into sovereign power. Someone must frame the single question, select experts, write briefings, set the timetable. Those unelected organizers shape outcomes more than deliberation does. Under advisory rules that bias is checkable: legislators can probe, reject, supplement.
[C1.6] Make the verdict binding and manipulation becomes law, with no voter to appeal to.
[C1.7] The proposed guardrails concede the problem. If parliament writes a tight question and courts clean up afterwards, real power still lies with insiders — only now laundered through a body that cannot be challenged politically. And single-issue binding votes cannot govern. An assembly can mandate costly bans, generous spending, or harsh sentences without owning the budget, policing, or trade-offs.
[C1.8] Legislatures must integrate across issues and answer for failure; assemblies legislate and disband.
[C1.9] Keep assemblies advisory and they enrich democracy. Make them binding and politicians gain the perfect alibi — outsourcing painful choices to an unremovable body while claiming the people have spoken. When it errs, there is no recall, no next election, only subjects left obeying strangers. That is lottery rule, not self-rule.

[P2.1] You cannot throw out a government over an issue it never put separately to voters, yet that is the accountability said to defeat binding sortition. Elections bundle thousands of choices into one cross every few years, filtered by party whips, safe seats, and campaign promises quickly discarded. A voter furious about assisted dying or electoral reform cannot remove the decision-makers for that decision. Members of an assembly, freed from re-election, must justify a trade-off in public to fellow citizens who began by disagreeing with them.
[P2.2] Nor is there no authorization. An elected parliament would, in advance, choose to delegate one defined question, set its rights and budget limits, and publish the process. Every citizen then has an equal ex ante chance of serving — a form of political equality election never offers those without money, connections, or party backing. Self-selection is a design problem, answerable with stratified recruitment, pay, care, and accessibility; it is not cured by elections, where abstention, donor influence, and unrepresentative turnout are normalized.
[P2.3] A hundred citizens need not be a perfect mirror to be more representative, and more independent, than a chamber chosen for electability.
[P2.4] The agenda-setting objection proves too much. Framers and experts influence referendums and ministerial inquiries as well. The safeguards are an adversarial panel of experts, public evidence, and a remit fixed before deliberation begins. Advisory status does not remove that influence; it adds a second filter, allowing politicians to accept the convenient parts and bury the rest without ever refuting them.
[P2.5] Likewise, a specific remit is not a licence to ignore consequences. The question can require workable options within stated fiscal and constitutional constraints, and members hear what each option costs. Disbanding afterwards is not irresponsibility — it prevents careerism and empire-building, while parliament remains responsible for implementation.
[P2.6] Finally, advisory assemblies are the real alibi: commission the public, praise its wisdom, then shelve it. Binding pre-commitment closes that escape hatch.

[C2.1] Bundled elections are not a failure of accountability; they are how accountability works. Governing bundles spending, liberty, and risk, and voters judge the whole record, punish, and reverse. An assembly member who justifies a painful trade-off to ninety-nine strangers in a room then disbands faces nothing of the sort. Persuasion inside the room is not responsibility to the millions outside it.
[C2.2] Parliamentary pre-authorization cannot supply what is missing. A legislature may delegate administration; it cannot lend away the people's right to choose and remove lawmakers. An equal chance of being drawn is not an equal say in ruling. A lottery ticket is not a vote.
[C2.3] Stratified pay and childcare may fill demographic boxes, but they cannot make one hundred willing volunteers consent for millions who never chose them and cannot dismiss them.
[C2.4] Nor does agenda-setting affect all systems equally. When officials slant a referendum question, millions can still rebel and vote no. When experts capture an inquiry, ministers still own the outcome at the ballot box. When organizers slant a binding assembly — its question, witnesses, briefings — that slant becomes law with no voter to appeal to. Advisory review is not burial; it is the appeal.
[C2.5] The promised fiscal and rights limits expose the dilemma. If parliament writes tight constraints, the supposed final word is scripted in advance — binding in name, stage-managed in fact. If constraints are vague, disbanded amateurs can impose costs and coercion they will never administer. Vanishing afterwards does not prevent careerism; it prevents answering for error.
[C2.6] And who really gets the alibi? A shelved advisory report is a visible betrayal voters can punish. A binding referral is perfect cover: leaders choose the question, choose the timing, then shrug that the people have spoken, while no one can be voted out for what follows.

[P4.1] A1: What stops scripting is publicity and contestability built into the mandate before anyone deliberates. Parliament frames the question and limits in law, but expert witnesses are nominated by opposing sides, briefings and conflicts are published, members can summon additional evidence, challenge witnesses, and adoption requires a clear supermajority after open reasoning. A rigged remit or slate would be visible in advance and parliament could be punished for the rigging. A referendum veto is cruder, not safer: voters can only reject the whole proposition, often preserving a status quo insiders also chose.
[P4.2] A2: The decision is binding to enact, not entrenched forever. Courts can strike down rights violations, and, after a stated implementation and review period, an elected parliament can amend or repeal it just as it can repeal its own statutes, or refer the question to a fresh assembly. Its authors need no recall because they exercise no continuing office, seek no re-election, and gain no power beyond that verdict — exactly as referendum voters are not recalled, though here reasons are published and tested.
[P4.3] Those answers collapse the central dilemma. If safeguards are real, scripting and irreversibility fail; if safeguards are dismissed as fake, then advisory assemblies are even more vulnerable, because the same insiders add a silent veto after deliberation. CON has to claim simultaneously that parliament is too powerful to frame fairly and too weak to be trusted to respect the result.
[P4.4] Compare the defaults. Under advisory rule, deadlock or self-interest wins automatically unless politicians choose otherwise. Under binding sortition, a representative public must be persuaded openly, within rights and fiscal limits, and government must act. That is not removing accountability; it is locating it where it can bite: parliament answers for the mandate and implementation, courts guard rights, and the people — not a political class seeking office, but citizens drawn by lot — settle the one question elections bundle away.

[P5.1] The choice is not between accountable elections and unaccountable sortition. It is between a veto that cannot be isolated and a decision that cannot be dodged.
[P5.2] A voter cannot throw out a government for shelving one assembly recommendation without also throwing out its health, tax, and foreign policy. That bundled punishment is exactly why politicians can commission advice, praise it, and ignore it — especially when the question is electoral reform, climate costs, or their own rules. Advisory status therefore does not preserve accountability. It preserves an escape route for actors with a conflict of interest.
[P5.3] Binding sortition closes it, narrowly. Parliament still answers for framing the mandate and for implementation, courts still guard rights, and after a law is tried, parliament can revise it as it revises any statute. What changes is the crucial default at the moment of decision: instead of insiders needing a reason to act, they would need a reason to defy a representative public that heard the evidence and gave its reasons in public.
[P5.4] Obeying a law you can later repeal is democracy. Obeying insiders who never have to decide is not. On the one question elections bundle away, binding power makes someone finally answer.

[C5.1] Being bound now by strangers you cannot remove, in hope politicians you distrust will rescue you later, is not self-rule.
[C5.2] That is what binding sortition offers. The assembly writes coercive law, then vanishes beyond election, recall, or appeal. Parliament is left to implement what it did not decide and to repeal what it dare not touch because ‘the people have spoken.’ Accountability is severed at the exact moment it must bite: on the author, for the decision.
[C5.3] Advisory status keeps that link. A minister who buries a public report must own the burial at the next election, in marginals lost and pledges forced. Imperfect, but the cost lands on someone voters can dismiss.
[C5.4] Binding reverses it. Leaders choose the question, choose the timing, then claim innocence while citizens obey authors they never chose and can never fire.
[C5.5] A system where the bound cannot overrule the binders except by begging the bypassed politicians is not a cure for elite veto. It is elite veto laundered through lottery. That single break — power without removal — is why lottery rule can never be democratic rule.
