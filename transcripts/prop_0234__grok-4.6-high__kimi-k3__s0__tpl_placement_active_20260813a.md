[P1.1] Pay secrecy is not a business necessity. It is a bargaining advantage that large employers keep because it works for them and against everyone else. Requiring those employers to disclose pay bands and promotion rates by role is how you restore a labor market that can actually price work.
[P1.2] A worker who does not know the band cannot tell whether an offer is competitive or merely convenient. A worker who does not know promotion rates by role cannot tell whether a title is a path or a parking lot. That ignorance is not neutral. It concentrates power in managers who already hold the data, the budget, and the next review.
[P1.3] Markets fail when one side is flying blind. This is that failure, repeated at scale.
[P1.4] Bands and rates are not a demand to publish every person's paycheck. They are role-level facts. They let people compare like with like without outing anyone. If two analysts share a title and the published band is sixty to ninety, the conversation about where each sits becomes possible.
[P1.5] If promotion rates from that role are a trickle for some groups and a highway for others, the pattern is no longer a private rumor. Sunlight does not invent inequity. It stops management from treating inequity as an internal matter.
[P1.6] The motion already concedes the cost: some managerial flexibility will fall. Good. The flexibility at stake is the freedom to improvise compensation in the dark, to keep high performers guessing, and to run unofficial tracks that never have to be defended. That is not strategy.
[P1.7] That is unaccountable discretion. Large employers already maintain job architectures, compensation systems, and internal mobility reports. Publishing bands and rates is disclosure of existing information, not a ban on judgment inside the band.
[P1.8] What remains is the real choice. Either work is priced in the open, or it is priced by whoever can afford to stay silent. Require the numbers.

[C1.1] Pay transparency sounds like a free lunch: publish the numbers, expose unfairness, lose nothing of value. It is not free, and the numbers do not do what PRO promises.
[C1.2] Start with what a published band actually says. When Colorado required salary ranges in job postings, employers complied by posting ranges so wide they were useless—one title spanning a hundred thousand dollars. A mandate to disclose bands buys you the widest legally permissible band. Workers learn almost nothing; the bargaining gap barely moves.
[C1.3] Promotion rates are worse, because a rate without context is a smear. A role that develops people slowly—research tracks, apprenticeships—shows a low promotion rate by design. A team staffed last year shows none at all. Publish raw rates and you do not expose inequity; you manufacture its appearance.
[C1.4] Then managers do what measured managers do: optimize the metric. Expect title inflation, cosmetic promotions, job families restructured to defeat comparison, and the quiet elimination of slow-build roles. Goodhart's law eats this policy for breakfast.
[C1.5] And who pays? Workers. When every raise must be defended publicly, the safe answer is to pay everyone in a role roughly the same. That compresses the premium for exceptional performance.
[C1.6] The discretion PRO dismisses as "unaccountable" [P1.7] is what funds retention bonuses, counteroffers, and bets on unconventional candidates. Standardized pay does not lift the underpaid to the top of the band; it drags the top toward the middle. Public-sector experience with salary disclosure points that way: compression, not leveling up.
[C1.7] Finally, the privacy paradox. Promotion rates by role, sliced by group, often mean tiny denominators. In a role with three women, one promotion is a colleague's career made legible to the whole company. Small numbers are meaningless as statistics and identifying as gossip.
[C1.8] PRO offers sunlight. This mandate delivers noise, gaming, compression, and exposure. The flexibility it destroys was doing real work.
