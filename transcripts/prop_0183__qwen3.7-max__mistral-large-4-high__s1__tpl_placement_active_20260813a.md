[P1.1] Digital heritage is evaporating. Traditional copyright, designed for an era of physical scarcity, is actively accelerating this loss in the digital realm. Nonprofit digital libraries like the Internet Archive must be exempt from traditional enforcement to serve as humanity's essential safety net.
[P1.2] First, we face a crisis of digital decay. Unlike physical books, digital files rot. Servers die, software formats become obsolete, and domains expire. When a publisher goes bankrupt or a website shuts down, the content vanishes.
[P1.3] Traditional copyright enforcement treats every digital copy and format migration as a potential infringement, paralyzing archivists. To preserve a digital file, an archive must make redundant, migrated copies. Strict enforcement makes this basic preservation illegal without impossible-to-secure permissions.
[P1.4] Second, traditional enforcement creates a catastrophic market failure for orphan and out-of-print works. Millions of texts, early websites, and academic papers are commercially dormant but legally locked. Enforcement demands permission from rights holders who cannot be found or no longer exist. If the Internet Archive had to clear traditional copyrights for every defunct 1990s webpage or out-of-print academic journal, the archive would simply not exist.
[P1.5] An exemption is the only mechanism to rescue these orphaned works from the digital dark age.
[P1.6] Finally, this exemption targets strictly nonprofit entities and causes no genuine market harm. Archival snapshots and controlled digital lending do not cannibalize primary commercial markets; they preserve access when the market has already abandoned the work or when the original medium is lost. We are not discussing the piracy of new, commercially viable bestsellers. We are discussing the rescue of our shared cultural record.
[P1.7] The opposition will defend a rigid system that prioritizes hypothetical, unexercised commercial rights over the guaranteed destruction of our history. When a work is commercially dead, enforcing traditional copyright does not protect the creator; it only ensures the work is deleted. Exempting nonprofit digital libraries is the only pragmatic, proportionate way to preserve humanity's digital heritage before it is permanently lost.

[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false choice: that humanity must either abandon its digital heritage or grant nonprofit archives blanket immunity from copyright enforcement. That dichotomy collapses under scrutiny. Preservation does not require infringement, and exemption is not the only path to salvation. The law already provides robust tools for lawful preservation that the Internet Archive has deliberately bypassed in favor of unauthorized distribution.
[C1.2] First, legitimate mechanisms exist. National libraries operate under mandatory legal deposit requirements. Copyright statutes contain specific exceptions allowing libraries to make preservation copies for deteriorating or obsolete formats. Works enter the public domain when terms expire, ensuring permanent public access.
[C1.3] None of these require the Internet Archive to reproduce and distribute copyrighted books one-to-one through Controlled Digital Lending, or to release millions of in-copyright works during the National Emergency Library. These practices are not preservation; they are market substitution, distributing exact digital copies of works that publishers are actively licensing and selling to libraries and readers.
[C1.4] Second, the economic logic is backwards. Copyright is not a barrier to heritage; it is the engine that creates it. Authors and publishers invest in new works because they can control and monetize them. Granting blanket exemptions to massive digital platforms transfers value from creators to aggregators.
[C1.5] When the Archive distributes a contemporary novel without permission, it does not rescue an orphan from digital decay; it competes directly with the bookstore, the library ebook license, and the author's royalty stream.
[C1.6] Third, "nonprofit" is a tax classification, not a moral shield. Exemptions invite mission creep. Once scale and public benefit justify immunity, the boundary between preservation and piracy dissolves. We can fund preservation, expand fair use, and enforce deposit laws without dismantling the rights that make cultural production sustainable.
[C1.7] We need not choose between our heritage and the rule of law. We need only insist that preservation respect the rights that create the culture worth preserving.
