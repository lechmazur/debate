[P1.1] Languages are dying at a rate that makes species extinction look slow. A language falls silent every couple of weeks, and with it goes entire systems of knowledge—ecological, medicinal, spiritual—that took centuries to develop and cannot be reconstructed once lost. This is not a problem that charity can solve, and treating it as one is a moral and practical failure. I'll make three arguments for why standing public funds are necessary.
[P1.2] First, irreversibility demands sustained commitment. Unlike infrastructure that can be rebuilt or businesses that can restart, a last fluent speaker's death is final. Oral histories passed down through generations vanish completely when that chain breaks. Preservation requires consistent, long-term work—decades of documentation, education programs, and community support.
[P1.3] Charitable funding is inherently episodic: it follows trends, donor moods, and economic cycles. A standing public fund survives electoral changes and budget fluctuations, providing the continuity that cultural memory requires.
[P1.4] Second, governments bear direct historical responsibility for much of the loss we're trying to address. Minority languages were systematically suppressed through colonial education policies, residential schools, forced assimilation programs, and legal prohibitions on indigenous speech. The state didn't merely fail to protect these cultures—it actively destroyed them. Asking private charity to repair what public policy broke is an abdication of a debt, not a neutral preference for small government.
[P1.5] Third, cultural heritage is a collective good, not a private luxury. When a language dies, we all lose irreplaceable knowledge about human cognition, ecological relationships, and possible ways of organizing society. But no individual has sufficient incentive to fund preservation, and donor-driven philanthropy systematically favors charismatic languages over less visible ones. A public fund directed by community needs, not donor preferences, corrects this market failure.
[P1.6] The proposition is straightforward: governments should establish dedicated, standing public funds because the alternative—hoping charity fills the gap—has already failed. The question is whether we treat cultural memory as a public responsibility or watch it disappear while waiting for private generosity that history shows will not arrive in time.
