Building or renovating a library, recreation center, or late‑hour plaza requires capital outlays of millions, ongoing staffing, utilities, and security—expenses that dwarf the per‑user cost of a well‑designed app, which can be developed for a few hundred thousand dollars and scaled to tens of thousands of residents for a fraction of the price.
[C1.2] Second, apps solve the access problem that physical spaces cannot. The PRO side rightly notes that apps need internet, yet many municipalities already provide free Wi‑Fi in libraries and transit hubs, and lightweight apps can operate offline or on basic smartphones. [C1.3] Third, digital interventions are uniquely measurable and targeted. [C1.4] Fourth, speed matters. Deploying an app takes weeks; constructing a new community hub takes years. In a crisis, the city that can launch a pilot in a month and iterate based on real‑time usage data will reduce loneliness far sooner than waiting for a ribbon‑cutting ceremony.
[C1.5] Finally, the proposition forces a false binary. Investing more in apps does not preclude later infrastructure spending; it frees budget for the projects that truly need capital.
