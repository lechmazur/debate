[P1.1] Cultural memory is not a luxury owned only by those wealthy enough to preserve it. When these disappear, the loss is irreversible. A road can be rebuilt.
[P1.2] The last fluent speaker cannot be.
[P1.3] Private responsibility predictably fails here. Preservation creates broad, long-term benefits, but the immediate costs fall on small communities, often already disadvantaged. Donors favor visible, fashionable projects and shift priorities with economic cycles. Commercial markets reward content with large audiences, precisely excluding endangered languages.
[P1.4] A community facing poverty cannot reasonably be told to choose between present necessities and recording elders for citizens fifty years from now. That is a classic public-good problem, and public funding is the appropriate response.
[P1.5] The fund must be standing because delay is uniquely destructive. If an elderly speaker dies while volunteers await a grant drive, knowledge vanishes. Stable funding supports trained archivists, recording equipment, language teaching, digitization, secure storage, and long-term maintenance—not merely one-off collection. It also creates institutional memory and allows communities to plan rather than repeatedly plead for rescue.
[P1.6] There is also an obligation of justice. Governments have often helped produce linguistic decline through schooling, administrative standardization, displacement, and the privileging of dominant languages. [P1.7] Public funding need not mean bureaucratic ownership. An arm’s-length fund can use transparent criteria, independent review, community consent, and community control over sensitive materials.
