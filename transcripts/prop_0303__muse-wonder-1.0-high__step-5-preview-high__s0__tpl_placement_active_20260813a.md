[P1.1] Modern meritocracy tells a comforting story: talent rises, effort pays, and elite places are won by those who earned them. The story is not false because successful people worked hard. It is overstated because it treats hard work performed with every advantage as proof the contest was fair.
[P1.2] The reproduction begins long before an admissions test. Wealthier families can buy housing in strong school districts, books and tutoring, music and travel that build vocabulary and confidence, and rescue when a child falls behind. Poorer students often meet the same curriculum while working, commuting, caring for siblings, or lacking quiet space and help. By eighteen, what looks like individual merit is already the accumulated return on unequal investment.
[P1.3] Elite pathways then launder that advantage into credentials. Standardized scores track preparation as much as aptitude, and preparation is for sale. Holistic admissions reward expensive extracurriculars, polished essays, unpaid service trips, and recommendations from known institutions. Legacy and donor familiarity, early application strategies that favor those who do not need to compare aid, and private counselors all tilt a supposedly open process without ever announcing a preference for class.
[P1.4] The same conversion continues into work. Prestigious firms recruit from a narrow set of universities, screen for polish and cultural fit, and value referrals from inside networks. Unpaid or low-paid internships in expensive cities are feasible mainly for those subsidized by parents. A wealthy graduate can wait for the right opportunity, start a venture, or survive a failure; a poorer graduate must take the secure paycheck.
[P1.5] Risk itself becomes inherited.
[P1.6] None of this denies mobility or effort. Exceptions prove that a ladder exists, not that its rungs are equally spaced. Most winners naturally remember their late nights and forget the floor that never gave way beneath them. That selective memory is the point: privilege reproduces itself most efficiently when each generation can honestly say, I worked for this, and stop the story there.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes the existence of head starts for the futility of merit. Wealthy families can buy tutoring, quiet rooms, and safety nets; nobody should deny that. But the decisive question is not whether advantage exists. It is whether elite schools and professions convert that advantage into inherited positions regardless of ability.
[C1.2] Increasingly, they do not.
[C1.3] Standardized examinations, professional licensing, and measurable technical screens remain the spine of serious advancement. Preparation can be purchased, but competence is much harder to fake in a multivariable calculus proof, a coding assessment, a bar exam, or a surgical board. Those instruments are imperfect, yet they force at least some applicants with polish, legacy names, or parental connections to compete against students with better raw capability. The alternative PRO treats as neutral often makes class reproduction worse: when universities de-emphasize tests, essays, service trips, and referrals become more decisive—and those are exactly the currencies that money buys.
[C1.4] The pathway is also wider than the story admits. Scholarships, need-blind aid, public universities, transfer routes, certifications, and remote work have made elite-adjacent credentials available to people without inherited networks. Prestigious outcomes still cluster in wealthy ZIP codes, but that clustering is consistent with talent, family expectations, and early investment as well as with exclusion. If class were the master variable, we would not see so many first-generation entrants into elite programs—or so many wealthy heirs falling out of them.
[C1.5] PRO’s strongest move is to treat honest effort as false consciousness. It is not. A student can accurately say, “I worked for this,” while also acknowledging help. Self-attribution does not erase privilege; it records the part of success that was not assigned at birth.
[C1.6] Meritocracy is imperfect, not overstated. Class gives a head start. Ability and performance still decide far more of the race than PRO allows.
