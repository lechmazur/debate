[P1.1] Public pension systems face a simple arithmetic problem: people live longer, yet the age at which they stop working has barely moved. That mismatch turns longer lives into longer claims on a shrinking base of contributors. Raising the retirement age in step with life expectancy restores the balance these systems were built on. Relying mainly on higher payroll taxes papers over the imbalance by extracting more from those still working.
[P1.2] When public pensions fixed retirement near 65, the typical spell of benefits was far shorter than it is today. Longevity gains have added years of payments without a matching increase in years of contributions. Indexing the eligibility age to life expectancy keeps the ratio of working life to retirement roughly stable. It asks each cohort to share the gains of longer life rather than to finance them entirely through higher taxes on other people’s wages.
[P1.3] Higher payroll taxes are the weaker path. They fall on labor, cutting take-home pay and the incentive to work, hire, or stay formal. Younger workers already carry housing costs and slower wage growth; a permanent tax hike to preserve an unchanged retirement age shifts the longevity dividend entirely onto them. As retirees outnumber workers further, the required increases grow larger.
[P1.4] Each round makes work less attractive precisely when the system needs more contributors.
[P1.5] Raising the age is not punishment. Many people in their sixties remain productive, and older labor-force participation has already risen where policy allows. A worker who stays two years longer and draws benefits two years less changes the balance far more cleanly than a tax rate that must keep climbing. Systems that link pension age to life expectancy treat longevity as a shared fact, not a fiscal surprise.
[P1.6] Ever-higher taxes on a smaller workforce delay the adjustment, concentrate the burden, and leave the mismatch intact. Public pensions stay viable when retirement age moves with the lives they support.

[C1.1] The arithmetic on the other side hides the question that decides this debate: whose longer life are we actually talking about? Longevity gains have flowed overwhelmingly to higher earners in less punishing work. In many rich countries the lifespan gap between the best-off and worst-off workers runs to several years, in some places close to a decade. Indexing the retirement age to the average therefore cuts deepest where the gains were smallest.
[C1.2] A cleaner pays the same contribution rate as a lawyer, then collects a pension for far fewer years. Raise the age, and the system transfers lifetime benefits from the short-lived poor to the long-lived rich. That is not sharing the longevity dividend; it is confiscating it from those who received the least of it.
[C1.3] Second, the adjustment mechanism fails exactly where it is needed. The case assumes a 66-year-old can simply stay two more years. Office workers often can. Roofers, care workers, and warehouse staff often cannot — their bodies give out first.
[C1.4] What follows is not two extra years of contributions but two years on disability benefits, unemployment, or a reduced early pension. The fiscal savings shrink while the hardship lands on people with the fewest alternatives. Hiring discrimination makes the older-worker job market far weaker than the phrase "productive sixties" suggests.
[C1.5] Payroll taxes do the same fiscal job without these defects. A modest, gradual increase spreads the cost across tens of millions of workers in small increments, and it can be designed progressively — reaching higher wages rather than concentrating the entire burden on whichever workers happen to die younger or break down earlier. The objection that taxes discourage work concerns a small slice of income; an impossible extra year of physical labor is not a disincentive but a dead end.
[C1.6] If longevity is a shared fact, it deserves shared financing. Averages should not decide who must work until 69.
