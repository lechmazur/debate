[P1.1] Cinema thrives as a mass cultural habit when it functions as a democratic public square—a place where a student, a family, and a retiree pay the same price for standard seats, sit in the same darkness, and share the same gasp at the same moment. Aggressive dynamic pricing shatters this social contract. By allowing algorithms to inflate the cost of basic tickets based on real-time demand, theaters transform a predictable weekly ritual into a volatile auction that prices out exactly the casual patrons whose habitual attendance sustains the industry.
[P1.2] A mass habit dies by a thousand cuts of uncertainty. When the price of a standard seat swings wildly from a Tuesday matinee to a Saturday evening, or spikes the moment a trailer drops, moviegoers face a paralyzing new barrier: budget anxiety. They cannot plan ahead, cannot form the reliable routine of "Friday night at the movies," and increasingly retreat to the predictable flat subscription of streaming at home. The theater ceases to be a destination of habit and becomes a financial gamble that few can afford to lose.
[P1.3] This is not about premium recliners or IMAX upgrades. Standard seats are the foundation of cinema's civic function. When those seats become subject to surge pricing, the theater signals that it is no longer a shared space for the community but a marketplace of scarcity, where cultural participation depends on timing and economic luck. The result is a self-defeating cycle: fewer casual attendees create emptier showings, which management then tries to fill with even more aggressive pricing tactics, accelerating the exodus toward home screens.
[P1.4] Protecting standard seats from dynamic pricing preserves the accessibility that turns occasional viewers into lifelong cinephiles and sustains the communal ritual that defines cinema as mass culture. It maintains the theater as a place where communities gather as equals, not merely where the highest bidder sits.
