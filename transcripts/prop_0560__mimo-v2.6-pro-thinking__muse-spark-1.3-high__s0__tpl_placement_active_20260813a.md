[P1.1] The word that decides this debate is "marginal." Nobody is proposing to abandon the dying. The question is narrower and harder: when a health budget has one more dollar to spend, does it go to stretching an already high-cost end-of-life intervention by a few weeks, or to a preventive measure that keeps people out of the hospital entirely? On that question the case for prevention is overwhelming.
[P1.2] First, the returns are not close. Immunization, tobacco taxation, hypertension detection and control, clean water, maternal care — these deliver healthy life-years at a small fraction of what a marginal ICU day or a late-line oncology regimen costs for an extra month or two. Cost-effectiveness rankings across health systems consistently cluster prevention at the very top and marginal end-of-life expansions at the bottom.
[P1.3] Second, "marginal expansion" is precisely where the value is thinnest. The first dollars of end-of-life care — pain control, basic palliative support — are cheap and precious, and nothing in this motion touches them. The contested dollars are the last ones: another cycle of a toxic regimen for a two-month survival gain, another week in intensive care for a patient who will never leave it. That is the spending we are being asked to weigh, and it is the weakest use of scarce public funds in the entire system.
[P1.4] Third, prevention compounds. A child vaccinated, a smoker reached before the cancer, a hypertensive treated before the stroke — each averts an entire downstream cascade of expensive acute and terminal care. Starving prevention to fund end-of-life expansions is not a neutral choice; it manufactures the very demand for those expansions a decade later. Underfunded prevention is why our ICUs overflow in the first place.
[P1.5] The dying deserve dignity. But dignity is mostly cheap; marginal survival is expensive and brief. A system that spends its next dollar on weeks for the few rather than decades for the many has inverted its priorities.
