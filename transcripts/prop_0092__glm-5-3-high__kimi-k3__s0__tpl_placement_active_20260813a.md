[P1.1] Cities already price street space and its risks everywhere else. Heavy trucks pay higher tolls. Utility customers pay by usage. Airlines charge by weight and size.
[P1.2] The only anomaly here is that a three-ton pickup pays the same curb fee as a subcompact, despite occupying more street, more curb, and more crash physics. The proposition simply asks cities to finish a pricing logic they already accept.
[P1.3] The safety mechanism is not speculative. Pedestrian struck by a taller, heavier vehicle is hit higher on the body—chest and head instead of legs—and carries far more kinetic energy at the same speed. That's why pedestrian deaths in the United States have risen even as other road fatalities fell, tracking the shift toward ever-larger light trucks and SUVs. The front-end height of modern pickups sits at or above many adults' chests; children are effectively invisible in blind zones that have grown with hood lines.
[P1.4] A driver choosing a heavier vehicle imposes a portion of that danger on everyone who never consented to it. Pricing it is textbook externality correction.
[P1.5] Curb space is the same story. Parking is city land—often the most valuable land a municipality owns—and a large vehicle consumes more of it, complicates sightlines at intersections, and narrows travel lanes for everyone else. Charging identical fees means small-car owners subsidize big-vehicle street use. That is regressive in exactly the wrong direction.
[P1.6] Notice what the motion does not do. It does not ban anything. It does not target rural workers who need a truck; a flat fee adjustment barely registers for commercial use, and cities can exempt trades or farm plates. It simply says: when your vehicle's size imposes higher costs on shared streets, you pay closer to what those costs actually are.
[P1.7] The alternative—the status quo—is a subsidy for bigness paid in lives, congestion, and street space. Opponents will cry fairness, but fairness is precisely what per-size pricing restores. Equal treatment of unequal burdens is not neutrality; it's a hidden transfer. Cities should end it.
