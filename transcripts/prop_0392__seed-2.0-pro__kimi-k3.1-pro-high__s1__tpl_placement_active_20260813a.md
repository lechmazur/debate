[P1.1] Right now, there is effectively no penalty when public digital services lock disabled users out. You can file a complaint, wait eighteen months, and at most get an apology and a promise to maybe fix it someday. No one loses budget, no one changes priorities. This is not acceptable.
[P1.2] When a bus company repeatedly leaves wheelchair users stranded at the curb, they get fined. When a public library refuses entry to someone with a mobility aid, they get fined. But when a tax filing app will not work with screen readers, when a hospital booking site uses flashing animations that trigger seizures, when a benefits portal has buttons too small for anyone with hand tremors? Nothing happens.
[P1.3] This policy only applies for repeated failure of basic standards. These are not obscure, expensive requirements. These are rules written twenty years ago, implemented with a few lines of standard code, required for every other public facing service already. Fines are not here to punish.
[P1.4] They are here to fix broken incentives. Right now, every product team has to choose: launch on time, or spend three extra days fixing accessibility. Right now, the choice is always launch on time. A meaningful, revenue-proportional fine for repeated failure makes that choice neutral.
[P1.5] Opponents will argue this harms small operators. That is why the motion specifies meaningful, not ruinous fines. Fines scale. Services get clear warnings, multiple remediation periods, free guidance.
[P1.6] Only when they keep choosing to ignore disabled users, over and over, do they pay. This is not radical. It is just extending the same basic fairness we already apply to every physical public service, to the digital ones that people now depend on to eat, pay rent, and see a doctor.

[C1.1] Accessibility should be enforceable, but this motion reaches for the bluntest instrument and assumes punishment will produce competence. A fine can penalise an inaccessible service; it cannot, by itself, supply the specialist testing, legacy-system repairs, accessible documents, or staff training needed to make that service usable.
[C1.2] The motion’s central terms are also dangerously elastic. What counts as a “basic” standard when websites combine code, uploaded documents, maps, video, authentication systems, and third-party tools? What makes failure “repeated”—the same defect, the same page, or the same service after an update? And “meaningful” to a large platform could be devastating to a charity, local library, or small healthcare provider.
[C1.3] PRO’s warnings, remediation periods, free guidance, and proportional penalties are additions to their speech, not safeguards written into the motion.
[C1.4] PRO’s claim that accessibility often requires “a few lines of standard code” or “three extra days” proves too much. If remediation were universally that simple, repeated failure would rarely occur. Real services can contain years of accumulated content and components that no single team controls. Faced with unpredictable liability, rational operators may remove optional features, stop publishing nonessential material, outsource everything to rigid templates, or withdraw digital services altogether.
[C1.5] Those responses can reduce accessibility while improving compliance on paper.
[C1.6] The physical-access analogy also fails. A blocked library entrance is comparatively visible and stable; digital accessibility varies across assistive technologies, content changes, and user needs. That does not excuse exclusion—it means enforcement must prioritise expert assessment and verified remedies, not assume that a financial penalty identifies or fixes the barrier.
[C1.7] For government-run services, the contradiction is sharper: fining a hospital or benefits agency may simply move public money around while leaving disabled users dependent on the same broken system. The incentive PRO celebrates is not neutral. A fine large enough to dominate launch decisions is also large enough to encourage defensive compliance and service reduction.
[C1.8] The fairer, more effective response is binding remediation deadlines, independent accessibility testing, procurement requirements, technical support, transparent monitoring, and enforceable user redress—with escalating restrictions where providers refuse to cooperate. [clipped]

[C2.2] The claim that CON is attacking “random, instant, unlimited fines” misses the argument. “Meaningful,” “repeated,” and “basic” are thresholds for liability, not an enforcement scheme. They do not specify who assesses failure, how users of different assistive technologies are heard, whether a cure period exists, or how proportionality protects a small provider. PRO’s nine-month, three-complaint example, with guidance offered, proves the point: those safeguards make the example reasonable, but they are not in the motion. We must judge the wording before us, not the careful regime PRO keeps adding.
[C2.3] Nor does a persistently broken button answer the perverse-incentive problem. An operator can remove the underlying feature, stop publishing optional material, or narrow the service, eliminating that “repeated failure” without giving disabled users equivalent access. A penalty designed to change launch priorities can therefore reward retreat rather than inclusion.
[C2.4] Finally, asserting that every alternative “already exists” and that there is “zero actual cost” does not establish what fines add. If deadlines and monitoring are genuinely ignored, PRO must explain why the same institutions will reliably assess, impose, and collect fines—and why collection will produce repairs. An organisation may instead treat the penalty as an operating cost. A public agency may pay from its service budget while users still cannot book care or claim benefits.
[C2.5] PRO establishes only that fines can make neglect costly. Their case requires more: that repeated failure reliably identifies blameworthy neglect, and that punishment delivers access rather than withdrawal or paper compliance. This motion guarantees neither. It imposes costs after repeated exclusion while leaving diagnosis, repair, user redress, and equivalent continued service unsecured.
