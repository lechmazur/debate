[P1.1] A social platform may personalize the content in each feed, yet still impose essentially the same goal on everyone: maximize predicted engagement, retention, or another platform-defined proxy. That is not genuine personalization. Users should instead be able to choose among clear recommendation goals—close relationships, chronological updates, local information, learning, novelty, ideological diversity, or simply less emotionally intense material—and adjust those goals over time. A default can remain, and ordinary safety rules still apply; the proposition requires meaningful choice, not an algorithmic control panel or permission to promote illegal content.
[P1.2] The central problem is incentive mismatch. A platform benefits when users keep scrolling, while users may value being informed, finishing a task, maintaining friendships, or leaving after ten useful minutes. Clicks and watch time cannot reliably distinguish satisfaction from compulsion, fascination from anger, or lasting value from momentary stimulation. Optimizing that proxy at enormous scale predictably privileges whatever captures attention.
[P1.3] Asking users directly what outcomes they want is a better signal than inferring their welfare from behavior the feed itself has shaped.
[P1.4] One universal objective also fails because people and contexts differ. The same person may want professional discovery in the morning, updates from friends at lunch, and calming entertainment at night. A teenager, a job seeker, and a disaster-response volunteer should not be trapped inside the same corporate definition of relevance. User-selected modes make recommendation responsive to purpose rather than merely to past clicks.
[P1.5] Control also improves accountability. When outcomes are poor, users can change the objective instead of helplessly hiding individual posts while the underlying system remains untouched. Competing goals reveal what people actually prefer and give platforms incentives to improve dimensions other than engagement.
[P1.6] The likely objection—too much complexity—is an argument for good design, not imposed uniformity. Simple presets, explanations, and an optional default preserve convenience. Nor is user choice uniquely responsible for echo chambers: engagement optimization already rewards emotionally reinforcing content. Diversity can itself be a selectable, transparent goal.

[C1.5] A feed is not a private appliance. It is shared infrastructure shaping elections, public health, and what neighbours believe is happening. [C1.6] Those drawn to conspiracism can choose affirmation, provocateurs can choose outrage, and society fragments into incompatible realities with no common standard to defend.

[P2.1] The CON case wins only by rewriting “far more control” as “purely personal goals with optional safety.” That is neither the motion nor our model. Platforms can enforce one non-negotiable floor for illegality, fraud, harassment, and crisis protections while letting users choose how eligible content is ranked. Shared infrastructure needs common rules; it does not require one corporate purpose. Traffic laws do not require everyone to choose the same destination.
[P2.2] CON also confuses choosing an end with designing an algorithm. A user need not set weights or thresholds; they can select “close relationships” or “learning,” while the system still learns which posts best serve that purpose. Behavioral signals need not disappear; they become evidence within a user-chosen objective. And “millions of small choices” are not neutral revelations of welfare: the platform first chooses the menu and ordering, then treats an impulsive click as endorsement.
[P2.3] That circularity is precisely why explicit direction matters.
[P2.4] The objection that labels need engineering underneath proves too much. “Relevance” and “engagement” also require proxies and thresholds. The meaningful difference is visibility and contestability: a mode can have a plain-language description, measurable behavior, and an easy switch. If “diversity” is implemented badly, users and auditors can identify that failure. Under a single opaque objective, there is no alternative to compare.
[P2.5] Nor does fragmentation rescue CON. Feeds already differ across billions of histories, so “one system” does not produce one common reality. A limited set of modes may actually improve evaluation by creating explicit cohorts and clearer expectations. Most users keeping a safe default is compatible with giving motivated users genuine agency; minority uptake is not an argument for denying everyone the option.
[P2.6] Finally, accountability does not vanish when users choose. Choosing a goal is not waiving platform responsibility for foreseeable harms. CON’s dilemma—central safety or user agency—is false. Keep the guardrails; democratize the destination.
