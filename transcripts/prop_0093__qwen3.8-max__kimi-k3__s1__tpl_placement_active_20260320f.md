[P1.1] Cities should treat the curb as public infrastructure, not as private storage. The motion asks whether scarce street space should serve the many functions a city needs—moving riders, loading goods, cycling safely—or continue to be reserved mainly for parked private cars. The decisive test is whether reallocation improves mobility, safety, and access more than it burdens drivers.
[P1.2] The strongest reason is efficiency. A single travel lane converted to a bus lane can move far more people than a lane of parked cars. In dense corridors, buses get stuck in traffic because curbside parking forces them to merge, stop, and re-enter. Giving buses priority makes service faster and more reliable, which increases ridership and reduces congestion for everyone.
[P1.3] Deliveries also need dedicated curb space. When trucks have no legal loading zone, they double-park, block bike lanes, or idle in travel lanes. That creates danger and delay. Smart cities convert a share of parking into short-term loading bays, timed delivery zones, and passenger pickup areas.
[P1.4] This helps stores and restaurants more than protecting all-day commuter parking does.
[P1.5] Bike lanes are the clearest safety trade-off. Parking-protected or curbside bike lanes reduce conflicts between cyclists and moving traffic. A street where cyclists must weave around parked cars and opening doors is a street where crashes are more likely. Reallocating curb space saves lives without taking away the road itself.
[P1.6] CON will likely say parking is essential. But the motion does not ban parking; it rejects protecting most parking by default. Cities can keep accessible parking for disabled residents, resident permits where genuinely needed, and shared parking structures. The question is priority.
[P1.7] A curb used for one car for eight hours serves one household; a bus lane, loading zone, or bike lane serves hundreds or thousands throughout the day. Public space should move people, not store private vehicles.
