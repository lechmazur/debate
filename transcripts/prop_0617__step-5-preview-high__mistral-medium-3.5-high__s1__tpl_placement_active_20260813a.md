[P1.1] The digital economy is not a market where the usual antitrust assumptions safely hold. Scale, data, and attention create feedback loops that can lock in an incumbent before a rival reaches viability. When a dominant platform acquires a nascent rival, the most obvious consumer cost often never appears: the product that would have existed is never launched. That absence is difficult for a regulator to quantify and easy for the merging parties to describe as speculative.
[P1.2] The proposed presumption is designed to correct precisely this blindness. It shifts the burden to the firms with the best information, strongest incentives, and greatest resources to prove that the acquisition will not foreclose future competition.
[P1.3] This is not a call to ban deals. It is an evidentiary allocation. If consumer benefit is substantial and cognizable, the firm can make that showing. But the burden should not rest on an agency to disprove a counterfactual while the acquirer can point to efficiencies that are vague, delayed, or internal to the transaction.
[P1.4] Dominant platforms can afford to document their claims; startups are gone, and the competitive process loses its chance to test the claim.
[P1.5] The practical stakes are familiar: acquisition by a platform that can bundle, preference, or copy a feature turns a potential competitor into an asset of the incumbent. Procompetition is served only when a deal is shown to expand output, access, quality, or price in ways the market could not otherwise achieve. Where that showing is absent, doubt should not resolve in favor of concentration.
[P1.6] The motion therefore protects the process on which consumer welfare ultimately depends. It treats proof as a responsibility, not a nuisance.

[C1.1] Presuming acquisitions by dominant digital platforms are anti-competitive is a reckless policy that would strangle innovation in the digital economy. The rule is devastatingly over-inclusive. Google’s 2005 acquisition of Android, then a tiny startup with fewer than two dozen employees, directly enabled the mobile revolution that broke Microsoft’s desktop monopoly and gave consumers unprecedented choice in computing. Facebook’s early purchases of infrastructure startups like Parse and Onavo delivered immediate improvements in service reliability and data efficiency for billions of users worldwide.
[C1.2] These are textbook examples of pro-competitive acquisitions that enhanced consumer welfare—exactly the kind of deals this presumption would block or deter. The assumption underlying the proposition—that digital markets are static and dominance is permanent—is empirically false. Market history shows constant disruption: MySpace, Yahoo, and AOL were once celebrated as ‘dominant’ platforms; TikTok achieved global scale despite Facebook’s entrenchment; Netflix transformed television; the iPhone redefined mobile computing. Competition in digital markets is dynamic, not static.
[C1.3] The burden shift is also practically unworkable. It demands that firms prove a counterfactual—demonstrating what products a nascent startup might have launched, or how markets would have evolved without the acquisition. This is an impossible standard, reducing merger review to a speculative exercise where regulators must predict alternative futures. The vague requirement for ‘substantial consumer benefit’ compounds the problem, creating a moving target that invites arbitrary enforcement.
[C1.4] Existing antitrust law already provides effective remedies. The substantial-lessening-of-competition test and potential-competition doctrine give authorities the tools to block genuinely harmful deals without this blunt, innovation-stifling presumption. Most damagingly, the rule would chill the startup ecosystem it purports to protect. If entrepreneurs know that acquisition by dominant platforms is presumptively illegal, they will avoid developing complementary services, fearing their exit options will vanish.
[C1.5] The result: reduced investment, stifled innovation, a litigious environment that deters deal-making, and a calcified digital economy where successful firms are punished and challengers are denied the capital and pathways they need to grow. The proposed cure is far more destructive than the disease it claims to treat.

[P5.5] So the presumption is not anti-merger; it is anti-unproven merger. Where substantial consumer benefit can be shown, the deal survives. Where it cannot, an incumbent's confidence is not evidence. The doubt should resolve against concentration.
