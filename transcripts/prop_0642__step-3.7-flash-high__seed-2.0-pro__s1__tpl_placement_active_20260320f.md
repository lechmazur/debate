[P1.1] Minimum parking requirements are a failed one-size-fits-all mandate that worsens car dependence, hikes housing costs, and wastes land—even in regions where driving is the dominant mode of travel. Switching to maximum parking requirements fixes these harms without eliminating parking for drivers who rely on it.
[P1.2] First, minimum rules act as a hidden, regressive tax on all households, regardless of car ownership. In even the most car-dependent U.S. metro areas, more than 1 in 5 households do not own a car, yet mandatory parking forces developers to build costly spaces bundled into every home’s price. These spots add tens of thousands of dollars to a new apartment unit’s cost, raising rents for low-income families, seniors, and young people who will never use the parking they are forced to pay for.
[P1.3] Amid severe housing shortages across nearly every car-dependent region, this mandate needlessly suppresses affordable home supply.
[P1.4] Second, minimum requirements make the congestion they claim to solve worse. By artificially lowering the effective cost of driving (since parking is "free" for tenants, baked into rent rather than priced separately), these rules encourage even short, walkable or transit-accessible trips to be taken by car. Over time, this fills roads with drivers who would have chosen other modes if parking was not bundled, leading to longer commutes and more gridlock exactly in the car-dependent regions that rely on these mandates.
[P1.5] Maximum requirements do not ban parking—they simply stop forcing developers to build more than the market will support. Where car-dependent cities like Phoenix and Atlanta have implemented parking caps in commercial and residential corridors, developers have shifted from wasteful surface lots to shared, structured parking that cuts impervious land cover and reduces urban heat island effects, while still maintaining ample availability for drivers who need it. Even in regions where most people travel by car, maximum requirements let the market build the right amount of parking, not a wasteful surplus that drives up housing costs, worsens congestion, and harms public health.
