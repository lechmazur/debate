[P1.1] Copyright cannot pretend that foundation models are just another book, and it cannot pretend that billions of training tokens can be licensed one contract at a time. Where a socially valuable use collides with impossible bargaining, the law should do what it has done before: grant a compulsory license, and require payment to rightsholders.
[P1.2] The case starts with transaction costs. A frontier model draws on text and images from millions of authors, photographers, publishers, and estates, many untraceable and many represented by no one. Voluntary licensing at that scale is not a market. It is a filter that favors incumbents who already hold vast corpora, or who can absorb years of clearance, and leaves everyone else choosing between infringement risk and exclusion.
[P1.3] A statutory license removes the veto without removing the duty to pay.
[P1.4] That is not a rupture. Mechanical licenses let musicians record songs they do not own; retransmission schemes let distributors carry works without hunting down every owner. The justification was always the same: holdout power plus fragmented rights produces either unpaid use or paralysis. Training data is that problem at industrial scale.
[P1.5] Fair-use litigation is not a substitute. It is a coin flip that varies by court, burns creator and developer money alike, and pays no one while it is pending. If training is treated as free, creators subsidize products that may compete with their markets. If it is forbidden unless every right is cleared, the public loses models that smaller labs and public institutions could otherwise build.
[P1.6] Compulsory licensing is the third path: permission is automatic, compensation is mandatory, and rates can reflect statistical learning rather than the price of buying the work itself.
[P1.7] Payment should be real. Collective administration, audited disclosures of training corpora, and tariffs tied to revenue or compute can make compensation scale with commercial success without letting any single rightsholder tax the entire technology. Creators get a durable claim. Developers get a rule they can obey.
[P1.8] The alternative is a decade of lawsuits that neither funds art nor governs the technology.

[C1.1] The motion is sold as a third path, but it is a price-control regime for expressive works whose central variable cannot be priced. PRO says a statutory license “removes the veto without removing the duty to pay” [P1.3]. That is exactly the problem. Copyright’s core is not a guaranteed royalty; it is the right to authorize.
[C1.2] A compulsory license would tell every writer and artist that their work may be ingested by any qualifying model, for a rate set by regulators or collectives, whether they consent or not. For a novelist whose style is cloned, or a photographer whose catalog trains a competing stock-image generator, the harm is not cured by a few cents distributed through a collective.
[C1.3] Nor can a regulator sensibly value each work’s contribution. Foundation models learn statistical patterns from millions of items; the marginal value of any one image or article is unmeasurable and highly context-dependent. Any tariff will therefore be arbitrary—tied to revenue, compute, or sampling—and distribution will predictably flow to large publishers, stock agencies, and estates with the best metadata and lobbying power. Individual creators get a bureaucratic claim, not a market.
[C1.4] The scheme also entrenches the very incumbents PRO worries about [P1.2]. Large firms can absorb audits, disclosure, collective fees, and compliance departments. Small labs, public-interest researchers, and open-source projects cannot. A compulsory license does not level the playing field; it creates a tollbooth that only the biggest players can comfortably pass through.
[C1.5] And it short-circuits the real legal question: is training permissible at all, and on what terms? A statutory license would lock in a bargain before we know whether the use is fair and before voluntary licensing markets can develop. The result is not order. It is a legislated subsidy from creators to AI firms, paid through an opaque collective.
[C1.6] CON wins because a compulsory license is the wrong instrument: it overrides consent, cannot measure contribution, and centralizes control in the hands of those already powerful.

The real alternative is existing copyright plus voluntary licensing, opt-outs, and fair-use adjudication. The photographer who could license her catalog to one stock generator and refuse another is now forced to feed every qualifying model.
[C2.2] Her only remedy is a share of a fund—a bureaucratic claim, not a bargain. That is not protecting a right that cannot be exercised; it is extinguishing the most valuable part of it.
[C2.3] [P2.3] says ingestion is not output infringement, and style is not protected. Exactly: the training copy is often the only choke point where a rightsholder has leverage. Once ingestion is licensed by statute, a creator’s remaining claim depends on proving output similarity—expensive, fact-intensive, and easy to evade with models that imitate without copying protected expression. “Those uses stay actionable” is cold comfort when the actionable use is the one nobody can detect.
[C2.4] The proxy problem [P2.5] is not solved by calling it clumsy. Revenue and compute tariffs measure the licensee, not the work. Distribution through collectives will track registrations, metadata, and bargaining power—large publishers and stock agencies first, individual illustrators last. A mechanical license prices a known use of a known song; training prices an unknown contribution to an unknown model.
[C2.5] And a fee that is “near zero until there is revenue” [P2.7] is not payment; it is a liability shield for the most commercially valuable training, with the bill deferred until after the model is built.
[C2.6] Nor does compulsory licensing spare small labs. It adds registries, audits, collective administration, and rate disputes—precisely the compliance machinery incumbents can staff. Voluntary standard licenses and opt-outs can be simple; a statutory regime is a permanent regulator.
[C2.7] PRO has shown that bargaining is hard. That is an argument for better collective bargaining, not for abolishing consent by statute.

[C5.1] The closing argument asks us to choose between a no that cannot be delivered and a claim that arrives without a bargain. But the claim does not arrive. It is an IOU written by a regulator, funded by a tariff tied to the licensee’s revenue, and divided by disclosed inclusion. Inclusion is not contribution.
[C5.2] A single image can be central to a model or noise in a billion-token corpus, and no collective can tell which. So the no-catalog author is not made powerful; she is made a passive creditor of a fund whose rate and distribution she does not negotiate. The lab gets certainty; the intermediary gets administration; she gets a share set by others.
[C5.3] That is the decisive defect. The motion does not make payment mandatory in any meaningful sense; it makes permission mandatory and payment politically allocated. Copyright’s authorization right is hard to exercise at scale, but the answer is collective bargaining, default opt-outs, and targeted liability—not deleting the right and calling the remainder payment. Compulsory licensing guarantees access, not compensation, and it does so by conscripting the very creators it claims to protect.
