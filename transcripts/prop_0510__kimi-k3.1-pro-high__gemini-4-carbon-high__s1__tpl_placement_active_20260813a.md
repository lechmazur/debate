[P1.1] Automatic stabilizers are the fiscal equivalent of circuit breakers: when income and demand fall, unemployment support and tax relief expand without waiting for a fresh political bargain. That expansion should continue when public debt is elevated, because a downturn is precisely when suppressing support can make both the recession and the debt problem worse.
[P1.2] Start with the debt ratio itself: debt divided by GDP. A government can cut spending in the name of reducing the numerator while shrinking the denominator faster. The result may be less output and no durable improvement in public finances.
[P1.3] A worker whose benefits expire does not merely buy fewer groceries; the landlord, shop, and supplier lose income too. Supporting that worker temporarily can prevent a local shock from becoming a self-reinforcing contraction.
[P1.4] Automaticity matters as much as generosity. Discretionary packages arrive after negotiations, legislative delays, and implementation bottlenecks. Stabilizers respond when conditions deteriorate and recede as recovery returns. That speed limits business failures and unemployment before they become expensive structural damage.
[P1.5] It also gives households and firms a predictable floor rather than forcing them to guess whether rescue will arrive.
[P1.6] Elevated debt is a reason to design stabilizers well, not to disable them. [C1.4] Heavily indebted governments have no margin for blind rules; policymakers need discretion to judge financing capacity and shock sources before expanding deficits.
[C1.5] Promising "credible consolidation once recovery is established" [P1.6] ignores why debt ratios remain elevated in the first place. Each cycle leaves a higher debt plateau, diverting more tax revenue from public investment and safety nets toward bondholders.
[C1.6] High-debt downturns demand bounded, discretionary triage: protecting vulnerable households by reprioritizing existing budgets rather than writing uncapped checks on the bond market. Stabilizers work only while public debt itself remains a safe asset; once automatic borrowing breaks market confidence, the state loses its power to stabilize anything at all.

[P2.1] CON’s case depends on replacing the motion with something easier to attack: “unconditional,” “uncapped,” “blind” expansion. Automatic stabilizers are not an open-ended stimulus program. They are temporary tax and transfer responses, with eligibility, duration, and withdrawal rules. The choice is not market discipline versus a blank cheque; it is predictable insurance versus delayed discretion or procyclical cuts.
[P2.2] The sovereign-risk argument also proves too much. Elevated debt is not lost market access. CON describes a possible crisis—higher yields, bank losses, forced austerity—as if it were the inevitable result of every expansion. But markets also price growth, revenues, and the credibility of the future fiscal path.
[P2.3] A slump destroying firms and employment can worsen that path. Support that limits contraction and expires in recovery cannot be judged by today’s deficit alone. CON must compare the risks of action and inaction, not enumerate only the former.
[P2.4] Nor does the supply-shock objection justify disabling automaticity. Stabilizers insure against income loss; they do not pretend transfers create energy or relieve bottlenecks. When inflation binds, support can be more targeted and less aggregate. Withdrawing it according to debt level would make indebted countries impose the harshest losses when households are least resilient.
[P2.5] The debt-ratchet objection assumes expansion is automatic but consolidation optional. That asymmetry is unnecessary: sunset provisions, recovery triggers, and pre-committed medium-term consolidation can make the rule symmetric. Discretion is hardly a safeguard against political delay; it is often where delay occurs.
[P2.6] Reprioritizing existing budgets can improve targeting, but moving money within a fixed budget cannot fully offset an economy-wide collapse in revenue and demand. It complements stabilization; it does not replace it.
[P2.7] At most, CON establishes special limits for governments already in acute financing distress. It does not show that elevated debt ratios generally warrant suppressing automatic support. Well-bounded stabilizers remain the safer debt strategy because they protect the recovery on which repayment depends.
