[P1.1] A statue on a pedestal in a central square is not a history lesson; it is an act of praise performed in the present tense on behalf of the whole community. Textbooks teach; monuments venerate. The architecture itself—elevation, bronze, looking up—announces that this person deserves admiration now. So the question is not whether to erase the past, but whom a living community should exalt in its shared space.
[P1.2] Nothing about the past makes that choice for us.
[P1.3] Honor is a present-tense act that needs present-tense justification. Many Confederate monuments were raised decades after the war, during Jim Crow and the civil rights era, as deliberate statements of defiance; they were always political speech, and keeping them up is speech too—a choice renewed daily. When a figure's defining legacy is human bondage, that renewal tells the descendants of the enslaved that the square does not fully belong to them. Civic honor must be revisable, not frozen by whoever held power a century ago.
[P1.4] The motion does not forget; it relocates statues to museums and contextualized sites, where they teach more history, not less. A Confederate general beside the testimony of the people his cause fought to keep enslaved is far more educational than an untouchable silhouette above traffic. Germany remembers the Nazi era without one honored statue of its leaders; post-communist states removed their Lenins and Stalins, and no one forgot the twentieth century. Memory has never required veneration.
[P1.5] Finally, "primarily" does real work. This is not about whether any flawed figure falls; it covers those whose central achievement is domination itself—a slave trader, a colonial governor who crushed rebellions. Washington's primary association, however tainted, is founding a republic; the line is administrable.
[P1.6] The test throughout: does continued civic honor serve the community's values today? Defending these statues means claiming either that honor, once granted, is permanent, or that public praise means nothing. Neither claim survives.

[C1.1] The proposition converts justified condemnation of oppression into a crude rule for public memory: when a biography becomes intolerable, export its material traces. Too often, that produces a cleaner but less instructive landscape.
[C1.2] PRO assumes that a pedestal can only mean endorsement. Yet monuments acquire meanings beyond their founders’ intentions. They can testify to who controlled a city, which myths it manufactured, and how later generations resisted them. A Jim Crow-era Confederate statue accompanied by an unflinching account of its purpose, alongside memorials to those it intimidated, teaches something a museum cannot: propaganda occupied this very center of civic power.
[C1.3] Removal can destroy that evidence while flattering the present as innocent.
[C1.4] Nor does “primarily associated” create the clean boundary PRO promises. According to whom, and when? Columbus signifies navigation, settlement, Indigenous catastrophe, and heritage differently across publics; revolutionary and imperial leaders often combine state-building with repression. Where classification is contested, the label becomes a political weapon.
[C1.5] Invoking Washington merely declares a hard case away rather than supplying a principle for deciding it.
[C1.6] Relocation is not neutral preservation either. Site-specific works lose meaning when moved, while museums have limited space and their own curatorial perspectives. Most importantly, the motion prescribes one outcome even where residents democratically prefer reinterpretation, counter-monuments, or retention as a warning. It replaces continuing civic argument with a standardized moral purge.
[C1.7] PRO’s strongest examples—Nazi leaders and monuments erected to enforce Jim Crow—cannot justify a categorical rule spanning slavery, colonialism, and mass repression across radically different societies. Germany and post-communist states show that memory can survive removal, not that removal is always the superior form of memory.
[C1.8] Civic honor is revisable; inherited space is not therefore disposable. A democracy can answer objectionable monuments with fuller history and visible dissent without empowering successive majorities to strip the common landscape down to whatever the present applauds. Reject automatic removal because it sacrifices historical evidence, local judgment, and democratic contest for symbolic tidiness.

[C2.2] A courthouse statue documents official sponsorship of white supremacy partly because it stood at the courthouse; a gallery preserves the artifact while sacrificing that spatial indictment. Photographs record appearances, but do not make later generations confront where authority exercised power. This does not justify retaining every monument. [C2.3] Nor does past exclusion answer the democratic objection. It justifies changing who decides and whose testimony counts, not predetermining the outcome. PRO treats local choice as suspect when it preserves a statue but sufficient when it designs the museum. Descendants of the oppressed should be participants, not a rhetorical veto claimed on their behalf, as though their communities must all prefer the same landscape.
[C2.4] On clear cases: no, CON need not defend honoring a slave trader. Removal may often be the best answer. But the proposition does not say “removal is sometimes justified”; it prescribes relocation for an enormous class spanning slave traders, colonial governors, nation-founders, and revolutionary regimes. “Primarily associated” is therefore not a fringe difficulty like whether Columbus falls just inside a line. It asks later publics to rank navigation against conquest, state-founding against repression, and changing symbolism against original intent.
[C2.5] Those disputes define the category, not merely its border.
[C2.6] The real comparison is not “equal belonging or a seminar.” It is contextual judgment—including removal when warranted—against a rule that declares every alternative inferior before hearing the community or examining the site. PRO has shown why honor is revisable, not why historical space must be exportable. Removal can serve civic equality; making it the only permissible form of remembrance does not.
