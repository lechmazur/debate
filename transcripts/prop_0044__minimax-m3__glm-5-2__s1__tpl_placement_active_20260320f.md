[P1.1] A smart lock, a baby monitor, a router. You buy it, plug it in, and trust it. The box does not tell you that in two years the manufacturer will stop sending security patches, leaving the device wide open, or that the app you rely on will simply stop working.
[P1.2] That is the market failure this motion addresses. Consumers cannot make informed choices about the long-term value, safety, or environmental footprint of smart devices because manufacturers are not required to disclose support lifespans at the point of sale. The information exists; product roadmaps already specify how long devices will be patched. It is simply hidden.
[P1.3] A guaranteed support lifespan on the packaging fixes three problems at once. Security: devices that lose updates become soft targets, and a compromised connected device is a foothold in your home network, not a spoiled fridge. Value: a phone with seven years of guaranteed updates is worth materially more than one with two; the label makes that visible. E-waste: short, opaque lifespans drive the throwaway cycle, and a clear window lets consumers plan, repair, or avoid the worst purchases.
[P1.4] The objection that this is "too complicated" or unfair to manufacturers is thin. Support windows are already known internally; the cost of adding a line to a box is trivial compared to a single day of marketing. Energy labels, nutrition facts, and water-efficiency ratings already impose similar disclosure duties, and we accept them because the alternative is a worse market. The motion extends that established logic to a category where buyer ignorance costs more, because an abandoned connected device is not a quiet failure.
[P1.5] It is an active hazard.
[P1.6] The clash is straightforward. PRO says the consumer deserves a known, guaranteed support window before paying for a connected product. CON must defend a status quo in which buyers purchase blind and absorb the security, financial, and environmental costs of abandonment they were never warned about. That is a hard side to defend.

[C1.2] Third-party libraries lose maintenance. Cloud infrastructure costs shift. Regulatory frameworks evolve. Forcing a manufacturer to print a "guaranteed" support lifespan on a box does not reveal hidden truth—it manufactures a false promise backed by legal liability.
[C1.3] That liability creates perverse incentives. A guarantee is a legally enforceable commitment. If a manufacturer prints "five years" and falls short, they face class actions and regulatory penalties. Rational manufacturers will understate—printing "two years" for a device they could support for five.
[C1.4] The label will not inform consumers; it will push manufacturers toward conservative, self-fulfilling prophecies. Devices that might have received patches for six years will be abandoned at three because the box said three, and extending support creates no obligation while failing to meet the printed guarantee creates exposure. The motion does not unlock hidden information—it punishes honesty about uncertainty.
[C1.5] The comparison to energy labels also breaks down on enforcement. Energy efficiency is testable by a lab at the point of sale. Support lifespan is a multi-year promise about future behavior, verifiable only in hindsight. And what counts as "support"?
[C1.6] Security patches? Feature updates? App compatibility? Cloud connectivity?
[C1.7] A smart TV might receive security patches for five years but lose streaming-app support in two when platforms drop older operating systems. A single number on packaging flattens that complexity into a lie of false precision.
[C1.8] The real problem is not information asymmetry. It is that connected devices create dependency on decisions manufacturers have not fully made yet. A mandate to print guarantees does not solve that—it converts irreducible uncertainty into misleading consumer confidence and calls it disclosure.
