[C1.1] The real question here is not whether low-income neighborhoods deserve good mobility. They do. The question is whether a rigid "reasonably similar reliability" mandate is the instrument that delivers it. It is not — and the people PRO wants to help are the ones who lose most when it backfires.
[C1.2] Start with the deployment problem. Robotaxi networks are expensive, fragile, and still scaling. Every dollar and every operational hour spent guaranteeing equal wait times on a twelve-minute deadhead to a low-density block is a dollar and an hour not spent expanding the fleet at all. Mandate equal reliability before the technology is mature and you don't get equity — you get a smaller, slower rollout, or operators who simply choose friendlier cities.
[C1.3] The home health aide waiting at 5am is not helped by a rule that keeps cars off the road entirely. A growing fleet that reaches her next year beats a "fair" fleet that never launches.
[C1.4] Now the analogy at the heart of PRO's case. Medallions and universal-service telephone rules came bundled with guaranteed monopoly profits — the state protected the operator's revenue, so the operator could absorb unprofitable routes. Robotaxis have no such protection. They compete with each other, with rideshare, with transit.
[C1.5] Force internal cross-subsidy onto a competitive, unprofitable business and the money comes from somewhere: higher fares on every rider, including low-income riders downtown, or exit from the market. You cannot demand universal-service obligations while offering none of the universal-service bargain.
[C1.6] Third, "reasonably similar reliability" is a metric that punishes geography rather than discrimination. Longer distances and thinner demand mean real, unavoidable cost differences. A rule that treats those as violations invites endless litigation over what "similar" means and rewards gaming, not coverage.
[C1.7] If we want cars reaching poor neighborhoods, fund the outcome directly — targeted subsidies, per-trip credits, transit partnerships — cheaply and transparently. Don't freeze early-stage economics with a mandate that mistakes a slogan for a policy.
