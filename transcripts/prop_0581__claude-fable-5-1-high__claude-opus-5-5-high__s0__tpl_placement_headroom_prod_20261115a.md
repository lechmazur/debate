[P1.1] The case turns on one asymmetry: a merger that was wrongly allowed is nearly impossible to unscramble, while a merger that was wrongly blocked costs almost nothing, because the firms can still grow by building what they wanted to buy.
[P1.2] Start with prices. When economists go back and measure what actually happened after approved mergers—hospitals, airlines, beer, cable, rental cars, consumer goods—the recurring finding is that prices rose and the promised efficiencies either never materialized or never reached consumers. Hospital consolidation is the cleanest example: merged hospitals in concentrated markets routinely raised prices by double digits while quality did not improve. The efficiencies that merging parties present to regulators are forecasts written by the people who want the deal approved.
[P1.3] The price effects are measured afterward by people with no stake. Strict enforcement means believing the second group.
[P1.4] Innovation is where the current regime does the most damage. Facebook bought Instagram and WhatsApp precisely because they were threats, and nobody seriously claims the acquisitions made those products better. Pharmaceutical firms acquire startups developing competing compounds and then quietly shelve the research—the "killer acquisition" pattern. Every one of these deals cleared.
[P1.5] A world that blocks more vertical and horizontal deals is a world where the challenger has to keep challenging, and the incumbent has to keep answering.
[P1.6] Now the cost side. Economies of scale are real, but they are typically exhausted well below the size that mergers in already-concentrated industries produce. A fourth airline merging with a third does not discover new fuel savings; it discovers pricing power. And nothing about blocking a deal prevents a firm from investing, expanding capacity, or building a distribution network organically.
[P1.7] Enforcement removes the shortcut, not the destination.
[P1.8] So weigh it honestly: documented, persistent price harms and suppressed rivals on one side; speculative, self-reported synergies on the other. That is not a close call.

[C1.8] Third, "build it yourself" ignores time and failure. Organic duplication means years of redundant investment, and struggling firms often exit rather than wait. When a weak rival is blocked from selling, its capacity doesn't compete. It disappears.
[C1.9] PRO offers cherry-picked harms. CON offers the predictable costs of a broad rule. That is the real asymmetry.
