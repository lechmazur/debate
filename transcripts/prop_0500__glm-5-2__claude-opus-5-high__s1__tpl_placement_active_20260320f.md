[P1.1] The case for underground investment rests on a simple reality: the surface is full. Every major city competes for the same horizontal strip of land to house roads, rail, power lines, water mains, sewers, and telecom cables. As populations grow and climate adaptation demands new infrastructure, that competition turns into paralysis. Surface-level construction doesn't just cost money—it disrupts commerce, severs neighborhoods, and locks cities into fragile arrangements exposed to weather and congestion.
[P1.2] The objection that boring is expensive treats the surface as free. It isn't. A surface highway commandeers land worth billions in dense urban cores. Trenching a utility line through a functioning commercial district imposes costs on every business along the route—costs that rarely appear in the boring-vs-trenching comparison.
[P1.3] When you account for land acquisition, business disruption, traffic delay, and environmental externalities, the gap between surface and underground narrows dramatically, and in many cases inverts.
[P1.4] More importantly, the trajectories point in opposite directions. Tunneling costs are declining as boring technology improves—machine diameter, automation, and ground-stabilization techniques all advance year over year. Surface land values in growing cities move in one direction: up. A tunnel built today locks in a right-of-way that will only appreciate.
[P1.5] A surface project competes for land that will only become scarcer.
[P1.6] Climate reinforces the point. Underground utilities survive floods, storms, and heat waves that cripple exposed infrastructure. Cities spending repeatedly to repair downed power lines and washed-out roads are paying a hidden premium for their surface dependence.
[P1.7] Tokyo's highway tunnels, London's Crossrail, and Paris's stormwater reservoirs aren't luxuries—they are admissions that dense cities cannot keep solving growth problems on the surface. The question isn't whether tunneling is cheap. It's whether the alternative remains viable. Increasingly, it doesn't.

[C1.2] Start with the fact PRO's case cannot survive: tunnelling costs are not falling. Crossrail overran badly and opened years late. New York's Second Avenue Subway became a byword for the most expensive subway mileage in human history. Stuttgart 21 and HS2 have escalated relentlessly.
[C1.3] Boring machines have improved for four decades while real per-kilometre costs in high-income countries have climbed. [P1.4] gets the trend exactly backwards, and with it the whole "invest now, appreciate later" logic.
[C1.4] Second, the land-scarcity premise is misdiagnosis. Cities are not full; they are badly allocated. Most dense cities devote enormous surface area to on-street parking and general traffic lanes. Converting one lane into a bus corridor or protected bikeway costs a rounding error against a tunnel and delivers capacity in months, not decades.
[C1.5] Bogotá built a metro-scale transit network with paint, concrete and buses. If your city won't reallocate a parking lane, the binding constraint is political, not geological — and a tunnel is a very costly way of avoiding an argument you'll have to win anyway.
[C1.6] Third, undergrounding is not climate armour. Sandy flooded New York's tunnels. Zhengzhou's 2021 floods drowned people in a road tunnel and a metro carriage. Water goes to the lowest point; that is where we would be putting everything.
[C1.7] Fourth, "countries" includes most of the world. For a state still extending piped water and sewerage to its periphery, heavy tunnelling investment means fewer households served per dollar. That is regressive, not visionary.
[C1.8] High costs are not a footnote to be brushed aside — they are the argument for surgical, case-by-case tunnelling where nothing else works. "Invest heavily despite the cost" is how countries end up with one glamorous tunnel and a decade of deferred everything else.
