[P1.1] Tariffs are taxes. When the price of steel, lumber, cars, or groceries jumps because of a duty at the border, American families and businesses pay it. The Constitution gave the power to impose taxes to Congress for a reason: taxation without broad, deliberative representation is tyranny by another name.
[P1.2] Allowing one president to impose sweeping tariffs unilaterally by invoking emergency turns that principle upside down. Emergency statutes were written for genuine, time-limited crises — financing terrorism, wartime shortages — not as a blank check to rewrite the entire trading system overnight. Stretching them to cover fentanyl, trade deficits, or industrial policy redefines every policy disagreement as an emergency and makes congressional authority meaningless.
[P1.3] That concentration is economically ruinous. A single threat of 25% duties can wipe out supply contracts, freeze hiring, and send markets tumbling. Farmers lose export markets to retaliation, manufacturers pay more for inputs than foreign competitors, and consumers absorb the cost. Congress legislates slowly, in public, with hearings, amendments, and compromise.
[P1.4] That friction is a feature: businesses can plan, allies can negotiate, and losers can be heard.
[P1.5] It is also more democratic. A president answers to a national base and a four-year clock, rewarding dramatic gestures. Members of Congress answer to steel towns and farm counties and port cities at the same time, forcing tradeoffs into the open. The current system encourages the worst of both worlds: sweeping presidential tariffs followed by a scramble for secretive exemptions and bailouts for favored industries.
[P1.6] Reclaiming sole authority for Congress would not end tariffs. It would end taxation by emergency decree.
