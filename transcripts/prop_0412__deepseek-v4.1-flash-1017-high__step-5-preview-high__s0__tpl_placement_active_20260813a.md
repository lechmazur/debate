[P1.1] When a television presenter is paid to praise a skincare cream, the audience is entitled to know. Moving that same pitch to a phone screen, delivered by someone viewers invite into their daily lives, does not make the commercial relationship less important. Disclosure duties should track persuasive function and scale, not the job title of the speaker.
[P1.2] Influencers with large audiences are not casual hobbyists. They are commercial actors whose recommendations can move products at television-like scale. Their advantage is intimacy: followers treat recommendations as advice from a trusted friend, not a sponsored slot. That makes an undisclosed ad more dangerous, not less.
[P1.3] A clear “ad” label preserves the audience’s ability to discount the pitch. A buried hashtag, a vague “collab,” or disclosure after the link does not.
[P1.4] “As strict as traditional media” does not require identical scripts. It requires equal substantive rigor: clear, prominent, timely disclosure before the persuasion lands; no misleading omission; and real accountability when money changes hands. Traditional media already lives with those obligations. Influencers should not get a lighter rulebook because their medium is newer or their content feels informal.
[P1.5] The objection that this is burdensome or hostile to creativity fails. Disclosure is not censorship. It does not ban endorsements or dictate scripts; it tells audiences the one fact that changes how they read everything else. The burden of a label is trivial compared with the cost to consumers of mistaking paid promotion for independent advice.
[P1.6] Large-audience influence is advertising when it is paid to sell. It should be disclosed like advertising.

[C1.1] Traditional media personalities operate inside institutions. When a television presenter is paid to praise a skincare cream, that segment has already passed through network compliance lawyers, standards-and-practices review, and advertising operations teams. “As strict as” means importing that entire apparatus—not just a visible label—onto an individual filming alone in their bedroom. That is not equal protection; it is a category error that ignores who actually bears the burden.
[C1.2] Scale is not infrastructure. A creator with two million followers may have no staff, no legal counsel, and no ad-ops clearance. They negotiate directly with brands, self-fund production, and monetize through fragmented revenue streams. Demanding the same substantive rigor and institutional accountability that traditional media provides would not level the playing field.
[C1.3] It would entrench the few creators wealthy enough to hire compliance teams while pushing everyone else toward silence or evasion.
[C1.4] Strict parity also backfires on transparency. Traditional media has natural chokepoints: broadcast licenses, network distribution, and national ad markets that regulators can reach. Influencer content is global, algorithmic, and borderless. Identical rules would not eliminate paid promotion; they would push it into direct messages, private communities, and platforms beyond easy oversight, where disclosures vanish entirely.
[C1.5] The clear labeling PRO wants would become harder to find, not easier.
[C1.6] Finally, the relationship differs in kind. A viewer watching a sponsored television segment knows they are consuming programmed content shaped by editors and producers. A follower reading a creator knows they are engaging with an individual whose informal, first-person tone is precisely what makes the recommendation feel human. Forcing broadcast-grade legal rituals onto that peer-to-peer dynamic does not build trust; it breeds cynicism and makes every genuine recommendation look scripted.
[C1.7] Honest disclosure is the goal. Identical strictness is the wrong method. It demands institutional burdens from individuals, invites regulatory arbitrage, and confuses a job title with a structural reality. I oppose the motion.
