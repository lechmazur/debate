[P1.1] Advisory citizens' assemblies are democratically gorgeous and politically powerless. A randomly selected body that mirrors the population—by age, income, geography, education—can spend months hearing experts, deliberating, and producing recommendations. And then a legislature built on donor networks, party discipline, and safe seats can file those recommendations in a drawer. The advisory model treats citizens as consultants to their own democracy.
[P1.2] Binding power on specific, scoped questions changes that relationship entirely.
[P1.3] The case rests on three pillars. First, representativeness. Sortition produces bodies that actually look like the public. Elected legislatures systematically overrepresent wealthy, male, highly educated, politically ambitious people.
[P1.4] When a citizens' assembly decides on pension reform or drug policy, the people bearing the consequences are in the room. Binding power means their deliberation becomes decision, not a suggestion that career politicians can strategically ignore.
[P1.5] Second, incentive alignment. Politicians avoid certain issues—climate policy, end-of-life care, constitutional reform—because the electoral payoff is distant and the interest-group backlash is immediate. Citizens' assemblies don't face re-election. They can make hard trade-offs that legislators structurally cannot.
[P1.6] But that capacity is wasted if it only produces advice that re-enters the same political bottleneck that created the deadlock in the first place.
[P1.7] Third, scope discipline. The proposition says specific policy questions, not general governance. An assembly can be charged with a defined question, operate under transparency rules, and face judicial review of its process. Ireland used citizens' assemblies to break deadlock on abortion and same-sex marriage.
[P1.8] They worked—but only because political actors voluntarily chose to act. Voluntary uptake is not a system. It is luck.
[P1.9] The counterargument will be accountability: random citizens aren't elected, so how can they hold binding power? But accountability doesn't require elections—it requires transparency, clear mandates, and sound process. Juries decide fates in criminal law through sortition. We already trust randomly selected citizens with liberty and life.
[P1.10] Binding power on scoped policy questions is a lesser leap and a more democratic one.

[C1.1] The central flaw in binding sortition is not that ordinary citizens are incompetent; it is that binding power severs decision from answerability. A randomly selected assembly may resemble the public, but it is not elected, cannot be removed, and dissolves once its decision is made. If the policy fails or imposes unexpected costs, voters cannot vote the decision-makers out. Transparency helps, but it does not replace the ability to hold power accountable at the next election.
[C1.2] PRO’s “specific questions” do not solve this; they magnify it. Specific policy questions are never isolated. A binding decision on drug policy, pensions, or land use collides with budgets, enforcement capacity, rights, and other commitments. Elected governments must integrate those trade-offs across society.
[C1.3] A single-issue assembly can pursue the best answer to its narrow brief while externalizing consequences onto everyone else. That is not scope discipline; it is fragmented power.
[C1.4] The jury analogy also fails. Juries apply settled law to particular facts under judges, rules of evidence, and appeal. They do not choose the law itself. A binding citizens’ assembly makes value choices and distributional decisions that are closer to legislation.
[C1.5] Even with transparent hearings, the agenda, question, evidence, facilitation, and voting rules are set by conveners—often politicians or consultants—who can shape outcomes while the assembly absorbs the legitimacy.
[C1.6] Advisory assemblies are valuable because they preserve the best of both: informed public judgment can guide, pressure, and legitimize, while elected bodies remain responsible for final choice and correction. Ireland’s experience shows assemblies can break deadlock precisely by making it harder for politicians to ignore public judgment, without stripping legislatures of responsibility. Binding power would turn a useful consultative tool into an unaccountable veto point, vulnerable to manipulation, participation bias, and backlash when a small, temporary body imposes irreversible decisions.
[C1.7] Keep citizens’ assemblies influential, not final.

An assembly with one question can maximize its narrow objective while leaving budgets, enforcement, rights, and conflicting programs to everyone else. PRO’s carbon-tax example shows the danger: a binding assembly could impose a tax without owning the compensating budget, industrial strategy, or mandate to sustain it. Judicial review does not solve that; courts review legality and process, not whether the assembly underestimated fiscal knock-on effects or ignored implementation capacity.
[C2.4] PRO says manipulation favors binding power because politicians cannot cherry-pick [P2.5]. That mistakes the locus of manipulation. In a binding regime, the fight moves upstream to the question wording, selection rules, expert list, facilitation, and voting threshold. [C2.5] Advisory assemblies reduce this risk because elected officials still must publicly justify adopting or rejecting recommendations.
[C2.6] On Ireland [P2.6], PRO calls voluntary uptake luck. But the legitimacy came from assemblies feeding into institutions with authority to act—referendums and parliament. That is the safer design: assemblies can break deadlock by clarifying informed public judgment, while final decisions remain where responsibility can be assigned. If the recommendation is bad, voters know whom to blame.
[C2.7] Binding sortition turns temporary participants into final decision-makers who owe no future account. The bottleneck is real; the remedy should not be a one-shot veto beyond democratic correction.
