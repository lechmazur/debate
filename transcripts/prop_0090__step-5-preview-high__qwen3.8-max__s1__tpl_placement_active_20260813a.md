[P1.1] Driving is one of the few everyday tasks where a two-second distraction can turn into a crash. When climate, defrost, and hazard controls are buried in a touchscreen, drivers must look at a display and navigate a menu to perform actions that are urgent by nature. A physical button can be found by feel, without taking eyes off the road. A flat glass panel cannot.
[P1.2] Core controls are time-critical. When fog begins forming on a windshield, the driver should hit defrost immediately—not swipe through a climate submenu, wait for an animation, or adjust fan direction on screen. A hazard light after sudden braking or a disabled vehicle should be one unambiguous press, not a search through icons. Even routine climate changes matter: a fogged window, overheating cabin, or freezing windshield can degrade visibility and grip.
[P1.3] These are safety operations, not entertainment preferences.
[P1.4] Touchscreens also fail in ways physical controls generally do not. Glare, rain, gloves, fingerprints, low light, lag, or a software freeze can turn a simple adjustment into a distraction. A mechanical switch still works when the infotainment system reboots. Distinct shapes, textures, and fixed positions let the hand recognize controls by memory, which is especially important for older drivers, people with reduced vision, and anyone wearing gloves or facing limited dexterity.
[P1.5] The answer is not rejecting technology. It is allocating technology correctly. Screens are excellent for navigation, media, and settings that can change over time. Core driving functions should remain fixed, tactile, and immediate.
[P1.6] Automakers can still modernize interiors and update software behind the scenes without making urgent safety controls depend on visual navigation.
[P1.7] Physical buttons reduce eyes-off-road time, work reliably, and match how humans actually act under pressure. Routing core driving functions mainly through touchscreens sacrifices safety and usability for visual minimalism.

[C1.1] The proposition treats hardware as the safety mechanism, but the real question is whether drivers can reach critical functions quickly, accurately, and under stress. A well-designed touchscreen can do that, while a badly placed row of buttons can fail the same test. Modern interfaces can give climate, defrost, and hazard functions permanent, high-priority places on the screen: large one-tap icons, fixed positions, haptic feedback, high-contrast modes, and voice shortcuts. If the system is designed properly, the driver is not hunting through menus; the function is already visible and can be activated with a glance, a tap, or a spoken command.
[C1.2] Physical buttons are not automatically safer. They occupy limited panel space, force designers to hard-code priorities, and vary from model to model. A driver who switches cars may need to search for the right switch just as much as for an icon. Buttons can wear, stick, break, or be misread in poor light, and a dense cluster of unlabeled switches can create its own eyes-down distraction.
[C1.3] A touchscreen, by contrast, can adapt: showing defrost first in winter, making hazard controls larger during an emergency, increasing text size for older drivers, or simplifying layout in rain. It can also be improved after sale through updates instead of requiring new hardware.
[C1.4] The stronger standard is functional redundancy, not mandatory buttons. Automakers can route core functions mainly through screens while preserving safety with persistent shortcuts, steering-wheel controls, voice activation, and fail-safe defaults. If a display freezes, hazard lights and defrost should remain available through separate circuits or backup commands, but that is an engineering requirement, not a reason to freeze interface design in mechanical form.
[C1.5] Judges should reject the proposition because it protects one input method instead of demanding better outcomes. Touchscreens, when engineered for driving, can be faster, more adaptable, and more accessible than physical buttons.
