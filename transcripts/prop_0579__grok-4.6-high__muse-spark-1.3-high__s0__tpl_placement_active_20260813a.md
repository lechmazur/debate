[P1.1] Fraud thrives because identity is easy to copy and expensive to check. We reuse Social Security numbers as if they were secrets, photocopy licenses into every file, and run remote services on mailed papers and quizzes that criminals answer first. That is how tax refund fraud, benefit theft, and account takeovers scale. Every extra photocopy is another breach and another opening for a thief.
[P1.2] An optional national digital ID lets you present a signed, revocable proof of who you are—or of one attribute, age or eligibility—without handing a permanent dossier to every counterparty.
[P1.3] Access follows the same logic. The people locked out of banking, healthcare portals, and public programs are often those without a current driver's license, without spare time to visit an office, or without papers that survived a move or a disaster. A parent applying for benefits from a rural town should not need a bus ride and a folder of originals. A phone-based option you enroll in once and use remotely does not replace paper; it stops treating paper as the only door.
[P1.4] Optionality is the safeguard: nobody is forced into a digital trail. Those who opt in choose fewer trips, fewer denials, and fewer chances to be impersonated.
[P1.5] Surveillance and breach harm are not waiting for this program. They already sit in credit bureaus, state databases, and every merchant that keeps a license image. A digital ID can disclose less, expire, and refuse secondary tracking if law and architecture require it. The comparison is not privacy paradise versus a camera state.
[P1.6] It is a leaky analog system that already surveils poorly and excludes many, versus an optional tool that shrinks impersonation and opens services more than it adds harm. Design can be constrained. The fraud and the locked doors are not hypothetical; the analog past never protected anyone.
