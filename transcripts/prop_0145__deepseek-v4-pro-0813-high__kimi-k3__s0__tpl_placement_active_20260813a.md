[P1.1] Adolescence is a controlled experiment in becoming an adult. Always-on location tracking and private-message monitoring as a default rule fail because they treat a teenager’s privacy as something to be earned rather than something needed for judgment.
[P1.2] A 15-year-old who decides when to update her parents, weighs a risky invitation, and calls home when plans go wrong is building the skill parents say they want. Location tracking replaces that practice with a digital leash. A 16-year-old who is always watched never learns to be accountable; he learns to be findable. Those are not the same.
[P1.3] Private messages are where teenagers rehearse intimacy, conflict, embarrassment, and asking for help. Monitoring them by default turns awkward conversation into potential evidence. The predictable result is not openness; it is second accounts, vanishing messages, and selective disclosure. A teenager who knows Mom reads everything will not type “I’m scared.” She will stop typing to Mom at all.
[P1.4] The central safety mechanism in adolescence is voluntary disclosure. A teenager in trouble must choose to tell an adult. Default surveillance poisons that choice because it announces that the teenager is not trusted until verified. It makes the parent an inspector, not an ally.
[P1.5] The teenager then treats concealment as self-defense, and the parent loses the information the system was meant to collect.
[P1.6] This is not naive parenting. With a concrete signal—threats, sharp change, dangerous company—targeted checking is justified. But absent that specific concern, the default should be conversation before surveillance. Default suspicion buys hypothetical safety with real trust and leaves teenagers less prepared and less candid.
