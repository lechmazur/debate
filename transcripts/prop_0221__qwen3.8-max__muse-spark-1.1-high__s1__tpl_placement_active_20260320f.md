[P1.1] The central question is not whether both policies can help, but which buys more learning per dollar. High-dosage tutoring wins because it converts dollars into targeted instructional time for the students furthest behind, while class-size reduction buys a smaller average change spread across every seat.
[P1.2] High-dosage tutoring means three or more sessions a week, in pairs or small groups, using assessment-driven practice and immediate correction. That design attacks the bottleneck in K-8 reading and math: children fail to decode, to build number sense, and to fill missing prerequisites. A tutor can spot a second grader confusing vowel teams, adjust the next drill, and get a response every few minutes. In a smaller class of 25 rather than 30, the teacher still faces the same spread of needs; many students get a little more attention, but the students who need daily corrective instruction may still be hidden.
[P1.3] The cost arithmetic favors tutoring because it can be focused. You do not need a new room, a new lead teacher, and a new schedule for every one-student reduction; you pay for targeted staff time. Schools can use trained paraprofessionals, retired teachers, college students, and proven software, with coaches and short scripted lessons. If a tutor sees two groups of three for a half-hour each day, dozens of students receive intensive intervention over a term.
[P1.4] Class-size reduction, by contrast, is one of the most expensive ways to buy small average gains because it raises payroll and facilities across the system.
[P1.5] The comparison becomes sharper when funding is scarce. Tutoring can be assigned to the lowest performers and measured with short diagnostics before and after. Smaller classes are a blunt instrument. For the same budget, high-dosage tutoring produces more concentrated practice, more feedback, and therefore larger reading and math gains per dollar.

[C1.1] The PRO case only works if you assume tutoring at national scale looks like a small controlled trial. It doesn't.
[C1.2] At scale, high-dosage tutoring is not a cheap add-on. To give every struggling K-8 reader and math student three real sessions a week, you must recruit, train, schedule and retain an army of tutors equal to a quarter of the teacher workforce, find rooms constantly, pull kids out of science or core instruction, and chase absenteeism. Districts that tried post-COVID found the model collapsed into twice-a-week, irregular attendance with college volunteers cycling out mid-semester. The per-dollar cost is not a stipend and a script; it is coordinators, substitutes to cover pull-outs, curriculum coaches, and endless retraining for low-wage, high-turnover staff.
[C1.3] Cheap tutors produce cheap results.
[C1.4] Class-size reduction, by contrast, buys better instruction all day, in both reading and math, for every child. A class that goes from 28 to 20 doesn't get "a little more attention" — it crosses a threshold where the teacher can actually run guided reading groups every day, catch arithmetic misconceptions in the moment, cut discipline interruptions that currently steal 20 minutes a period, and keep new teachers from burning out. Those minutes compound 30 hours a week, 36 weeks a year, not 90 minutes of add-on.
[C1.5] And it can be targeted. No district has to shrink every middle-school elective; the efficient move is K-3 and high-poverty schools, where the learning returns per teacher are largest. Same budget that would fund inconsistent pull-out tutoring for 20% of kids funds a permanent improvement in core instruction for 100% of them.
[C1.6] Measured honestly — sustained gains in both subjects across all students per real dollar spent to run it year after year — shrinking the core classroom beats renting a shadow system. Per dollar, that is the larger, more durable gain in reading and math.
