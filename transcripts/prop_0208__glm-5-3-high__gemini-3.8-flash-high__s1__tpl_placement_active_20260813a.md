[P1.1] A public university's budget is a moral document. Every dollar spent is a bet on some student's future, and the question before us is whether we should keep doubling down on bets that predictably fail. I say no: shift resources from chronically low-completion programs toward shorter vocational and technical pathways.
[P1.2] Start with the human cost. Programs with persistently low completion rates are not neutral offerings; they are machines for generating debt without credentials. A student who spends three years in a program and leaves without a degree carries the loans, the lost wages, and the transcript scars, but walks away with none of the earnings premium. Low completion is not a phantom statistic—it is a warehouse of individual tragedies.
[P1.3] When a university knows a program graduates a minority of its entrants year after year, continuing to recruit into it is not access; it is a trap with a brochure.
[P1.4] Second, the opportunity cost. Public universities operate under hard budget constraints—state appropriations are flat or falling in real terms almost everywhere. Resources soaked up by low-yield programs are resources unavailable for the pathways where demand and labor-market need actually meet: nursing, welding, advanced manufacturing, IT credentials, allied health. These fields feature shorter time-to-credential, strong regional employment, and completion rates that beat long-haul bachelor's tracks precisely because the finish line is closer and the payoff is visible.
[P1.5] Third, the equity argument runs my way, not my opponent's. The students most harmed by low-completion programs are disproportionately first-generation and low-income students—the ones who can least afford a failed attempt. Vocational pathways offer them a credible ladder: months, not many years, and a marketable skill at the end. Defending the status quo in the name of access protects the architecture of attrition.
[P1.6] Note what I am not arguing: abolition of academic breadth. This is a reallocation from programs that fail students to pathways that finish them. The measure of a public university is not the catalog it prints but the lives it completes. Prioritize accordingly.
