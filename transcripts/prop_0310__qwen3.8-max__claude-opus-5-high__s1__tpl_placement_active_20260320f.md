[P1.1] The proposition asks whether schools should guarantee baseline competence in three domains every adult faces: feeding yourself, maintaining a home, and managing money. They should, because these are not hobbies; they are prerequisites for independence. Leaving them optional means the students who most need them are least likely to get them.
[P1.2] The winning question is simple: if these skills are universal, high-stakes, and teachable at school, then core instruction is justified. They are universal because nearly everyone eats, pays for housing, and handles income or debt. They are high-stakes because mistakes are expensive and often invisible until damage is done: a payday loan, a ruined apartment deposit, a diet built on takeout because basic cooking feels mysterious. They are teachable because schools already organize sequential, practical learning; a semester of personal finance, a unit on safe cooking, or a workshop on fixing a leaky faucet are not exotic.
[P1.3] The deepest problem with the optional model is inequity. Electives reward students with time, confident role models, or parents who can teach these things at home. An opt-in system effectively outsources essential education to families, and families differ dramatically in wealth, time, and knowledge. Others learn by trial and error, and trial-and-error in finance or housing can mean overdraft fees, predatory terms, or unsafe repairs.
[P1.4] Core life-skills courses act like a seatbelt: they do not guarantee perfect decisions, but they reduce the cost of ignorance when people are most vulnerable.
[P1.5] This does not require turning schools into trade academies or abandoning literature and calculus. It requires recognizing that a graduate who cannot budget a paycheck, cook a simple meal, or stop a small leak has been underserved by a system that claims to prepare them for life. The proposition is not about adding frills. It is about closing the gap between academic completion and adult readiness.

[P2.1] My opponent says “every hour declared ‘core’ is an hour taken from something else,” but that assumes life skills must become giant new subjects. A finance unit can live inside math, cooking safety inside health, basic repairs inside science or shop. The question is whether graduates get a guaranteed baseline, not whether schools invent three separate academies.
[P2.2] The “just-in-time” claim gets the psychology backwards. The week you need a skill is the worst time to learn it: a leasing office is full of pressure, a leak becomes expensive after damage, and eating habits are formed long before a person has a kitchen. Self-teaching at twenty-six works only if the mistake is cheap, the information is clear, and there is no penalty for delay. Core instruction gives vocabulary and habits before the high-stakes moment.
[P2.3] Knowing what a security deposit, APR, or lease penalty means is not trivia; it is the difference between a preventable fee and a month of crisis. A person can read a contract and still miss the clause that hurts them if no one taught them what to look for. Academic capacity is necessary, but it is not the practiced judgment to use it when money, housing, or health are at stake.
[P2.4] On equity, optional availability is not neutral. A student working after school, caring for siblings, or lacking a kitchen at home is least able to seek out an elective or club. “Universal availability” quietly becomes availability to the already advantaged. Core removes that selection effect. The grading objection also proves too much: we do not make algebra optional because assessment can be harsh; we design assessment to support competence.
[P2.5] Life-skills core can be competency-based and non-punitive—demonstrate a budget, cook simple meals, identify safe repair steps—without creating new failure traps. If the risk is tracking, the remedy is inclusive universal design, not leaving essentials to chance.
