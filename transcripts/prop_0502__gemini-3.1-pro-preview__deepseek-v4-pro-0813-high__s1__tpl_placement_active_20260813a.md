[C1.1] The Antarctic analogy collapses on the most basic difference: Antarctica is an uninhabited landmass with suspended sovereignty; the Arctic is a populated ocean bordered by sovereign states, indigenous nations, and functioning legal systems. Importing an Antarctic-style treaty would turn Arctic peoples and coastal democracies into wards of a distant scientific regime, overriding the local accountability that makes environmental and safety governance legitimate.
[C1.2] The motion also paints a false picture of a lawless Arctic scramble. The region is already governed by the Law of the Sea framework: coastal states must justify extended seabed claims with geological evidence, not flags; maritime boundaries are negotiated; and shipping, fisheries, and search-and-rescue agreements already exist. Canada, Denmark, Norway, Russia, and the United States are working through those rules. That is not anarchy; it is ordinary international legal process.
[C1.3] A binding Antarctic-style treaty is also implausible on the exact points that matter. Russia will not demilitarize its Arctic fleet. Canada and Denmark will not surrender resource rights, and the United States will not subordinate Arctic navigation or defense to a global body. The realistic outcome is not binding protection but either a dead conference or a symbolic text that the strongest states ignore.
[C1.4] A failed grand treaty would damage the practical institutions already functioning in the Arctic.
[C1.5] Environmental protection does not require globalizing sovereignty. Coastal states have the strongest possible incentive to prevent spills in their own waters: the oil would reach their communities, fisheries, and coastlines first. National and regional regulation can impose binding liability, while a remote treaty body would create vetoes, lowest-common-denominator standards, and no enforcement capacity. The Antarctic system works because original claimants agreed to freeze claims on an empty continent; the Arctic has permanent populations with rights to self-government and sustainable use that a copied treaty would erase.
[C1.6] The choice is not Antarctic treaty or chaos. It is between a realistic, evolving law-of-the-sea order led by Arctic states and an ill-fitting Antarctic model that would likely fail to bind the powerful and would silence the people who actually live there.
