[P1.1] Tax exemption is a public subsidy, not a blank check. When the state exempts an institution from property and income taxes—and lets donors deduct contributions from their taxable income—every other taxpayer makes up the difference. Society strikes that bargain because charitable and spiritual institutions provide shared community goods beyond electoral combat. Once a religious organization operates instead as an overt partisan actor, the justification for public subsidy vanishes.
[P1.2] Notice the precise boundary here. This rule does not penalize houses of worship for preaching moral truths on contested issues like poverty, war, or human dignity. Moral witness is spiritual work. Overt partisan action is fundamentally different: endorsing candidates, coordinating with party machines, or dedicating institutional resources to elect a specific ticket.
[P1.3] When a religious body operates like a political action committee, it should be taxed like one.
[P1.4] Anything else violates basic democratic fairness. Secular charities are strictly barred from partisan campaigning if they wish to keep tax-deductible status, and political campaigns cannot offer donors a charitable write-off. Granting religious institutions a special privilege to run partisan campaigns with tax-subsidized dollars creates two injustices: it forces taxpayers of all faiths and no faith to bankroll candidates they oppose, and it treats secular charities as second-class citizens.
[P1.5] Worse, erasing this boundary creates a disastrous campaign-finance loophole. Because religious institutions typically enjoy both tax-deductible donations and minimal financial disclosure, letting them act as overt partisan vehicles invites wealthy operatives to funnel campaign cash through religious shells—securing a tax cut and donor anonymity at once. That does not protect faith; it turns sanctuaries into dark-money conduits.
[P1.6] Withdrawing a tax subsidy is not censorship. Religious organizations remain completely free to endorse candidates, mobilize for political parties, and run partisan operations. They simply must do so on their own dime, under the same tax rules as every other partisan actor in a democracy.

The government would be imposing a content‑based penalty on religious speech, a move the Supreme Court has struck down.
[C1.2] Consider the practical impossibility of the line the proposition draws. “Overt partisan” is a judgment call, not a bright‑line rule. Which sermon is moral witness and which is partisan endorsement? Which rally is spiritual mobilization and which is campaign activity? The answer will always depend on who is asking.
[C1.3] History shows that when the IRS is given power to police religious political activity, it quickly becomes a tool for targeting disfavored faiths—selective revocation would punish minority religions while leaving majority ones untouched, violating both free exercise and equal protection.
[C1.4] The claim that secular charities already face stricter rules misses the point. Secular organizations can organize under a different tax category that permits limited political work without donor deductions; they are not forced to surrender a unique status that protects the free exercise of religion. Stripping churches of exemption forces a binary choice that no other institution faces: speak politically or lose a privilege rooted in religious liberty.
[C1.5] Moreover, donor deductions are a personal benefit; revoking them punishes individuals who contribute out of faith, not just the institution. The result is a double penalty: the church loses its exemption and its members lose a tax break, a punishment that extends far beyond the political act in question.
[C1.6] A better answer is to enforce existing campaign‑finance limits on all actors, including religious ones, without using tax status as a bludgeon. The proposition creates a chilling effect, invites politicized enforcement, and undermines the very principle of church‑state separation it purports to protect.
