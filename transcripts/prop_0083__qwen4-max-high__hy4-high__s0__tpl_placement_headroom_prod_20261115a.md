[P1.1] A dating profile is not an advertisement for a mood; it is a claim about a particular person. When someone swipes right, they accept a proposition: this face exists on a body I might sit across from, and these words came from a mind I might spend an evening with. AI-generated photos and AI-written conversation break that link while preserving its appearance. That is deception — not lying outright, but arranging evidence so another person draws a false conclusion they had no way to check.
[P1.2] Notice the asymmetry. Disclosure costs the user one badge, one toggle, a sentence. Non-disclosure costs the other person a train fare, an evening, and the specific humiliation of discovering they were charmed by a language model. When one side's burden is trivial and the other's is not, the default belongs to the party who can cheaply prevent the harm.
[P1.3] The obvious reply is that everyone edits. But there is a bright line between correcting and generating. Cropping a photo, fixing lighting, or fixing a typo alters how your own signal is transmitted. Generating a face or outsourcing your wit replaces the signal with a different one.
[P1.4] A flattering chin angle is still your chin. A synthesized jawline is nobody's.
[P1.5] And the platform is the right place to enforce this, because it is the only actor that can see generation metadata and the only actor whose revenue depends on users believing profiles are real. Trust is the product. A marketplace that lets counterfeit goods circulate unlabeled isn't neutral; it is selling the counterfeit.
[P1.6] Undisclosed synthesis should carry the label. Nobody is banned from using these tools; they are only required to let the other person know. Users can then decide for themselves — which is exactly what a no-disclosure default quietly takes away.

[C1.2] That collapses fraud, styling, accessibility, and ordinary self-presentation into one moral category.
[C1.3] The bright line PRO offers in [P1.3] is not bright. Modern portraits are already generated in part: portrait-mode blur, face-aware lighting, blemish removal, background replacement. Text is assisted too: spellcheck, predictive phrases, templates, translation, a friend suggesting an opener. If a non-native speaker uses AI to say, accurately, that she likes hiking and wants to meet Saturday, the thought is hers; only the phrasing improves.
[C1.4] If a stylized avatar still resembles them, the signal is curated, not counterfeited. "A synthesized jawline is nobody's" proves only that false faces are wrong, not that every generated pixel or polished line is a false person.
[C1.5] The "one badge" in [P1.2] is not cheap either. A mandatory deception label marks the whole profile suspect, discloses private facts about disability, language, body image, or creative preference, and will be demanded for harmless assistance. Worse, platforms cannot reliably know. Metadata is stripped by screenshots and re-encoding; assisted text is edited, paraphrased, or typed from memory; detectors are fallible.
[C1.6] Enforcement becomes privacy-invasive surveillance or arbitrary accusation.
[C1.7] Trust is protected better by concrete rules: ban impersonation, materially false photos, bait-and-switch appearances, and automated chat no human is reading. Let platforms require authenticity at the meeting point and punish undisclosed impersonation. But treating all undisclosed AI assistance as "deceptive" turns an anti-fraud norm into a vague, stigmatizing, unenforceable label.
