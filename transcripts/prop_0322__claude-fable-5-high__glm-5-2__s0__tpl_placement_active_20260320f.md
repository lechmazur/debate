[P1.1] Host-read ads command premium prices for one reason: listeners believe the host actually recorded them. Advertisers pay two to three times more for host-reads than programmatic spots precisely because a familiar voice saying "I use this product" carries the weight of a personal recommendation from someone the listener spends hours with every week. When that voice is synthesized by AI and the host never spoke the words—perhaps never even read them—the entire transaction rests on a false premise. The listener is being deceived about the single fact that makes the ad valuable.
[P1.2] The principle here is not new; only the technology is. Advertising law already requires disclosure of material facts a reasonable consumer would want to know before trusting an endorsement. Paid influencers must disclose sponsorships. Testimonials can't be fabricated.
[P1.3] A mandatory disclosure rule for AI voice replication simply closes a loophole that technology opened faster than regulation could follow. Nobody is banned from anything—creators can clone their voices all they like. They just have to say so.
[P1.4] Notice why voluntary norms won't work. Synthetic voices are now indistinguishable to the human ear, which means listeners cannot protect themselves, and honest creators cannot differentiate themselves. The economics reward silence: a host can license their voice model, "record" fifty ads a day, and pocket the authenticity premium without the authenticity. Without a legal floor, the disclosing creator loses to the concealing one, and trust in the entire medium erodes—harming the many honest creators most.
[P1.5] The burden is trivially small: a sentence of disclosure, the audio equivalent of "#ad." Weigh that against the harm: consumers making purchasing decisions based on endorsements the endorser never voiced, and a creeping normalization of synthetic speech passed off as human sincerity.
[P1.6] If disclosure genuinely doesn't matter to listeners, it costs nothing. If it does matter—and the price advertisers pay says it does—then concealment is fraud by another name. Either way, disclosure wins.
