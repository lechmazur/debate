[P1.1] The core problem is a conflict of interest that no amount of monitoring can cure. When a single company owns the marketplace and also sells products inside it, that company writes the rules, sees everyone's data, controls the search ranking, sets the fees, and approves or rejects its rivals—then competes against those very rivals. That is not a market. That is a referee who also owns one of the teams, keeps the other teams' playbooks, and decides which plays are legal.
[P1.2] My case rests on three claims.
[P1.3] First, the incentive to self-preference is structural, not accidental. A platform owner maximizes profit by steering users toward its own apps, burying competitors a few ranks down, timing approvals slowly, and quietly using rivals' sales data to clone their best features. These levers are subtle, continuous, and individually deniable. You cannot regulate away an incentive that is baked into ownership.
[P1.4] Second, behavioral remedies have repeatedly failed against exactly this incentive. "Fair ranking" rules require regulators to second-guess opaque algorithms the platform redesigns constantly. Conduct decrees produce years of litigation over whether a tweak counts as a violation. The watched party always knows more than the watcher.
[P1.5] Structural separation is the only remedy that removes the motive instead of policing the symptom—split the store operator from the first-party developer arm, and the gatekeeper no longer profits from sabotaging tenants.
[P1.6] Third, separation expands the very thing platforms claim to deliver: innovation and consumer choice. When developers trust that ranking and approval are neutral, more of them invest, compete, and build. A neutral marketplace is a more valuable marketplace. The losers are not consumers; the losers are the artificial advantages the owner currently grants itself.
[P1.7] The burden CON must meet is severe. They must show that letting the same entity be judge, landlord, data-holder, and competitor somehow protects users better than removing that conflict. I'll argue the opposite: as long as the operator competes on its own platform, self-preferencing is the rational, profitable, and nearly invisible default. The clean fix is to separate the roles.
