[P1.1] Single-family-only zoning is not a neutral planning tool that merely needs tweaking at the margins—it is a blanket legal prohibition on duplexes, triplexes, and small apartment buildings across the majority of residential land in most American cities. In places like Los Angeles or the Twin Cities before reform, that meant seventy to eighty percent of land zoned so that only one housing type could ever be built there, no matter how badly a neighborhood needed more homes near jobs and transit.
[P1.2] Incremental density bonuses leave that prohibition standing and merely bribe developers with extra height or reduced parking if they jump through discretionary hoops. Bans go to the root: they change the default rule so that modest multi-unit housing is simply legal everywhere, the same way single-family homes are legal everywhere.
[P1.3] That distinction matters because density bonus programs are voluntary, project-by-project, and discretionary. A developer must apply, often face a public hearing, absorb legal risk, and hope a planning commission doesn't cave to neighborhood objectors. Each project becomes a political fight. A wholesale ban on single-family-only districts removes the fight entirely—triplexes become as-of-right, just like the single-family homes are today.
[P1.4] That is the difference between a rule that occasionally produces a project and a rule that reliably produces a market.
[P1.5] Scale is the whole point here. Cities facing housing shortages don't have a "some infill here and there" problem; they have a "we need tens of thousands of new units and don't have decades to wait for case-by-case approvals" problem. California's incremental bonus and ADU programs, layered for years, moved the needle far more slowly than blanket upzoning did in places like Minneapolis or Auckland, where legalizing multi-unit housing citywide produced measurable, broad-based construction gains almost immediately.
[P1.6] Incrementalism also entrenches exclusion precisely where it's most needed to end: the wealthiest, most transit-rich neighborhoods that fight hardest against every discretionary density bonus application. A ban forecloses that veto. That is why PRO's path—full elimination of single-family-only zoning—is the only version of this policy that actually delivers on housing affordability goals at [clipped]

[C1.1] Banning single-family-only zoning outright mistakes permission for production. Making three units legal on every lot does not make three affordable units appear on every lot. What appears is what pencils out: high-rent smallplexes in desirable neighborhoods, financed by whoever can pay most for the land.
[C1.2] Incremental density bonuses understand that difference. They trade added value for public value. Allow an extra floor or waive parking, but only near transit, only with setbacks and infrastructure review, often only with below-market units locked in. The city gets homes it needs where it can support them.
[C1.3] A blanket ban gives the value away for free and hopes affordability trickles down.
[C1.4] That hope is misplaced. When every single-family lot becomes a potential multi-unit site overnight, land prices adjust to that potential. Modest starter homes become teardown targets. The affordable single-family rental down the street gets scraped for luxury duplexes that rent for more per bedroom, not less.
[C1.5] Longtime owners face rising assessments and investor knock-ons. The neighborhoods this approach claims to open up become the first to be speculated on.
[C1.6] Blanket bans also ignore physical reality. Sewers, stormwater, street width, school capacity, and fire access were not designed uniformly. Targeted bonuses steer density to corridors and transit nodes that can absorb it. Citywide as-of-right conversion scatters it where capacity is thinnest and opposition to needed infrastructure spending is fiercest.
[C1.7] Finally, blunt mandates break politically what they cannot build physically. They nationalize every block fight into one citywide fight, provoke lawsuits, ballot reversals, and council repeals, freezing construction while lawyers argue. Incremental reform survives because it respects homeowners while adding homes.
[C1.8] Keep single-family districts as one tool, and use sharp, conditional bonuses to add density where it serves. That builds affordable supply instead of just legalizing luxury.
