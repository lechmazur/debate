[P1.1] Loneliness and youth violence are not primarily information problems; they are attachment, status, and belonging problems. A teenager does not join a fight because he failed to spot manipulative online content. He often does so because he has no team that needs him, no coach who notices he vanished, no stage or studio where his reputation is built through skill rather than risk. Public funding for sports, arts, and clubs attacks that mechanism directly.
[P1.2] It buys recurring, face-to-face membership: the same peers, same adults, same place, multiple days a week. That is the strongest known social vaccine: structured belonging with accountable adults.
[P1.3] The mechanism matters. A teen on a basketball team or in a theatre group cannot scroll alone; they learn conflict through a missed pass or a dropped cue and are made to repair it in person. They acquire status from practice and performance, not from fighting for clicks. They fill the after-school hours when violence and online harm often concentrate.
[P1.4] And because public funding reduces fees, transport, and equipment barriers, it reaches isolated, low-income youth who are least likely to benefit from a media-literacy lesson they may never attend or internalise.
[P1.5] Social-media literacy campaigns alone remain largely cognitive and individual. They tell young people to interpret content more critically, but they do not give them somewhere to belong when the screen is off. A lonely young person can be highly media-literate and still have no team. A violent young person can know exactly how algorithms provoke them and still lack a safer route to respect.
[P1.6] Literacy may reduce vulnerability to harmful content; it cannot replace the missing protective infrastructure.
[P1.7] The stronger tool changes the social environment, not just its interpretation. Fund the gym, art room, and club bus; treat media literacy as a supplement, not the primary strategy.
