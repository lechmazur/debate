[P1.1] Compensation is a market price for labor, not a reward for a passport stamp on a Zoom call. When an employee leaves a high-cost headquarters city for a significantly cheaper region, the rational and fair response is to adjust pay to the new local market. That is the only system that respects both economics and equity.
[P1.2] First, labor is priced locally. A headquarters band in San Francisco or London reflects the cost of attracting talent in that specific market: its housing, taxes, and competing employers. When a remote worker relocates to a region where those competitive pressures are lower, the market price for their labor changes. Paying headquarters rates regardless of location treats salary as a pension rather than a wage.
[P1.3] It ignores that the same job carries a different opportunity cost and market clearing price.
[P1.4] Second, the move already delivers a massive real-terms windfall. Consider an employee earning $160,000 in New York who relocates to a smaller city where equivalent housing costs half as much. Even with a location adjustment to $130,000, their disposable income and standard of living can rise sharply. The adjustment captures some arbitrage for the employer, yet the worker keeps a substantial quality-of-life gain.
[P1.5] Expecting both the cheap location and the premium salary is expecting to eat the surplus twice.
[P1.6] Third, this is about internal equity. Headquarters staff bear higher living costs, often greater office overhead, and stronger competitive pressure from local employers. If remote workers in low-cost areas receive identical pay without those burdens, the company effectively penalizes loyalty and penalizes physical presence. That erodes morale and creates a two-tier workforce defined by geography, not contribution.
[P1.7] Location-adjusted pay does not punish mobility; it prices it honestly. It lets workers enjoy cheaper lives while ensuring employers do not subsidize arbitrage. The alternative asks the company to pretend that labor markets and cost structures do not exist. They do exist, and pay should reflect them.
