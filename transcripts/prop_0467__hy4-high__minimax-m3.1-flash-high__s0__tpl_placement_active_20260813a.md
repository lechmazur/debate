[P1.1] A state that bombs a power station can be answered in self-defense; it should not gain immunity by using malware instead of missiles. The armed-attack threshold should follow consequences, not weapon pedigree. This is about attacks, not routine intrusions: operations causing death, physical destruction, or prolonged disabling of systems on which civilian life depends. Critical infrastructure is where those effects converge.
[P1.2] Code that collapses a winter grid, disables hospital backup power, corrupts water treatment, or opens a dam gate can coerce a population as effectively as explosives. Treating such attacks as equivalent to armed attacks under international law gives equal effects equal legal gravity.
[P1.3] Nor does classification make war automatic. It creates lawful options, constrained by necessity, distinction, attribution, and proportionality. A victim might disable the responsible military cyber unit, neutralize launch infrastructure, or in grave cases strike the military assets directing the operation. The point is removing the source of coercion and restoring deterrence.
[P1.4] Proportional military response is a ceiling and a discipline, not a blank check.
[P1.5] The current ambiguity rewards the aggressor. If blackouts, contaminated water, or paralyzed ports are always below the line, an adversary can impose strategic harm while keeping its own forces insulated. Ambiguity invites probing and salami-slicing: one outage, one compromised safety system, one port closure, each defended as "not quite war." A clear rule tells planners before deployment that attacking civilian life-support systems may trigger legitimate defense, and gives governments reason to improve attribution, evidence-sharing, and disciplined targeting.
[P1.6] Warnings about escalation have the arrow backward. Known thresholds and proportionality bound the contest; legal silence leaves victims choosing between doing nothing or acting outside a settled framework. Civilian protection also favors this rule: hospitals and grids are safer when they are not cheap, deniable levers, and any response remains limited to military objectives. A city should not have to wait for smoke before it may defend its lights, water, and hospitals.
