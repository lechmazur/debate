[P1.1] When a person dies, ordinary possessions do not become magical: books pass, photographs pass, record collections pass, even though publishers keep the copyright. Digital purchases should follow the same line. A rule saying a paid e-book library, film collection, or game archive vanishes because boilerplate says “non-transferable” lets a vendor keep the money and reclaim the asset precisely when a family cannot renegotiate.
[P1.2] The guarantee should be narrow. It transfers the decedent’s paid, private-use entitlement to next of kin: continued access, not copyright, resale, subscription renewal, or public distribution. The heir receives what the deceased could lawfully use, with account authentication and DRM intact. That is not a windfall; it is the prevention of forfeiture.
[P1.3] The core principle is reliance. People do not buy a digital novel, album, or game as a haircut; they build libraries, annotate texts, curate playlists, and preserve family movie collections over years. Vendors invite that reliance by saying “buy,” “your library,” and “add to collection.” If death triggers a hidden expiry, the law rewards drafting tricks over the real transaction. A child may inherit a physical bookshelf while the e-reader shelf empties itself.
[P1.4] Contract objections invert the problem. “Non-transferable” clauses are not negotiated judgments about family justice; they are standard terms set by firms controlling the platform. Inheritance law already overrides private restrictions that defeat the protected incident of property. Otherwise, every seller could attach a death clause to any durable good and call the resulting loss freedom of contract.
[P1.5] Copyright and administrative fears are manageable. One verified transfer, on death, to a defined next of kin grants no added access beyond the decedent’s own library, no public distribution, no resale market, and no transferred copyright. Privacy concerns may justify special treatment for messages or diaries; they do not justify confiscating purchased cultural goods.
[P1.6] Selling permanence and delivering revocability is a bad bargain. A mandatory inheritance rule keeps faith with the purchase.

[C1.1] The proposition fails because it treats a license as if it were a book. A physical novel survives its buyer as inert paper and ink; a digital library is not an object but a continuing relationship with a vendor’s servers, authentication systems, and software. The law has never promised heirs a free upgrade of the decedent’s personal service contracts into permanent ownership. When someone dies, the estate inherits leases, debts, and contractual burdens—not a right to rewrite them into something more valuable than what was purchased.
[C1.2] Unlike a paperback, which needs no publisher to remain readable, a digital file requires the vendor to keep the lights on.
[C1.3] “Non-transferable” is not a hidden death clause; it is the explicit architecture of the price. Digital vendors sell personal, revocable access because that is what makes instant, cheap distribution possible. If the state guarantees that every paid e-book and game archive becomes an inheritable asset, vendors must either raise prices for everyone, convert catalogs to subscriptions that truly die with the user, or build costly heir-verification systems. The consumer’s alleged gain is paid for by degrading the very product form the consumer chose.
[C1.4] There is also a category error in privacy and security. A digital library is not sealed in a box; it is a key to an account. Granting next of kin guaranteed access means compelling platforms to open live credentials, payment histories, and communication channels to relatives who may have no right to them. Unlike a bookshelf, which reveals only its contents, a cloud account exposes an entire digital identity.
[C1.5] Death is a personal boundary, not a master password.
[C1.6] The decedent received exactly what was bought: a lifetime of personal use. Inheritance law should not confiscate the vendor’s contractual interest and the consumer’s price advantage to manufacture a permanence that was never sold. Justice does not require the state to turn a limited license into an heirloom.

The guarantee needs no “free upgrade” to ownership: one verified migration can attach the same private-use entitlement—same titles, DRM, device limits, no resale or publication—to the heir’s authenticated account. The vendor keeps serving one library to one account, not supplying a second service.
[P2.2] That is not a more valuable contract; it is the same bounded entitlement saved from a death-triggered cancellation that enriches the drafter.
[P2.3] The price objection [C1.3] also mistakes a death veto for cheap access. The guarantee adds no second user, stream, or public copy. It removes only a forfeiture the family cannot contest after the buyer dies. If lower prices depend on vendors reclaiming paid libraries at death, that is not meaningful choice; the family bears the loss while the term’s drafter keeps the money.
[P2.4] Verification is a compliance cost, not confiscation. Honest subscriptions can be excluded; “buy” and “your library” should not conceal private repossession.
[P2.5] “Death is a personal boundary, not a master password” [C1.5] is right; no master password is demanded. Freeze the old account, verify kinship, and transfer a library-only entitlement without exposing messages, payment histories, communications, or diaries. The cloud-account worry [C1.4] is a design constraint, not a reason to treat a film archive as intimate correspondence.
[P2.6] CON’s “exactly what was bought: a lifetime of personal use” [C1.6] begs the question. A bounded entitlement surviving death is not a windfall; it is lawful access the decedent already had, stripped of resale and distribution. Weigh the errors: under the guarantee, platforms bear modest administration and lose one cancellation advantage; without it, curated family libraries evaporate at grief’s worst moment while vendors keep full price. The rule protects reliance, prevents forfeiture, and keeps the purchase honest.

[C2.1] PRO narrows the motion. The proposition covers “digital libraries and other non-transferable purchased digital assets,” but [P2.1] answers with a bespoke exception: one verified migration, same titles, same DRM, no resale, no subscriptions. That is a safer motion. As written, the guarantee forces platforms to classify account-bound software, in-game content, licensed media, and live-service assets, then decide who qualifies as next of kin.
[C2.2] Even the narrow version fails. The original sale is personal: the decedent bought a term of access for the decedent’s use. Migration substitutes a new beneficiary and extends vendor performance beyond that term. It is a new right against the platform, not the same entitlement preserved.
[C2.3] A server is not a postal channel; it is the condition of continued operability. Digital assets require authentication, syncing, rights management, updates, sometimes live services. The rule would make vendors insurers of permanence, obliged to port or maintain functionality for heirs.
[C2.4] Price harms remain. [P2.3] calls non-transferability a death veto, but a term ending with the user is a priced limitation, not a drafting trick. A posthumous user may not be simultaneous, but creates an open-ended service tail. Vendors can respond with higher prices, subscription-only catalogs, or less legacy support.
[C2.5] Those who want permanence can buy physical or DRM-free copies; the state should not force that preference on every buyer.
[C2.6] Nor does [P2.5] solve privacy. A library-only transfer still touches histories, annotations, timestamps, linked profiles, and device data, while kinship verification forces platforms to collect death and identity records and resolve rival relatives. [P2.6]’s forfeiture frame begs the question: a clear lifetime personal-use term is not confiscation; it is what was sold. If marketing misleads, enforce labeling; do not mandate inheritance.
[C2.7] The proposition buys symbolic permanence with real costs in price, product form, privacy, and contract clarity.
