Yet in the current landscape, the last honest moment in a transaction has been moved to the checkout screen — the point of maximum commitment, where the customer has already invested time, hunger, and social momentum. Mandatory service charges surfacing only at payment aren't disclosure; they're ambush.
[P1.2] Consider the ordinary sequence. You choose a restaurant, browse the menu, select items, enter your address, and only then see a "service fee" of ten or fifteen percent stacked on top of taxes and delivery costs. Every step before that was a decision made on incomplete information. Economists have long recognized this as a "drip pricing" dynamic: firms advertise an attractive base price and reveal mandatory add-ons later, exploiting the customer's sunk costs and hassle aversion.
[P1.3] The fee isn't optional and it isn't a tip — it's part of the real price, and hiding it until payment is a structural choice that profits from deception.
[P1.4] Early disclosure changes behavior in ways late disclosure cannot. The proposition simply restores price competition.

[C1.2] A few sunk clicks are not a trap when exit is free.
[C1.3] That breaks on “all mandatory service charges.” Many are conditional. Restaurant charges can turn on party size, discounts, or holiday rules. Ticket fees can change with venue, seat, delivery method, or add-ons. Delivery-app service charges are not final until address, basket, and demand are known.
[C1.4] Forcing numbers “one screen earlier” [P1.5] yields speculative estimates that are often wrong or worst-case prices that repel buyers from cheaper transactions. Early certainty becomes fake certainty.
[C1.5] The “price competition” claim [P1.4] is larger than the remedy. Service charges alone are not “the price” [P1.1]: taxes, modifiers, delivery costs, tips, and availability still shift after comparison. In ticketing, primary inventory is often exclusive; no rival seller may offer that seat. In restaurants, people compare cuisine, location, quality, and party needs before a fee line.
[C1.6] Pulling one label forward does not create the clean market imagined.
[C1.7] “No technical obstacle” [P1.5] is too easy. Menus, ticket feeds, and delivery marketplaces must classify every charge, recompute it through each browsing state, and police labels sliding among service, processing, and delivery. Many firms will simply bury the amount in base prices. Then buyers pay the same total, advertised prices look higher, and low-fee sellers cannot show the difference.
[C1.8] The relevant protection is informed assent: no mandatory charge should be imposed until the complete total is plainly itemized and freely accepted. That stops surprise billing without requiring inaccurate guesses during browsing. A rule making early numbers less reliable in the name of honesty is price theater.
