[P1.1] Elite universities are not broken ladders; they are polished gates. A ladder changes who can climb; a gate certifies families already near the top as deserving. The issue is not whether any low-income student gets in. The issue is institutional function: do these schools redistribute life chances, or convert inherited advantage into credentials and authority?
[P1.2] Mobility exists, but it is the exception that exposes the mechanism. A few dramatic success stories do not make a hereditary trust an engine of fairness. Admissions reward the curated childhood: stable housing, private counseling, test preparation, polished essays, travel teams, internships found through parents’ friends, and the ability to apply early without comparing aid. Legacy and donor preferences are merely the honest version of the same principle: family position counts.
[P1.3] Picture two applicants—one with a tutor, counselor, and summer research placement; one working evenings after a long school day. Holistic review calls the first “well-rounded” and the second “underdeveloped.” Class advantage is not a bias against merit; it is the material from which merit is assembled.
[P1.4] After enrollment, the same institution sells proximity: finance and consulting pipelines, faculty sponsorship, elite graduate routes, unpaid internships, alumni networks, and social matching. A student with parental support can take the prestige internship in an expensive city; a first-generation student often needs paid work now. The diploma may be identical, but the freedom to exploit it is inherited.
[P1.5] Public policy then completes the transmission. Tax exemptions, charitable deductions, public funds, and borrowed prestige allow vast endowments to accumulate while politics celebrates “access” at a gate that admits only a tiny fraction. Meanwhile the broad institutions where mobility has scale are treated as second-tier.
[P1.6] Policy should treat elite universities less as engines of fairness than as institutions of inheritance: scarce credentialing trusts that stabilize class position. Curb legacy and donor preferences, condition tax privileges, and redirect mobility money to institutions that enroll many, not institutions that consecrate a few.
