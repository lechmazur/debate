[P1.1] A two-hour cap cannot tell a video call with grandparents from a solo binge of autoplay junk. Advice that pretends those minutes are interchangeable is not cautious. It is crude. Media works through content, pacing, and whether another person is there to interpret or join in.
[P1.2] Quality and social context should therefore outweigh a simple daily limit.
[P1.3] The same screen can teach, soothe, isolate, or inflame. A child who watches a nature film with a parent and then talks about it is not having the same experience as one left alone with rapid, reward-driven clips. One builds language and shared reference; the other trains restless clicking. Caps erase that difference.
[P1.4] They tell parents the harm lives in the clock, so parenting becomes a fight over leftover minutes. Real harm lives in lost sleep and play, overstimulating content, and media used as a substitute for people.
[P1.5] Social context is the lever. Co-viewing, creating together, and talking after a show turn media into family practice. Solitary, endless, late-night use turns it into a wedge. Advice that starts with hours never reaches those questions.
[P1.6] It also collapses once school, homework, and relatives live on the same device. Then the cap is either unenforceable or a daily fight that teaches children to hide screens rather than think about them.
[P1.7] Better advice is comparative. What is this replacing? Who is with them? Can they explain what they saw?
[P1.8] Does it leave them more agitated or more curious? Time still matters at the edges, especially near bedtime, but it is a check, not the policy.
