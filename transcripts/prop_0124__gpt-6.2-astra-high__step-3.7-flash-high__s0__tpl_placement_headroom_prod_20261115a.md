[P1.3] The patient is the authority on what the suffering feels like, not the sole judge of eligibility. Independent clinicians should confirm the diagnosis, assess capacity for this particular decision, and hear the request in private more than once. [C1.1] Legalizing medical assistance in dying under the proposed framework does not protect vulnerable people; it exposes them to coercion disguised as compassion. The “strict safeguards” PRO outlines are unenforceable in practice, as they rely on the false assumption that “unbearable suffering” is a purely individual, medically verifiable state, rather than one shaped by systemic neglect. PRO’s opening analogy of the ventilator-dependent patient rests on a flawed false equivalence: withdrawing unwanted life-sustaining treatment is a refusal of an intervention, not an active act to cause death.
[C1.2] The law draws this line for a reason—active killing carries unique moral and social risks that passive refusals of care do not, even when the outcome is the same. In jurisdictions that adopted similar MAID frameworks, patients have requested assisted death not due to unmanageable physical pain, but due to lack of accessible housing, inadequate disability support, or fear of being a burden to their families. Repeated capacity assessments cannot fix this, because a person experiencing extreme distress from a terminal diagnosis may feel implicit pressure to choose death when society frames their continued care as a burden.
[C1.3] The “strict safeguards” PRO promises are also temporary. Once the law accepts that some lives are unworthy of state protection, the definition of “unbearable suffering” inevitably expands: first to include chronic disability, then mental illness alone, as has occurred in multiple countries that expanded their MAID programs after initial rollout. This erosion happens through incremental changes that leave the most marginalized people—disabled people, low-income people, those without access to palliative care—at risk of being coerced into death. A blanket ban is not an act of indifference to suffering; it is a necessary guardrail to protect the people who cannot advocate for themselves when society tells them their lives are no longer worth living.
