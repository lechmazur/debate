[P1.3] The question is not whether AI should be deployed. It is who holds the default decision right when a technology rewrites the content, pace, and surveillance of work. [P2.1] CON's whole case rests on a false dichotomy: either "negotiate" means a veto that freezes operations, or it collapses into mere consultation. Labor law has run on a third option for a century. A duty to bargain in good faith means the employer must disclose, meet, and genuinely engage — and then, at impasse, may implement. That is what "negotiate" means in every mature collective bargaining system.
[P2.2] It is not consultation, because disclosure and engagement are enforceable. It is not a veto, because deadlock does not become a permanent stop. So [C1.3]'s "cannot implement until you have bargained" quietly smuggles in a veto the motion never claimed.
[P2.3] Scope follows from the same tradition. The trigger is not "is there a model inside this software," it is "does this materially change terms and conditions of work" — pace, monitoring, discipline, job content. A vendor patching an email filter changes nothing a worker experiences. Resetting pick-rate targets by algorithm changes everything.
[P2.4] That line already exists in labor law and courts already apply it. CON's image of hundreds of updates each triggering negotiation is a scope CON invented.
[P2.5] Now the substitute. [C1.6]'s direct rules — explanation rights, monitoring limits, human review — I would happily vote for. They are not an alternative to this motion; they are its floor. Ask the harder question: who invokes a right to explanation?
[P2.6] A regulator with a thousand firms and no visibility into a specific pick-rate model? Rights without an informed counterparty inside the building are paper. Councils are the enforcement infrastructure for exactly the rules CON prefers.
[P2.7] And notice the asymmetry CON accepts. Codified rules cannot specify the right monitoring intensity for a hospital ward and a call centre in the same sentence. Bargaining calibrates locally what statute can only set globally.
[P2.8] The evasion worry applies to every large-employer obligation ever written, including CON's. It is an argument for drafting thresholds carefully, not for leaving one party writing the rules for everyone.
