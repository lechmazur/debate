[P1.1] Restitution is not a gift to the past; it is rent owed for the present. When a state university is funded by an endowment built from dispossessed Indigenous land, the benefit is not merely historical—it appears every year as scholarships, salaries, buildings, and investment income. When a state forest, reservoir, or public land trust collects fees from territory taken after broken treaties, the government is still doing business on stolen capital. The correct response is not sympathy.
[P1.2] It is land return, long-term revenue sharing, or equivalent restitution.
[P1.3] Continuing benefit is the decisive issue. If a bank received a stolen deposit and used it to endow a school, no one would call the theft too old to fix while the school still spends the interest. We would trace the asset and require restitution. The same logic applies to public institutions, often with clearer records.
[P1.4] Many public universities and state land systems were built from land grants that converted Indigenous territories into institutional endowments. Those institutions remain identifiable beneficiaries. Their present wealth cannot be separated from the wrong that produced it.
[P1.5] Feasibility strengthens the case. Restitution need not mean mass eviction or confiscating private homes. It means states should ask, institution by institution, what remedy fits. Some land can be returned outright.
[P1.6] Some can support long-term revenue sharing. Some can be co-managed. Some can be offset by an equivalent stream of funding or land. That is not vague aspiration; it is ordinary remedy design used in trusts, settlements, and public land management.
[P1.7] Opponents will say today’s citizens did not seize the land. But public institutions are not innocent bystanders. They are the organized continuation of the state, and they still claim the benefit. An institution that accepts an endowment in perpetuity can also accept the obligation attached to it in perpetuity.
[P1.8] The harm is not frozen in history; it is an ongoing unjust enrichment with an identifiable beneficiary. Restitution is how public institutions stop profiting from dispossession and begin honoring the consent-based relationship that legitimate government requires.
