[P1.1] A curb in a dense city is some of the most valuable land on Earth, and we currently give it away for free to Amazon vans and Uber drivers. The predictable result is exactly what you see every day: delivery trucks double-parked in travel lanes, ride-hail cars idling in bike lanes and bus stops, drivers circling the block hunting for space, and the public eating the cost in congestion, crashes, and bus delays. Anything priced at zero gets overconsumed. That's the whole debate.
[P1.2] Dynamic pricing fixes this through a simple mechanism. When a loading zone costs more at 5 p.m. than at 10 a.m., delivery companies—who are sophisticated logistics operators—shift routes to off-peak hours, consolidate trips, and stop treating the curb as free storage. When ride-hail pickups carry a small peak fee downtown, platforms steer pickups to designated zones instead of mid-block stops that snarl traffic. The price doesn't punish anyone; it coordinates.
[P1.3] Airports already do this with ride-hail staging fees, and pickup chaos at terminals dropped dramatically. Several cities running smart loading-zone pilots have seen double-parking fall sharply once drivers could reliably find—and pay for—legal space.
[P1.4] Notice who actually pays today's "free" system: bus riders stuck behind a blocked stop, cyclists forced into traffic, small shops whose customers can't reach them. Free curbs are not neutral; they are a public subsidy flowing to a handful of billion-dollar logistics and ride-hail firms, with the costs dumped on everyone else. Pricing reverses that. A fifty-cent curb fee spread across a van carrying eighty packages is invisible per parcel, but it transforms behavior at the fleet level—and the revenue can fund loading zones, bus lanes, and enforcement.
[P1.5] So the question for the opposition is stark: why should cities keep handing their scarcest public asset, free of charge, to commercial fleets, while pretending the resulting gridlock is an act of nature? Price the curb, and the curb starts working.
