[P1.1] Your social graph is not Mark Zuckerberg's or Elon Musk's private kingdom. It is your relationships. The proposition starts from a simple principle: if you can choose a platform, you should be able to leave it without losing the people who make it valuable. Today you cannot, and that is not an accident.
[P1.2] Lock-in is the business model.
[P1.3] When a new messaging app offers better privacy or a niche network serves a community better, users face a brutal calculation: switch alone and lose everyone, or stay where you are. That is not a fair market. It is a hostage situation with terms of service. Requiring interoperability and social-graph transfer changes the default.
[P1.4] You authorize the transfer, the new service receives your contacts and connections, and, crucially, those connections can choose whether to be reachable there. That is not forced public exposure; it is user-initiated portability with consent.
[P1.5] Without the social graph, data portability is theater. Downloading your posts as a ZIP file does not let you rebuild your social life elsewhere. Imagine number portability if your contacts could not follow: you keep your old number forever because everyone else does. We solved that for phones.
[P1.6] We can solve it for platforms.
[P1.7] Incumbents will warn about privacy and security. Those are engineering and governance problems, not reasons for permanent monopoly. Standards can limit transfers to authenticated users, honor blocks and privacy settings, and prevent spam. The alternative is worse: platforms decide when your relationships are portable, which means never when portability threatens their dominance.
[P1.8] This law is not about punishing success. It is about making success contestable. If a platform keeps users only because leaving means social death, it is not winning on quality. It is winning on captivity.
[P1.9] The side that defends that captivity may call it innovation; it is really a veto over the next better platform. Users should own the exit.

Requiring interoperable transfer would force platforms to treat those third parties as portable objects, sacrificing their privacy and the platform’s integrity for a benefit it cannot deliver.
[C1.2] The fatal weakness is the motion’s own consent design. It says you authorize the transfer and “those connections can choose whether to be reachable there” [P1.4]. If contacts must opt in again, then the new platform still faces the empty-room problem described in [P1.3]. The captivity the motion condemns in [P1.8] is not cured; you have not left with your people, you have merely emailed them an invitation.
[C1.3] You cannot port willingness, presence, or shared context. [C1.4] The phone-number analogy [P1.5] collapses under this pressure. A number is a state-administered identifier routed by common switches, not a curated trust graph. We already have meaningful exits—exportable contacts, multi-homing, ordinary data downloads—so the “theater” complaint is overstated. Picture a small support community for abuse survivors: forced graph portability would let anyone export verified member connections into an unmoderated clone, handing harassers a map.
[C1.5] Worse, continuous interoperability imposes heavy standardization and security costs. Attackers gain a universal import channel, and only large incumbents can afford compliance; the niche platforms the proposition claims to empower are crushed by the burden and frozen by backwards-compatible design. The motion is disproportionate, privacy-eroding, and competitively self-defeating.

[P4.2] Email invites are not equivalent: they are one-off, burnable, and lose mutual lists, group identity, roles, and block settings. A transferred edge is two-way and revocable; an invitation is a stranger asking to be re-added. So the motion does deliver a real exit, not a forwarding trick.
[P4.3] A2: [C3.2] says one-to-one analogies fail for many-to-many. Universal import channels can be authenticated, rate-limited, and audited; the alternative is not zero risk, it is incumbent-controlled risk, with every new platform forced to rebuild the graph from zero.
[P4.5] CON's whole case reduces to "scoped portability is incomplete, and broad portability is invasive." But the motion can mandate a floor: my edges, my memberships, my reachability, subject to recipient settings. That is neither empty nor coercive.
