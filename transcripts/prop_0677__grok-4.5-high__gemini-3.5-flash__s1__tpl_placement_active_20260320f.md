[P1.1] Automated decision systems already shape who gets hired and who gets fired, often without the people affected ever knowing. When an algorithm ranks résumés or flags productivity scores, those outputs routinely decide livelihoods. The law must require employers to disclose when such systems meaningfully influence those choices.
[P1.2] Opacity is not neutral; it conceals injustice. Tools trained on historical records embed past discrimination—favoring certain names, schools, career gaps, or performance patterns that track race, gender, age, or disability. A firing model may read accommodation requests or caregiving leave as “low engagement.” Without disclosure, the rejected applicant or dismissed worker has no way to investigate, request human review, or bring an equal-employment claim. The black box becomes a legal shield.
[P1.3] Disclosure restores minimal fairness and agency. A plain notice that an automated system played a meaningful role lets people gather evidence, contest criteria, or insist on human override. This is not a ban on useful technology. Employers already record many decision factors in regulated contexts; noting algorithmic influence is no greater administrative step than recording a test score.
[P1.4] The burden is tiny; the information is decisive.
[P1.5] Voluntary disclosure fails. Employers keep systems quiet precisely when results look discriminatory or expose liability. Those most harmed—workers with nonstandard histories, applicants from underrepresented groups—are least positioned to force the truth. A legal floor levels that power imbalance, pressures better system design, and prevents secrecy from becoming standard practice.
[P1.6] Hiding algorithmic influence treats people as inputs to secret machinery rather than as individuals entitled to understand what controls their work. The rule is narrow, practical, and non-negotiable: disclose the meaningful influence, or the decision process remains indefensible.
