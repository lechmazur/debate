[P1.1] Public decision-making is not an engineering problem wearing democracy's costume. It is the exercise of coercion over people who did not choose the same risks, bear the same costs, or share the same priorities. That is why democratic legitimacy matters more than technocratic competence: legitimacy is what turns power into rightful authority, while competence is a tool for using that authority well.
[P1.2] The clash becomes clear when expertise reaches its limit. Experts can tell us a policy's likely effects—say, during a pandemic, how a lockdown might reduce transmission. But they cannot tell us, from their models alone, how much liberty we should sacrifice to protect the elderly, whether lost livelihoods are an acceptable price, or who should bear the burden. Those are value judgments, not technical calculations.
[P1.3] If we let officials resolve them because their spreadsheets are smarter, we do not get neutrality; we get someone else's preferences disguised as efficiency. Democracy exists to force those preferences into the open, to make decision-makers answerable, and to give the people who live with the outcome a say in choosing it.
[P1.4] This priority is not sentimental. Legitimacy is practical. A democratically chosen policy that is rough at the edges earns compliance because people helped author it and can throw out its authors. An imposed optimum invites evasion, protest, and institutional decay; its competence evaporates when it cannot command acceptance.
[P1.5] Worse, insulated technocracy is not reliably competent. Without electoral accountability, public contestation, and the clash of viewpoints, expertise slides toward groupthink, capture, and unnoticed error—the same overconfidence that has preceded financial crashes and top-down planning disasters.
[P1.6] None of this denies expertise a major role. It denies experts the last word. Where the two conflict, legitimacy must dominate, because it decides who is entitled to decide at all. Competence improves how we govern; legitimacy determines whether governance is ours.

[C1.1] The motion sets up a false hierarchy. A public decision is not made legitimate merely by being democratic, nor made wise merely by being expert; the question is which failure is more basic. It is the second. A democratic procedure cannot turn an impossible, infeasible, or destructive policy into a rightful one; it can only authorize a failure more inclusively.
[C1.2] Take a city that democratically resolves to end homelessness within a year. The vote may be perfectly legitimate, but without housing supply, services, and budgeting, it is not a decision about the world—it is a wish with a mandate. When it fails, the public does not praise the process; it learns that democratic authorization cannot substitute for competence. Legitimacy is partly downstream of competence: institutions earn the right to decide by being able to decide well, and they lose it when confident votes produce collapsed bridges, stalled vaccine rollouts, or inflationary promises.
[C1.3] The PRO side treats competence as a tool and legitimacy as the source of authority. But authority without capacity is not authority over reality. Voters can set ends—whose interests count, what risks are acceptable—but they cannot vote away scarcity, epidemiology, engineering constraints, or implementation complexity. Experts embedded in democratic institutions do not replace those choices; they make them executable.
[C1.4] Elected leaders choose values and trade-offs; competent agencies supply the feasible path; voters judge the result and can replace the decision-makers. That is not technocracy. It is democracy made effective.
[C1.5] The decisive test is not 'who decides' versus 'whether it works,' but which defect destroys decision-making first. A competent but insufficiently democratic process can be corrected by widening participation. A legitimate but incompetent process cannot govern at all; it produces ruin with consent. Legitimacy’s payoff—compliance, stability, trust—depends on decisions that address the problem, so competence is not a rival to legitimacy but its condition of success.
[C1.6] Where the two pull apart, competence must prevail.

[P2.1] Those examples do not show competence outranking legitimacy; they show democracy malfunctioning when it ignores feasibility. A city that votes to "end homelessness within a year" [C1.2] has not pitted legitimacy against expertise. Either experts failed to make constraints clear—a competence failure inside the democratic process—or voters chose an impossible end and must answer for it at the ballot box. Neither case lets experts cancel the vote.
[P2.2] It shows democracy needs good information, not that information should rule. Democracy chooses among feasible ends; expertise maps feasibility. It does not license experts to choose the end, only to expose trade-offs.
[P2.3] Nor is legitimacy "downstream of competence." It is the prior question of who may bind whom. People accept authority not merely because it works, but because it is theirs, revisable, and accountable. If legitimacy were only a reward for success, every disappointing outcome would license experts to replace voters.
[P2.4] That is not democracy made effective; it is democracy made optional whenever it errs.
[P2.5] That asymmetry is also backwards. A competent but undemocratic process can be corrected only by the very experts who may be wrong; insulated agencies defend their model, and those harmed have no formal route to force reconsideration. A democratic process corrects values and facts: it can demand better data, replace planners, and renegotiate who bears the burden. Its messy feedback surfaces local knowledge and dissent that insulated models miss.
[P2.6] Pandemic school closures show this. Models could estimate infections avoided, but only open contestation could judge whether children’s lost years and low-wage parents’ lost jobs were an acceptable price—and force compensation for those forced to pay. Competence improves execution; legitimacy determines whose losses count. The opposition’s claim that "a legitimate but incompetent process cannot govern at all" [C1.5] confuses a bad outcome with lost authority.
[P2.7] Authorized failure is reversible: officials can be removed and the mandate rewritten. Efficient custody is not self-government.

[C2.3] PRO claims authorized failure is reversible while "efficient custody is not" [P2.7]. This confuses one election cycle with durable legitimacy. A single electoral loss is reversible; a pattern of incompetent decisions—collapsed infrastructure, currency crises, failed public health responses—is not. The democracy that authorizes ruin does not gain moral credit for letting voters throw out the authors after the damage is done.
[C2.4] Compliance, the practical payoff PRO celebrates [P1.4], evaporates when competent outcomes stop arriving. Legitimacy is downstream of competence precisely because trust is earned through results, not procedure. A process that consistently fails is not self-correcting; it is self-liquidating.
[C2.5] On school closures [P2.6], PRO assumes democratic contestation discovered the true trade-off between infections and lost learning. But open debate without epidemiological competence produced cacophony, not clarity—parents shouting past scientists, politicians ignoring transmission dynamics and developmental data. The "acceptable price" was never democratically calculated; it was imposed by panic or technocratic overreach, neither of which constitutes the legitimacy PRO praises. Competence is not a rival to weighing losses; it is the precondition for knowing what the losses actually are.
[C2.6] Without technical grounding, democratic legitimacy becomes a vote on fantasy, authorizing suffering it cannot comprehend.

[P4.1] A1: [C3.1] No. Feasibility narrows the menu; it does not choose the meal. Experts can say a one-year homelessness mandate is impossible given supply and budgeting—that is a constraint, not a value judgment. The vote is not “less legitimate” in authority; it is incomplete as a decision because it picked a phantom rather than a real trade-off. The democratic answer is not expert cancellation but revision: informed voters choose among attainable packages—shelter now, housing later, tax levels—and replace planners who failed to clarify limits.
[P4.2] If an expert claim of infeasibility could void the result, experts would define the perimeter of choice; that is the discretion we deny them.
[P4.3] A2: [C3.2] Irreversibility does not transfer authority. Later removal cannot restore a lost life or a passed tipping point, but neither can expert rule; agencies also impose irreversible harm when models are wrong or values are hidden. Climate and pandemic policies have both irreversible benefits and irreversible costs—lost schooling, ruined livelihoods, shattered trust. Democratic legitimacy never promised restoration; it promised that those who bear the coercively imposed loss authorize it, can demand compensation, and can change course.
[P4.4] Emergency does not create expertise over consent; it intensifies the need for consent, because the stakes are highest and most unequally borne.
[P4.5] Those answers show why democratic legitimacy still outweighs competence. CON’s case treats competence as a trump because failure is costly. But every costly failure is also a distribution of pain; someone decides who pays. That decision is not technical.
[P4.6] Where experts map constraints and warn of catastrophe, they inform choice. Where they select ends, impose burdens, or void voter mandates as “infeasible,” they rule. Democratic legitimacy matters more because it keeps the last word—over aims, sacrifices, and who bears irreversible loss—with the people who live with the outcome, rather than with the technicians who merely model it.

When citizens abandon a failed democracy, they abandon a procedure that no longer feels like theirs. Their remedy is not to find a smarter custodian, but to reclaim authorship.
[P5.3] Handing the last word to competence removes the very thing that makes failure theirs to judge.
[P5.4] So democratic legitimacy must outweigh technocratic competence. When democracy errs, sufferers can vote out the authors, rewrite the mandate, and force compensation; when insulated expertise errs, the same errors arrive as necessity, with no ballot to reclaim the loss [P2.5].
[P5.5] Competence improves execution. Legitimacy decides who may coerce whom. In public decision-making, that question comes first.
