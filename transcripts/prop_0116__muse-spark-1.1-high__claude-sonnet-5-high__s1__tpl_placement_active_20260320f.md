Treating "poor earnings relative to debt" as proof of institutional failure conflates correlation with fault.
[C1.3] PRO's own examples — film MFAs, for-profit criminal justice degrees — already exist inside a market that mostly punishes them through declining enrollment. The rare bad actors don't justify redesigning incentives for the entire sector.
[C1.4] Now the mechanism PRO wants: cost-sharing based on cohort earnings. Universities facing that liability will not respond by generously investing in advising. That means first-generation students, students from under-resourced high schools, students entering historically lower-paid but socially vital fields like early education or social work.
[C1.5] The people this policy claims to protect are exactly the people who get quietly filtered out of admission before they ever take on debt at all.

Layering a second, vaguer institution-wide penalty on top invites litigation over what counts as a "cohort," what counts as "repeatedly," and which economic shocks get excluded — creating years of disputes rather than the swift market discipline PRO promises.
[C1.7] The honest fix is expanding income-driven repayment and clearer program-level disclosure before enrollment — not asking colleges to underwrite the unpredictable economy for every graduate they admit.

[C2.2] The policy can't tell the difference between institutional failure and bad luck, and it doesn't need to — the bill comes due either way.

That is the predictable institutional response to open-ended financial exposure, and it happens without anyone ever writing "we discriminate" anywhere.
[C2.4] On the gainful-employment point — if the real gap is that nonprofit graduate programs are exempt, the answer is closing that specific exemption with a standard that's already litigated and administratively workable, not layering a vaguer sector-wide liability on top that invites years of new disputes over cohorts, timeframes, and excluded shocks.
[C2.5] Federal loan backing exists precisely so government, not the registrar, absorbs macro-level earnings risk. Handing that risk to universities doesn't fix incentives — it makes them insurers of the economy, and insurers hedge by excluding the riskiest applicants.
