[C1.2] PRO compares chargers to gas nozzles, but liquid fuel is simple; EV charging is a complex, high-voltage software handshake. The interface dictates charging speed, thermal management, weight, and reliability. Regulators and open committees rallied around CCS—a bulky connector plagued by communication failures and broken payment handshakes.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, proprietary competition produced NACS: lighter, faster, and vastly more reliable. Because governments did not ban proprietary interfaces, the market was free to adopt the superior system. Automakers voluntarily converged on NACS because it worked. Had the government mandated open standards five years ago, drivers would be permanently saddled with an inferior, government-picked plug.
[C1.4] Standardization committees do not innovate; they entrench political compromises.
[C1.5] Moreover, proprietary advantage is what financed the charging infrastructure in the first place. Deploying capital-intensive fast chargers ahead of mass EV adoption is commercially irrational as a standalone utility. Pioneers invested billions in private networks precisely as a competitive differentiator to sell cars. Forcing day-one interoperability destroys that incentive, turning charging into a low-margin commodity and choking off the private risk capital needed to eliminate range anxiety.
[C1.6] Finally, open access does not require government coercion. As proprietary networks reach scale, commercial incentives flip: operators want to monetize surplus capacity by selling kilowatt-hours to every driver. That dynamic evolution is already unfolding through voluntary licensing and adapters, preserving reliability without state mandates. Nor does public funding justify banning proprietary systems; governments can condition subsidies on access without outlawing private networks built with private capital.
[C1.7] The real danger is not proprietary competition; it is regulatory lock-in. A mandate freezes immature technology in amber and protects obsolete designs from market discipline. We should let superior engineering win the market, not let bureaucrats mandate yesterday’s standard.
