[P1.1] Prohibition doesn't stop kidney sales. It just guarantees they happen in the worst possible way, while thousands die waiting for them not to.
[P1.2] Right now in the US nearly a hundred thousand people wait for a kidney, waiting four to five years. Thousands die each year on that list, and ten times more never get listed and grind through dialysis, which cuts life expectancy in half and offers miserable quality of life. A transplant adds many years, often a decade or more of relatively normal life. Altruism alone will never close that gap — living donation has flatlined, deceased donation can't meet demand from diabetes and hypertension.
[P1.3] Paying compensated donors changes the shortage. Donating a kidney is not risk-free, but for a healthy screened adult the surgical risk is extremely low, comparable to other jobs we not only allow but pay danger premiums for. Offer a regulated package — say $40,000 plus lifetime health insurance, lost-wage coverage, independent donor advocates, mandatory work-up and follow-up — and many people who would never give away an organ for free will rationally consent to give one safely. Even a modest increase in supply saves thousands of life-years annually and saves the system money versus endless dialysis.
[P1.4] That same legal supply crushes the black market. Today desperate recipients travel abroad to buy from debt-trapped sellers who get a fraction of the price, no aftercare, and often fraudulent promises, with brokers taking the cut. A domestic, government-run monopsony with fixed compensation, no private brokering, strict screening and a cooling-off period removes the customer base for traffickers and gives donors what prohibition denies: informed consent, safe surgery, recourse.
[P1.5] A ban meant to protect the poor now abandons poor donors to the mafia and poor patients to early death. Regulation protects both.

Regulators cannot screen away eviction, medical debt, or a family’s survival pressure. Sellers will say what evaluators need to hear.
[C1.4] After donation, they carry the long-term risks: hypertension, kidney disease, lost work, and the irreversible fact of having sold a body part. A promised insurance fund does not restore renal reserve; it just assumes someone else will pay later.
[C1.5] The life-year arithmetic is one-sided. If one paid kidney replaces one altruistic kidney, the net gain is small, but a new class of injured sellers has been created.
[C1.6] A law that makes the poor’s spare kidney collateral for their debts does not beat prohibition. It gives exploitation a regulatory seal.
