[P1.1] Governments already channel enormous public money into conventional inputs—synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and the monocultures built around them—while the damage stacks up in thinner soils, fouled rivers, fewer pollinators, and higher emissions. Those payments were sold as yield insurance. Regenerative practices such as cover crops, reduced tillage, diverse rotations, and managed grazing already return public goods: richer soil organic matter, stronger water infiltration, less nutrient runoff, and measurable carbon storage. Refusing to match subsidy scale until multi-decade yield trials are conclusive treats ongoing ecological loss as acceptable and farmer transition risk as irrelevant.
[P1.2] Yield need not be proven superior in every system first. Public money’s return is not private bushels alone. Conventional subsidies socialize environmental harm and privatize short-run output. Equal support for regenerative methods pays for resilience and natural capital that markets systematically underprice.
[P1.3] Early transition years often bring yield dips or extra labor. Without parity, farmers rationally stay inside the subsidized status quo, so the very evidence demanded never appears at commercial scale.
[P1.4] Soil will not pause for another review cycle. Every season of extraction strips topsoil, deepens input dependence, and shrinks the climate window in which either system can reliably feed people. Postwar subsidies scaled chemical inputs long before their external costs were fully known. The same fiscal tool should now scale the practices that reverse those costs.
[P1.5] Disappointing regional yields can be met by adjusting payments later. Failed watersheds and exhausted soils cannot be restored by a subsequent study.
[P1.6] Public finance should not insist on conclusive private profitability before it corrects a distortion of its own making. Match the scale now, and let working landscapes produce the record without bankrupting the shift.
