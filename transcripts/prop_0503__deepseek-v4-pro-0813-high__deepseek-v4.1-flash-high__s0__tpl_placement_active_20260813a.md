[P1.1] Gulf diversification should be judged by the dull but decisive evidence: non-oil revenue, non-oil exports, employment, and investable physical capacity. [C1.3] The real test is whether these economies generate private-sector productivity and government revenue when oil prices fall. On that test, they are failing. Private sectors remain dependent on expatriate labor and state contracts.
[C1.4] If oil revenue drops, the non-oil economy drops with it—through cancelled projects, unpaid contractors, and reduced consumer spending. That is correlation with rent, not independence from it.
[C1.5] Dubai is PRO’s best exhibit, but it proves our point: its oil is marginal because it became the service hub for a hydrocarbon-rich region, moving goods, people, and capital generated by neighbors’ oil. When credit froze, it relied on Abu Dhabi’s oil-backed rescue. That is a regional entrepôt, not a generalizable escape from rent.
[C1.6] The political economy also points against success. Genuine diversification requires painful reforms: cutting subsidies, taxing citizens, shrinking public employment, enforcing competition, and exposing nationals to private-sector wages. [C1.7] The likely outcome is a more expensive, more indebted version of the same rentier model. PRO’s rising non-oil numbers prove spending, not success.

A demand slowdown when oil income falls is normal macroeconomic exposure, not proof that non-oil activity is merely rent recycling. The stronger inference from non-oil revenue, tradable capacity, and actual reform is that diversification is substantive and likely to continue.

[C2.1] PRO's answer to the recycling charge is to rename it "converting advantages" [P2.1]. But conversion is only diversification if the new sector can stand after the subsidy and the state contract are removed. If petrochemicals and aluminum depend on gas priced below opportunity cost, and logistics, tourism, and finance depend on sovereign-backed construction, aviation, and regional oil wealth, then the revenue is real but the independence is not. That is the distinction PRO keeps blurring.
[C2.2] PRO's "actual test"—external revenue, private productivity, survival amid price weakness—sounds right but is applied too generously. Non-oil exports can rise while net fiscal and foreign-exchange contribution remains dependent on oil. Saudi non-oil revenue can grow because VAT, fees, and domestic spending are levied on an economy still pumped by oil-financed public investment. That is not proof of a self-sustaining second engine; it is proof that the state can tax the circulation of rent.
[C2.3] Dubai actually illustrates the limit. A regional entrepôt is not generalizable: its customers are oil-rich neighbors, and its 2009 rescue was a sovereign oil backstop, not a routine central-bank operation. If the region's hydrocarbon income shrinks, so do the trade, tourism, and property flows Dubai intermediates. PRO cannot call that "independent" merely because the crude is not pumped locally.
[C2.4] On reform, VAT and subsidy cuts are real but small compared with the missing bargain: no income tax, no hard budget constraint, public payrolls still crowding out private wages for nationals, and state-linked entities dominating the new sectors. Gradualism can be prudence, but here it is also a strategy of avoiding the coalition-breaking choices that would make diversification self-sustaining.
[C2.5] The decisive comparison is not whether non-oil activity exists; it does. It is whether it would survive a sustained oil-price and oil-demand shock without another round of state priming. PRO has shown adaptation while rents remain available. That is not likely success; it is a more diversified dependency.
