[P1.1] Obesity drugs like GLP-1 receptor agonists have transformed weight management, but their $10,000–$15,000 annual list prices pose an existential threat to sustainable broad coverage for the 40% of U.S. adults living with obesity. Health systems cannot responsibly reimburse these drugs for all eligible patients without guardrails that align manufacturer profits with real-world patient outcomes and system budget stability. Outcome-based pricing (OBP) or agreed spending caps deliver exactly that, making this proposal the only path to equitable, long-term access.
[P1.2] First, OBP fixes the fundamental misalignment where manufacturers profit even if their drugs fail for large swathes of patients. Under OBP agreements, payers only pay full price for patients who achieve sustained, clinically meaningful weight loss (e.g., 5%+ total body weight maintained at 12 months) and reduced obesity-related comorbidity risk. If a patient does not respond, the manufacturer refunds part or all of the cost. This eliminates wasteful spending on non-responders, freeing up funds to cover more patients overall.
[P1.3] Second, spending caps offer a simpler alternative for health systems that lack the administrative capacity to track individual patient outcomes. A per-patient annual cap of even $3,000—far below current list prices—would let public and private payers expand coverage to middle-aged and low-income patients currently locked out by cost, without forcing cuts to maternity care, mental health services, or chronic disease management for other conditions. Critics will claim these requirements limit access, but the opposite is true: without them, health systems will either restrict coverage to narrow, high-need groups or pass unchecked costs to patients via higher premiums.
[P1.4] OBP and caps turn obesity drugs from a budget-busting luxury into a broadly accessible public health tool.

[C1.4] Spending caps are worse. [P1.3] imagines manufacturers happily accepting $3,000 for a drug that costs $10,000 to $15,000 to sell today. They won't. They will walk away from public systems, limit supply, or prioritize private cash-pay channels where they keep the price.
[C1.5] That is exactly what happened early with GLP-1s — shortages for diabetes patients while new obesity indications flooded self-pay clinics. A confiscatory cap does not expand access for low-income patients, it cedes broad access to the wealthy.
[C1.6] Broad preventive reimbursement itself is the wrong starting point. The evidence for lifelong preventive use in low-risk populations is thin, discontinuation is high, and weight regain after stopping is the norm. What health systems need is targeted coverage for high-risk disease, time-limited trials, and traditional competition from imminent generics and second-generation entrants — not a conditional promise that holds patient access hostage to a pricing model that fails on this disease and that manufacturers will never accept.
