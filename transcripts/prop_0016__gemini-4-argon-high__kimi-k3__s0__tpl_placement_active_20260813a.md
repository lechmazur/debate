[P1.1] A police officer can clear an encampment by noon, but if the people living there have nowhere to go, they will simply pitch their tents three blocks away by nightfall. That is the fundamental flaw of treating homelessness mainly as a public-order problem: policing can relocate visibility, but it cannot house a single person.
[P1.2] Treating homelessness primarily as a housing and service-access problem does not mean ignoring violent crime or abolishing law enforcement. It means aligning a city’s primary budget, personnel, and strategy with what actually ends street homelessness rather than what merely shuffles it around.
[P1.3] First, a policing-first approach actively entrenches the crisis it claims to solve. The standard tools of public-order enforcement—sweeps, citations, and short-term arrests—create a bureaucratic trap. During sweeps, people routinely lose the exact items required to escape the streets: state IDs, birth certificates, and prescribed medications. When cities issue fines that unhoused people cannot pay, those citations turn into warrants.
[P1.4] Arrest records and warrants then disqualify those individuals from passing landlord background checks or getting hired. Policing does not clear the streets; it manufactures permanent housing ineligibility.
[P1.5] Second, housing and service access are the prerequisite for stability, not the reward for achieving it on a sidewalk. Expecting someone to overcome severe addiction, stabilize untreated mental illness, or hold a job while sleeping under a bridge gets recovery backward. Without a locked door, a place to store medication, and a fixed address where a caseworker can find them, treatment fails. Supportive housing and wraparound services solve the root deficit—shelter and care—rather than criminalizing its symptoms.
[P1.6] Finally, even if a city cares only about clean sidewalks and taxpayer budgets, housing wins. Cycling the same individuals through patrol cars, municipal courts, county jails, and emergency rooms is the most expensive, least effective shelter system ever invented. Cities only achieve genuine public order when people move indoors for good.

[C1.2] Three reasons to reject "primarily housing."

[C1.6] Third, order is the precondition for service delivery. Outreach workers cannot safely operate in entrenched encampments, and homeless people themselves suffer most inside them—assault, fire, predation, overdose. Tolerating encampments is not compassion; it is abandonment.
