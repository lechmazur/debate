[P1.1] A fundamental biological line separates treating a sick individual from permanently altering the human gene pool. Allowing somatic, non-heritable gene therapies while banning heritable germline editing for reproduction draws the exact boundary medicine and ethics require.
[P1.2] First, germline editing introduces irreversible, multi-generational risks without consent. In somatic therapies, a patient accepts personal risk to treat an existing condition, and those genetic changes die with them. In germline editing, off-target cuts, mosaicism, and unintended genetic disruptions do not just affect one child—they are inherited by every subsequent generation. Future descendants who never agreed to participate in an experimental trial are permanently burdened with the biological consequences.
[P1.3] Second, heritable editing lacks genuine medical necessity. Advocates often frame germline modification as the only hope for parents carrying severe hereditary disorders. In reality, existing technologies like Pre-implantation Genetic Testing (PGT) alongside standard IVF already allow parents to select embryos free from debilitating monogenic mutations without altering their genetic code. In the vanishingly rare cases where PGT is insufficient, adoption and donor gametes offer safe family-building pathways.
[P1.4] Risking the human genome cannot be justified when safe reproductive and somatic alternatives already exist.
[P1.5] Third, opening the door to germline reproduction inevitably unleashes commercial eugenics. Once we normalize engineering embryos, the boundary between preventing disease and enhancing traits—such as physical stature, memory, or disease resistance beyond normal baselines—becomes impossible to enforce. Genetic advantages will naturally be commodified by the wealthy, converting temporary economic inequality into a permanent, biologically stratified caste system.
[P1.6] Somatic therapies let us heal the sick today without gambling with the genetic inheritance of tomorrow. We must protect patients while preserving the integrity of the human genome. A complete ban on reproductive germline editing is the only responsible choice.

[C1.1] The central issue is not whether heritable editing is risky; it is whether a categorical ban is the best response. It is not. A ban forecloses the only genetic route to unaffected children for a narrow class of families, while outsourcing any future practice to places with the weakest oversight.
[C1.2] The claim that PGT removes the medical need is incomplete. When both prospective parents are affected by the same autosomal recessive condition, every embryo they produce will carry disease-causing mutations; there is no unaffected embryo to select. For those families, donor gametes or adoption are not neutral “safe alternatives”: they end the possibility of a genetically related child. That is a profound cost, and the state should not impose it on the basis of speculative fears.
[C1.3] Irreversible multi-generational risk warrants rigorous limits: proven safety in animal models, phased human trials, long-term follow-up, and restriction to serious monogenic disease. It does not by itself justify permanent prohibition. Blanket bans do not make experimentation disappear; they push it into less regulated jurisdictions and create the very unmonitored practices opponents fear. Regulated translation, by contrast, keeps data, oversight, and liability in view.
[C1.4] The future-consent objection proves too much. No child consents to the genome it inherits naturally, or to being born with a severe genetic disease. If correcting a known pathogenic mutation is reasonably expected to benefit the future child and descendants, that is more respectful of their welfare than preserving the mutation.
[C1.5] The eugenic slide is not inevitable. Somatic medicine already separates treatment from non-therapeutic enhancement through licensing, ethics review, and payment rules. The same instruments can govern reproductive germline use. If equity is the worry, the answer is access policy and enhancement bans, not prohibiting a therapy that could spare children from sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, or Huntington’s.
[C1.6] The resolution mistakes caution for an argument for prohibition. It is actually an argument for strict, narrow regulation. Countries should reject a blanket ban and build that regulatory path instead.
