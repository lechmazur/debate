[P1.1] A child born with severe mitochondrial disease—Leigh syndrome is the classic case—often develops normally for months, then loses the ability to move, swallow, and finally breathe, because their cells cannot produce energy. There is no cure. Care means feeding tubes, ventilators, and usually a funeral before secondary school. A single affected child can consume a lifetime's worth of healthcare spending in a few years, and the system changes nothing.
[P1.2] Mitochondrial replacement therapy is the one intervention that stops this disease before it starts. When a mother carries pathogenic mitochondrial mutations, clinicians transfer the parents' nuclear DNA—the genome that makes a child who they are—into a donor egg with healthy mitochondria. The child is genetically the couple's own and disease-free; so are that child's descendants. One procedure ends an entire disease lineage.
[P1.3] This fits squarely within what public systems already do. We fund vaccines, newborn screening, and embryo genetic testing because prevention beats palliation on every metric. The existing alternatives fail here: embryo selection cannot guarantee a healthy embryo, because mitochondrial mutation loads vary unpredictably from egg to egg, leaving families a choice between childlessness, gambling on conception, or termination. Nor is this speculative—Britain legalized MRT in 2015 after years of scientific review and public consultation, and Australia followed.
[P1.4] This is regulated, licensed medicine.
[P1.5] Expect two objections. First, "three-parent babies": the donor contributes mitochondria—37 genes devoted to energy production, a minuscule fraction of the genome. Calling her a third parent is like crediting the battery as co-author of the laptop. Second, safety: serious regulators have scrutinized this technology for years, and refusing to fund it does not eliminate demand.
[P1.6] It pushes desperate families toward unregulated clinics abroad—the least safe outcome available.
[P1.7] The choice is stark: fund one preventive procedure, or fund decades of intensive care that ends in grief. Prevention is the mission of public health. Fund it.

It does not follow that because the state sometimes helps infertile couples conceive, it must also pay for heritable mitochondrial replacement, lifelong surveillance, and donor-egg manipulation so another group can avoid using a donor egg. [C2.3] The consent response also misses the asymmetry. Parents may consent to surgery or vaccination because the same child who bears the small risk receives the direct benefit. With MRT, parents and the first child gain the desired outcome, while daughters’ children and grandchildren inherit any mismatch or carried-over mutation without any say and without having faced the original near-certain disease in the same way. Ordinary reproduction does not involve clinicians deliberately introducing a third person’s heritable biology.
[C2.4] IVF in 1978 is therefore a false parallel. IVF helped an egg and sperm meet; it did not replace inherited cellular machinery in every cell and transmit that replacement down the female line. Calling this a transplant hides precisely that difference: a heart transplant dies with its recipient, donor mitochondria do not.
[C2.5] On safety tourism, the state is not obliged to fund a doubtful procedure domestically lest someone seek it abroad. We do not fund every unproven therapy to compete with foreign clinics; we regulate, warn, and fund proven care.
[C2.6] Finally, a tiny caseload does not make cost-effectiveness automatic. Per-family costs include repeated IVF cycles that may fail, donor provision, and decades of follow-up, while claimed savings assume the alternative was inevitably an intensive-care child—not donor conception, testing, or no pregnancy. Speculative avoided tragedy is not a budget.
