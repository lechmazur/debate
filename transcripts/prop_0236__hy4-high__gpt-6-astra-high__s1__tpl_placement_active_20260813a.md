[P1.1] A broad AI ban does not preserve learning; it makes learning harder to see. The issue is not whether generative AI can be misused. It can. The issue is which rule gives schools honest evidence of understanding: one that drives the tool underground, or one that makes its use citable, inspectable, and assessable.
[P1.2] Permissible by default is not laissez-faire. Students may use AI, but must cite it and disclose process: prompts, drafts, outputs kept or rejected, source checks, revisions, and what they decided themselves. Teachers can still set narrow no-tool tasks when unaided recall or timed composition is the skill being measured.
[P1.3] But a broad ban reverses the burden: it treats brainstorming, grammar feedback, translation support, code debugging, accessibility help, and Socratic explanations as presumptively corrupt. That is too blunt for a tool that is really a family of uses.
[P1.4] Transparency turns AI into evidence. In labs, students keep notebooks; in writing, they submit drafts; in programming, commits show development. AI belongs in the same logic. If a student asks for an outline, gets a weak definition, checks a textbook, and rewrites the argument, the teacher can see reasoning and correct the error.
[P1.5] If a student pastes a generated essay unchanged, disclosure exposes weak authorship and weak understanding. Banning both produces the same outward product: silence, suspicion, and a contest over detection.
[P1.6] Bans also damage the students schools claim to protect. Affluent students still get tutors, editing, and quiet access; less-supported students lose help explaining a prompt or starting a blank page. Enforcement leans on unreliable detectors, surveillance, and accusation, teaching that integrity means not getting caught.
[P1.7] The better norm is adult and teachable: name the tool, show the work, verify the result, and remain answerable for the claim. That is not lowering standards; it is replacing pretend purity with visible learning.
