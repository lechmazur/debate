[P1.1] Start with the skull on the shelf. Nobody donated it. It was dug out of a grave at night, scalped from a battlefield, or shipped from a mission hospital to a race scientist measuring cranial capacity to rank humanity. That is the provenance of most Indigenous remains in Western collections.
[P1.2] The question is whether a researcher's assertion that the skull is still "useful" cancels the claim of the people it was stolen from. It does not. Usefulness is not a title deed.
[P1.3] We already know this everywhere else. No university would keep a settler grandmother's bones because her genome is interesting. We require consent from the living and honor the wishes of the dead, and we do it even when science loses something. Research ethics boards would reject in an afternoon a proposal to study tissue taken without consent.
[P1.4] The only reason the rule bends here is that these particular dead were colonized people, and the institutions holding them inherited the colonizer's assumption that their consent never counted.
[P1.5] "Promptly" is the operative word, because delay is how refusal hides. Laws requiring return have existed in the United States for decades, yet tens of thousands of remains stayed in drawers, labeled "culturally unidentifiable" or "under study." The Ancient One, Kennewick Man, took roughly twenty years of litigation and a DNA result before reburial, and the DNA confirmed what the tribes said at the start. Every "just a few more years" is another generation that buries its grandparents without their grandparents.
[P1.6] Nor does return end science. It ends science without consent. Communities that control their ancestors routinely permit non-destructive study, oral-history collaboration, even genomic work, on their terms. What they refuse is being told they must earn their dead back by first paying a research tax.
[P1.7] Honoring that refusal builds the trust that makes future work possible; defying it poisons it.
[P1.8] So the clash is simple: does a researcher's interest outrank a descendant's right? Say yes, and you have endorsed the logic that filled these shelves to begin with.

[C1.1] The skull on the shelf is not a title deed; it is evidence of a crime. But PRO's leap to automatic, prompt return whenever a researcher says continued scientific study would be valuable turns repair into an evidentiary blindfold. Human remains are not ordinary property or data. They may be the only record of a massacre, a hidden cemetery, a disease history, or, decisively, the identity of the community entitled to receive them.
[C1.2] Return cannot be ethically prior to finding out whose dead they are. In clear cases, with known provenance and a recognized descendant community, delay is colonial possession under polite language. But many collections are documented badly because collectors treated people as specimens. A hasty return may hand ancestors to the wrong claimant, close off proof of an atrocity, or destroy context.
[C1.3] Deadlines, inventories, community-led review, and presumptive return are defensible; “promptly, science notwithstanding” is negligence wearing a moral costume.
[C1.4] The Ancient One example [P1.5] damages PRO's rule. Genetic and osteological work was not a research tax; it helped establish the affiliation on which repatriation depended. The scandal was prolonged litigation and obstruction, not inquiry. A ban on pre-return study would not have honored the tribes sooner; it would have asked officials to decide without the tools that made the claim legible.
[C1.5] The grandmother analogy [P1.3] conceals the hard cases. A settler grandmother usually has records, next of kin, and a will. Ancient remains often have fragmentary records, colonial labels, and several possible descendant communities. Consent is crucial, but it needs an identifiable rights-holder; institutions must determine who speaks for the dead through a fair process, not speed alone.
[C1.6] The clash is not “researcher interest versus descendant right.” It is accountable, time-limited stewardship that identifies, documents, and returns, set against a blanket rule that may bury truth along with bones. PRO offers a remedy for delay; that remedy must not repeat colonial arbitrariness in a new vocabulary.
