[P1.1] When a certified welder or a licensed nurse completes an employer-funded training program, the knowledge is permanently theirs; the account that paid for it should not evaporate the moment they change employers within the same field. The proposition is straightforward: workers should retain accrued training benefits when switching jobs inside a sector. This converts a tool of corporate retention into genuine, portable professional capital.
[P1.2] Opponents will claim this encourages poaching, but that diagnosis reverses the actual incentive problem. Right now, individual firms chronically underinvest in upskilling because they expect rivals to free-ride on their spending. Portability solves that collective-action failure. When training accounts follow the worker within a sector, the entire industry gains access to a larger, better-qualified labor pool, and firms must compete for talent by offering wages and conditions rather than debt traps.
[P1.3] The capital stays inside the industry; it simply refuses to shackle the human being who earned it.
[P1.4] Without transferability, these accounts operate as golden handcuffs. A warehouse operator declining a safer facility or an electrician refusing a higher-paying shop inside the same trade because of forfeiture clauses is not exercising loyalty. They are submitting to coercion. Dynamic labor markets depend on workers moving toward better matches based on talent and ambition, not on the threat of confiscated investments.
[P1.5] Yet that is precisely what CON must defend: a system where deferred compensation can be seized simply because a worker finds a better opportunity.
[P1.6] The same-sector boundary calms the employer’s legitimate worry about cross-industry flight. A construction firm’s spending on scaffolding certification still benefits the next construction employer. The sector retains the skill, while the worker retains dignity, bargaining power, and the freedom to seek advancement. That distinction is not a handout.
[P1.7] It is the difference between treating sector skills as shared infrastructure and treating workers as captive company property.

[C1.2] The immediate, predictable response will not be more generous training offers, but a sharp cut to employer-sponsored upskilling altogether. Faced with that risk, many will slash training budgets entirely, or push all training costs onto workers via lower base wages and mandatory certification fees, a shift that disproportionately harms entry-level, low-income, and marginalized workers who rely on employer-funded pathways to better jobs.
[C1.3] PRO’s claim that this policy expands worker bargaining power is backwards: by eliminating the ability of firms to earn a return on training investments, it removes the core incentive to offer training at all, leaving workers with fewer skill-building opportunities and less leverage, not more. The so-called “golden handcuffs” PRO decries are not coercion—they are the agreed-upon terms of a voluntary exchange where a worker receives paid, job-specific training in exchange for a commitment to stay long enough for the firm to recoup its investment.
[C1.4] Breaking that exchange to benefit a small number of workers who want to job-hop within a sector will leave the entire labor pool worse off.
