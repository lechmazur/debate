[P1.1] The whole case against a cap rests on one word: "access." Strip away the euphemism and ask what is actually being accessed. A card at 28 or 30 percent APR, marketed to someone already struggling, is not a lifeline. It is a product engineered so that a $6,000 balance costs roughly $1,700 a year in interest alone, so that minimum payments stretch across a decade, and so that the borrower's original emergency becomes a permanent tax on their income.
[P1.2] Losing "access" to that is not a harm. It is the point.
[P1.3] Consider what the cap actually does. Lenders cannot repeal risk, so they respond by lending less to the riskiest applicants, by lowering limits, or by shifting them to secured cards and small installment products. Every one of those outcomes is better for the borrower than an open revolving line at a rate that mathematically outruns most people's ability to repay. The proposition concedes the trade-off honestly and still wins, because the marginal credit being cut is precisely the credit most likely to end in default, collections, and ruined scores.
[P1.4] Second, this market does not price competitively, so the usual "let the price clear" objection fails. Almost no one chooses a card by APR. People choose by rewards, brand, or whatever offer arrives in the mail, then discover the rate when they cannot pay. Issuers know this, which is why card rates have drifted upward even in periods when funding costs fell.
[P1.5] That widening spread is not the price of risk; it is the price of inattention. A cap corrects a market failure, not a fair market.
[P1.6] Third, caps already work. Federal credit unions have operated under an interest ceiling for decades and still serve low-income members. Servicemembers have a rate cap, and card companies did not stop serving military families. Many wealthy democracies cap consumer rates and still have widespread card use.
[P1.7] The choice is not credit versus no credit. It is affordable credit for most versus predatory credit for some.

That stance ignores what actually happens when low-income households lose liquidity. Carrying a $600 emergency balance on a 29 percent card for two months costs under $30 in interest.
[C1.3] That is expensive, but it keeps the car running and the paychecks coming.

[C1.6] Furthermore, lenders capped by statute will not simply subsidize high-risk accounts. To offset the margin collapse, banks will slash credit lines, eliminate entry-level cards, and reintroduce punitive annual fees across the board.
[C1.7] The real choice is not between cheap credit and expensive credit. It is between expensive, regulated credit and absolute financial exile. Price caps do not liberate vulnerable borrowers; they abandon them.
