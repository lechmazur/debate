[P1.1] The organ shortage is a quiet catastrophe. In the United States alone, over 90,000 people sit on the kidney transplant waiting list. Roughly a dozen die every day waiting. Meanwhile, the prohibition on compensating donors doesn't eliminate transactions—it drives them underground into exploitative black markets where donors receive no follow-up care, no legal protections, and often no agreed-upon payment.
[P1.2] Our policy achieves the worst of both worlds: not enough organs, and the organs that do change hands are procured through coercion and fraud.
[P1.3] A regulated legal market attacks both halves of this failure. First, it increases supply. Economic incentives reliably change behavior—that's not controversial; it's the basis of every public health campaign that pays for blood plasma, compensates surrogates, or offers tax deductions for charitable giving. Even modest compensation, carefully structured, would bring in living donors who currently have no rational reason to accept surgical risk and lost wages.
[P1.4] Iran, which has permitted compensated kidney donation since the late 1980s, eliminated its transplant waiting list entirely. We don't need to adopt Iran's exact model, but it proves the mechanism works.
[P1.5] Second, regulation eliminates the black market's worst harms. A legal market means standardized medical screening, informed consent protocols, post-operative follow-up care, and contractual protections for donors—all things that disappear when transactions are forced underground. Today, a desperate seller in an unregulated market has no recourse if a broker cheats them or if surgical complications arise. Legalization doesn't create exploitation; it replaces unregulated exploitation with accountable oversight.
[P1.6] Critics will invoke dignity concerns or fear that only the poor will donate. But we already tolerate financial incentives in contexts with comparable bodily risk—military service, paid clinical trials, dangerous manual labor. The question isn't whether compensation touches kidneys; it's whether we prefer a system that kills thousands annually while protecting no one, or one that saves lives under enforceable rules. The moral case for prohibition collapsed the moment the waiting list became a death list.
