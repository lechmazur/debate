[P1.1] If a hostile state bombs a municipal power plant in midwinter or poisons a city’s water supply, international law unmistakably recognizes an armed attack. Yet if that same state induces the exact same blackout or contamination using malicious code, outdated legal ambiguity treats the strike as sub-threshold sabotage. Adversaries actively exploit this artificial gray zone. They target electrical grids, hospitals, dams, and fuel pipelines precisely because their expected costs are safely capped at diplomatic protests, unserved indictments, or sanctions they have already priced in.
[P1.2] International law exists to protect civilian life and state survival, not to privilege explosives over algorithms. Treating cyberattacks on civilian critical infrastructure as armed attacks aligns legal doctrine with physical reality: human casualties and systemic paralysis matter far more than the weapon’s physics. Crucially, pairing this classification with proportional military response options restores broken deterrence. Several aggressive states field sophisticated cyber commands while possessing minimal digital infrastructure or economic integration vulnerable to symmetric retaliation.
[P1.3] When cyber-for-cyber responses cannot impose meaningful pain, only cross-domain military options, such as neutralizing the physical military facility orchestrating an ongoing hospital shutdown, can compel an aggressor to stop.
[P1.4] Establishing this legal equivalence does not put escalation on a hair trigger. Granting military options creates sovereign capability, not an automatic mandate, and subjects every decision to the strict legal guardrails of necessity, verified attribution, and proportionality. Just as states already demand high-confidence intelligence before answering covert bombings or proxy strikes, they gain no license to launch airstrikes over unattributed glitches or minor disruptions where kinetic force would be manifestly disproportionate. What changes is the strategic calculus before an attack begins.
[P1.5] Today’s legal loophole invites escalating recklessness against civilian lifelines; drawing an unmistakable red line backed by credible military consequences forces adversaries to stand down.

[C1.7] Third, the red line would not be credible. No government will launch airstrikes over ransomware, so the line would be crossed, left unenforced, and deterrence would be weaker than before.
