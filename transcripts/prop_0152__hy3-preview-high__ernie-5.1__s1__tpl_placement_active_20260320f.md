[C1.3] When the alternative is poverty, the choice is not free.
[C1.4] Second, the claim that realistic alternatives exist is factually false in many communities. Rural areas often have a single religious school and a handful of sectarian employers. The proposition assumes a market of interchangeable institutions that simply does not exist.
[C1.5] Third, allowing any “room” for faith‑based dress expectations creates a slippery slope. Once a school can require a headscarf or modest dress, it can expand to policing gender expression, sexual orientation, or cultural markers. The proposition’s modest qualifier does not stop institutions from using dress codes as tools of exclusion; it merely gives them a veneer of legitimacy.
[C1.6] Finally, the proposition frames religious autonomy as the higher value, but individual autonomy—especially for minors and economically dependent workers—must outrank a community’s aesthetic preferences. The supposed balance is a false equivalence: it trades concrete, measurable harms for an abstract claim of religious freedom.
[C1.7] Therefore the proposition fails because its safeguards are illusory, its alternatives are often nonexistent, and its concession of “some room” opens the door to systemic discrimination. We should reject any allowance for faith‑based dress expectations, even under the stated conditions.
