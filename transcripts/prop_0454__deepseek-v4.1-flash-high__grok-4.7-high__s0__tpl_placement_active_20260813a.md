[P1.1] When unemployment spikes, speed is the whole ballgame. A family that loses a job does not need a program debated for six months; it needs cash before rent is due, before medicine is skipped, before a temporary shock becomes eviction and a permanent exit from work. Automatic stabilizers—transfers triggered by rising unemployment—deliver that. Discretionary relief is politically delayed, often too small, and vulnerable to partisan hostage-taking.
[P1.2] By the time it passes, the recession has already done its deepest damage.
[P1.3] The debt objection mistakes accounting for economics. Downturns are precisely when public debt should rise. Falling demand causes closures and layoffs, which shrink tax revenue and raise safety-net costs anyway. Refusing to transfer cash does not preserve the budget; it deepens the slump and makes the debt burden worse.
[P1.4] If automatic transfers keep consumption from collapsing, they shorten the recession and protect the tax base. Debt used to prevent a downward spiral is not reckless; it is self-limiting.
[P1.5] The design matters: triggers should expand with unemployment and phase down as it falls, so support is temporary by design. Broad triggers can also be paired with tax-system recapture for higher earners, making aid fast while recovering overpayments. The realistic comparison is not perfect targeting versus waste. It is timely, broad support versus delayed, inadequate, arbitrary relief.
[P1.6] Nor is this only about the unemployed. When workers know a job loss will not destroy their housing or health, they keep spending, and the whole economy suffers less. That is the meaning of an automatic stabilizer: a rule that acts before politics can.
[P1.7] Opponents will call it moral hazard, but unemployment in a downturn is not a lifestyle choice; it is a macroeconomic shock. The choice is a resilient economy with temporary, recoupable debt, or a sluggish economy with deeper hardship and, often, larger debt anyway. Adopt the stabilizer.

[C1.1] The proposition does not ask whether governments should cushion recessions. It asks them to pre-commit to expanding cash transfers whenever unemployment rises, and to treat the extra debt as acceptable. That pre-commitment is the mistake.
[C1.2] Unemployment is a lagging signal, not a steering wheel. By the time the rate climbs enough to trip a trigger, the layoffs have already landed, and the same lag keeps checks flowing after hiring has resumed. A national unemployment number cannot tell a recovering city from a still-depressed one, or a worker between jobs from a household that never lost income. Speed at the wrong moment is not stabilization.
[C1.3] Phase-downs will not save the design. [P1.5] promises triggers that retreat as unemployment falls, plus tax-system recapture. Once payments are statutory, cutting them off looks like cruelty, and recapture is slow, partial, and easy to waive. Automatic expansion is politically durable; automatic contraction is the part that fails.
[C1.4] Temporary by design is an aspiration, not a mechanism.
[C1.5] Nor is the debt self-limiting. [P1.4] says transfers that protect consumption shorten the recession and defend the tax base. That is true only of spending that is timely, targeted, and withdrawn. Cash keyed to a blunt aggregate fails on all three.
[C1.6] Payments to households that kept their jobs are leakage. Payments that land while the central bank is tightening feed inflation, a tax on the households the checks were meant to help. Debt not retired in the upswing remains for the next downturn, at higher interest cost and with less room to maneuver.
[C1.7] Governments already have automatic stabilizers that fit the shock better: unemployment insurance that tracks actual job loss, and a tax system that loses revenue when incomes fall. Piling a new cash expansion on top does not buy resilience. It buys mistimed spending, softer re-employment just as jobs return, and a ratchet in the debt stock. Do not adopt it.

[P2.5] On debt, CON treats the no-transfer world as fiscally clean. It is not. Mass layoffs shrink revenue and raise safety-net costs anyway, while lost output permanently scars workers. The relevant comparison is debt-to-GDP after a short shock versus debt after a long one.
[P2.6] Transfers that keep consumption from collapsing can make the debt path smaller, not larger.
[P2.7] The political ratchet concern [C1.3] is real but not decisive. Expansions can sunset by formula; if politics makes contraction hard, that same politics makes CON's discretionary relief hard to target and hard to end too. The choice is not perfect policy versus imperfect policy. It is a rule that acts early and may overshoot, versus a system that acts late, narrowly, and often too little.
[P2.8] In a recession, timeliness is not a design flaw. It is the point.
