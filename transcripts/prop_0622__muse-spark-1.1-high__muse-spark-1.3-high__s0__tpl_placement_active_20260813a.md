[P1.1] A carbon price fixes the externality at its source. Every ton costs society the same, so every ton should cost the emitter the same. Industrial subsidies don't. They bribe one technology in one sector while leaving diesel trucks, gas furnaces, and imported steel cheap.
[P1.2] You pay to displace emissions that pop up elsewhere. A rising carbon price cuts wherever it's cheapest, because everyone feels it.
[P1.3] The dividend makes that price fair. Carbon use rises sharply with income - frequent flying, large homes, large cars. Return all revenue equally and the math flips: a low-income family emitting 4 tons gets the same check as a wealthy family emitting 20. The wealthy pay net, the majority gains net.
[P1.4] Subsidies reverse that flow. You raise energy costs on everyone, then hand cash to battery plants, to capital owners, and to households who can afford a new EV. That's regressive by design, and voters know it.
[P1.5] Price also finds cuts subsidies cannot imagine. No agency can know whether the cheapest ton in 2026 is a heat pump in Ohio, less clinker in cement, or one fewer flight. A high price lets thousands of firms and millions of families find it. Subsidies pretend government knows.
[P1.6] It picks hydrogen this year, direct air capture next, and locks in bets at hundreds of dollars per ton that the market would have cut for $40 through efficiency and behavior.
[P1.7] And a dividend is politically durable. When most people get more back than they pay, raising the price has a constituency. Subsidy regimes create the opposite: a lobby to keep carbon cheap so the subsidy stays necessary, plus endless fights over who gets the factory, WTO disputes, and Solyndra-style backlash.
[P1.8] If we want less carbon, make carbon expensive and make the public a shareholder in that expense.

[C1.1] Making pollution expensive sounds tidy. Decarbonizing an economy is not tidy.
[C1.2] A dividend check does not build a transmission line, reopen a bus route, or retool a steel plant. Those are the bottlenecks that actually determine emissions. Punishment without alternatives is not efficiency; it is abdication.
[C1.3] Industrial subsidies solve the missing-alternative problem. Deployment support drove down the cost of solar, wind, and batteries by creating scale, supply chains, and learning. No diffuse price signal could have financed the first gigafactory or the first offshore wind port. Those required concentrated bets, guaranteed demand, and shared infrastructure that thousands of isolated consumers cannot coordinate.
[C1.4] Returning the money equally wastes the scarcest resource in climate policy: public revenue that can unlock private capital. A hundred dollars per person disappears into groceries or even more fuel. That same money pooled into grid upgrades, clean manufacturing, and targeted retrofits cuts emissions every year for thirty years and pulls private investment with it.
[C1.5] The fairness story also inverts on contact with reality. High earners can absorb higher fuel and food prices, install solar, and still collect the same dividend. Poor households in cold regions, car-dependent towns, or leaky rentals pay immediately at the pump and the meter, while the check arrives later and erodes. Subsidies can be aimed: weatherization for low-income homes, transit where people actually live, factories in left-behind regions.
[C1.6] Durability comes from things built, not checks mailed. Factories employ people, supply chains create lobbies for clean power, and costs fall permanently. A dividend creates no cheaper clean option, invites repeal the moment energy prices spike, and leaves emissions largely intact. Price without provision fails; provision makes price bearable.
