[P1.1] Gross domestic product counts every market transaction as a plus, no matter what it does to people's lives. Rebuilding after a flood, treating pollution-related illness, longer commutes, divorce lawyers and prison cells all raise GDP. So do a pay rise, a useful bridge and a lifesaving drug. A measure that cannot distinguish damage from progress cannot be the headline test of progress.
[P1.2] That blindness is systematic. An economy can grow while median pay stagnates, rents absorb the gains, working hours lengthen, loneliness rises and healthy life expectancy stalls. Growth concentrated at the top, bought with exhaustion, or that poisons air and water can look like success. If the scoreboard declares victory while ordinary lives feel harder, policy is answering the wrong question.
[P1.3] Targets govern trade-offs. When GDP growth is the primary headline, ministers ask first what boosts output this quarter: approve the development, cut the regulation, push people into any job, delay environmental repair because it costs output now. Well-being reverses the test: does this raise healthy, secure, satisfying lives over time? A job counts more if it is stable and not soul-crushing.
[P1.4] Transport counts for access and time saved, not just construction spend. Prevention counts because avoiding illness is better than paying to treat it.
[P1.5] This is measurable, not misty. Governments already track life satisfaction, mental health, healthy life expectancy, financial security, social connection, safety and time pressure. Combined into a transparent headline framework — published, audited and debated like GDP — those measures are closer to what citizens actually ask from prosperity. They are also harder to fake with a spending splurge, asset bubble or resource boom.
[P1.6] GDP should remain a useful instrument, a gauge of market capacity and tax resources. But instruments serve ends. The end of economic policy is not more transactions; it is people living better lives. Make well-being the headline, and GDP finally works for that purpose instead of replacing it.

[P2.1] Objectivity is the strongest card against well-being, and it is thinner than it looks. GDP is not found in nature. It counts paid childcare but not a parent's care, counts extracting oil as income without deducting depletion, and is routinely revised months later. Those are conventions — reasonable, but conventions.
[P2.2] If independent statisticians can apply agreed rules to output, they can apply agreed rules to life satisfaction, healthy life expectancy and financial security, with methods published and audited just as GDP is.
[P2.3] The weighting objection proves too much. GDP already weights everything, silently: a pound spent on a luxury yacht counts the same as a pound of nurses' pay, and a pound spent cleaning an oil spill counts for more than a pound spent preventing one. Well-being frameworks at least force the weights into the open, where parliament and voters can dispute them. Hidden value judgments are not objectivity; they are unaccountability.
[P2.4] Nor does a new headline repeal arithmetic. Making well-being primary does not abolish budgets, borrowing limits or GDP itself — it demotes GDP to the instrument it should be. Indeed GDP primacy weakens the very discipline claimed for it: borrow for a pre-election splurge, inflate a housing boom, or defer maintenance, and output rises now while the bill arrives later. A well-being target tracked over time, including household debt stress and health, catches that harm earlier instead of applauding it.
[P2.5] Finally, that growth funds well-being is exactly the point. An input can be necessary without being the goal. The satisfied rich countries cited do not just produce output; they convert it into security, public health, leisure and trust — and it is that conversion policy should be judged on. Where conversion fails, GDP can rise while median pay, healthy years and free time do not.
[P2.6] Telling voters that 2.5% growth means success when their rent, waiting list and exhaustion say otherwise is not transparency. It is insisting the thermometer is warm while the patient feels cold.
