[P1.1] Merger review guards against concentration, yet it operates with a glaring asymmetry: it scrutinizes product-market power while systematically ignoring equivalent power over workers. Labor-market monopsony deserves the same seriousness as consumer-price effects because the mechanism, the harm, and the measurability are all comparable.
[P1.2] The mechanism is symmetric. A monopsonist suppresses wages, cuts hours, and degrades safety exactly as a monopolist inflates prices and restricts output. Both create deadweight loss, shrink total transactions, and extract surplus from the weaker party. The logic does not change because the sticker reads “wage” rather than “rent.” If antitrust law blocks a merger over a modest projected increase in consumer prices, consistency demands it block one projected to impose a comparable wage cut on thousands of employees.
[P1.3] Current practice does the opposite. Agencies routinely clear hospital consolidations that leave nurses or technicians with fewer employers, stagnant pay, and worse scheduling, provided patient prices to insurers remain stable. The same pattern appears in retail warehousing and meatpacking, where mergers shrink the pool of competing employers without triggering review. Workers bear the full cost of concentration, yet merger guidelines treat that cost as legally irrelevant.
[P1.4] This is not merely an oversight; it reflects an unjustified hierarchy that privileges consumer welfare above worker welfare. Competition policy should protect participants on every side of a transaction, not exempt employers from scrutiny merely because they buy labor rather than sell widgets.
[P1.5] Nor is the task administratively impossible. Regulators already define labor markets by occupational skill sets and commuting zones. Payroll records, online job postings, and turnover rates provide ready metrics for concentration and mobility. Courts accept analogous evidence elsewhere.
[P1.6] The barrier is institutional, not analytical.
[P1.7] Treating labor monopsony with equal seriousness is not adventurism; it is coherence. A merger that undermines competition should be halted whether it pickpockets shoppers or exploits workers.
