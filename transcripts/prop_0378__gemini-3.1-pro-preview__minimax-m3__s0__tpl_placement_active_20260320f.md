[P1.1] The collapse of adult friendship is no longer a personal failure; it is a structural crisis. When millions of adults report having zero close friends, we are not witnessing a sudden drop in individual social skills. We are seeing the predictable result of how our cities, economies, and workplaces are built. Consequently, adult friendship must be elevated to an active social-policy concern.
[P1.2] First, the barriers to connection are deeply systemic. The traditional ecosystems of friendship—accessible "third places" like community centers, public squares, and affordable local venues—have been largely zoned or priced out of existence. Simultaneously, always-on work cultures and sprawling commutes have stripped adults of the consistent, unstructured time necessary to cultivate relationships. You cannot solve a structural deficit with individual willpower alone.
[P1.3] Telling an exhausted worker to simply "put themselves out there" fails when their neighborhood lacks communal spaces and their employer demands constant availability.
[P1.4] Second, the consequences of social isolation are fundamentally public. Chronic loneliness is a recognized public health crisis, carrying severe mortality risks, driving up healthcare costs, and fueling epidemics of depression and anxiety. In the workplace, isolation breeds burnout, high turnover, and reduced productivity. These are not private struggles; they are massive societal burdens.
[P1.5] When institutions ignore the fraying of social bonds, they inevitably pay for the fallout through overwhelmed healthcare systems and degraded economic output.
[P1.6] Therefore, institutions must actively intervene. Cities must treat social infrastructure with the same urgency as physical infrastructure, actively zoning for walkable neighborhoods and funding accessible community spaces. Workplaces must enact structural policies that protect personal time—such as a rigid right to disconnect—giving employees the actual margin required to maintain personal ties.
[P1.7] Treating friendship as a purely private matter assumes individuals have the power to overcome societal design. They do not. By recognizing friendship as a policy concern, we shift the burden from isolated individuals back to the institutions that shape our daily lives, ensuring the environment we live in makes connection possible rather than impossible.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to take one of the most intimate, voluntary relationships in adult life and submit it to bureaucratic management. That is the wrong response to a real problem.
[C1.2] First, the premise is overstated. Adult friendship has not collapsed; it has transformed. Most adults still report at least one close confidant. The shape has changed - smaller circles, looser geographic ties, more digital contact - but the capacity for chosen intimacy has not evaporated.
[C1.3] Declaring a structural crisis inflates the case for intervention and licenses exactly the kind of overreach the proposition invites.
[C1.4] Second, the motion commits a category error. Policy is the right tool for collective-action problems with public-goods features: roads, clean air, disease control. Friendship is none of these. It is selective, reciprocal, and voluntary by definition.
[C1.5] Its worth comes partly from being unwilled and unmanaged. You cannot zone it into existence, fund it through grants, or legislate it into being without corrupting the very thing you claim to protect.
[C1.6] Third, the historical record is grim. When states set out to engineer social bonds, the results tend toward coerced participation, performative community, and hollow institutions. Managed friendship becomes either propaganda or paperwork - never the warm, candid ties the policy is supposed to deliver.
[C1.7] Fourth, the workplace plank is especially dangerous. Employers already intrude into personal time; the motion asks them to manage friendship itself. That path leads to mandated social hours, wellness surveillance, and HR-administered "connection," not real bonds. A right to disconnect is defensible on labor grounds; a duty to befriend is not.
[C1.8] Finally, who decides what counts as a good friendship, and who pays when institutions get it wrong? Light public support for genuine third places is reasonable; declaring friendship itself a policy domain is not.
