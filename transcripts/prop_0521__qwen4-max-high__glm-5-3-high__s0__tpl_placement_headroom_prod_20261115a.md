[P1.1] An employer-funded training account is deferred pay. Nobody funds a training budget out of affection; it is part of the package a worker accepts instead of higher cash wages. So when a nurse's aide changes hospitals and her training balance resets to zero, she has not lost a perk. She has lost earnings she already worked for — and the industry keeps the certified worker her wages helped build.
[P1.2] That forfeiture is not an accounting quirk; it is a leash. Non-portable benefits and training repayment clauses make the cheapest raise a worker can get the one she never asks for. Employers know the cost of leaving includes an unused tuition balance and a certification clawback, so they can underbid her wage. Across a sector short of staff, that means stagnant pay in the jobs we claim to be desperate to fill.
[P1.3] The motion already answers the objection CON will lead with. Portability stops at the sector line. A hospital cannot train a nurse and watch an unrelated industry harvest her; the money follows the skill inside the sector whose labor pool it improves. Building trades have run multiemployer apprenticeship and training funds for generations on precisely this logic: contractors contribute, workers carry the credential from site to site, and the trade does not collapse from poaching — it gets a deeper bench.
[P1.4] And portability makes training worth buying. A benefit you can lose is a benefit you discount; workers skip courses they cannot carry, and employers then complain about skill gaps. Attach the account to the person and uptake rises.
[P1.5] So the clash is narrow. CON must defend forfeiture: explain why a worker's earned training dollars should evaporate over a change of employer while the sector retains every dollar of value she created. The account should follow the worker.

The nurse's aide who leaves after two courses has already captured the real benefit: the skill, the certification, the higher wage it commands.
[C1.2] She carries that out the door. The account balance is not her paycheck; demanding it is demanding the next course as well.
[C1.3] That matters because portability doesn't redistribute value — it destroys the incentive to create it. Make training portable and you create a free-rider problem with no enforcement mechanism. The hospital that invests $8,000 per worker watches the clinic down the street hire that worker away, trained, while contributing nothing. Rational employers respond exactly as you'd expect: they stop training, or they slash budgets to token levels and shift the cost into wages workers must spend themselves on tuition.
[C1.4] The worker who now gets $800 more in cash but must fund her own $5,000 certification is worse off. Portability feels generous and produces scarcity.
[C1.5] The trades analogy proves the opposite of what PRO claims. Multiemployer apprenticeship funds work because they are collectively governed — joint labor-management boards, mandatory contributions from every signatory contractor, shared curriculum control. That is mutual insurance against poaching, not a right of individual workers to walk with a balance. Portability survives there precisely because it isn't what this motion proposes.
[C1.6] And notice what the motion cannot answer: "same sector" is undefined. Hospital to staffing agency? Platform work? Every boundary dispute becomes litigation and arbitrage — and every clawback dispute becomes a lawsuit.
[C1.7] The choice is not portability versus a leash. It is a functioning training market versus a legal rule that makes investment in people irrational. Vote it down.
