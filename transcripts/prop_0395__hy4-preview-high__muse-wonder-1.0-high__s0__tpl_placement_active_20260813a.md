[P1.1] Mandatory retirement survives not because it is good management, but because it is a routine workforce-management tool—a lazy default that lets employers avoid assessing individuals and planning succession. The proposition is right: in most occupations it should be abolished.
[P1.2] The central clash is age versus ability. Work should be allocated by what a person can do, not by how old they are. If a job requires memory, judgment, or precision, we can test that directly. A 66-year-old radiologist, engineer, or accountant can be evaluated by audit, output, and peer review; a blanket age rule, however, retires the excellent older worker while keeping a less competent younger colleague.
[P1.3] When ability is measurable, calendar age is not merely crude; it is irrational discrimination: it removes the capable 67-year-old and leaves the unfit 55-year-old untouched.
[P1.4] This is not a neutral tool. It forcibly exits people at the peak of their experiential value. Senior nurses, teachers, engineers, and tradespeople carry institutional knowledge and mentoring capacity that vanish overnight. In aging societies facing labor shortages, mass forced exit wastes human capital and deepens staffing crises.
[P1.5] It also demeans workers: being told to stop, not because they failed but because they aged, strips dignity and economic security, often before pensions are adequate.
[P1.6] The usual defenses fail. 'Orderly succession' can be achieved through phased retirement and knowledge transfer, not coercion. 'Protecting older workers' is paternalism that removes their choice. 'Opening jobs for the young' relies on the lump-of-labor fallacy; experienced workers raise productivity and complement younger colleagues rather than stealing a fixed number of slots.
[P1.7] Abolition is not utopian universalism. In the narrow set of roles where safety depends on an objectively measured capacity, the answer is frequent, job-specific testing—not a birthday. For most occupations, mandatory retirement is an outdated, discriminatory shortcut. Judge workers as individuals, not by date of birth.

[C1.1] Mandatory retirement is not prejudice dressed as management. It is a predictable, impersonal bargain that solves problems individual testing cannot solve reliably or humanely.
[C1.2] Testing sounds neutral until you run it. Capacity declines gradually and unevenly, and a snapshot exam, audit, or peer review often misses early impairment, fatigue, or slower emergency response until after a failure. Testing every older worker frequently is expensive, contested, and litigated, and managers predictably delay hard calls about respected seniors. A known retirement age avoids that trap: people exit not because they were branded incompetent at 71 after a humiliating process, but because a rule known for decades applies to everyone alike.
[C1.3] Organizations are also ladders, not open fields. Universities, partnerships, hospitals, firms, and civil services have limited senior posts, salary budgets, and partnership shares. If top incumbents can remain indefinitely, promotion stalls, recruitment of new skills stalls, and ambitious younger staff leave for places where advancement is possible. This is not a fixed-economy fallacy inside a fixed hierarchy; a chair, a partnership, or a funded attending post occupied for five extra years is genuinely unavailable to the next cohort.
[C1.4] Voluntary phased retirement does not fix it, because those earning the most and holding the most status have the least incentive to volunteer.
[C1.5] Mandatory retirement also underwrites the deal workers get earlier. Job security, rising senior pay, and pensions are more sustainable when employers know the endpoint of their most expensive years. Remove that endpoint and employers will not become sentimental; they will hire fewer older workers, flatten senior pay, and manage decline through harsher dismissals.
[C1.6] Predictability is a benefit, not laziness. It lets workers plan savings and succession, lets employers train replacements, and lets mentorship happen toward a real handover. In most occupations, retaining that routine rule is fairer across generations and kinder in practice than abolishing it in the name of individual choice.

[C2.1] Their case rests on a seductive assumption: because ability can sometimes be measured, refusing to rely solely on measurement is discrimination. That ignores how workplaces actually fail. Performance systems tolerate mediocrity because errors are usually correctable. With decline, the danger is the false pass — the senior surgeon whose reviews stay glowing until reaction time matters, the partner whose clients drift away, the professor whose students absorb outdated material for years.
[C2.2] By the time audits prove the case, damage is done and the exit becomes exactly the humiliating, litigated branding abolition was supposed to avoid.
[C2.3] That is why a universal endpoint is not a claim that everyone fails on the same birthday. It is a second-best rule for institutions that predictably flinch. It trades a blunt boundary for a system that will either test too little or dismiss too cruelly.
[C2.4] The ladder answer misses the same point. Yes, any retained employee occupies a post. But a 45-year-old occupant sits under a known horizon that lets departments plan cohorts, tenure lines, and partnership tracks. Remove that horizon and occupancy becomes open-ended, concentrated where pay, authority, and veto power are highest. “Succession planning” and part-time conversion sound gentle until you ask who must agree: the incumbent who loses income and status by agreeing.
[C2.5] Without a default exit, planning is just a request the most powerful can decline indefinitely.
[C2.6] Their fallback — enforce anti-discrimination law harder — proves too much. Hiring bias against a 58-year-old applicant is notoriously hard to prove amid a pile of resumes; performance dismissal of a 72-year-old invites the very lawsuit and stigma they deplore. Mandatory retirement makes employing older workers safer for firms, because the commitment is bounded.
[C2.7] Finally, an age everyone reaches under a rule known from entry is not comparable to exclusion by race or sex. It sequences a career across a lifetime: protection and rising reward while serving, then room for the next generation who will accept the same terms.
