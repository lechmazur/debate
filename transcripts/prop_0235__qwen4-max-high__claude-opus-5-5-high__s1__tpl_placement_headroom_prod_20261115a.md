[P1.1] The decisive word in this motion is rebuilding. A hard defense isn't a purchase, it's a subscription: a seawall buys a couple of decades of confidence, then needs raising, armoring and renourishing as the water climbs, each round costlier than the last. Managed retreat is the only intervention that permanently removes what gets damaged. Everything else leaves the houses in the ocean's path and asks taxpayers to hold that line forever.
[P1.2] Walls also manufacture the risk they claim to control. Behind a new levee, lenders relax, developers build, and exposure grows, so the eventual overtopping is worse than if nothing had been defended. Wealthy countries have run this experiment repeatedly: public money underwrites private beachfront, storms flatten it, and the same houses get rebuilt on the same sand. That isn't protection; it's a subsidy with a storm attached.
[P1.3] Being rich is exactly why we can do retreat well. Managed means voluntary, phased, fairly compensated buyouts and relocation planned with residents — the buyout programs New York and New Jersey ran after Sandy show the mechanism exists and can be humane. It also means moving people together, keeping neighbors and tax bases intact, instead of letting insurers withdraw and prices sag until only those who cannot leave are still holding the risk.
[P1.4] The Netherlands will be invoked, but a dense delta with centuries of water institutions and enormous concentrated value is not the template for thousands of kilometres of scattered shoreline — low roads, failing septic systems, a few thousand homes per stretch. Defending Rotterdam makes sense. Defending every empty mile is how a country spends a fortune to postpone a decision it will still have to make, only poorer and with fewer options.
[P1.5] Retreat isn't abandoning communities. It's the alternative to abandoning them later, without warning, after the wall fails. Fund the move while people still have a choice.
