[P1.1] Corporate “net-zero” has become an accounting sleight of hand rather than an industrial transition. Today, a heavily polluting firm can burn fossil fuels as usual, purchase a pile of cheap external carbon credits, and market itself to consumers and investors as climate-neutral. Legally capping offsets at a small residual share—while requiring most reductions to happen inside a company’s own value chain—is essential to align corporate claims with physical reality and fair competition.
[P1.2] First, unlimited offsetting treats two fundamentally unequal things as identical. Emitting a ton of fossil carbon locks heat-trapping gas into the atmosphere with certainty for centuries. Offsetting that ton typically relies on fragile biological storage, like tree planting, or counterfactual baselines, like paying landowners not to log forests they might never have logged anyway. Forests burn and suffer blight; avoided-emissions baselines are chronically vulnerable to manipulation.
[P1.3] Even without fraud, there is simply not enough spare land on Earth to absorb ongoing industrial emissions. Treating uncertain, temporary external projects as a one-to-one substitute for permanent fossil pollution is physical nonsense.
[P1.4] Second, unconstrained offsets actively delay the technological transition we need. Overhauling a value chain—electrifying transport, retooling factories, or decarbonizing suppliers—requires serious upfront capital. When the law allows a company to buy the exact same “net-zero” badge for pennies on the dollar through offset brokers, it creates a severe moral hazard. Executives rationally choose cheap paper credits over hard operational change, locking in high-carbon infrastructure for decades.
[P1.5] Worse, this punishes honest innovators: firms that make expensive, genuine investments in clean production are undercut by rivals buying identical green credentials on the cheap.
[P1.6] This policy does not ban companies from funding external conservation. It simply enforces truth in advertising. By legally restricting offsets to the small fraction of truly unavoidable emissions, we stop greenwashing, reward real industrial innovation, and force corporate capital to actually cut emissions at the source.

[C1.1] The proposition confuses geography with physics. A ton of carbon dioxide traps the same amount of heat whether it is prevented in a factory in Ohio or a coal plant in India. Legally capping offsets at an arbitrary small share does not reduce global emissions—it merely makes compliance more expensive while shrinking the total pool of climate finance available to the planet.
[C1.2] Carbon markets exist precisely because abatement costs vary wildly across the global economy. Electrifying a legacy steel furnace in a developed nation might cost hundreds of dollars per ton, while retiring a coal plant in a developing economy or funding clean energy infrastructure costs a fraction of that. Forcing companies to spend climate dollars only within their own value chains means deploying capital where it does the least good, achieving fewer total atmospheric reductions for the same corporate expenditure. This is not rigorous climate policy; it is economic nationalism dressed as environmental virtue.
[C1.3] Worse, the proposal severs the crucial link between corporate capital and the regions that need decarbonization most. Offsets channel billions into renewable energy, forest conservation, and clean technology in the Global South—economies that lack the domestic capital to transition on their own. Restricting offsets to value chains traps that money in wealthy countries, starving the very projects that could deliver the deepest, fastest emissions cuts available to humanity.
[C1.4] Finally, the motion ignores the reality of hard-to-abate sectors. Aviation, cement, and maritime shipping cannot fully decarbonize in-value-chain with current technology. Demanding that residual emissions stay within arbitrary geographic boundaries makes genuine net-zero claims legally impossible for the industries that need transitional flexibility the most. The predictable result is not more reduction; it is companies abandoning net-zero pledges entirely because the legal standard has been set impossibly high.
[C1.5] The path to real decarbonization runs through efficient global markets that maximize reductions per dollar, not through accounting rules that prioritize local spending over planetary impact.
