[P1.1] Land-value taxation targets what owners cannot hide, move, or produce less of: the underlying site. Because land supply is fixed, taxing its unimproved value does not destroy land. Instead, it changes the owner’s calculation. A vacant lot, surface car park, and apartment building on equally valuable neighboring sites face similar land charges, so leaving a scarce site idle becomes costly while constructing homes is not penalized.
[P1.2] That is precisely the incentive cities with housing shortages need.
[P1.3] Transaction taxes work backward. They charge people when property changes hands, discouraging moves, sales, redevelopment, and the transfer of sites to owners who would use them more intensively. A household may remain in an unsuitable home to avoid the tax; a developer may delay acquiring fragmented parcels; revenues collapse when the market slows. Transaction taxes therefore reduce both market fluidity and fiscal reliability.
[P1.4] Rent caps can offer immediate relief to some incumbent tenants, but they do not create sites, labor, or capital. If rents are held below viable returns without a fully funded substitute, marginal projects are delayed, conversions increase, and maintenance suffers. Benefits also attach to particular tenancies rather than reliably reaching the households in greatest need. Most importantly for this motion, rent caps raise no revenue with which councils can finance infrastructure or affordable construction.
[P1.5] Land-value taxation improves both sides of the ledger. It encourages development while giving local government a comparatively stable base tied to location value. Roads, transit, schools, parks, and planning permissions often increase nearby land values; recapturing part of that uplift lets the community finance the investments that produced it. This creates a virtuous cycle: better infrastructure supports more homes, and rising site values help pay for that infrastructure.
[P1.6] Implementation requires regular valuation and sensible transition rules, but those are administrative challenges, not defects in the underlying incentive. Compared with taxing transactions or suppressing rents, taxing land rewards building, penalizes withholding, reduces volatility, and aligns private land use with public investment. That is the stronger architecture for both housing supply and local finance.

[C1.4] Worse, in the short run housing supply is fixed, so a tax on site value is capitalized into higher rents charged to incumbent tenants before a single new unit arrives. [C1.7] So the comparison resolves against Pro twice: rent caps beat LVT on preserving housing that exists; transaction taxes beat LVT on collecting reliable revenue without fabricating an unobservable base. LVT is not "more" on either axis.
