[P1.1] Countries should ban prophylactic antibiotic use in healthy livestock because it converts a shared medical resource into a cheap production input. This is not a ban on treating sick animals; it targets dosing whole herds that are not ill, day after day, to mask crowding, poor hygiene, feed changes, or to get small growth gains. Producers get predictability; the public gets selection pressure for resistance.
[P1.2] That trade is indefensible. Each unnecessary dose gives bacteria more chances to survive and share resistance genes. Resistant organisms travel through workers, manure, water, slaughterhouses, and food chains. The harm is not abstract: it erodes drugs used for pneumonia, surgery, cancer care, sepsis, and ordinary infections.
[P1.3] Lower yields can be absorbed by higher prices, innovation, and diet shifts; lost antibiotic effectiveness is cumulative and often not recoverable.
[P1.4] The price objection proves too much. Higher meat prices hurt poor households; lower yields hurt producers. But a cheap cut that omits resistance costs is not cheap—it is subsidized by future patients. Clean-water, safety, and hazardous-input rules also raise prices, yet we accept them because the alternative is making bystanders pay.
[P1.5] Antibiotic resistance is exactly that kind of unpriced damage, with a longer shadow.
[P1.6] Nor does a ban abandon animal health. Vaccination, lower stocking density, ventilation, sanitation, better weaning, testing, isolation, and veterinary treatment for actual disease all remain. They may cost more, but they address why routine prophylaxis looks attractive. If a system needs continuous antibiotics to keep healthy animals healthy, its low price depends on depleting a common medical shield.
[P1.7] The decisive comparison is manageable price increases now versus permanent weakening of medicine later. The narrow metric counts tons and shelf prices; the prior question is whether antibiotics still work when people need them. Governments can cushion consumers, support farmers, and accept somewhat less meat. They cannot credibly restore susceptibility once resistance spreads.
[P1.8] Reserve antibiotics for illness, not habit.

[C1.1] The proposition’s central mistake is treating “prophylactic use in healthy livestock” as one practice. It is not. It covers routine growth promotion, which deserves restriction, but also preventive treatment of animals that are clinically healthy yet predictably at risk: newly weaned pigs, transported cattle, flocks facing a known seasonal pathogen, or herds recovering from an outbreak. A blanket ban cannot separate prudent prevention from lazy husbandry.
[C1.2] It simply forbids the veterinarian’s tool and waits for disease.
[C1.3] That waiting game is the weak point in the PRO case. If producers cannot prevent a predictable outbreak, they treat it after animals get sick. Sick animals suffer more, produce less, and often need longer or broader antibiotic courses—and sometimes more animals are treated than would have been with targeted prevention. A ban can therefore reduce doses on paper while increasing disease, mortality, and total therapeutic use.
[C1.4] The proposition promises less resistance pressure but does not establish that a blunt prohibition delivers it.
[C1.5] The motion also concedes the costs—higher meat prices and lower yields—and then asks us to accept them for an uncertain benefit. Those costs fall hardest on poor households and on producers with thin margins. Lower yields can encourage more land, feed, and water use per kilo of meat. A policy that raises food prices and intensifies resource pressure needs a demonstrated resistance dividend, not an assumption that all prevention is abuse.
[C1.6] The better comparison is not “ban versus do nothing.” It is “blanket ban versus regulated stewardship”: ban growth promotion, require veterinary oversight, set withdrawal periods, monitor resistance, and restrict the most critical human antibiotics. That targets indiscriminate mass dosing without sacrificing prevention of genuine disease risk. PRO’s ban is a sledgehammer that cannot tell the difference, and its costs are certain while its resistance gains are not.
[C1.7] Reject the proposition: it punishes good animal husbandry along with bad, and may leave us with sicker animals, costlier food, and no reliable gain for human medicine.
