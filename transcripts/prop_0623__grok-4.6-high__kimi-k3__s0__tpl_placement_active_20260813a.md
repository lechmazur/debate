[P1.1] Low-wage work is organized by industry, not by isolated firms. A care worker, a warehouse picker, and a hotel cleaner do not lose power because one employer is uniquely weak. They lose it because the sector fragments employment, subcontracts risk, and rewards whoever pays least. Firm-by-firm bargaining cannot reach that structure.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining can.
[P1.3] Coverage first. Private-sector enterprise bargaining now covers only a shrinking sliver of workers. The people who most need standards—in retail, hospitality, logistics, and care—are least likely to have a contract. A statutory minimum wage is better than nothing, but it is crude.
[P1.4] It cannot set industry scheduling rules, fix misclassification, or attach safety and training duties to the firms that actually control the work. Sectoral frameworks can. Workers get a real floor without organizing every outlet from scratch.
[P1.5] Fair competition next. When one nursing home or franchisee undercuts another on labor costs, the “efficient” firm is often just the one most willing to degrade the job. Industry minima convert that race into competition on quality and productivity. High-road employers stop being punished.
[P1.6] The modern firm makes this urgent. Supply chains, franchising, and platforms have already turned the legal employer into a thin shell. Asking workers to bargain only with that shell is a category error. Standards should follow the real economic unit.
[P1.7] Flexibility objections miss the design. These frameworks set floors, not identical pay packets. Firms can still pay more and organize work differently above the line. What they lose is the option to compete by impoverishing the workforce.
[P1.8] The status quo already chooses a policy: atomized workers facing coordinated cost-cutting. Sectoral bargaining is the correction, because a labor market that works as an industry must be governed as one.

[C1.1] PRO's whole case rests on one move: because labor markets function as industries, they must be governed as industries. That skips the decisive question—who pays when an industry-wide floor meets the firms, regions, and workers who cannot bear it.
[C1.2] Start with exclusion. An "industry" contains capital-city contractors and rural family firms, high-margin leaders and marginal survivors. A floor set high enough to matter for the first group prices out the second. South Africa extended its clothing-sector agreement to non-parties; small manufacturers in lower-wage towns faced compliance costs written in the metros, and jobs disappeared or went informal.
[C1.3] Southern Europe's rigid sectoral grids coexisted for years with youth unemployment stuck above thirty percent while insiders kept their protections. The workers PRO invokes—the least organized, least credentialed—are precisely those whose jobs sit below the negotiated line. Sectoral floors protect incumbents by amputating the bottom rung.
[C1.4] Second, look at who sits at the table. With private-sector union density near a tenth in many economies, "the industry" in practice means big employers and established unions drafting rules everyone else must obey. That is a cost-raising cartel armed with quasi-legislative power and accountable to no ballot. PRO says high-road firms will stop being punished; in reality they gain a legal tool to punish entrants, raise rivals' costs, and lock in their own business model as the mandatory standard.
[C1.5] Third, the promised flexibility is illusory. Real sectoral agreements are not a bare floor—they are wage grids, job classifications, scheduling codes. They compress differentials, dull the return to training, and when demand falls, force adjustment onto headcount instead of margins.
[C1.6] Finally, the diagnosis does not compel the cure. Fissured work and monopsony justify enforcement, portable benefits, safety regulation, and a smarter statutory minimum—not handing rule-making to the private parties least accountable to the workers outside the room. PRO counts the workers inside the room. Count the ones the door closes on.
