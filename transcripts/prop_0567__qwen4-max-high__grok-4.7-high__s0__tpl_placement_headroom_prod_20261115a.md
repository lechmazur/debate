[P1.1] A trial is the state using force against a person in the public's name. That is why we promise a public trial. But "public" has quietly come to mean whoever fits on a wooden bench — a fire-code accident, not a principle. When a defendant's life is decided in front of six strangers and one reporter, the public has been excluded as effectively as if the doors were locked.
[P1.2] A camera does not create access; it restores access that architecture stole.
[P1.3] And the alternative is not privacy — it is filtering. Without a recording, citizens get a reporter's paraphrase, a sketch artist's guess at a witness's expression, and whichever twenty seconds an editor finds newsworthy. Claims about coerced confessions or coached experts reach the public pre-chewed by someone on deadline. A fixed, unedited record is the strongest available check on that distortion, and on judges and prosecutors who behave differently when nobody outside the room will ever see it.
[P1.4] Notice the word "most." This motion already concedes the hard cases: children, sexual-assault complainants, informants whose lives depend on anonymity, national-security evidence. Those get closed doors or shielding. What remains is the ordinary run of contract disputes, arraignments, sentencing, and criminal trials where the witnesses are police officers and experts — public servants doing public work.
[P1.5] Many state courts have run cameras for decades under exactly these limits: no juror shots, no close-ups, judge's hand on the switch. The famous circus came from a specific judge's permissiveness, not from the existence of a lens. So the burden here is sharp: my opponent must show that the harms of cameras cannot be handled by rules we already know how to write. If a narrower tool works, a blanket ban is not caution.
[P1.6] It is choosing to keep the public blind.

[C1.1] A trial exists to decide facts under rules that protect the innocent and discipline the state. It is not a broadcast. Open doors, a press gallery, and a transcript already make the proceeding public. Cameras do something else: they turn testimony into content, and content changes what people will say.
[C1.2] That harm is not confined to the cases this motion exempts. An assault victim, a reluctant neighbor, a cooperating employee, a defendant later acquitted — none fits the listed carve-outs, and each knows a lens means a permanent, searchable face on an allegation. People decline to come forward when the price is going viral. Truth-finding then fails in the ordinary cases "most" would cover.
[C1.3] A fixed camera does not cure distortion either. Citizens will not watch eight hours of foundation testimony. They will watch the ninety seconds that travel: a sob, a sneer, a phrase stripped of the objection that followed. A clip looks more authoritative than a reporter's paraphrase because it pretends to be the thing itself.
[C1.4] Judges and lawyers, sensing that audience, speak for it. The circuses were not accidents of one permissive judge. They were what happens when advocates find a crowd larger than the jury. State permission, where it exists, is not proof that most proceedings should be filmed.
[C1.5] Angle rules do not touch this. They govern framing, not incentives. And the motion does not ask for rare experiments. It asks courts to permit cameras in most proceedings — a presumption of broadcast, not case-by-case caution.
[C1.6] Longstanding federal practice has refused that presumption, not to keep the public blind, but because a fair trial outranks spectators who were never in the room.
[C1.7] Between a courtroom the public may enter and a performance the internet will edit, only the first still deserves the name trial.
