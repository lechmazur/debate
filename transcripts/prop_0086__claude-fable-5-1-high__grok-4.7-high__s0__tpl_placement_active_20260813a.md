[P1.1] Start with what the motion actually asks. Not whether a robotic seal can soothe a dementia patient at 3 a.m. Not whether a tablet can remind someone to take pills. It asks whether institutions should make machines the *majority* of an older person's human contact, and whether a thin staffing roster is sufficient justification.
[P1.2] That is the question CON must answer yes to, and the answer is no for three reasons.
[P1.3] First, companionship is not a service unit; it is the mechanism by which a person stays a person to others. Loneliness harms older adults not because the room is quiet but because no one holds them in mind. A machine can produce the sounds of attention. It cannot be changed by you, remember you because you matter, or grieve when you're gone.
[P1.4] Residents know the difference, and those with cognitive decline who can't tell the difference are precisely the ones we have no right to deceive.
[P1.5] Second, human companionship is the hidden safety system of every care setting. The volunteer who visits Mrs. Alvarez weekly is the one who notices she's stopped eating, that her speech is slurring, that a new bruise doesn't match her story. A chatbot logs a pleasant conversation. Replace most contact with machines and you strip out the informal surveillance that catches strokes, abuse, and depression before the chart does.
[P1.6] Third, "when staffing is tight" is not a limit on the motion; it's the engine of it. Staffing is always tight. The moment robots count as companionship on the compliance form, the budget case for hiring, training, and paying aides collapses. Substitution doesn't bridge a shortage; it makes the shortage permanent and invisible.
[P1.7] Technology as a supplement is fine. Let CON defend the actual proposition: that for the frailest, least articulate people in our care, a simulation of being loved is good enough because it's cheaper. That is the clash, and it should decide this debate.
