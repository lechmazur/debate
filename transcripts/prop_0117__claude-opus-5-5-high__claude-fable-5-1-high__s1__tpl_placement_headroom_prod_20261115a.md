[P1.1] The motion targets the cases where the death penalty seems most justified. Those are exactly the cases where it performs worst. Three reasons.
[P1.2] First, extreme crimes produce the most dangerous convictions. After a bombing or a massacre, police face enormous pressure to name someone quickly, juries are frightened, and the public demands blood. Britain learned this with the Guildford Four and the Birmingham Six, convicted of IRA pub bombings and imprisoned for well over a decade before their convictions collapsed amid coerced confessions and unreliable forensic evidence. Had Britain still been hanging people, those innocent men would likely be dead, and no apology could reach them.
[P1.3] The death penalty demands infallibility at the very moment justice is least reliable. Prison lets you correct a mistake; a grave does not.
[P1.4] Second, executing terrorists serves the terrorist. Many attackers already intend to die. Execution hands their movement a martyr, a date to commemorate, and years of appeals that keep their name in headlines. Contrast Norway: Anders Breivik murdered dozens of people, many of them teenagers, and received a sentence that can be extended indefinitely.
[P1.5] He was not made a martyr; he faded into the obscurity of a cell. That is how a confident state answers terror: not with spectacle, but with permanent, boring containment.
[P1.6] Third, "terrorism" and "mass crimes" are categories governments define. Regimes routinely brand dissidents, protesters, and ethnic minorities as terrorists. Once a country accepts that the state may kill for the worst crimes, it has handed rulers a lethal tool whose boundaries are drawn by whoever holds power.
[P1.7] Meanwhile, life imprisonment achieves every legitimate goal CON can name: it protects the public permanently, expresses society's condemnation, and punishes severely. The burden on CON is to show what the execution itself adds, beyond irreversibility, martyrdom, and a weapon for abuse, that justifies risking innocent lives.
