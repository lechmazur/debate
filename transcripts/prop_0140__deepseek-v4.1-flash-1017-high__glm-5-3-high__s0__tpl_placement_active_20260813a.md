[P1.1] The choice isn’t between drinking and no drinking. It’s between drinking we can see and regulate, and drinking pushed into places where regulation cannot reach. Open-container bans do not stop social drinking; they scatter it into parked cars, pre-game apartments, unlit park corners, alleyways, and unlicensed pop-ups. Those spaces have no security perimeter, no sanitation, no medics, no noise cap, and no one accountable when a crowd turns ugly.
[P1.2] A tightly managed nightlife zone reverses that. Draw a clear boundary. Set fixed hours. Require non-glass cups, limit serving, post security and medics, provide toilets, lighting, transit, and resident buffers.
[P1.3] Then enforcement has a target: one perimeter, one set of rules, one place where help can be summoned. Police are not chasing Solo cups across a whole city; they are managing a known crowd in a known place. Residents get something they can negotiate and monitor, not noise and urine spreading into every side street.
[P1.4] That is the central comparison. Diffused drinking is not safer because it is hidden. A house party with no crowd management, a parking lot with no bathrooms, a car with no oversight—those are the less regulated spaces. Visibility is not the danger; invisibility is.
[P1.5] When cities concentrate nightlife and attach conditions, they can see crowding, cut off service, add medics, clean streets, and close early. When they prohibit public cups, they lose all of that leverage and keep only the fantasy that the drinking went away.
[P1.6] The proposition is modest: not open slather, not every neighborhood, but tightly managed zones. The question is whether cities govern the drinking they already have or pretend it disappears behind a legal line. PRO chooses governance.

[C1.1] The framing "visible drinking versus invisible drinking" is seductive, but it hides the real choice. Cities already have a tightly managed space for social drinking: the licensed bar. It has trained servers, dram-shop liability, age checks, capacity limits, and a manager who can be fined or shut down. An open-container zone doesn't add regulation to street drinking; it strips out the last accountable actor—the server—and replaces the bar with a cup.
[C1.2] So the true comparison is not "managed zone versus dark alley." It is drinking under a licensee's supervision versus drinking on a sidewalk where nobody pours, nobody counts, nobody cuts anyone off. "Limit serving" is a slogan; on a public street there is no one to enforce it against. The zone imports the bar's problems and discards the bar's controls.
[C1.3] Second, concentration is not safety—it is risk amplification. Crowds plus alcohol in a bounded perimeter is precisely the recipe for surge, violence, and mass-intoxication incidents that are hard to break up precisely because everyone is packed into one place. Diffuse drinking is annoying; concentrated drinking can turn ugly at scale, and "close early" doesn't un-break a jaw.
[C1.4] Third, "tightly managed" is a promise cities routinely fail to fund. Perimeter security, medics, sanitation, transit, monitoring—each is a budget line that quietly gets cut in year three, while the open-container rule, once granted, never gets repealed. What remains is the permission without the management. Look at famous open-street drinking districts: the enduring lesson is crowds, noise, trash, and police saturation, not tidy governance.
[C1.5] Finally, the displacement story is overdrawn. Prohibition doesn't eliminate drinking, but availability matters: making public drinking legal, cheap, and ambient increases total consumption and public intoxication, it doesn't merely relocate it. The neighborhood around the zone absorbs the spillover—same complaints, fewer tools.
[C1.6] The bar already is the managed nightlife zone. We should strengthen it, not dissolve its walls.
