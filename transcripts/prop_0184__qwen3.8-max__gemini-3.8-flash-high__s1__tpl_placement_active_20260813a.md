[P1.1] The central choice is between eliminating a known poison source and relying on continuous chemical management to keep it dormant. Municipal water systems have a basic duty to deliver water that is safe at the tap, not water that is only safe when corrosion control is perfectly designed, continuously dosed, and never disrupted. Lead service lines and legacy pipes violate that duty by their presence. They can release lead particles when water chemistry changes, when mains are disturbed, or when treatment slips.
[P1.2] Chemical treatment lowers risk, but it does not remove the hazard; it converts public health protection into an operating procedure.
[P1.3] That operating-procedure model fails in predictable ways. Treatment depends on monitoring, staffing, budget discipline, and stable source water. If a plant changes disinfectants, alters pH, or experiences dosing equipment failure, lead can mobilize quickly. Flint is the obvious warning: corrosion-control failure turned aging lead pipes into a delivery route for a potent neurotoxin.
[P1.4] Even without a catastrophe, treatment cannot fully address particulate lead, partial service lines, or disturbances from maintenance. It is a partial shield, not a cure.
[P1.5] Replacement is costlier upfront, but it is also the only durable fix. Pipes are capital assets with decades-long lives; financing replacement through significant rate increases or new debt can match the cost to the asset and to the customers who benefit over time. Rate increases can be phased, and assistance can protect low-income households, but those financing tools do not change the core fact: every year a lead pipe remains in service, the city continues to expose residents to a preventable poison.
[P1.6] CON asks the public to accept permanent risk management. PRO asks municipalities to end the risk by removing the source. For drinking water, the safer and more accountable standard is replacement, not chemical mitigation.
