[P1.1] Land is not a stock with a soil layer. It is the physical base of a food system and the civic base of rural life. A cap on the share non-farming institutional investors may own is a modest, targeted rule: it says finance may participate, but it cannot become the dominant landlord. That is the decisive line.
[P1.2] First, resilience. Institutional owners answer to return targets, not harvest cycles. When land is a line item in a diversified portfolio, the rational move is often to bid up rents, consolidate parcels, simplify cropping, and sell when returns disappoint. That makes farmland more expensive for the people who actually farm and more responsive to financial shocks than to soil, water, or local supply chains.
[P1.3] A cap keeps a critical mass of land under owner-operators and community-rooted farmers who know the ground, live with the consequences of a bad season, and are less likely to abandon a region when a fund rebalances.
[P1.4] Second, rural communities. Absentee ownership drains local governance. A farmer who lives in the county sits on the school board, hires local labor, uses local equipment dealers, and has an incentive to protect waterways because her neighbors drink the water. A fund can capture the rent and exit.
[P1.5] When enough land shifts, towns lose the businesses and institutions that depend on farming households. A cap is not expropriation; it preserves a floor for that local economic and civic base.
[P1.6] The con side will say capital is needed. But the proposition does not ban institutional investment; it caps its share. Capital can still enter, partner, and finance. What it cannot do is convert the countryside into a concentrated asset class and then call that efficiency.
[P1.7] The choice is not between investment and no investment. It is between land governed by stewardship and land governed by exit. A share cap is how a society keeps the former from being swallowed by the latter.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes the deed for the farm. Institutional ownership is neither necessary nor sufficient for resilient food production or thriving rural communities, and a cap can make both harder.
[C1.2] The relevant questions are who operates the land, whether leases are secure, and whether soil, water, and businesses are being maintained. A fund that owns parcels under long leases to competent farmers can provide patient capital and stable tenure. Conversely, a locally owned parcel can be neglected, rented out, or stripped of local value. The opposition’s contrast between “stewardship” and “exit” is not a distinction between institutional and local ownership; it is a description of conduct.
[C1.3] A categorical cap targets a label rather than the behavior threatening resilience.
[C1.4] More seriously, a cap changes bidders rather than behavior. Removing non-farming institutions from the market reduces demand for farmland. That can raise prices for the land that remains eligible, making it harder for young farmers and farming families to buy or expand. It may also favor wealthy established owners, concentrating operational control even as formal institutional ownership falls.
[C1.5] A rule advertised as protection for farmers could thus deepen exactly the barriers it claims to remedy.
[C1.6] Nor is title a reliable measure of community attachment. An institutional owner may work through resident farm managers, contract with local businesses, and support local services; a landlord may operate remotely. Institutional capital can also help retiring farmers transfer land to successors without fragmenting it or burdening a family heir. Restricting that route can accelerate abandonment or idle land, rather than preserve active agriculture.
[C1.7] The better approach is to target the actual harms: transparent beneficial ownership, competition rules against consolidation, secure leasing, soil and water standards, succession assistance, and controls on conversion of prime farmland. These can apply regardless of owner identity.
[C1.8] Protect farming by regulating land markets and agricultural practice. Do not assume the threat wears an institutional address.

[C2.1] “Ownership is the power to set the conduct rules” [P2.1] proves too much. Landowners have decision rights, but those rights operate through their bargain with farmers. Binding long-term leases, renewal rights, soil covenants, and limits on arbitrary termination can align a sophisticated landlord with long-term farming. Local title guarantees none of this: an owner can sell, default, strip soil, or evict a tenant.
[C2.2] If unstable control is the harm, mandate those protections. The cap removes a class of owners without securing those outcomes.
[C2.3] P2.3 assumes that lower prices necessarily mean greater access. Remove one bidder, and the winner may be a larger established farm or local holding company. If a fund cannot bid, an established neighbor may buy two parcels and lease one to a young farmer on less favorable terms: institutional ownership falls while entry barriers and local concentration rise. Lower prices help a young buyer only if that buyer has credit and a realistic chance to win.
[C2.4] If excluded institutions can still finance farmers or lease land, the cap does less than P2 suggests; if driven away, succession can suffer. Either way, it does not democratize access.
[C2.5] An aggregate cap also cannot reliably create the local “critical mass” promised in P2.8. Absentee ownership is a county-level threat: a fund could dominate one community while institutional holdings remain small nationally. A national cap misses that community; a local cap imposes arbitrary boundaries and may freeze incumbent advantage.
[C2.6] P2.5 says conduct standards can be met on paper; ownership caps have the same weakness. They specify who may own, not how land must be farmed, leased, governed, or integrated locally. If stewardship and secure tenure are the objectives, lease regulation, ownership transparency, anti-consolidation rules, and succession programs address them directly. P2’s companion rules would do the actual protecting; the cap adds distortion without proving that excluding institutions improves outcomes.
[C2.7] It may instead replace absentee decision rights with concentrated local ones while leaving land-market power untouched. Reject the proposition.

[P4.1] A1: The question sets an impossible evidentiary bar and misidentifies what a cap is for. We do not need to prove every institutional parcel is worse than every owner-operated one after controlling for lease terms, tenant quality, and local concentration. Those “controls” are not fixed background facts; they are outcomes of bargaining power. An owner with an unlimited share and a credible exit option can renegotiate, sell, or wait out a tenant, and a covenant is enforced after the harm.
[P4.2] A good fund may be good until its portfolio strategy changes. Average performance cannot answer correlated exit risk. The relevant evidence is the mechanism: control rights determine who can exit and on what terms. The cap is insurance against that structural power, not a claim that every fund is a bad farmer.
[P4.3] A2: The precise percentage should be set by an independent farmland regulator using local market data; the principle is a minority ceiling, not a ban. Define “non-farming institutional investor” by look-through beneficial ownership: financial entities whose primary purpose is return rather than cultivation, with exemptions for family farms, cooperatives, and community land trusts. Apply a national ceiling plus tighter local subcaps where land markets are thin—precisely because [C2.5] says dominance can be county-level. Excluding absentee bidders eases access, but not alone; pair the cap with beginning-farmer preferences and anti-consolidation review.
[P4.4] The motion permits companion rules; it does not require a naive cap.
[P4.5] That is why PRO still wins. CON’s questions demand design details, but their alternative—conduct rules alone—still leaves the owner holding the exit option. The cap changes the default: finance may participate, but cannot become the dominant landlord. Where CON sees distortion, we see the structural floor that makes lease, soil, and succession rules bite.
[P4.6] Decide for the regime in which farming communities are not just tenants of finance.

[C4.2] A cap, by contrast, changes only the titleholder: the same rent extraction, soil mining, consolidation, or sale can occur under a wealthy individual or local company, while a minority institution may still shape decisions through leases or management agreements. The relevant power is control over farming, not the word “institution.”
[C4.3] A2: If local dominance is the harm, then local enforcement is sensible, but that does not make an institutional-only cap the right rule. Disclosure rules, concentration limits covering all owners, anti-assignment protections, conservation covenants, and tenant remedies can prevent local dominance without excluding capital. An institutional subcap simply removes one bidder and can transfer land to larger incumbent holdings, strengthening the very local concentration PRO claims to oppose. County boundaries can also invite relocation and regulatory arbitrage.
[C4.4] If the answer is a cap on total concentrated control, that is a different policy from the motion; if it is a cap on institutions, the demand-distortion problem remains.
[C4.5] PRO’s answer reveals the winning point: the protections that make the regime credible are conduct rules, a regulator, and local implementation. The cap supplies an additional presumption against a category of owners without showing that category causes the harm. Exit can occur through owners of every identity, and ownership is neither necessary nor sufficient for either resilient farming or local attachment. Because the cap may raise entry barriers and freeze incumbent advantage while leaving the underlying conduct untouched, it is an unreliable food-system safeguard.
[C4.6] Target control and stewardship directly; do not confuse institutional finance with institutional irresponsibility.

[P5.1] The decisive question is not whether a good fund can be a good landlord. It is what happens when many funds face the same pressure at once. CON's conduct rules are valuable, but they operate after the owner has power to sell, renegotiate, or exit; a covenant can compensate a tenant for lost irrigation, but cannot restore the orchard or local processor once land changes hands. The cap changes the default before that pressure arrives: finance may participate, but cannot become the dominant landlord.
[P5.2] CON calls this a different policy from capping concentrated control generally [C4.4]. But the motion targets a real mechanism: institutional owners' decisions are tied to portfolio returns and liquidity, so exits can cluster. A county can lose its tenant base because several funds rebalance in the same bad year. Local subcaps and companion rules handle the boundaries and incumbent-risk problems CON raises; the cap supplies the structural floor those rules need.
[P5.3] Conduct rules manage owners; a share cap preserves a farming base that does not depend on any owner's exit option. Resilience is not a clause in a lease; it is a critical mass of decision-makers who stay.
