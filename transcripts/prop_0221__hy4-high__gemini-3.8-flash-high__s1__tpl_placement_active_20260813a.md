[P1.1] The marginal public dollar should be judged by conversion into learning. High-dosage tutoring buys repeated, targeted instructional time: a tutor sees the gap, gives practice, corrects errors, and returns three or more times a week. Class-size reduction buys a smaller denominator: fewer children in the same room, with the same lesson pace, no guarantee of extra feedback for the child who is lost. In K–8 reading and math, gaps are sequential—phonics before fluency, multiplication before fractions—so frequency and correction are the active ingredients.
[P1.2] Scale strengthens that advantage. A funded program can put tutoring inside the school day, use common diagnostics and curriculum-aligned lessons, and draw tutors from a wider pool than certified teachers. It can concentrate dosage on pupils below grade level, not purchase a thin benefit for everyone. A third grader stumbling over multisyllabic words or a sixth grader missing equivalent fractions gets several short loops of assessment, practice, and repair this week, before the next unit leaves them further behind.
[P1.3] Class-size reduction is an expensive way to create a weak treatment. Moving a grade from, say, the mid-20s to low-20s requires more teachers, classrooms, hiring, induction, and sometimes space. At scale, shortages mean new posts are often filled by less experienced or less stable staff. Even in a well-run small class, the benefit is spread across all pupils, including those already on track, while transitions, independent work, and whole-class explanation remain.
[P1.4] It pays for capacity before it pays for changed instruction.
[P1.5] Calmer rooms are not the metric. The question is which intervention turns a dollar into more reading and math growth. Tutoring turns it into direct practice and corrective feedback for the child whose gap blocks progress. Smaller classes turn it into thin, potential attention across the room, much of which leaks away.
[P1.6] At scale, with finite money and finite talent, targeted instructional dosage beats a blunt staffing-and-capital mandate.

[C1.1] When high-dosage tutoring leaves boutique pilot programs and meets district-wide scale, its theoretical efficiency collapses. PRO’s argument relies on a laboratory illusion: that school systems can cheaply recruit, schedule, and supervise millions of reliable tutoring hours without catastrophic operational friction. In reality, the marginal dollar spent on scaled tutoring buys administrative overhead, not pure instruction.
[C1.2] At scale, tutoring is an operational quagmire. Delivering three or more sessions weekly requires an army of non-certified tutors who suffer from chronic turnover, triggering continuous spending on recruitment, vetting, and training. During the school day, pulling students out fractures master schedules and cannibalizes core classes or electives, undermining the very learning tutoring seeks to patch. After school, attendance plummets among the lowest-performing students who need it most.
[C1.3] Even when delivered, tutoring purchases at most forty to fifty hours of targeted remediation per year at steep contractor rates, with gains that rapidly fade once services cease.
[C1.4] Class-size reduction, by contrast, transforms the core environment where children spend over nine hundred hours annually. A smaller class is not a passive denominator; it fundamentally alters the teacher’s daily practice. Certified educators can conduct small-group guided reading, identify early phonics errors, and diagnose arithmetic misunderstandings in real time before they compound into multi-year deficits.
[C1.5] Crucially, smaller classes act across reading and math simultaneously, while curbing teacher burnout and costly staff turnover—the single largest hidden drain on school budgets.
[C1.6] PRO advocates an expensive remediation tax: funding transient workers for a few fragile hours of triage while leaving overwhelmed classrooms intact. Smaller classes invest directly in the permanent, professional capacity of the school. Dollar for dollar, preventing academic failure through manageable classrooms produces far greater, more enduring gains than attempting to tutor millions of students through a brittle external bureaucracy.
