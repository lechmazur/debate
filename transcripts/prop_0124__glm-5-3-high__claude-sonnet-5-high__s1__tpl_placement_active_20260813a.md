[P1.1] At the heart of this motion is a simple claim: a competent adult, facing suffering that cannot be relieved and a future that holds only deterioration, has the right to decide when enough is enough—and a compassionate medical system should be permitted to help.
[P1.2] Consider what we're actually asking of people. A patient with advanced ALS who can no longer move, breathe without support, or swallow, but whose mind remains sharp. Someone with terminal cancer whose pain management leaves them sedated or screaming. For these people, the choice is not between suffering and relief; it is between suffering on others' terms and relief on their own.
[P1.3] Denying medical assistance in dying doesn't prevent death—it forces people into desperate alternatives: violent suicide, refused nutrition, or prolonged agony. Jurisdictions that have legalized this practice see some violent suicides displaced by peaceful, medically supervised deaths. That is harm reduction, not harm.
[P1.4] Three pillars carry the case. First, autonomy: we let patients refuse life-saving treatment, accept palliative sedation, and sign do-not-resuscitate orders. The moral difference between refusing death-delaying care and requesting death-accelerating help is vanishingly thin when the outcome—control over one's dying—is identical. Second, compassion: when suffering is unbearable and irreversible, prolonging it involuntarily serves no one.
[P1.5] Palliative care is valuable and should be expanded, but it is not a cure-all; a meaningful fraction of patients' suffering persists even under excellent palliation. Third, the safeguards: the motion requires terminal or degenerative conditions, demonstrated competence, voluntariness, and strict procedural controls—multiple assessments, waiting periods, independent review. These are exactly the mechanisms that jurisdictions like Oregon, Canada, and the Benelux countries have refined over decades, with eligibility rates that remain a small fraction of deaths and no credible evidence of systematic abuse of the vulnerable.
[P1.6] The opponents will invoke slippery slopes and pressure on the disabled or elderly. Those concerns deserve answers, and the safeguards provide them—but anxiety about hypothetical misuse cannot justify the certain, ongoing torment of real people who beg for a choice we refuse to give.
