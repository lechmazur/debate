[P1.1] Treating Chinese clean-tech imports primarily as a decarbonization shortcut is a dangerous illusion. It trades Europe’s long-term economic sovereignty for marginal, short-term emissions gains. [P1.2] First, Europe has already seen this playbook. A decade ago, European leaders welcomed cheap Chinese solar panels to accelerate climate targets. The result was the near-total extinction of Europe's domestic solar manufacturing base. Today, the automotive sector—the backbone of European industrial employment, regional vitality, and technological innovation—faces the exact same predatory squeeze.
[P1.3] China's massive state subsidies and structural overcapacity are designed to crush foreign competitors, monopolize clean supply chains, and establish decisive market power. Once European capacity is lost, the pricing power and innovation ecosystem will disappear with it.
[P1.4] Second, true decarbonization cannot survive extreme strategic dependency. [P1.5] Finally, decarbonization requires democratic legitimacy. If European citizens experience climate mandates as the deliberate export of their high-wage jobs and industrial dignity to China, the political consensus sustaining the green transition will inevitably shatter. The EU must recognize Chinese clean-tech imports primarily as an aggressive industrial threat, deploy decisive trade defenses, and safeguard the economic core that makes any enduring green transition possible.

[C1.2] That bluntness has immediate climate costs. Imported EVs and clean equipment at lower prices help households, businesses, and governments adopt zero-emission technology sooner. Tariffs, quotas, or blanket distrust raise the transition’s price. They can make an electric bus or grid battery harder to finance precisely when rapid deployment is needed.
[C1.3] Cheaper clean technology is not a loophole; it helps turn climate targets into physical change.
[C1.4] Competition is not industrial extinction. Lower-cost products pressure European manufacturers to improve batteries, vehicle design, recycling, and production. [C1.5] But presuming Chinese products dangerous protects firms from the discipline—and learning—that makes them stronger.
[C1.6] The solar example does not prove the proposition’s rule. It shows the danger of importing heavily while neglecting domestic manufacturing, skills, finance, and recycling. [C1.7] The lesson is to manage supply chains, not abandon the market.
[C1.8] Strategic dependence can be reduced without treating every clean-tech import as a threat. Europe should welcome the opportunity, confront the risks, and use competition to build capacity.
