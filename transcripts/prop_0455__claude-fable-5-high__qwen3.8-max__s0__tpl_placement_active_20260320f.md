A ban with strict thresholds fixes both.
[P1.2] Start with the physics. Methane traps roughly eighty times more heat than CO2 over a twenty-year window. Cutting methane is the single fastest lever humanity has to slow near-term warming — faster than any transition in cars, grids, or buildings. A government that bans routine flaring and leakage buys measurable climate benefit within a decade, not a century.
[P1.3] Now the economics, which cut sharply in favor of a ban. Captured methane is a sellable product. Leak detection, better valves, and vapor recovery frequently pay for themselves; energy agencies have long noted a large share of methane abatement costs producers little or nothing at prevailing gas prices. The only reason waste persists is that regulation permits it.
[P1.4] Norway has effectively banned routine flaring since the 1970s — and remained one of the world's most profitable, reliable producers. The supposed trade-off between strict rules and a functioning energy sector is refuted by five decades of evidence.
[P1.5] We accept the motion's premise honestly: some marginal wells may shut in, and supply may dip briefly while operators build capture infrastructure. [P1.6] The question is whether governments should permit producers to destroy a public resource and heat the planet because capturing it is mildly inconvenient. No serious theory of governance says yes. The ban is cheap, proven, and urgent.

[C1.1] The proposition fails because it turns a complex infrastructure problem into an ultimatum: capture immediately or shut in, even when doing so cuts domestic energy. [C1.2] In many fields, flaring persists not because gas is worthless, but because there is no pipeline, no compression, no local buyer, and no affordable way to re-inject or use it. A threshold ban does not create that infrastructure overnight. The PRO’s promised supply gain assumes captured gas can reach market; where no pipeline exists, a ban simply removes energy. It forces marginal wells offline, reduces associated gas and oil output, and raises prices for households and industry.
[C1.3] The motion accepts short-term supply falls; in real economies that means less reliable heat, higher electricity bills, and factories cutting back while imported fuels fill the gap.
[C1.4] The PRO’s Norway example is not a template. Norway’s regime rests on decades of state capacity, offshore platforms, rich reservoirs, nearby markets, and high margins. Landlocked basins, aging fields, fragmented small operators, and low-pressure wells cannot replicate it by decree. Strict thresholds also invite perverse outcomes: operators may plug wells hastily, abandon sites, or shift to dirtier fuels if domestic gas disappears.
[C1.5] Methane problems can worsen when assets go bankrupt and leaky wells are orphaned rather than maintained.
[C1.6] There is a faster, less self-defeating route: require monitoring and reporting, impose escalating fees on venting and flaring, fund pipeline connections, streamline permitting for capture equipment, and allow safety venting. That captures methane without deliberately cutting domestic energy. The real question is whether a blunt ban that knowingly reduces supply is justified when better tools can get the same climate result. It is not.
