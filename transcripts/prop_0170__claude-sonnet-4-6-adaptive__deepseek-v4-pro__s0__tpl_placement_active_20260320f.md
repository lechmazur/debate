[P1.3] Conservation is a demand-management strategy. It works by shrinking the human footprint relative to a fixed supply. But in regions facing genuine scarcity—the Arabian Peninsula, the American Southwest, coastal North Africa—the gap between supply and need is not a marginal efficiency problem. It is structural.
[P1.4] Population grows, agriculture demands more, aquifers are already being drawn down faster than they recharge. You cannot conserve your way to a larger water budget. Once you've installed low-flow fixtures and priced water more aggressively, you've harvested most of the gains. Meanwhile, the shortage compounds.
[P1.5] Rationing is even worse as a long-run strategy. It distributes a shrinking resource rather than expanding it. Rationing is appropriate in emergencies; as primary policy it suppresses economic development, creates black markets, and imposes the heaviest burden on the poorest households who cannot buffer shortfalls.
[P1.6] Desalination, by contrast, converts an effectively unlimited resource—the ocean covers seventy percent of the Earth's surface—into fresh water. Israel now produces enough desalinated water to meet the majority of its domestic water needs, and it did so by committing to large-scale reverse osmosis infrastructure. The UAE and Saudi Arabia have built the same capability at scale. The cost per cubic meter of reverse osmosis water has fallen by roughly an order of magnitude over the past four decades and continues to drop, especially as renewable energy makes the power costs more manageable.
[P1.7] The proposition asks where primary investment should go. The answer is supply creation, because only supply creation changes the underlying constraint. Conservation is a sensible complement, but a complement cannot be the primary strategy when the gap is structural and growing.
