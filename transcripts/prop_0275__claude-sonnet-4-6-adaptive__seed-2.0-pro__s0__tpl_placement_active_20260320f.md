[P1.1] The entire point of browsing vacation rentals is to find the right fit before committing. When a platform hides cleaning fees, resort fees, and house rules until checkout, it has already extracted something valuable from you: your time, your raised expectations, and in many cases a psychological commitment to a listing you've mentally settled on. That's not an accident. It's a design choice, and it's the design choice this resolution is asking us to fix.
[P1.2] The practice has a name: drip pricing. You see a headline rate that looks affordable, you click through a dozen photos, you check dates, you imagine the trip—and only when you hit the booking screen does the real cost appear. Cleaning fees alone can exceed the nightly rate on short stays. Resort fees, common on certain property types, can add thirty to fifty dollars a night or more.
[P1.3] By that point, most users complete the booking anyway because abandoning feels like a loss. The industry has engineered this friction deliberately, and the result is that the advertised price systematically misleads.
[P1.4] House rules compound the problem. A guest who learns only at checkout that the property prohibits pets, requires check-in before 3 p.m., or mandates a minimum five-night stay has wasted time they could have used finding a genuinely compatible listing. The information asymmetry doesn't just inconvenience individuals—it degrades the whole market by making comparison-shopping between platforms and listings nearly impossible.
[P1.5] Requiring upfront disclosure costs platforms and hosts nothing they can legitimately claim. It doesn't cap fees, ban rules, or restrict inventory. It simply demands that relevant terms appear where decisions are actually made. Any platform that refuses to do this is implicitly admitting the fees and rules would change user behavior if shown earlier—which is precisely the argument for showing them.
[P1.6] Transparency at the decision point is the minimum standard a functioning market requires. The regulation this resolution proposes merely enforces that standard.

[C1.1] This rule does not solve the problem it claims. It creates far worse, universal harms that the PRO opening never even acknowledges exist.
[C1.2] First: There is no single, correct number that can be displayed upfront for most of these fees. Cleaning fees are almost always charged per stay, not per night. For a one-night booking it will double the headline rate; for a ten-night booking it adds barely 10%. Resort fees often only apply during peak seasons, holiday weekends, or for guest counts over two.
[C1.3] There is no way to show an accurate, fair number on a generic browse tile. Any mandate to display these fees early will force platforms to show the worst possible default number for every listing. Users will not get better comparisons—they will simply scroll past good, affordable options that the rule forced to look artificially expensive.
[C1.4] On house rules: This is even more unworkable. Hosts have dozens of rules, most of which will never apply to 95% of guests. If you mandate that all material rules be shown before a booking request is even sent, every host will dump every possible edge case restriction at the top of the listing to avoid compliance liability.
[C1.5] Instead of browsing photos, location, and guest reviews, users will scroll past walls of fine print. The entire core function of these platforms breaks.
[C1.6] This rule does not eliminate friction. It moves friction from the end of the booking process, where it only touches users who were already ready to book, and slaps it onto every single search result for every single user. That is an enormously bad tradeoff.
