[P1.1] Low-income countries rarely lose lives because a device cannot be invented; they lose lives because nobody can buy it, repair it, or adapt it once it breaks. That is the ground on which this debate must be fought. Traditional IP-protected pipelines are built to reward invention through temporary monopolies. That model can work where patients or insurers can pay Western prices.
[P1.2] It fails where health budgets are thin, import supply chains are slow, and the highest-value devices are those that keep working for a decade in a dusty clinic.
[P1.3] Open-source hardware attacks the real bottleneck. When schematics, bills of materials, and firmware are public, a local workshop can manufacture, substitute available components, and repair the device. The design can be ruggedized for intermittent power and high humidity. Clinics are not locked into proprietary consumables or foreign service contracts.
[P1.4] That diffusion is what saves lives at scale.
[P1.5] Concrete cases make the mechanism plain. Open, hand-powered centrifuges cost a tiny fraction of an electric lab centrifuge and can prepare blood samples for diagnosing diseases like malaria or sleeping sickness where no reliable power exists. Open 3D-printed prosthetic designs let a child who has outgrown a limb be refitted locally for the cost of feedstock, not repeated imports. During COVID, publicly released ventilator and breathing-assistance designs were downloaded and adapted by factories and maker spaces worldwide, showing how fast open blueprints spread when supply chains fracture.
[P1.6] The metric is lives saved, not novelty protected. A patented device that sits in a capital-city hospital because district clinics cannot afford it, or that becomes e-waste after a minor fault, is a moral and practical failure in this context. Open-source hardware trades monopoly profit for distributed capacity: more units, more repairs, more local iteration, and therefore more patients reached. If the question is which pipeline saves more lives in low-income countries, the answer is the one that can be copied, fixed, and made local.
