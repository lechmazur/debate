[P1.1] The transition to a decarbonized housing stock is not a question of *if*, but *when*. The fundamental choice for wealthy nations is whether to build cleanly today, or pay double to clean up tomorrow. Requiring all new residential construction to be net-zero-energy-ready (NZER) at the point of building is the only economically rational and environmentally viable path forward.
[P1.2] First, the economics of prevention vastly outperform the economics of cure. Integrating high-performance insulation, superior airtightness, and heat pump infrastructure at the design phase adds only a marginal fraction to initial construction costs. Conversely, retrofitting is a financial black hole. It requires ripping out drywall, replacing functioning HVAC systems prematurely, and overcoming structural barriers never designed for green tech.
[P1.3] Retrofitting is fundamentally invasive, labor-intensive, and far more expensive per unit of carbon saved than building correctly from day one.
[P1.4] Second, we must prevent the "lock-in" effect. Residential buildings constructed today will stand for the next fifty to one hundred years. Every home built to legacy standards today represents a multi-decade liability—either a guaranteed source of high emissions or a massive, avoidable cost forced onto future homeowners. Relying on retrofits means kicking the can down the road, hoping that a future workforce can manually reconstruct millions of occupied homes.
[P1.5] Finally, a universal mandate creates immediate market certainty. When governments establish NZER as the mandatory baseline, manufacturers scale up production of high-efficiency materials and builders standardize green techniques. This scale rapidly drives down costs, making sustainable housing affordable.
[P1.6] Waiting for retrofits is a policy of planned inefficiency. By building right the first time, wealthy nations secure immediate emissions reductions, shield homeowners from volatile energy bills, and establish the only feasible pathway to a zero-carbon future.
