[P1.1] Restaurants do not sell food at a discount and then invoice diners for the plate. They sell a meal delivered by staff, and the motion simply asks that the price on the menu reflect the actual cost of what the diner will pay. When a mandatory service charge is hidden until the bill arrives, the displayed price is not a price; it is an incomplete quote. That gap is the heart of the problem.
[P1.2] A diner comparing two restaurants must guess what percentage will be added, how it interacts with tax, and whether it applies to drinks. The menu stops being a tool for comparison and becomes a shell game. Including the charge in the displayed price restores the diner's ability to choose based on real value rather than a hopeful estimate.
[P1.3] There is also a dignity argument. Service is not a charity tossed on at the end; it is a component of the meal. When restaurants disclose the cost of service upfront, they acknowledge that labor is part of the product rather than an embarrassing surcharge to be unveiled after the guest has already committed. Hidden charges create the worst of both worlds: the diner feels ambushed, and the server's compensation appears as a punitive add-on rather than an earned cost of hospitality.
[P1.4] Opponents will warn that menus with higher numbers scare customers. But the total bill does not change. What changes is that the shock moves from checkout to choice, where it belongs. Diners may eat out less often or order less extravagantly, but those decisions will be informed decisions.
[P1.5] A restaurant that cannot survive honest pricing was relying on the cover of ambiguity, not on the quality of its food or service.
[P1.6] Transparency does not cost the customer money. Deception about cost does.

[C1.1] The claim that a menu price becomes “not a price” when a mandatory service charge is disclosed later confuses disclosure with bundling. A price can be meaningful without pretending that every possible component of the bill is embedded in every item. Indeed, if the stated standard is the “actual cost” the diner pays, then mandatory tax must also be included. The proposition singles out service charges while leaving other unavoidable additions outside the displayed price, so it does not deliver the total-price certainty it promises.
[C1.2] Service charges also differ in function and incidence. A compulsory gratuity for a large group may reflect staffing needs that arise because of party size. If the restaurant must incorporate that charge into each displayed item price, it must either choose an assumed percentage and mislead smaller parties, or maintain multiple menus with different rates. The result is arbitrary: an individual diner subsidizes the group surcharge, while prices depend on how the charge is allocated among dishes and drinks.
[C1.3] Fixed-price tasting menus and à-la-carte restaurants face different accounting problems, yet the proposition imposes one rule on both.
[C1.4] Bundling is not transparency; it is accounting. It can obscure rather than improve comparison by turning one visible number into a composite that diners cannot easily separate into food price and service provision. It also exposes the restaurant to unnecessary repricing costs when rates, staffing arrangements, or event policies change. The PRO claim that “the total bill does not change” ignores demand: conspicuous all-in menu prices can deter ordering, while clearly disclosed add-ons allow customers to compare menus and still understand the final obligation.
[C1.5] The better remedy is advance disclosure in plain language: identify every mandatory charge, its rate, and its conditions on the menu, then repeat it prominently on the receipt. That gives the diner notice before ordering without forcing an administratively distorting bundle. Restaurants should not hide charges until checkout, but the proposition wrongly equates avoiding surprise with embedding every service charge in the item price. Consumers can have both informed consent and meaningful menu prices.

The genuinely corrosive case is the charge added regardless of party size: a couple's quiet Tuesday dinner becomes ninety dollars, and then sixteen dollars in service appears at checkout.

The diner comparing a twenty-eight-dollar entrée with a thirty-one-dollar one, adding drinks and dessert, cannot reliably compute the final bill under pressure, mid-conversation, with a server waiting.
[P2.5] CON offers informed consent in theory and arithmetic in practice. The displayed price must be the number the diner can act on.

[C4.1] A1: A prominently printed rate can be adequate notice when it appears before ordering, states its scope, and is repeated on the receipt. PRO’s premise that a diner must know the final total without doing arithmetic confuses an item price with the complete order, which also depends on quantities, substitutions, tax, and what others order. Moreover, meaningful comparison remains possible: a disclosed $28 entrée at 20% service can be compared with a $31 entrée at 15%. The diner has both inputs needed for comparison.
[C4.2] Moving the calculation into the menu changes its presentation, not its informational content.
[C4.3] A2: If an inclusive price and a plain-language service policy can both appear, then bundling solves little beyond preventing miscalculation, while risking loss of useful price information. Allocating one shared charge across dishes can distort their comparative food prices, especially when drinks or different courses are ordered separately. PRO’s anchoring argument establishes that the larger number attracts attention, not that disclosure is deceptive. A prominently stated 20% charge changes the customer’s anchor to the real obligation; if it is buried or absent, the restaurant has failed disclosure, not failed arithmetic.
[C4.4] Nor may PRO avoid [C2.4] by redefining conditional charges as non-mandatory. A large-party charge is mandatory once its disclosed trigger applies; embedding it in one standard menu price requires an assumed rate or multiple menus, neither of which necessarily represents the eventual bill. PRO instead narrows the debate to universal charges, but even there the claimed ambush is solved by advance disclosure. If a 15% charge is plainly stated before ordering, there is no checkout surprise.
[C4.5] The stronger policy is therefore notice before commitment, a clear rate and scope, and an itemized receipt. PRO treats bundling as the only way to make prices truthful, but a visible price plus visible mandatory terms is truthful without concealing the food price inside an administratively distorted total. The motion’s “all” makes that distortion unavoidable; clear disclosure achieves its legitimate objective.
