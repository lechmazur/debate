[P1.1] Obesity is not a lifestyle footnote; it is a chronic, relapsing disease that drives diabetes, heart failure, joint disease, and costly preventable complications. When an insurer denies an FDA-authorized medication for a clinically indicated patient—say, someone with significant obesity and prediabetes—it does not avoid spending. It shifts spending to later, sicker, more expensive care, while the patient absorbs the risk. A patient whose plan excludes the drug may deteriorate until insulin, dialysis, or surgery becomes the covered option.
[P1.2] That is cost-shifting, not cost control. The proposition fixes that misalignment: cover the medication when a clinician determines it is indicated, and use value-based or outcomes-based contracts so payment tracks real benefit rather than volume.
[P1.3] That design answers the affordability objection instead of ignoring it. Outcomes contracts can tie manufacturer reimbursement to sustained weight loss, glycemic improvement, or avoided complications; insurers can negotiate population-level risk sharing; prior authorization and clinical criteria keep coverage targeted. This is not a blank check for cosmetic use. It is a requirement that insurers stop treating a legitimate medical therapy as an excluded luxury while retaining tools to control cost.
[P1.4] The central comparison is simple. CON must defend a system where coverage depends on the accident of which plan a patient has, even when the same FDA-authorized drug is prescribed for the same clinical need. That produces inequity and worse outcomes, and it externalizes costs onto families, employers, and public programs. Denial feels cheaper only because the insurer’s ledger ends before the complication arrives.
[P1.5] Value-based coverage brings the drug’s price into line with its performance. If the drug works, patients and payers benefit; if it does not, manufacturers bear more risk. That is the fiscally disciplined, medically responsible path.

[C1.1] The proposition converts coverage from an option into a compulsory, open-ended benefit before establishing that it is worth its total cost. FDA authorization answers a drug-specific safety-and-efficacy question for defined uses. It does not establish comparative value, durable benefits for every eligible patient, or savings to the insurer. “Clinically indicated” widens that gap: it can describe an enormous chronic-treatment population, not a small rescue category.
[C1.2] PRO’s prediabetes example smuggles in the decisive assumption—that denial today produces insulin, dialysis, or surgery tomorrow, and that medication spending prevents enough of those events to pay for itself. None follows automatically. Many indicated patients will never suffer those complications; benefits vary; adherence is imperfect; and weight commonly returns after treatment stops. A therapy can be useful while costing substantially more than the complications it averts.
[C1.3] Compassion does not turn an unproven offset into a fiscal fact.
[C1.4] Nor do “value-based” contracts rescue the mandate. A rebate for nonresponse reduces payment for some failures; it does not cap the total bill when vast numbers remain on an effective drug indefinitely. Measuring sustained outcomes requires years, while discontinuation, plan switching, and other treatments confound attribution. Manufacturers will price that risk into negotiations, and insurers must build monitoring systems to collect rebates.
[C1.5] Most importantly, compulsory coverage weakens the insurer’s strongest leverage: the ability to exclude a drug whose net price is unacceptable. The motion requires coverage first and merely hopes discipline follows.
[C1.6] That cost returns as premiums, employer contributions, deductibles, and displaced coverage. Every dollar committed to a broad drug mandate is unavailable for primary care, diabetes prevention, or treatments with clearer value. PRO calls plan variation inequity, but mandates do not abolish scarcity; they force every policyholder to fund one contested priority. Reject a mandate with expansive eligibility, speculative savings, and no enforceable budget limit.

[P4.1] A1: No. The motion does not oblige insurers to accept any demanded price; it obliges them to cover clinically indicated anti-obesity medications through value-based terms. If a manufacturer refuses a reasonable outcomes contract, the plan need not cover that product at its asking price, but it cannot blanket-exclude the class; it must negotiate and cover a clinically appropriate authorized option. That is a real mandate: it removes the insurer’s power to deny the entire category while preserving price discipline. CON’s question assumes coverage and bargaining are mutually exclusive; the motion makes coverage the basis for bargaining.
[P4.2] A2: The enforceable ceiling is a net-spend cap inside the contract: if utilization or total cost exceeds forecast, manufacturer rebates rise, and reinsurance absorbs outlier cases. If a manufacturer refuses such a cap, it has refused the motion’s condition, so that product is not covered. If a hard budget still binds, the patients who lose coverage should be those with the lowest expected net benefit under published clinical criteria—not patients sorted by income or plan. That is rationing, but it is explicit, reviewable, and tied to outcomes.
[P4.3] Those answers expose CON’s weakness. CON demands a ceiling but offers only plan discretion; it never says how denial caps total spending or why an unlucky plan is a fair rationing rule. CON’s alternative cannot answer the same question; it merely relocates denial to underwriting and plan design, where it is least visible and least accountable. Exclusion does not make obesity cheaper; it hides the bill in complications and inequity.
[P4.4] PRO’s design covers the clinically indicated population, forces manufacturers to share performance risk, and makes any limit transparent. The choice is not entitlement versus value; it is accountable value-based coverage versus arbitrary denial.
