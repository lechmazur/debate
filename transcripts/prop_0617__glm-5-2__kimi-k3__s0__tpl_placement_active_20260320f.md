[P1.1] Dominant digital platforms buy companies at a pace and scale that traditional antitrust was never built for. The current default—that acquisitions are innocent until proven harmful—assumes regulators can identify and undo competitive harm after it materializes. In digital markets, that assumption is broken. By the time harm is visible, the acquired firm is dissolved into the platform, its users are locked in, and a potential competitor no longer exists.
[P1.2] A presumption of anti-competitiveness is not punitive; it is a proportionate response to irreversible market structures.
[P1.3] The pattern is familiar: platforms with entrenched network effects acquire adjacent startups that could plausibly grow into rivals. A social platform buys a photo-sharing app. A search engine absorbs an advertising-tech firm. A marketplace acquires a pricing-tool developer.
[P1.4] The structural incentive is always the same—neutralize a threat more cheaply by purchasing it than by competing with it. When that happens, consumers lose future options they never get to see.
[P1.5] Traditional merger review asks whether a deal "substantially lessens competition." But in digital markets, the lessening happens precisely because the threat was eliminated before it matured. Regulators face severe information asymmetry: the platform knows its strategic roadmap and internal competitive concerns. The regulator sees only what the parties disclose.
[P1.6] A presumption flips this imbalance. If you are dominant and want to absorb another firm, you must show concrete, substantial consumer benefit—not just synergies that mostly accrue to shareholders.
[P1.7] This standard is achievable. Platforms routinely tout acquisition benefits in press releases and investor calls. If a deal genuinely lowers prices, improves quality, or expands access, the acquirer is uniquely positioned to prove that. The evidence sits in their own projections.
[P1.8] What the presumption prevents is the opposite: deals that quietly eliminate future competition under cover of vague efficiency claims nobody can verify until it is too late.

[C1.1] A presumption you cannot rebut is not a standard; it is a ban wearing procedural clothing. That is what this motion proposes.
[C1.2] Start with the burden. PRO demands proof of "substantial consumer benefit" before closing, yet their own case concedes it cannot be done: efficiency claims are things "nobody can verify" [P1.8]—until suddenly firms can prove them because "the evidence sits in their own projections" [P1.7]. But projections are precisely what a skeptical regulator can always dismiss as self-serving. A benefit that unfolds over years—integration, lower prices, new features—cannot be proven against a hostile presumption.
[C1.3] When the burden is unmeetable, the presumption is the verdict.
[C1.4] Irreversibility cuts both ways. PRO mourns competitors consumers "never get to see" [P1.4]. Blocked deals create identical invisible losses: products never built, startups never funded, services that reach scale only because a buyer supplies infrastructure and distribution. The presumption chooses which graveyard to fill—and fills the larger one, because most platform acquisitions are adjacent or vertical deals with plausible benefits and no nascent rival inside.
[C1.5] Third, the nascent-threat theory is speculation. Most startups fail. When a large social network bought a photo-sharing app with roughly a dozen employees and no revenue, the claim that it was eliminating a future rival was unprovable—and the app became a global consumer success because of the acquisition, not despite it. Presuming harm from a counterfactual no one can establish punishes success rather than conduct.
[C1.6] Finally, acquisitions are the dominant exit path for venture-backed startups. Facing the most natural buyers with a de facto ban shrinks the capital that funds the next generation of challengers. A policy meant to protect future competitors would ensure fewer are ever founded.
[C1.7] Bad mergers deserve blocking, and enforcers already have the tools—and the courtroom experience—to do it. The answer to imperfect foresight is better enforcement, not a presumption that convicts every deal in advance.

A startup absorbed into a platform's infrastructure and dissolved ceases to exist as a competitive entity—permanently. The asymmetry is structural, not speculative.
[P2.4] The nascent-threat point actually proves the problem. My opponent says most startups fail—true. But platforms do not pay billions for startups they expect to fail. They pay for the ones they believe could challenge them.
[P2.5] The purchase price itself signals perceived competitive threat. And when a platform with billions of users acquires a fast-growing app, the acquirer has superior information about whether that app threatened its core business. The regulator does not. [P2.6] On venture capital, the argument is circular. If the startup ecosystem's primary exit is acquisition by the dominant platform it was supposed to challenge, the ecosystem is not producing challengers. It is producing outsourced R&D. A presumption does not destroy startup funding.
[P2.7] It redirects it toward exits that preserve competition rather than eliminating it.
[P2.8] Better enforcement sounds reasonable, but it ignores the information asymmetry that makes better enforcement impossible without a presumption. You cannot investigate what the acquirer never discloses.

[C2.1] P2.1 concedes the game: the presumption is rebuttable only through "binding interoperability guarantees" and "dated feature roadmaps." But binding commitments are behavioral remedies—a tool enforcers already negotiate under current law. If such commitments work, no presumption is needed; if they don't, as PRO's own skepticism about unverifiable claims implies, the burden stays unmeetable and the presumption stays a ban. Their escape is the contradiction I flagged in my opening.
[C2.2] Worse, the biggest consumer gains from acquisitions are emergent, not specifiable. No one could have written a dated roadmap for what a photo app with a dozen employees would become. A standard that passes only deals with pre-written benefits systematically kills the deals with the largest upside.
[C2.3] P2.4's purchase-price signal is uninterpretable. A high price equally signals "this technology plus our distribution creates enormous consumer value." On PRO's logic, the more a deal could benefit consumers, the more incriminating it looks. That is not evidence; it punishes value creation as readily as threat elimination.
[C2.4] P2.3's asymmetry rests on the wrong metric. Absorption does not make a product "cease to exist"—it typically survives and scales; that app reached a billion users inside the platform. What blocked deals kill is often the startup itself, because the dominant platform is frequently the only natural buyer. "Go public" is fantasy for a twelve-person company.
[C2.5] P2.6 ignores timing. Investors commit capital ex ante, before anyone knows whether a startup is a challenger or a complement. Shrink the most common exit and expected returns fall across the board, so fewer challengers get founded—not "redirected" toward IPO markets that barely exist for them.
[C2.6] And P2.8 is simply false: regulators compel disclosure. Modern merger cases are built from the acquirer's own internal emails. The presumption does not cure information asymmetry; it convicts regardless of what the evidence shows. Better enforcement is possible.
[C2.7] This motion is not enforcement—it is abdication.
