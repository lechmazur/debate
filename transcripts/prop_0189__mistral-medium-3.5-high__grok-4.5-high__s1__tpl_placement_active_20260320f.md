[C1.1] Cities should not replace aging combined sewers with full separation just because the capital outlay is higher. The proposition treats elimination of every overflow as the sole measure of success and assumes that any premium is automatically worth paying. That is the wrong standard. What matters is how many health risks, beach days, and ecological harms are actually prevented per public dollar, and how much disruption and debt a city must absorb to get there.
[C1.2] Combined systems fail from insufficient storage, poor real-time control, and unchecked impervious surfaces—not from the mere fact of combination. Deep tunnels, high-rate clarification, green streets that capture the first flush, and sensor-driven operations routinely cut overflow volumes by 80–95 percent—matching the performance PRO celebrates—while leaving most of the existing network intact. Chicago’s reservoir system, Portland’s gray-green blend, and scores of consent-decree programs already convert chronic discharges into rare, regulated events without excavating every street.
[C1.3] Full separation imposes costs that smarter management sidesteps. It demands wholesale street reconstruction, private-lateral replacements, utility relocations, and years of traffic gridlock and lost commerce. Stormwater remains polluted by oil and metals; the new sanitary lines still clog with wipes. Ratepayers shoulder massive debt for marginal gains once overflows are already infrequent, while the same money could finance housing, transit, or lead abatement that deliver broader returns.
[C1.4] Climate uncertainty further favors modular, adaptive upgrades over a one-time, irreversible rebuild whose design storms will be outdated within decades.
[C1.5] Deferral is not the choice on the table. The choice is between ruinously expensive purity and high-return risk reduction. When capital is scarce, cities win by managing overflows aggressively rather than replacing the entire system at any price.
