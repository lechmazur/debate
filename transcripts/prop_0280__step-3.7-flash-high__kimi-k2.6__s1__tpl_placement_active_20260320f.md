[P1.1] The modern wedding industry has transformed a personal commitment into a $35,000+ performance, and couples owe no moral duty to uphold outdated, costly traditional expectations that prioritize vendor profits and social approval over their own well-being. First, the financial burden is not trivial: that average U.S. wedding cost equals a 10% down payment on a median home, or full repayment of most private student loan balances. Yet traditional scripts demand couples sink those resources into a single day: mandatory $10,000 venue fees, overpriced catering for dozens of distant relatives they barely speak to, custom favors that end up in trash cans within hours, and exorbitant alteration costs for a "princess" dress that reinforces harmful body standards and pressures brides to crash diet for months.
[P1.2] Second, the performative demands erase the actual point of the event: the couple’s commitment. Traditional expectations enforce heteronormative, cisgender framing, demand religious rituals for secular couples, or impose elaborate protocols that make people with non-traditional family structures—queer couples, blended families, non-monogamous partnerships—feel like outsiders in their own celebration. The pressure to host a "perfect" event that meets other people's standards leads to skyrocketing stress, family conflict over payment obligations, and couples going into debt to avoid judgment from guests who would far rather eat cheap barbecue at a park than sit through a three-course meal at a country club.
[P1.3] Couples’ only obligation is to mark their commitment in a way that feels true to them, not to spend years of savings and months of stress to perform a script written by a $60 billion industry.

[C1.1] The proposition collapses two separate problems into one bad conclusion. That weddings have grown expensive does not mean traditional expectations themselves have lost their moral force. The real culprit is the commercial industry, not the institution. Traditional expectations center on family, community, and public commitment—not on inflated venue markups or designer favors.
[C1.2] Couples who reject tradition entirely to save money often inflict a deeper, less visible cost: they deny parents, grandparents, and siblings the shared ritual that marks a generational transition. That obligation is relational, not commercial, and it survives even when budgets shrink.
[C1.3] The performative dimension the proposition derides is precisely what gives a wedding its enduring social weight. Marriage is not merely a private lifestyle preference; it is a public institution that invites a community to witness vows and hold the couple accountable across decades. When two people stand before family and friends, they are not simply curating content for an audience. They are asking their community to recognize, remember, and sustain their bond.
[C1.4] Stripping away that performative element because it feels stressful reduces marriage to a consumer choice rather than a civic and familial milestone that binds generations.
[C1.5] Finally, cost and tradition are plainly separable. A couple can exchange rings, speak vows before an officiant, share a meal, and dance to a playlist without surrendering to the wedding industry at all. The proposition asks us to discard the ceremonial core because the packaging is overpriced.
[C1.6] But tradition encodes real obligations to the people we love and the communities that will support the marriage long after the party ends. Couples should feel significant, not minimal, obligation to honor those expectations, even when they adapt the form to their means.
