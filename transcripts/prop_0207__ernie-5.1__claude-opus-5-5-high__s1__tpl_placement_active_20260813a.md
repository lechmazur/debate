[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are one of the most regressive, wasteful forms of public spending in existence. They hand the largest benefits to those who can afford the biggest cars and the longest commutes—middle-class and wealthy households who consume disproportionate shares of subsidized fuel—while the poorest, who often lack vehicles, see almost nothing. That is not poverty alleviation; it is reverse redistribution dressed up as compassion.
[P1.2] The fiscal cost is staggering. Countries spending billions on blanket fuel price controls bleed revenue that could fund schools, clinics, or—critically—direct support for vulnerable households. Subsidies also distort market signals, encouraging overconsumption, smuggling, and black markets, all of which undermine the very energy security governments claim to protect.
[P1.3] Targeted cash transfers solve all of this in one stroke. Give money directly to low-income households, and they spend it on what they actually need—food, rent, medicine, or yes, fuel if that is their biggest cost. The money stays in the local economy, poverty is addressed at its root, and fuel prices reflect real costs, which is essential for long-term investment in cleaner energy and efficient infrastructure.
[P1.4] The proposition is not asking governments to do nothing. It is asking them to do something smarter: replace blunt price controls with precise, accountable transfers. Indonesia's experience in the early 2010s is instructive—when it redirected subsidy savings into cash programs for the poor, poverty rates fell measurably without the market chaos that critics predicted. The alternative, maintaining universal subsidies, means permanent fiscal drag, entrenched inequality, and a delayed energy transition.
[P1.5] The central question this debate must answer is simple: should governments keep subsidizing fuel for everyone, or spend the same money more effectively by helping those who genuinely need it? The answer should be obvious.
