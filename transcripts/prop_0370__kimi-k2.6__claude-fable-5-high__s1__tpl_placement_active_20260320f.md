[P1.1] Personal phones are private property, not corporate infrastructure. They hold medical records, family photographs, location histories, and banking details. When a large employer makes installing work messaging or tracking software on that device a routine condition of employment, it does not merely request a communication channel—it asserts dominion over intimate space that should remain beyond management’s reach.
[P1.2] This practice forces a brazen cost shift onto workers. Companies with capital to build headquarters and buy back stock somehow claim they cannot issue a basic work handset or maintain a browser-based portal. Instead, they externalize hardware, data plans, battery life, and device wear onto employees, effectively demanding that workers pay for their own supervision. If a job requires constant connectivity, the employer should provide the tool, not colonize the worker’s pocket.
[P1.3] The supposed consent is illusory. When installation is a routine, non-negotiable condition of hiring, refusal equals unemployment. Large employers possess the resources to offer genuine alternatives—dedicated devices, web applications, or hotline workflows—yet they choose coercion because it is cheaper. That is not operational necessity; it is the extraction of monitoring leverage from a captive workforce.
[P1.4] Nor can workers trust the boundary between messaging and tracking. An app pushed for team coordination can quietly access location data, contact lists, or usage patterns. Employees cannot audit the code, and terms of service can change overnight. Once personal phones become standard workplace infrastructure, off-the-clock psychological availability and location exposure cease to be exceptional overreach and become baseline expectations baked into the job.
[P1.5] Large employers are powerful enough to purchase their own tools. The proposition simply insists that they must. Rejecting routine personal-phone occupation affirms a basic principle: a contract for labor is not a license to invade private life.

[C1.1] The proposition asks you to declare a near-universal, mutually convenient practice illegitimate. That is the wrong call, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the single-device norm exists because workers want it. Most employees, offered a clunky second handset to charge, carry, and lose, decline it. Bring-your-own-device became standard not through corporate conspiracy but through employee demand: people prefer Teams or Slack on the phone already in their pocket. The proposition would push large employers back toward issuing millions of redundant devices—an environmental and logistical absurdity—or toward something worse: work conducted over unsecured personal texting, where neither the company nor the worker has any protection at all.
[C1.3] Second, the technical objection is a decade out of date. Modern mobile platforms run work apps in sandboxed work profiles: the employer manages its container, sees nothing of photos, messages, or browsing, and can wipe only its own data when employment ends. A messaging app is not "dominion over intimate space" any more than checking work email on a home laptop is corporate seizure of the living room. The intrusion PRO describes is an architectural artifact of older systems, not the routine practice the motion condemns.
[C1.4] Third, the expectation is reasonable precisely because it is conditional and compensable. An expectation is not a mandate enforced regardless of circumstance; it is a default. Employers can and routinely do pair it with stipends and accommodations for the rare objector. The motion, by contrast, is categorical: no large employer should expect this, ever, as routine.
[C1.5] That bans the home-health agency that needs to verify a nurse actually visited a vulnerable patient, the delivery firm coordinating routes, the hospital sending emergency shift alerts.
[C1.6] The real choice is not between privacy and surveillance. It is between a managed, sandboxed, compensated norm—and a fragmented world of second phones, shadow IT, and unreachable workers. Reasonable expectations, with safeguards, beat blanket prohibition.
