[P1.1] Regulators who invoke the precautionary principle usually mean something sharper than “be careful.” They mean: if harm cannot be ruled out, the innovation waits. That rule blocks more beneficial innovation than it prevents genuine harm.
[P1.2] Proof of safety is an impossible demand. Every new crop, reactor, drug, or vehicle carries residual uncertainty. Under the principle as commonly applied, that residue is enough to delay, restrict, or ban, while the harms of the status quo need no fresh proof. Traffic deaths, crop losses, pollution, and untreated disease are already happening.
[P1.3] They are simply familiar, so they do not count as the kind of risk that triggers action. Hypothetical harms from the unfamiliar do.
[P1.4] The record of that asymmetry is concrete. Genetically modified crops were frozen across much of Europe for a generation. The predicted ecological catastrophe did not arrive; the postponed gains in yield, pesticide reduction, and biofortified food were real losses for people already harmed by scarcity. Nuclear plants were stalled or closed on precautionary grounds while coal, with known deaths, continued.
[P1.5] Drug approval processes treat a patient who dies waiting as nobody’s regulatory failure, even when the disease was certain and the drug’s remaining risk was not.
[P1.6] Defenders will point to asbestos and other late-recognized poisons. Proportionate controls on demonstrated hazards are not what is at issue. Common application is different: any uncertainty justifies holding the new thing, and no uncertainty is required to tolerate the old thing. Regulators pay little for false alarms.
[P1.7] Society pays in innovations that never scale—cleaner energy, safer transport, faster therapies—whose benefits would have compounded. The people those delays injure cannot organize, because they never received the treatment, the crop, or the cleaner air.
[P1.8] Weigh the principle by its operation, not its slogan. A rule that prices imagined harm above ongoing harm will stop more good than it averts.
