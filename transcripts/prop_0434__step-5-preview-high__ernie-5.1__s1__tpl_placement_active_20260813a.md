[P1.1] Noise disputes are chronically under-proven. A tenant trying to sleep through a neighbor’s 2 a.m. bass, a family below constant stomping, or a shift worker awakened by early leaf blowers has a real injury but often weak proof. Complaint logs are called subjective, neighbors are accused of grudges, and an inspector usually misses the noise because it is not happening during the visit. Certified monitoring closes that gap by capturing sound levels and recurrence over time, turning “it happens every night” into a factual pattern the other side can test.
[P1.2] The current imbalance rewards denial and endurance. A noisy occupant can say nothing happened; a landlord can call complaints unverified; the affected neighbor is left to endure the condition or escalate into retaliation. That harms sleep, health, and safety, and it weakens both rental enforcement and nuisance claims, which should turn on conditions rather than personalities. A calibrated, time-stamped monitor with documented provenance and municipal noise thresholds supplies what ordinary testimony cannot: evidence that does not depend on being awake at the right moment.
[P1.3] Certification is the safeguard, not the problem. Phone clips invite cherry-picking and authenticity disputes; certified devices create chain of custody and a reproducible method. They do not make every decibel reading automatically decisive—context, duration, and local standards still matter—but they make the inquiry evidence-led instead of endurance-led.
[P1.4] The central question is whether residents can prove recurring noise reliably. Allowing certified noise-monitoring evidence more freely would do exactly that: it strengthens legitimate claims, exposes weak ones, and moves disputes from conflicting impressions toward verifiable facts.

[C1.1] Certified noise monitors sound like a neat solution, but expanding their admissibility would open a Pandora's box of privacy invasion, inequity, and weaponization that outweighs any marginal evidentiary gain.
[C1.2] First, noise is inherently relational. Allowing neighbors to deploy calibrated microphones in hallways, stairwells, or shared walls turns every dispute into a surveillance exercise. The law already protects against unreasonable intrusion; lowering the bar for admitting such data incentivizes constant recording, eroding the basic expectation that a home is not a monitored space. Tenants who already feel vulnerable under landlord power would face another tool of control.
[C1.3] Second, certification does not equal tamper‑proof. Devices can be placed strategically—right next to a vent, behind a thin wall, or angled to amplify a specific source—producing readings that meet the letter of a standard while distorting reality. A neighbor could claim a “certified” device proved a neighbor's music exceeded limits, when in fact the microphone was positioned to catch a single burst and ignore context. Courts would be flooded with battles over placement, calibration logs, and chain‑of‑custody, consuming resources that could fund mediation instead.
[C1.4] Third, the cost barrier deepens injustice. Certified monitors cost several hundred dollars, plus calibration fees. Wealthier tenants can purchase and present them; low‑income renters cannot, creating a tiered system where the ability to prove a nuisance becomes a function of bank balance rather than merit.
[C1.5] Finally, the existing legal toolkit already permits expert testimony, decibel logs, and inspector visits. What is missing is not more data but better enforcement and dispute‑resolution mechanisms. By privileging a technical metric, we risk reducing a lived harm—sleep loss, stress, health impacts—to a number that can be gamed, ignoring the human dimension that courts must weigh.
[C1.6] The proposition promises fairness but delivers a surveillance‑heavy, inequitable, and litigation‑prone regime. The smarter path is to strengthen mediation and enforcement, not to hand neighbors a certified microphone.
