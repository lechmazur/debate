[P1.5] That is what is at stake here, not marginal quarterly revenue for theatre chains. Uniform pricing for standard seats is a relic of an era when exhibition held a monopoly. [C1.2] When a multiplex closes in a working-class zip code, that isn't protecting mass culture—it's burying it. Dynamic pricing is the only mechanism left that keeps the lights on, the projectors running, and the seats filled. Without it, there is no cinema habit left to defend.
[C1.3] Consider the Tuesday text exchange where a spontaneous plan dies because the 7pm seat costs more. That's nostalgia, not reality. [C1.4] What dynamic pricing actually does is segment that planned demand: charging premium rates for Friday night blockbusters cross-subsidizes dirt-cheap Tuesday matinees for students, seniors, and shift workers. It transforms a flat, regressive structure—where the unemployed retiree pays the same as the hedge fund manager—into a progressive ladder that widens access rather than narrowing it.
[C1.5] And the "shared social norm" of predictable pricing is already fiction. We have IMAX premiums, dine-in surcharges, recliner upgrades, and subscription tiers. Audiences already accept that different experiences cost different amounts. Standard seats should be no different.
[C1.6] The real habit killer isn't a price that moves with demand; it's an empty auditorium. Price for survival, and the mass audience follows. Price for nostalgia, and you get a ghost town.

You cannot fund cheap Tuesday matinees without charging premium rates on high-demand Friday nights. By condemning "aggressive last-minute surge pricing," the proposition condemns the very revenue stream that keeps standard seats affordable for students and shift workers during the week.
[C2.2] They are attacking the solution while pretending to support its benefits.
[C2.3] Their claim that chains using dynamic pricing still closed screens is asserted, not demonstrated. Basic economics is unambiguous: price discrimination captures consumer surplus that uniform pricing leaves on the table. A theater charging twenty-five dollars on Friday and eight on Tuesday generates more total revenue and fills more seats than one charging fifteen for both. The PRO case offers no mechanism by which charging less for identical inventory prevents bankruptcy.
[C2.4] They mistake correlation for causation.
[C2.5] Here is the decisive point they keep dodging: the "spontaneous" Tuesday-night moviegoer is precisely who dynamic pricing helps. When that friend texts on Tuesday for Wednesday evening, demand is low and prices drop below the fixed-rate alternative. The "penalty" only hits high-demand slots where willingness to pay is genuinely higher. The monthly regular they describe does not vanish; they simply choose off-peak showtimes and pay less than they would under the uniform pricing regime PRO defends.
[C2.6] Their "shared trust" argument ignores market reality. Airlines, hotels, and ride-shares surge-price identical seats and rooms routinely. Audiences already accept that the early booker pays less than the walk-up customer. Cinema is adopting standard revenue management, not betraying a social contract.
[C2.7] The alternative to variable pricing is not a democratic "public space"; it is a shuttered multiplex with no seats at any price. You cannot preserve a mass habit by bankrupting the venues that host it. Survival requires solvency, and solvency requires prices that reflect demand.
