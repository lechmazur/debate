[P1.1] The proposition does not ban persuasion, targeting, or AI. It requires a warning label when a major platform carries a persuasion campaign whose message or audience was optimized by AI for specific users or micro-groups. That is the minimum disclosure a democratic public needs.
[P1.2] The reason is simple: the most dangerous influence is often not synthetic text or a deepfake. It is an ordinary-looking message, tested and retuned by a model against people like you. Your neighbor sees a different appeal; you never learn that a system found the version most likely to move your fear, anger, or insecurity. Labeling only AI-generated content misses that machinery entirely.
[P1.3] A sentence can be written by a human and still be AI-optimized persuasion.
[P1.4] Labels restore agency at the point of impact. “This message was selected or tailored using AI optimization for a specific audience” does not tell people what to think; it tells them how the message was built. That changes how a claim lands. We already accept disclosures for paid political advertising and sponsored content because source and method matter. This is the same principle for a more powerful technique.
[P1.5] It is also feasible at the platform layer. Platforms already know who was targeted, what segments exist, and which campaigns are running. They can require campaign disclosure, audit targeting systems, and attach a plain-language label. Imperfect enforcement is not an argument for darkness; a label that catches most coordinated campaigns still gives users, journalists, and regulators a trail they otherwise lack.
[P1.6] The alternative is a persuasion arms race conducted in secret, where the platform and the campaign know the model’s leverage and the target does not. Requiring labels puts that asymmetry back in public view. If a platform can auction a micro-group’s attention, it can post a label.

[C1.1] The proposal’s fatal flaw is that its definition of “AI-optimized persuasion” is so broad it captures ordinary, legitimate activity, while too narrow to address the actual harm PRO claims to target. Every digital ad campaign—from a local coffee shop promoting a seasonal drink to a community food drive asking for donations—uses A/B testing and algorithmic tweaks to improve performance. This is standard marketing, not the shadowy, user-specific manipulation PRO describes. If this counts as AI-optimized persuasion, every boosted post would require a label, flooding users with meaningless disclosures that erode trust in legitimate warnings like political ad labels.
[C1.2] The rule is also unenforceable at the scale PRO demands. AI can adjust messaging in real time for thousands of micro-groups, even individual users, at a volume platforms cannot possibly audit. The requirement would either be so overbroad it crushes small advertisers and independent creators who lack the resources to navigate complex compliance, or so full of carveouts that bad actors can easily slip manipulative campaigns through the gaps.
[C1.3] Worst of all, the rule would disproportionately silence marginalized voices. A mutual aid network in Detroit that uses basic AI tools to translate and adjust supply request forms for different immigrant neighborhoods, or a local tenant union that tweaks outreach copy for renters in specific buildings, would have to label every single post. These groups lack the resources to navigate complex compliance rules, so they will face content removal or suppression for labeling errors, diverting time and resources away from the community work they do, harming the very groups the policy is supposed to protect.
[C1.4] PRO claims labels restore user agency, but these disclosures will quickly become background noise. Users already ignore sponsored content tags; a generic “AI-optimized” label will be dismissed as a platform formality, while bad actors will simply adjust their copy to avoid triggering optimization flags, rendering the entire requirement useless.
