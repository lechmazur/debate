[C1.2] Tokyo, London, New York did not become economic engines because of policy bias; they grew because each added worker made the others more productive. That mechanism is real, powerful, and not negotiable by legislation.
[C1.3] The cost of fighting this reality is staggering. Japan has spent decades pouring infrastructure into shrinking rural communities—bridges to nowhere, bullet-train stops serving towns that no longer exist in meaningful numbers—and the outcome is demographic collapse nonetheless, plus national debt that exceeds twice GDP. [C1.5] The humane alternative is not abandonment; it is mobility. [C1.6] The resilience claim gets the diagnosis backwards. Hyper-concentration is not uniquely fragile; small towns dependent on single employers or industries are fragile by definition. When the mine closes or the factory automates, the town dies. Cities absorb shocks by diversifying across thousands of firms and sectors.
[C1.7] "Doing more" sounds modest. It is not. It means sustained, open-ended transfers from productive regions to declining ones, with diminishing returns and no exit strategy. The fair, effective, and honest policy is to help people thrive where opportunity actually exists—not to pretend that political will can rewrite urbanization's economics.
