[P1.1] The curb is the most contested piece of real estate in any modern city, and we are pricing it like it's 1955. A FedEx van, an Uber idling for a pickup, a DoorDash double-park, and a resident's car all compete for the same six meters of asphalt — and right now the city's answer is "first come, first blocked." That is the status quo CON has to defend.
[P1.2] Three reasons dynamic curb pricing wins.
[P1.3] First, it solves the actual problem causing the chaos: delivery and ride-hail vehicles have surged precisely because curb access is effectively free at the point of use. When something valuable is free, it gets misused — drivers double-park, block bus lanes, and circle endlessly. A price that rises at peak hours and on hot blocks forces fleets to internalize the bus delay, the bike-lane swerve, and the emissions they currently dump on everyone else. Free curb is a subsidy to the most disruptive users.
[P1.4] Second, pricing produces better behavior, not just revenue. UPS and Amazon already optimize routes to the minute; charge them more at 5 p.m. on a busy corridor and they shift to off-peak windows, consolidated stops, and proper loading zones. Ride-hail apps can nudge pickups around the corner to a cheaper curb in seconds. You cannot get that responsiveness from a painted sign or an occasional ticket — only from a price that moves.
[P1.5] Third, the alternative isn't "free and fine." It's enforcement theater, fines that fleets treat as a cost of doing business, and residents losing curb to commercial users anyway. Dynamic pricing replaces a lottery enforced by horns and hazard lights with a transparent market, with revenue that can fund loading zones, transit, and accessibility.
[P1.6] The clash is simple: should the scarcest surface in the city be allocated by chance and aggression, or by a price that reflects what it actually costs the rest of us? Defend the horn-honking status quo if you can.
