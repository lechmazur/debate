[P1.1] Tying a person's access to a doctor to their payroll relationship is a historical accident, not a design. Employer-based insurance grew out of wartime wage controls, and we've mistaken that accident for a system. Judged as a system, it fails on every count that matters for basic coverage.
[P1.2] First, it fails at the moment of greatest need. Serious illness is one of the leading causes of job loss — and in an employer-tied system, losing the job means losing the coverage. The two risks are correlated by design. During the 2020 recession, millions lost jobs and insurance in the same month, mid-pandemic.
[P1.3] A financing scheme that evaporates precisely when people get sick or the economy contracts is not insurance; it's a fair-weather benefit.
[P1.4] Second, it chains workers to employers. Job lock is real: people stay in jobs they've outgrown, delay starting businesses, delay retiring, because a child's asthma or a spouse's medication makes the employer plan too valuable to leave. Retirement rates jump at age 65 — the moment Medicare severs the link. That's the market telling us the link is a shackle.
[P1.5] Single-payer financing frees labor to move to its best use.
[P1.6] Third, fragmentation is waste. Thousands of employers each negotiating plans means hundreds of billing systems, armies of claims staff, and hospitals spending billions fighting denials. Single-payer systems run on a fraction of that administrative overhead, and one pool covering everyone can bargain down drug and hospital prices in a way no single employer can.
[P1.7] Note what the motion actually says: basic coverage. Supplementary private insurance for extras remains perfectly possible, as it does in many single-payer countries. So the choice here is not security versus freedom. It's between coverage that follows the person and coverage that follows the paycheck.
[P1.8] Only one guarantees basic medical care to everyone, all the time, at lower total cost. That is the comparison on which this debate should be decided.

[C1.1] Note the sleight of hand in [P1.1]: employer-based financing is treated as synonymous with America's patchwork. It isn't. Germany, Japan, and the Netherlands all run coverage through employment-linked contributions — payroll-based sickness funds — and they achieve universality with shorter waits and faster access to new treatments than the classic single-payer systems. So the gaps PRO describes are not intrinsic to work-linked financing.
[C1.2] They are American implementation failures, fixable with continuation rules, subsidized funds for the unemployed, and a mandate that no fund can turn anyone away.
[C1.3] That distinction decides the debate. The defects of employer-based systems are patchable. The defects of single-payer are structural.
[C1.4] Here is the structural one. Single-payer means health care becomes a line item in a government budget, competing with pensions, defense, and debt service. When money is tight, a private insurer must still honor the contract; a treasury simply doesn't expand the budget, and the shortfall comes out as queues, thin capital spending, and aging scanners. That is why the NHS runs chronic backlogs and Canadians wait months for elective surgery and specialist referrals — not because those countries are poor or badly run, but because a single budget-holder rations by delay rather than by price.
[C1.5] Delay is still denial; it just doesn't generate a bill.
[C1.6] And a monopoly payer removes exit. If your employer plan denies a treatment, you can change jobs, appeal, or buy around it. If the single national payer decides a drug isn't cost-effective, there is no second door. PRO's supplementary-insurance escape hatch in [P1.7] concedes the point: the reason those markets exist is that the basic tier under-delivers.
[C1.7] On job lock: the answer to a portability problem is portability — letting coverage travel with the worker — not abolishing plural payers entirely.
[C1.8] Finally, weigh the transition. Dismantling coverage that hundreds of millions currently rely on, in one legislative act, risks landing in a half-built system worse than either endpoint. Reform the link. Don't gamble everything on a monopoly.
