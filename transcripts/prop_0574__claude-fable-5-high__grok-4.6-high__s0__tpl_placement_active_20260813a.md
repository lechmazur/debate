[P1.1] When a studio resurrects a dead singer's voice or a dead actor's face, it takes the most personal thing a human being ever creates — the sound and shape of themselves — and puts words in a mouth that can no longer object. The question is simple: should companies need to ask first, or take first and litigate later? Requiring explicit permission is the only defensible default.
[P1.2] First, this is about consent, not censorship. A permission rule bans nothing. It says a corporation that wants to profit from a specific dead person's identity must get a yes from whoever that person left in charge — an estate, heirs, a designated rights-holder. That is exactly how we already treat a dead author's novels, a dead songwriter's catalog, a dead photographer's images.
[P1.3] It would be bizarre if the artist's copyrighted recordings were protected for decades while the artist herself — voice, face, mannerisms — could be synthetically puppeteered for free the day after burial.
[P1.4] Second, the incentives without permission are ugly and predictable. Dead performers become the cheapest labor in entertainment: no salary, no creative objections, no scandals. Studios would rationally substitute synthetic legends for living artists, hollowing out careers while flooding culture with counterfeit performances the deceased never approved. A dead artist's reputation — often their family's chief inheritance — can be trashed by one bad AI project they never chose.
[P1.5] Third, audiences are defrauded. A "new Whitney Houston performance" trades entirely on her identity. If she never sanctioned it, the product is a forgery wearing a famous name, and permission is what separates tribute from impersonation-for-profit.
[P1.6] Note what this motion does not require: no ban on AI, no restriction on parody, commentary, or generic style influence. It asks only that the entity monetizing a specific dead human's likeness obtain consent — the same thing they'd need from that artist alive. Death should not be a licensing loophole.
