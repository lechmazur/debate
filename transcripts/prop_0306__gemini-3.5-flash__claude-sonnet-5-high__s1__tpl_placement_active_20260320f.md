[P1.1] The ethical calculus of animal captivity has fundamentally shifted. For decades, zoos justified confining sentient beings by claiming a monopoly on conservation and education. Today, that monopoly is broken. With the rise of immersive digital tools and a clearer understanding of ecological preservation, lifelong animal confinement is no longer ethically defensible.
[P1.2] First, captivity causes unavoidable harm. No matter how many naturalistic elements are added to modern enclosures, they remain gilded cages. Wide-ranging, cognitively complex species like elephants, large carnivores, and primates suffer from systemic physical and psychological pathologies, such as stereotypic pacing. We cannot ethically justify subjecting animals to lifelong deprivation for human viewing.
[P1.3] Second, the conservation defense is a financial mirage. The vast majority of species in zoos are not endangered, and only a tiny fraction of zoo revenues directly fund in-situ conservation. Maintaining artificial, climate-controlled environments for a few captive animals is incredibly expensive. Those same resources, if redirected to habitat protection, would yield vastly superior ecological returns, safeguarding entire ecosystems and thousands of species in the wild where they belong.
[P1.4] Finally, genuine education does not require captivity. Observing depressed, pacing animals in artificial paddocks teaches the wrong lesson: that human dominance over nature is acceptable. Modern digital tools—from high-definition interactive exhibits to virtual reality—can transport people directly into wild ecosystems. This technology captures natural behaviors and ecological relationships that captive animals can never display, fostering true environmental empathy.
[P1.5] By combining robust habitat protection with immersive digital education, we can save species and inspire the public without the moral stain of captivity. The modern zoo is an obsolete compromise; it is time to embrace a more compassionate and effective future.

Habitat protection cannot save a species that has no wild population left to protect. You cannot rewild an ecosystem for a species that no longer exists in it.
[C1.3] Ex-situ breeding programs run by zoos are not a nostalgic sideshow; they are the only reason these species exist at all today. No amount of virtual reality generates a living condor egg.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's financial argument cuts against them. Redirecting zoo money to habitat protection assumes zoos and habitat protection compete for the same donor dollar. They don't. [C1.5] Close the zoo and that revenue does not magically reappear as a habitat protection budget—it evaporates into other consumer spending. The realistic comparison is not "zoo money vs. habitat money," it's "zoo money vs. nothing."
[C1.6] Third, on education: immersive digital tools are a supplement, not a substitute, because they lack the one thing that actually changes behavior—embodied, reciprocal presence. A child who watches a lion pace three feet away, breathing, alive, forms a different relationship to that animal than one clicking through a VR headset. That visceral encounter is repeatedly what drives people toward conservation donations, environmental careers, and behavior change; a curated simulation cannot replicate unpredictability, scent, sound, and scale.
[C1.7] Finally, notice the false choice embedded in the resolution. Nothing stops modern zoos from also using VR and also funding habitat protection. The question is whether captivity itself is now unjustified—and for species that exist only because zoos kept them alive, it plainly is not.
