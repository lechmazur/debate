The question is not whether some producers behave callously. Of course some do. The question is whether “routine” exploitation justifies stronger blanket constraints — and the answer is no, because the existing toolkit already reaches the actual wrongs, while a genre-wide regime would suppress legitimate speech and victim agency.
[C1.2] Start with the word “routine.” Vivid cases of families ambushed by producers are memorable precisely because they are failures, not the ordinary product. Much true crime is documentary journalism, legal analysis, missing-person appeals, and wrongful-conviction reporting. Those stories can reopen cold cases, correct miscarriages of justice, and give families a public voice they otherwise lack. A regime built on the worst actors would treat those projects as presumptively suspect.
[C1.3] Second, the harms PRO names are already actionable where they are genuinely wrongful: harassment, defamation, invasion of privacy, disclosure of private facts, breach of confidence, unauthorized commercial use of a person’s image. Platforms and publishers also face reputational and contractual pressure. If a family is hounded at work, that is harassment. If a program invents guilt, that is defamation.
[C1.4] The proposition needs to show why a new genre-specific layer is necessary, not merely symbolically satisfying.
[C1.5] Third, stronger legal constraints create a gatekeeping problem. Who consents for a murdered victim? Which relative speaks for a fractured family? What happens when an abuser, a police department, or a powerful family uses a consent requirement to bury a story?
[C1.6] Victims are not a single interest group. Some want privacy; others want publicity, reform, or justice. A blanket rule empowers the loudest or most connected relative to silence everyone else.
[C1.7] The real choice is not exploitation versus regulation. It is targeted remedies for demonstrable abuse versus genre-wide restrictions that chill reporting and strip victims of control. CON wins that comparison.
