[P1.1] Precise location data is not ordinary commercial information. It is a diary of the body: where someone sleeps, worships, gets treatment, meets a lawyer, attends a union meeting, shelters from an abuser, or spends the night. No name is needed. A repeated home-and-work pattern, clinic stops, and evening coordinates can re-identify an “anonymous” device and expose health, faith, politics, relationships, and vulnerability.
[P1.2] The data-broker market is therefore unlike a navigation app. A person may understand why maps or a delivery service needs location for that service. A data broker has no service relationship with the person; it acquires trails from apps, devices, ad systems, or other intermediaries, then sells them to buyers the person never chose. The injury is not an annoying ad.
[P1.3] It can enable stalking, coercive control, burglary targeting, discrimination, worker pressure, or intimidation around medical and civic visits.
[P1.4] Notice-and-choice cannot legitimate this trade. Buried permissions, vague policies, device identifiers, and retroactive opt-outs are not a clear agreement to sell movements. Tolerating collection by one app for one purpose is not permission for permanent resale, aggregation, and reuse by unknown third parties. Explicit opt-in fixes agency: the person must knowingly say yes.
[P1.5] Time limits fix durability: yesterday’s permission cannot become a lifelong surveillance asset.
[P1.6] Governments should draw a narrow line. This rule does not ban maps, emergency response, traffic counts, voluntary sharing, or genuinely aggregate statistics. It prohibits one dangerous transaction: a data broker selling precise individual trails unless the person explicitly opts in for a bounded purpose and period. If the data is valuable enough to support a market, asking first is a minimal cost.
[P1.7] Defenses of the status quo rest on convenience for buyers who profit from secrecy. That is thin against bodily safety, associative freedom, and control over intimate facts. A sale should not begin with an invisible opt-out; it should begin with consent, and end when that consent expires.
