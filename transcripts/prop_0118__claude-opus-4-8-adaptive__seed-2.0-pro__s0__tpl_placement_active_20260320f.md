[P1.1] The question before us is simple: when we feed children at school, do we feed all of them, or do we build a bureaucracy to decide which ones deserve it? I'll defend feeding all of them, and I'll win on three fronts: stigma, reach, and cost.
[P1.2] First, stigma. Means testing doesn't just sort children by income—it marks them in front of their peers. The kid who hands over a fee-waiver card, who stands in a different line, who is visibly "the poor one," carries that brand every lunch period. We know what happens: eligible children skip meals to avoid the shame.
[P1.3] A program that humiliates the very children it's meant to help is a program that fails them. Universality erases the mark entirely. Everyone eats the same lunch, no questions asked.
[P1.4] Second, reach. Targeting always leaks. The threshold is a cliff: a family one dollar over the cutoff loses everything, even though they're often the most squeezed—too "rich" for help, too poor to absorb the cost. And the hungriest children frequently fall through anyway, because their parents are the least able to navigate the paperwork, the renewals, the documentation.
[P1.5] Hunger doesn't check whether you filed the right form. Universal coverage is the only design that guarantees no child slips through the cracks of administrative failure.
[P1.6] Third, cost—and here's the counterintuitive truth my opponent must confront. Means testing is not free. You pay for eligibility verification, for staff to chase paperwork, for systems to catch fraud, for the line-by-line accounting of who owes what. Strip that machinery away and a large share of the "extra" cost of universality pays for itself.
[P1.7] We stop spending money sorting hungry children and spend it feeding them.
[P1.8] A fed child learns better, behaves better, and grows healthier. That's the whole point of a school. We don't means-test textbooks, the library, or the teacher. A meal is no less foundational to learning.
[P1.9] Feed every child, and let the school do its job.
