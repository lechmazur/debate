[P1.1] Historical acknowledgments without repair are a confession that stops at the treasurer's door. Universities that built early wealth through slavery, segregation, or dispossession still hold the proceeds in endowments, land, and prestige. Those proceeds compound. Plaques do not.
[P1.2] Slavery was unpaid labor, forced construction, and a pipeline of planter gifts. Segregation was an admissions and hiring regime that reserved tuition, faculty lines, alumni networks, and the university's brand for some families while locking others out by law and custom. Dispossession, including land-grant sales of Indigenous territory, converted other people's land into seed capital. None of that evaporated.
[P1.3] Buildings remain. Portfolios grow. Reputational advantage still opens doors for those already inside. A statement of regret leaves that machinery running.
[P1.4] "Sufficient" is the hinge. Harmed communities need a durable claim on education, credentials, jobs, and local economic power. Long-term scholarships are the fitting instrument because the original harm was exclusion from learning and mobility. They build pipelines across generations rather than a single ceremonial cohort that photographs well and disappears.
[P1.5] Community investment—neighborhood funds, school partnerships, clinics, local hiring—returns surplus to places the university drained, ignored, or walled off.
[P1.6] This is not a demand that current students confess a personal crime. It is asset accounting. Universities already honor centuries-old donor restrictions, named chairs, and legacy preference. They cannot suddenly decide that only the ugly capital is inert.
[P1.7] If wealth from a slave economy can still underwrite a professorship, it can underwrite a scholarship. If expropriated land can still host a quad, the university can fund the communities that land was taken from.
[P1.8] Stopping at acknowledgment treats extraction as sunk and memory as cheap. Repair says the opposite: keep the history honest, but do not keep the gain and outsource the grief. Words can name the theft. Only funded seats and long commitments begin to reverse it.

[C1.1] The case for the motion opens on a metaphor, not a balance sheet. “Those proceeds compound. Plaques do not.” [P1.1] But an endowment is not a vault of intact nineteenth-century coins. Money is fungible, restricted, and constantly spent and replenished. Later donors, tuition, and public grants built today’s balance sheet, often under binding terms.
[C1.2] To say the board “still holds the proceeds” is an accounting trick: it converts diffuse historical taint into a current asset and bills today’s students and donors as heirs to the crime.
[C1.3] That misdirection hides who pays. If the original gain cannot be isolated, redirecting a scholarship fund is not repayment; it is a retroactive assessment on people who never owned the founding capital. Boards are trustees, not tort courts. They cannot ignore donor restrictions or fiduciary duty because a modern committee finds the founding morally tainted.
[C1.4] The remedy is also badly targeted. “Harmed communities” are not this year’s applicants, and lineage is not need. A perpetual scholarship tied to historical identity can bypass a poor student whose family arrived after 1965, while rewarding someone with no tie to this campus’s specific wrong. That is not repair; it is genealogy plus a tuition discount.
[C1.5] Community investment sounds concrete, but universities are not city halls. They have no taxing power, no electoral mandate, and no democratic accountability to run clinics or neighborhood funds. Too often it becomes real-estate branding that raises local rents and feeds the prestige economy the motion claims to dismantle.
[C1.6] I do not deny ugly histories or the value of honest acknowledgment. The motion’s remedy is permanent lineage payments, and that remedy is wrong. A university that wants to confront inequality should expand need-based aid for the poor of every background, tell its history plainly, and support repair through democratic taxation—not appoint itself a reparations tribunal. Perpetual identity-based funds crowd out need-based aid and turn acknowledgment into a down payment on an endless claim.
