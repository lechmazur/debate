[P1.1] In 2007 a Russian submersible planted a titanium flag on the seabed beneath the North Pole. That gesture tells you everything about the status quo: three states—Russia, Canada, and Denmark via Greenland—all assert that the Lomonosov Ridge is a natural extension of their shelf, and all three submissions run straight across the same seabed. The body that reviews them cannot settle overlapping claims; it only issues recommendations, and then the claimants are told to work it out among themselves. "Work it out" currently means reopened Soviet bases, new icebreaker fleets, and NATO exercises above the Arctic Circle.
[P1.2] Competing claims do not produce order. They produce an arms race wearing a legal costume.
[P1.3] The Antarctic Treaty solved exactly this problem. Seven states had overlapping, hostile claims on a continent; rather than adjudicate them, the treaty froze them, demilitarized the territory, guaranteed scientific access, and later banned mining through its environmental protocol. Sixty-plus years of peace on a continent the Cold War could easily have carved up. No claimant surrendered sovereignty on paper.
[P1.4] They surrendered the ability to fight over it.
[P1.5] The Arctic needs that model more urgently than Antarctica ever did, because its stakes leak outward. Melting sea ice and thawing permafrost shift weather and sea levels on every coast. Opening shipping lanes raise questions of transit and spill liability that touch every trading nation. Fish stocks migrate north into waters nobody has ever regulated.
[P1.6] These are commons problems, and a commons cannot be governed by whoever drills fastest.
[P1.7] Nor is a binding treaty utopian. The Arctic states already signed a legally binding moratorium on commercial fishing in the central Arctic Ocean. The piece that's missing is a framework that freezes the shelf dispute, keeps weapons out of the high Arctic, and gives Indigenous peoples a seat the Arctic Council never backed with enforceable rules. The choice is a treaty now, or a flag on the seabed becoming a warship on the surface.

[C1.1] Antarctica is a continent with no citizens, surrounded by ocean. The Arctic is an ocean surrounded by countries, home to roughly four million people. That inversion sinks the proposition.
[C1.2] The Antarctic Treaty worked because it froze claims to land nobody lived on and nobody could then profitably exploit. Freezing was costless. In the Arctic there is almost nothing to freeze: the land is settled sovereign territory, and most oil, gas, and fish lie inside undisputed exclusive economic zones. An Antarctic-style regime either leaves all that untouched, accomplishing little, or asks Norway, Canada, and Russia to internationalize their own backyards, which none will sign.
[C1.3] Nor is the Arctic "left to competing claims." The Arctic Ocean is already under binding law: the law of the sea. The 2007 flag [P1.1] had zero legal effect; Russia then filed geological data with a UN commission. Russia and Norway settled their Barents Sea boundary by treaty.
[C1.4] Canada and Denmark split Hans Island by agreement. The overlapping Lomonosov submissions are a paperwork contest among scientists, the dullest arms race in history.
[C1.5] The real militarization has a different cause. Russia's Kola Peninsula hosts its submarine-based nuclear deterrent; NATO exercises respond to Ukraine, not to seabed sediment. No treaty demilitarizes a state's own coastline, and Antarctica never asked anyone to do that.
[C1.6] PRO's best evidence cuts our way. The fishing moratorium [P1.7] was negotiated under the current framework, alongside binding agreements on search and rescue, oil-spill response, and polar shipping standards. Targeted agreements work precisely because nobody must bargain away sovereignty.
[C1.7] And an Antarctic model gives any state running substantial research a vote, handing Beijing a say over Inuit homelands while diluting the Indigenous organizations already seated at the Arctic Council.
[C1.8] The Arctic has law. It needs maintenance, not a transplant from the wrong pole.
