[P1.1] For the first time in history, we can rewrite the genetic code to cure devastating conditions like sickle cell disease in a single treatment. Yet a cure people cannot afford is not a medicine; it is a ransom note. Today, gene-editing therapies enter the market at millions of dollars per patient. Without a public-interest licensing pathway to compel affordable access, these breakthroughs remain luxury goods while public health systems are held hostage.
[P1.2] First, this pathway is a fiscal and moral necessity. Monopolies price gene therapies not on production costs, but on the total lifetime expense of managing a fatal illness. When thousands of citizens need a multi-million-dollar therapy simultaneously, no health ministry can pay the sticker price without starving the rest of its healthcare budget. The inevitable result is cruel rationing.
[P1.3] Worse, the foundational science behind gene editing was heavily bankrolled by public universities and taxpayer grants. Citizens should not pay twice—once to fund the discovery, and again to bankrupt themselves buying it back.
[P1.4] Second, a dedicated pathway is essential because older patent workarounds are obsolete here. Traditional compulsory licensing works for simple chemical pills that any generic factory can copy. Gene editing relies on complex viral vectors and proprietary cellular manufacturing. A modern public-interest pathway gives governments leverage tailored to living medicines: if a firm refuses to offer a viable price, the state can compel licensing of the necessary platform to qualified hospitals or manufacturers, breaking the monopoly bottleneck.
[P1.5] Finally, this sustains real innovation. Compelling access is not confiscation; innovators receive guaranteed, reasonable royalties. Furthermore, the credible threat of a license brings companies to the negotiating table, shifting the market from a predatory low-volume model to a sustainable high-volume model. Rewarding companies based on how many people they actually cure aligns profit with public health.
[P1.6] When genetic cures exist, governments must ensure they reach human bodies, not just corporate balance sheets.

[C1.3] The scarce inputs are trained staff, manufacturing slots and hospital capacity. [C1.6] Third, the "pay twice" line in [P1.3] confuses discovery with development. Public grants funded the insight. Private risk capital funded the decade of trials and manufacturing that turned it into a treatment.
[C1.7] Affordability is a real problem with better tools: installment and outcomes-based payment, pooled purchasing, and public investment in treatment capacity. Each of these expands access without telling innovators that their work becomes state property once it succeeds.
