[P1.1] Nearshoring is not a market outcome. Supply chains are being redrawn by policy, not by prices. A region that refuses to use policy tools is not choosing neutrality; it is letting other governments decide where its factories go.
[P1.2] Capital does not chase the lowest tariff. It chases ecosystems: reliable electricity, a deep-water port, ten thousand trained technicians, permits cleared in months instead of years. No individual firm builds those, and no tariff cut conjures them. That is exactly what industrial policy and fiscal spending are for.
[P1.3] Costa Rica did not stumble into semiconductors and medical devices; it spent decades on technical education, investment promotion, and targeted incentives until the cluster became self-sustaining.
[P1.4] The window is narrow. Firms relocating out of Asia make location decisions once, and agglomeration locks them in for a generation. Monterrey's factories recruit Monterrey's suppliers. Every year spent defending fiscal purity is a year Vietnam or Poland signs the contract instead.

It is what the region already has: export baskets hostage to copper and soy prices, roughly half the workforce informal, productivity flat for decades. [P1.6] Protection here means scaffolding, not autarky: local-content rules tied to export performance, temporary tariff escalation, public procurement with sunset clauses. The test is whether an industry can eventually stand alone, and the spending is judged against the cost of never having one. A temporary distortion that buys an industrial base beats permanent dependence on the price of soy.
