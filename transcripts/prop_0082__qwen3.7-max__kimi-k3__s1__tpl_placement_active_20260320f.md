[P1.1] Dating apps face a fundamental conflict of interest: their business model relies on keeping users single and swiping, while their stated purpose is to help users find partners. Engagement-maximizing mechanics like infinite swiping and paid visibility boosts actively exploit this conflict. They are engineered using variable-ratio reinforcement—the exact psychological mechanism behind slot machines—to maximize time-on-app rather than facilitate successful connections.
[P1.2] When platforms prioritize raw engagement over match quality, the user experience severely degrades. Infinite swiping induces decision fatigue, causing users to treat potential partners as disposable commodities rather than human beings. Meanwhile, paid visibility boosts create a pay-to-win hierarchy. Organic compatibility is deliberately buried behind artificial scarcity and algorithmic paywalls, forcing users to pay just to be seen.
[P1.3] Consumers quickly realize the deck is stacked against them. This breeds profound cynicism and dating app burnout, destroying the baseline trust required for a matching platform to function. Once that trust evaporates, the ecosystem becomes a toxic environment of ghosting and superficial interactions.
[P1.4] We are not arguing for the abolition of dating apps or basic monetization. The proposition specifically mandates limiting these mechanics only when they undermine match quality and user trust. Just as we regulate casino mechanics to prevent predatory gambling, we must curb dark patterns in social matching. Capping daily swipes forces deliberate choice over mindless consumption, directly improving match quality by making users evaluate profiles thoughtfully.
[P1.5] Restricting paid boosts ensures visibility is driven by genuine compatibility rather than wallet size. By realigning the platform’s incentives with the user’s actual goal, we rescue the utility of dating apps and protect consumers from engineered addiction.
