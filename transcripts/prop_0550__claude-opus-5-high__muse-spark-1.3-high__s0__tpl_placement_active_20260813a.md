[P1.1] The question is not "is a bachelor's degree valuable?" It obviously can be. The question is what an extra dollar of public money buys **at the margin** — and that's where the proposition wins.
[P1.2] Start with who a college subsidy actually recruits. It doesn't recruit the future engineer who was always going anyway; it recruits the marginal student, the one who was genuinely on the fence. That's precisely the student most likely to leave with two years of debt, no credential, and no wage premium — because in the labor market the degree, not the coursework, is what pays. Roughly two in five American students who start a bachelor's never finish it, and the non-completers are concentrated exactly where subsidies push enrollment.
[P1.3] Subsidizing entry into a system with a leaky exit buys a lot of debt and not much earnings.
[P1.4] Second, follow the money. Tuition subsidies flow to institutions that face little competitive pressure to hold prices down, so a meaningful share is absorbed by list prices and administrative growth rather than delivered to students. Apprenticeship dollars go somewhere very different: into a wage, in a job, with an employer who is co-investing their own money and their own supervisors' time. Employer co-payment is a quality screen that no tuition voucher can replicate — a firm training a millwright or a medical technician is betting its own capital that the skill is worth something.
[P1.5] Third, the earnings arithmetic. An apprentice earns from month one instead of forgoing four years of wages, accumulates seniority and employer-specific capital early, and compounds from a higher base. And the employer link solves the problem that actually strangles young workers' earnings: matching. The Swiss and German systems aren't admired because their classrooms are better; they're admired because a young person exits training already inside a firm that knows their work.
[P1.6] A college subsidy hands a teenager a ticket and hopes. An apprenticeship hands them a paycheck, a mentor, and an employer with skin in the game. Over forty years, that difference compounds.

[C1.1] Paychecks at eighteen are seductive. They are also misleading when the question is lifetime earnings.
[C1.2] Apprenticeships win the sprint and lose the marathon. An eighteen-year-old earning while peers sit in lecture halls is ahead at twenty-two. By thirty-five the lines cross and then diverge for decades. That is because college does not just add a first job; it changes the slope of wage growth.
[C1.3] Degree-holders are promoted into management, licensed professions, and graduate school tracks where earnings keep climbing. Most skilled-trade and technician pathways pay respectably early and then flatten. Over forty years, a steeper curve beats an earlier start, even after subtracting tuition and forgone wages.
[C1.4] Second, generality beats specificity over a working life that will be disrupted repeatedly. An employer training a worker trains for its own vacancy: a particular machine, software, protocol. That firm-specific capital is valuable inside that firm and perishable outside it, which weakens bargaining power and traps workers when plants close or technologies shift. A bachelor's degree is portable human capital — writing, quantitative reasoning, credential recognition across states and industries — that lets workers switch occupations, cities, and employers without starting over.
[C1.5] In an economy where entire tasks disappear, adaptability is earnings insurance.
[C1.6] Third, employer skin in the game is not a quality guarantee for workers; it is a sign employers expect to profit. Firms expand training when they can pay trainees below productivity and retain them below market afterward. Expand apprenticeships at scale beyond elite manufacturing and health systems, and the marginal slot is not a Swiss precision apprenticeship but a low-wage service placement relabeled as training, with little instruction and no premium.
[C1.7] College subsidies, by contrast, do not merely push fence-sitters into debt. At the margin they let students work fewer hours, avoid dropout for a car repair, choose a better-fit school, and finish — raising completion, not just enrollment.
