[P1.1] A scooter hits a seventy-year-old woman on a sidewalk in a zone the company itself painted red on its own map. The rider is a tourist who flew home the next day, uninsured, effectively unfindable. The company's server logged the ride: exact location, exact speed, exact violation, in real time. It did nothing.
[P1.2] Today, the person who never downloaded the app, never clicked "I agree," and never chose to be near the machine is the one left holding the hospital bill. That is the arrangement CON has to defend.
[P1.3] The case for shifting more responsibility onto the companies rests on one principle that liability law has always used: place the cost on whoever controls the risk most cheaply and profits from it. That is the company, on every count. It designs the vehicle. It sets the top speed.
[P1.4] It owns the GPS and geofencing that can slow a scooter to a crawl the moment it enters a restricted zone. It chooses per-minute pricing that quietly rewards shortcuts across sidewalks. It decides how many devices to dump into a district with no protected lanes. Sidewalk riding is not a freak event; it is the predictable, statistically certain output of a business model, and the companies know it before the first rider unlocks a wheel.
[P1.5] "More" does not mean "all." Riders who behave recklessly should remain answerable. But an accountability regime that depends on locating and suing an anonymous stranger is, for most pedestrians, no regime at all. Mandatory injury coverage, a presumption of shared fault when a company's own data shows a geofence violation it failed to enforce, and duties to actually activate the safety tools they advertise—these make the harm land where it can be prevented.
[P1.6] The real test is simple: under the status quo, who is worse off, the company that keeps the ride fee, or the pedestrian who keeps the injury? Whoever chooses to profit from putting fast motors on crowded pavement should carry the risk they create.

PRO's principle assigns fault to the party that committed no wrongful act, because that party is reachable. That is not accountability; it is a search for a solvent defendant.
[C1.2] Legal responsibility deters best when it lands on whoever controls the moment of harm. The company designs the vehicle; the rider operates it. Mounting a crowded sidewalk at fifteen miles an hour is a decision made at the handlebars by someone who can comply with the law at zero cost. That makes the rider the cheapest cost-avoider.
[C1.3] Liability shifted to the company is deterrence removed from the only person whose choices actually cause these injuries.
[C1.4] Nor is the technology premise what PRO assumes. Consumer GPS in dense cities is routinely inaccurate by several meters—often unable to distinguish a sidewalk from the bike lane beside it. Geofences acting on that signal would brake riders mid-street, create new hazards, and still miss much sidewalk riding. The "they control the risk" claim collapses on inspection.
[C1.5] The law already recognizes this relationship: it is a rental. We do not sue the car-hire firm when a renter drives onto a curb, or a bike shop when a customer rides through a pedestrian plaza, though both profit and both could fit governors. Profit from enabling an activity has never meant fault for its misuse.
[C1.6] PRO's genuine grievance is a compensation gap: uninsured, unfindable riders. But fault and compensation are separable. A mandatory injury surcharge, priced into every fare and administered in the app, pays the victim's bills without corrupting fault doctrine or inflating costs until fleets shrink. Shared micromobility replaces car trips; strangle it with misallocated liability and cities lose cleaner air, less congestion, and access for riders without cars.
[C1.7] Hold the rider answerable. Fund compensation honestly. Do not confuse the deepest pocket with the wrongdoer.
