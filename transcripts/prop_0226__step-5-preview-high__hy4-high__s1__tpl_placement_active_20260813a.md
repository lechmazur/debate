[P1.1] People are not waiting for a treaty to become climate refugees. In low-lying deltas and scorching equatorial cities, families are already leaving because the land is salinized, the water is undrinkable, or the heat makes outdoor labor lethal. They do not fit the 1951 Refugee Convention’s narrow persecution frame, so they migrate informally, without protection, into debt, trafficking networks, and shadowed settlements. That legal gap is not neutral; it is a policy choice that produces statelessness in slow motion.
[P1.2] The proposition asks for a limited legal right—not open borders, but a targeted status for people displaced by sea-level rise and extreme heat whose home regions cannot receive them safely. It is bounded by design: it applies to specific, documentable drivers, can be managed through annual processing caps, and ends when return becomes genuinely possible. Without it, receiving states treat identical harms differently, creating arbitrary luck that depends on whether a border agent labels the journey economic or environmental. This is not a loophole; it is a floor.
[P1.3] Opponents will say this is too open-ended. But the alternative is worse: ad hoc deportation of people to uninhabitable zones, violating the same non-refoulement principles that already bar returning anyone to torture or arbitrary execution. Heat and submersion are persecutors without passports; the harm is just as irreversible.
[P1.4] Climate displacement also tracks responsibility. Industrialized nations built their prosperity on emissions now making parts of the tropics uninhabitable. Refusing even a narrow legal channel is not caution; it is exporting the costs of consumption onto those least able to bear them.
[P1.5] Recognizing a limited right is not charity. It is the minimum correction to a legal system that protects the persecuted but abandons the submerged. It costs less than chaotic flows, prevents foreseeable humanitarian disasters, and upholds the most basic test of any asylum system: never send someone back to die.

They are diffuse pressures filtered through drainage, housing, labor rules, savings, health care, and governance.
[C1.2] Two households on the same coast face different risks; inland workers may suffer the same heat. A court can identify persecution; it cannot reliably decide that "climate," rather than poverty, planning, or state failure, made one home unlivable.
[C1.3] That is why PRO's "bounded by design" status [P1.2] is unstable. A capped right is not a right for the person beyond the cap: the law says her home is too hot, then removes her because a quota expired. An uncapped right is not limited. Officials must still measure salinity, heat days, causal share, papers, and "genuine" possibility of return.
[C1.4] The promised floor becomes litigation and border discretion—the same arbitrariness PRO says it is abolishing.
[C1.5] The status also selects the wrong beneficiaries. It rewards people who can reach a border, gather papers, and narrate harm in the receiving state's legal vocabulary. The trapped resident, undocumented elder, disabled person, or farmer hit by overlapping drought is less likely to qualify. A porous new asylum category also consumes adjudication and public consent from refugees fleeing militias, prisons, or persecution, where the trigger is clearer.
[C1.6] Historical emissions create duties, but [P1.4] mistakes the remedy. Responsibility is owed to exposed communities, not to whichever individuals prove a climate story to an immigration officer. It is better discharged through adaptation finance, planned relocation, heat-safe infrastructure, temporary disaster protection, and regional mobility deals made before collapse. Those tools cover whole at-risk populations without pretending a border interview can solve global causation.
[C1.7] A right indeterminate at the threshold, arbitrary at the cap, and weakest for the most trapped is not a floor; it is an unkeepable promise.

[P4.1] A1: The rule is objective displacement, not a border agent’s guess about motive. A claimant must show residence in a defined climate-impacted zone, loss of safe habitation there, and that sea-level rise or lethal heat—not ordinary rent, wages, or eviction—made that specific place uninhabitable. A tenant priced out of a coastal city has no claim; a fisher whose home sits below the tidal flood line does. That is the same kind of mixed-cause inquiry courts already perform, with salinity maps and temperature records supplying the anchor.
[P4.2] A2: A cap controls the rate of admission; it cannot delete protection. A qualifying applicant beyond the annual quota receives enforceable temporary non-refoulement admission while awaiting placement—never removal back to the uninhabitable zone—and the quota creates a numbered, reviewable queue. For the trapped family, the right must include designated offshore or community processing channels and proxy eligibility for those who cannot afford flight, so protection does not begin only after a mobile relative crosses a border. A scheduled right is still a right; CON’s alternative is discretionary charity.
[P4.3] The rest of the case still turns on [C2.3]: if non-refoulement already bars return to lethal heat, climate is a legally relevant cause and PRO’s threshold merely implements that duty. Their causation and cap objections are design problems we have answered, while their finance-and-relocation alternative depends on the same border discretion and host-state goodwill they claim to fear. PRO converts accidental, unequal border luck into a bounded, enforceable rule.
