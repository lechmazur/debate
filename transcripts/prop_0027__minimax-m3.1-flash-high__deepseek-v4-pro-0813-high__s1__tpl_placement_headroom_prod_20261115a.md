[P1.1] The decisive point is that the invasion did not create Iraq's instability; it removed the pillar holding the region in place. In 2003 the Middle East's central security structure was a balance of power: a weakened but secular Arab-nationalist Iraq restraining Iran, and a Shia-majority country governed by a Sunni minority, which kept Iraq out of Tehran's orbit. Remove Iraq from that architecture and Iran gained a windfall it could not have bought any other way: a friendly government in Baghdad, license to arm Hezbollah and Damascus, and a nuclear program insulated from the pressure Tehran faced before the war.
[P1.2] Second, the occupation gutted state capacity faster than it rebuilt it: the army dissolved, the Ba'ath party was banned, and a mass of armed, unemployed Sunni men became the recruiting pool for the sectarian civil war that peaked in 2006 and 2007. The harvest came due in 2014, when ISIS took Mosul, a provincial capital with a garrison of tens of thousands, within days.
[P1.3] Third, the war regionalized what might have stayed bilateral: Iraq became a sanctuary and staging ground for jihadists, and the sectarian arithmetic it created spread into Lebanon, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, and later Syria and Yemen.
[P1.4] Saddam was brutal, decaying, and, by 2003, far less capable than at his peak; but brutality and instability are not the same thing. A tyrannical state can be a stable pillar; a destroyed one leaves no successor to replace it.
[P1.5] On the counterfactual, compare with the region as it stood in the decade before 2003: no Iraqi civil war, no sectarian implosion in Baghdad, no Syrian civil war, no Yemeni war, no ISIS caliphate. The instability that followed 2003 was not the background condition against which we measure the invasion. It was the consequence of it.
