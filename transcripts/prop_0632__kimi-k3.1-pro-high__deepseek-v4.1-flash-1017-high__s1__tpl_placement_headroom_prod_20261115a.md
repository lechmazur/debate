[P1.1] Obesity is not a failure of character that insurers may price as a luxury; it is a chronic, relapsing disease that drives diabetes, cardiovascular illness, sleep apnea, joint damage, and lost productivity. When an FDA-authorized medication is clinically indicated, coverage should follow medical need, not a patient's ability to pay thousands of dollars out of pocket. The motion therefore establishes a fair default while attaching a financial brake: value-based pricing and outcomes-based contracts.
[P1.2] The safety objection mistakes the proposal for an unrestricted drug benefit. It is neither. FDA authorization screens products for use within approved populations, clinicians determine indication, and coverage can require appropriate assessment, monitoring, and discontinuation when treatment is ineffective or unsafe. That is disciplined medicine.
[P1.3] The alternative is worse: patients cycle through complication-driven care while effective treatment remains available mainly to affluent patients, or seek unreliable compounded and gray-market substitutes when legitimate access is blocked.
[P1.4] The cost objection also looks at only one side of the ledger. A drug's price is visible; repeated hospitalizations, diabetes management, disability, and avoidable procedures are dispersed across years and budgets. Insurers should not be permitted to call that fragmentation "savings." Outcomes-based contracts directly address the uncertainty: payment can be tied to agreed clinical results, persistence, and real-world performance, with rebates or price adjustments when promised value does not materialize.
[P1.5] Manufacturers, not patients alone, then share the risk.
[P1.6] CON must defend a strange principle: that an insurer may cover the downstream consequences of obesity indefinitely while categorically refusing clinician-indicated treatment upstream. We reject blanket mandates for ineffective products just as firmly as CON does; that is why authorization, clinical indication, and value conditions matter.
[P1.7] But excluding an entire class regardless of individual benefit is not cost control. It is cost shifting. Require coverage, demand measurable value, and make payment follow results.

[C1.1] PRO's case rests on a seductive equation: FDA-authorized plus clinically indicated equals coverage owed. That equation fails because FDA authorization answers whether a drug may be marketed, not whether every insurance pool should finance it at any price. "Clinically indicated" is not a narrow gate; obesity diagnosis and treatment thresholds are broad, contested, and readily stretched, especially when a manufacturer has every incentive to widen the label and the mandate guarantees payment.
[C1.2] The supposed brake—value-based pricing and outcomes-based contracts—does not solve that. A mandate first creates an entitlement; the contract haggles later over rebates and metrics. Insurers lose the one credible threat that controls launch prices: refusal to cover. Outcomes contracts are also administratively fragile: weight loss is easy to measure, but sustained cardiovascular benefit, adherence, and long-term cost offsets are not.
[C1.3] Patients who stop therapy—and many do—still leave the plan paying for initiation, monitoring, and switching. A rebate for nonresponse is not a budget cap.
[C1.4] The cost does not vanish; it is spread across premiums. A small employer facing a double-digit renewal must cut somewhere: higher deductibles, narrower networks, or less generous coverage for other conditions. That is not abstract. It means a patient with cancer or rheumatoid arthritis subsidizes a mandate for a chronic product whose real-world value remains uncertain.
[C1.5] And the equity argument cuts against PRO. Insurance is a pooling arrangement, not a guarantee that every authorized product is covered. A mandate makes coverage regressive: everyone pays more so that manufacturers secure a protected market, while the well-off still access the drug and the poor absorb higher premiums.
[C1.6] CON's principle is simple: cover what the pool can justify, negotiate hard, and target scarce dollars to demonstrated value—not legislate an entire drug class into every benefit package before the evidence and the budget are secure.
