[C1.1] The proposition rests on a false binary and a shaky scientific category. "Ultra-processed food" is not a settled classification—it lumps nutrient-dense yogurt and fortified cereals together with soda and candy. Slapping warning labels on a loaf of whole-grain bread because it contains emulsifiers is not public health; it is regulatory overreach that stigmatizes foods millions consume safely as part of balanced diets.
[C1.2] The efficacy evidence is weaker than claimed. Chile’s 24% drop in sugary drink purchases coincided with a soda tax and aggressive industry reformulation—not merely black octagon stickers. Tobacco warnings work because smoking is unambiguously lethal in any dose. Food is categorically different: dose, context, and overall dietary pattern matter.
[C1.3] A skull icon cannot capture that nuance, and consumers desensitize to warning labels within months, rendering them background noise on crowded packaging.
[C1.4] Consumer choice campaigns are not the weak alternative being caricatured. Well-funded nutrition education, clear front-of-pack traffic-light labeling, and sugar taxes give people tools without infantilizing them. [C1.5] Mandatory labels also invite unintended consequences: black-market appeal for "forbidden" snacks among teenagers, or avoidance of fortified foods that carry warnings despite their nutritional value. Regulation should be proportionate. If the goal is reducing diet-related harm, governments should fund robust education and fiscal measures that demonstrably shift behavior, rather than plastering fear-based stickers on every package and calling it consumer empowerment.
