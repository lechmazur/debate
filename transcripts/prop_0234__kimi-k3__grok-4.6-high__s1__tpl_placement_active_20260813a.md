[P1.1] A labor market cannot work when one side negotiates blind. In nearly every other consequential transaction—securities, mortgages, food labels—we treat disclosure as the precondition of fair dealing. Yet in the most important contract most people ever sign, their job, the employer holds every card: the pay range, what predecessors earned, who advances and how fast. This motion corrects that asymmetry, only for large employers, using aggregate data—bands and rates, not individual salaries.
[P1.2] First, transparency is the only workable enforcement mechanism against pay discrimination. A woman paid less than the man at the next desk cannot challenge a gap she cannot see. Lilly Ledbetter spent nearly two decades before learning she earned less than every man in her role; courts then said she was too late. Secrecy is what lets discrimination compound quietly.
[P1.3] Publish bands and promotion rates, and unjustifiable gaps either get fixed before exposure or become visible enough to act on.
[P1.4] Second, disclosure disciplines the market, not just the courtroom. Employers who know ranges will be public rationalize their pay structures in advance—broadly what we've seen as Colorado and New York began requiring ranges in job postings: wide compliance, minimal disruption, faster matching between workers and firms that pay fairly. Promotion rates matter because the deepest inequality isn't starting pay; it's who climbs. Aggregate rates expose the bottleneck without naming anyone.
[P1.5] Now the conceded cost: managerial flexibility. Ask what that flexibility actually consists of. It is not the power to reward performance—bands are ranges, and firms keep level placement, bonuses, and promotion timing. What disappears is the freedom to pay two people doing the same job differently without ever explaining why.
[P1.6] That is not a management tool; it is the absence of accountability.
[P1.7] The burden falls on firms with HR departments and compensation consultants; the benefit flows to millions negotiating in the dark. Sunlight doesn't distort this market. It finally creates one.

[C1.1] The proposition treats pay as if it were a canned good with a nutrition label. It is not. Compensation is a live instrument for matching scarce talent to changing work, retaining people who would otherwise leave, and paying more when a person or a market suddenly becomes more valuable. Forcing large employers to publish pay bands and promotion rates by role freezes that instrument into a public scoreboard.
[C1.2] The cost is not a manager’s bruised ego. It is worse matching, flatter rewards, and less mobility for the workers the mandate claims to help.
[C1.3] Labor markets already transmit information through quitting, competing offers, recruiter networks, and voluntary postings. What this rule adds is compulsory, coarse, lagging data that litigants will treat as proof and managers will treat as a cage. Once a band is public, paying above it looks like a confession and paying below it looks like a lawsuit. The rational response is to compress everyone toward the middle, delay promotions until the published rate looks respectable, and stop making exceptions for high performers or hard-to-fill jobs.
[C1.4] That is not accountability. That is wage flattening sold as fairness.
[C1.5] Promotion rates by role are worse still. Roles are not laboratory categories. People sit in hybrid jobs, change titles mid-year, take stretch assignments, or get held for reasons that have nothing to do with bias: a failed project, a team that needed continuity, a downturn. Publishing rates invites firms to game titles, split roles, or stall promotions so the dashboard looks clean.
[C1.6] Discrimination law already exists. This mandate converts imperfect judgment into a public statistic that will be misread as evidence.
[C1.7] Ranges in job ads are not the same as ongoing public reporting of internal mobility. Large firms will comply on paper. What disappears is the ability to meet a competing offer, hire a career-changer into a band that looks odd on a spreadsheet, or promote faster than last year’s average. Workers lose that flexibility too.
[C1.8] Sunlight does not automatically create a better market. It can freeze a worse one in place.
