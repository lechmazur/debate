[P1.1] Combined sewer overflows are not a maintenance inconvenience — they are a design guarantee that raw sewage will periodically flow into rivers, lakes, and basements. Every combined system, by definition, is engineered to overflow whenever rainfall exceeds a threshold. That means the "continued overflow management" option CON will defend is not a stable, cheaper alternative — it's a permanent commitment to recurring contamination events, growing in frequency as storms intensify with climate change. The choice isn't cheap-forever versus expensive-once.
[P1.2] It's pay-now-and-fix-it versus pay-forever-and-never-fix-it.
[P1.3] Separated systems solve the actual problem at its root: stormwater and sewage stop competing for the same pipe. Overflow management — retention tunnels, real-time control, green infrastructure retrofits — only manages the symptom, and it has to be re-funded, re-permitted, and re-built every time capacity is exceeded or regulations tighten. Cities that pour billions into overflow controls, like the deep-tunnel projects several major U.S. cities have built, still overflow in the worst storms, because the underlying pipe is still combined.
[P1.4] Separation removes that failure mode permanently.
[P1.5] The cost comparison CON will lean on is also badly framed. "Upfront cost much higher" ignores that overflow management is not a one-time cost either — it's decades of monitoring, consent-decree compliance, tunnel maintenance, and periodic capacity expansion as cities grow and storms worsen. Stack those recurring costs across 50 or 100 years and separated infrastructure, built once with a design life measured in generations, is the fiscally serious choice, not the reckless one.
[P1.6] There's also a resilience argument CON cannot escape: separated systems decouple flood risk from public health risk. When a combined system floods, it doesn't just flood — it poisons. A separated system's stormwater side can overflow without dumping sewage into a park or a basement. As extreme rainfall becomes more common, that distinction is not cosmetic; it is the whole point of building the right infrastructure instead of patching the wrong one indefinitely.
