[P1.1] Emotion-recognition systems in the workplace are digital physiognomy: they claim to infer internal feelings and job fitness from facial movements, vocal pitch, and gaze. Just as governments restrict workplace polygraphs because machines cannot reliably read minds, countries should enact a bright-line prohibition on algorithmic emotion scanning in hiring and worker evaluation.
[P1.2] First, these tools rest on a scientifically bankrupt premise that punishes human difference. Emotion has no universal biometric fingerprint. A furrowed brow or flat tone can signal hostility, or simply deep concentration, interview nerves, or culturally reserved speech. By encoding a narrow baseline of "normal" expression, these systems turn human variation into defect.
[P1.3] An autistic engineer who avoids camera eye contact, an applicant with a speech impediment, or a worker with facial paralysis gets flagged as disengaged or untrustworthy. That is automated discrimination disguised as objective math.
[P1.4] Second, even if this technology worked flawlessly tomorrow, that would make prohibition more urgent, not less. Employment is a contract for labor and professional conduct, not a surrender of mental privacy. If a customer service agent resolves an angry caller's problem politely and accurately, they have done their job, even if a microphone detects private frustration in their voice. Even when marketed as "coaching" or "wellbeing," tying emotion data to worker evaluation turns support into surveillance, forcing employees to fake cheerfulness and hide stress to protect their paychecks.
[P1.5] Finally, soft regulation cannot salvage a fundamentally illegitimate tool. Audits cannot make facial twitches a valid measure of competence, and "worker consent" is fiction when refusing an algorithm's scan costs you the interview or promotion. Where bargaining power is lopsided and technology intrudes where bosses have no right to look, guardrails fail. Countries should require employers to judge people by their skills and actions, and prohibit machines that police their feelings.

[C1.1] The motion should be rejected because it is a categorical ban aimed at a diverse set of tools, not a precise remedy for genuine harms. Employers already observe facial expression, tone, and speech in interviews and evaluations. An algorithm that standardizes those observations does not create a new privacy wrong; it may make an existing informal judgment more consistent and auditable. If the problem is bias, the answer is to regulate validity, transparency, and human review, not to forbid the entire category.
[C1.2] PRO’s examples reinforce this. An autistic applicant, a worker with facial paralysis, or a candidate with a speech impediment may be harmed by a badly designed tool. But those harms are already actionable under disability and anti-discrimination law, and they are correctable with validation, accommodations, and human override. A ban is not needed to stop the bad version; it simply also blocks the lawful versions.
[C1.3] Some emotion-related signals are job-relevant. A crisis hotline assessing whether a candidate can stay composed with abusive callers, a hospital evaluating de-escalation skills, or a transport operator using fatigue detection in safety-critical roles is not reading private minds; it is measuring conduct that affects performance and safety. Prohibition would force employers to rely on unstructured human impressions of “attitude” and “fit” instead.
[C1.4] PRO says soft regulation cannot work, but that is an assertion, not an argument. Governments already apply context-specific limits to polygraphs, medical inquiries, and background checks. They can require that emotion scores not be the sole basis for hiring or firing, that systems be validated for the specific job, and that workers know when they are used. A blanket prohibition sacrifices useful applications, chills innovation, and does not target the actual harm.
