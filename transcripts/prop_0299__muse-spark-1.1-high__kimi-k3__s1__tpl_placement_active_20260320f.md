[P1.1] Cultural memory is infrastructure. A community that loses its language loses its law, its medicine, its jokes, its map of the land. When that disappears, it does not come back. Treating that kind of loss as a hobby for philanthropy to maybe save is a category error.
[P1.2] Private charity cannot do this work because it is not designed to. Endangered languages and oral histories are classic public goods: irreplaceable, non-profitable, and most valuable to people with the least money. Foundations fund what is photogenic, new, and measurable this year. A language needs twenty years of toddlers being spoken to, elders being recorded with care, apprenticeships, dictionaries built word by word.
[P1.3] That requires patient, boring, continuous money. Only a standing public fund provides it.
[P1.4] There is also a debt. Most of these endangerments are not natural accidents. They are the direct result of state action: boarding schools that beat children for speaking their mother tongue, bans on ceremonies, urban removal that scattered archives. The state broke the transmission chain; it cannot shrug and tell the victims to start a GoFundMe.
[P1.5] A standing fund solves the trust problem that one-off grants create. Native speakers have seen researchers parachute in, take stories, and leave when funding ends. A permanent office with community governance — paying fluent elders as teachers, funding local archive rooms, digitizing tapes on community terms — turns preservation from extraction into self-determination.
[P1.6] We already fund memory. We fund presidential libraries and battlefield monuments because we understand they teach citizenship. A public endowment for minority languages and community archives simply says everyone's memory counts as part of the country, and it pays local people to do expert work their communities need at a cost that is a rounding error in a national budget. The loss if we leave it to charity is permanent.
[P1.7] That is what governments are for.

[C1.1] Start with the fact PRO skips: you cannot buy native speakers. A language lives at the kitchen table, in a decision a parent makes ten thousand times about which words to use with a child. No standing fund reaches that table. The famous revivals — Hebrew, Welsh — ran on mass populations, compulsory schooling, and state direction of daily life, not grant cycles.
[C1.2] Where thirty elderly speakers remain, public money produces excellent recordings of the last conversations: an archive of a death, not a rescue. And archiving is precisely the part universities, libraries, and volunteer networks already do well and cheaply.
[C1.3] Second, state custody changes what it touches. To spend public money a fund must decide which dialect is standard, which history is authentic, which archive is legitimate. Committees canonize; living culture is plural and mutates. The moment a ministry funds one version, the others begin dying faster.
[C1.4] Note the irony inside PRO's own case: the state broke these transmission chains. The remedy for state suppression is not state custodianship — the institution that banned the ceremony should not get to decide which recording of it becomes official.
[C1.5] Third, "standing" is the trap. Permanent funds answer to application forms, not need. Money flows to communities with grant writers and institutional partners, not to the elder with forty years of memory and no internet. A standing fund builds a preservation industry whose budget depends on endangerment continuing; none has ever declared its language saved and dissolved itself.
[C1.6] Finally, the dichotomy is false. Memory survives today in community schools, diaspora networks, church halls, open-source dictionaries, and millions of hours of volunteer labor — accountable to speakers, not ministries. Māori language nests began as community defiance; the state followed. Preservation is a practice, not a project.
[C1.7] Governments should stop suppressing, teach history honestly, and keep public archives open. What they cannot do is purchase intergenerational love for a mother tongue. This fund promises to — and that promise is the most expensive part of the failure.
