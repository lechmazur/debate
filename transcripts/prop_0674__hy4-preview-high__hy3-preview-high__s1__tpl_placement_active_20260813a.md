[P1.1] The choice here is not between peace and war; it is between two theories of deterrence. An immediate negotiated settlement tries to stop today's fighting by conceding something of value. Sustained military aid tries to prevent tomorrow's wider war by ensuring Russian conquest fails. Deterrence rests on two things: the capability to impose unbearable costs and the credibility to sustain them.
[P1.2] A settlement that freezes Russian gains while Russia still occupies Ukrainian land strips away both, because it teaches Moscow that brutality plus patience delivers concessions.
[P1.3] The capability argument is straightforward. Russia's ability to threaten wider aggression—against Moldova, Georgia, the Baltic states, or NATO's eastern flank—depends on offensive mass: armor, artillery, missile stocks, and the belief that offensives are quick and cheap. Sustained Western military aid has attrited that power and forced Russia to expend older stockpiles. A weakened Russia is less able to open new fronts.
[P1.4] At the same time, aid turns Ukraine into a heavily armed, combat-hardened barrier. That is not charity; it is forward defense outside NATO, paid largely in Ukrainian resistance and Western stockpiles.
[P1.5] The credibility argument matters more. Moscow is not only watching Ukraine; it is watching how the West reacts. If the West pushes Kyiv to talks now, before Russia abandons maximal aims, future aggressors learn that seizing land and threatening escalation eventually brings relief. Wider aggression becomes more likely because the expected return on violence rises.
[P1.6] Sustained aid sends the opposite signal: borders cannot be changed by force, and democracies can outlast coercion.
[P1.7] Critics warn aid risks escalation. Calibrated aid can be managed; rewarding conquest cannot. The alternative is worse. A settlement that rewards invasion leaves a rearming, aggrieved Russia on new borders, probing Europe's weakest points later, under conditions more favorable to Moscow.
[P1.8] Deterrence is not bought by pause; it is bought by proving aggression cannot win. We prevent a wider war by ensuring Russia's wider war has already failed in Ukraine.

[P1.3] ignores that degradation increases incentive for irregular or surrogate strikes against NATO logistics or neighbors to sever support.
[C1.2] Aid does not remove offensive mass; it redirects it toward wider, cheaper escalation.
[C1.3] The credibility argument in [P1.5] is backwards. Pushing Kyiv to talks before Russia “abandons maximal aims” is not a reward for conquest if the settlement is paired with firm external security guarantees and a monitored freeze. Conversely, sustained aid that aims to make Russia’s war “already failed” signals to Moscow that the West intends unconditional subjugation. That perception raises the expected return on preemptive wider aggression: if the regime believes it will be crushed regardless, it may strike first across borders to rally domestic unity and fracture Western resolve.
[C1.4] An immediate negotiated settlement, even one that temporarily concedes occupied terrain, shrinks the uncertainty that fuels miscalculation. A frozen line with international observers and credible penalties for violation denies Russia a moving target and denies extremists in Moscow the narrative of an encroaching existential threat. Compare the prolonged proxy bleeding to a monitored armistice: the latter lowers the daily pressure to escalate.
[C1.5] The determinative point is risk probability. Sustained aid extends a high-intensity war indefinitely, multiplying the chances of a spiral—accidental strike, misread red line, proxy attack—that drags in neighboring states. A negotiated settlement caps that probability. Therefore, sustained aid is not more likely to deter wider aggression; it is more likely to provoke it.
