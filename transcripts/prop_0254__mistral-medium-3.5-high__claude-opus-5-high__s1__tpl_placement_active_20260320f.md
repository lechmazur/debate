[P1.1] GDP growth counts hurricane cleanup as economic gain but ignores the loss of a forest. It values a commuter’s wasted hours in traffic but not a parent’s time with a child. This is not a minor quirk—it is a structural blind spot: GDP measures production, not welfare. It treats inequality, pollution, and unpaid care as irrelevant, or worse, as acceptable costs for higher output.
[P1.2] For instance, GDP rises when a society spends more on healthcare to treat preventable diseases caused by pollution, even as overall health declines.
[P1.3] National well-being, by contrast, directly targets the conditions that make life good: health, education, environmental quality, strong communities, and meaningful work. It accounts for the full spectrum of what constitutes a good life, not just what can be monetized. The shift in target changes incentives fundamentally. Policies that once pursued growth at any cost—overwork, environmental degradation, and widening inequality—become unacceptable when the metric demands broad human flourishing.
[P1.4] New Zealand’s Wellbeing Budget exemplifies this: by making mental health, child poverty, and domestic violence priority areas, it delivered measurable improvements in citizens’ lives without harming economic performance. Bhutan’s Gross National Happiness framework has similarly guided policies that preserve forests, reduce inequality, and foster cultural resilience. International bodies like the OECD now advocate well-being frameworks, recognizing the limitations of GDP.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, countries fixated on GDP have often tolerated ecological destruction, social fragmentation, and the erosion of public health in the name of a number that fails to capture true progress. Well-being metrics force policymakers to confront these trade-offs. They reveal that high GDP and low well-being can coexist, as seen in nations where economic growth has not translated into longer, healthier, or happier lives. Conversely, countries like Costa Rica demonstrate that prioritizing health, education, and environmental sustainability can yield high life satisfaction with modest GDP.
[P1.6] This shows that prosperity is not solely a function of output, but of how that output is used to enhance lives. The choice is not between growth and well-being, but between growth that serves people and growth that consumes them. [clipped]

[C1.1] GDP growth is not a claim about the good life. It is a headline *target*: a single, hard, externally audited number that tells voters whether a government has expanded the resources available to society. Well-being is the goal. Growth is the lever.
[C1.2] The motion confuses the two, and that confusion is not harmless.
[C1.3] Start with accountability. GDP is compiled under international statistical standards, revised by independent agencies, and comparable across borders. A national well-being index is a weighted average of things a government chooses to weight. Whoever picks the weights picks the score.
[C1.4] Put that number on the front page and you have handed every incumbent a dial: fold in community trust when housing collapses, emphasise leisure when unemployment rises. Nobody can fake a recession. Anybody can rebalance a dashboard.
[C1.5] Second, subjective well-being is precisely the wrong headline instrument because it barely moves. Human adaptation flattens life-satisfaction scores; they drift within narrow bands while real incomes swing violently. Britain after 2008 endured the worst wage stagnation in modern memory, and reported life satisfaction was broadly stable. Under this motion, that lost decade registers as a policy success.
[C1.6] GDP growth is a demanding target exactly because it is unforgiving.
[C1.7] Third, the resources. Mental health services, child poverty payments, insulated housing, decarbonisation — every item on the well-being agenda is a spending item. New Zealand's Wellbeing Budget [P1.4] was financed by tax receipts from a growing economy; it reallocated growth, it did not replace it. And New Zealand's persistent productivity weakness is now the binding constraint on those very services.
[C1.8] Costa Rica did not achieve its health outcomes through contentment; it achieved them through decades of rising income spent well.
[C1.9] Fourth, whose luxury is this? Tell a government in Lagos or Dhaka that output growth should no longer be its headline aim. Growth is what buys clean water, electricity, and the escape from subsistence.
[C1.10] Keep well-being as the purpose, measured on a published dashboard. But the headline target should be the thing governments can actually move, and can actually be caught [clipped]

[P2.1] GDP’s supposed objectivity is a myth. It systematically omits unpaid care and environmental degradation—choices as political as any well-being weighting. Moreover, GDP’s own boundaries are subjective: nations debate whether military spending or R&D counts as investment, and underground economies are estimated with wide margins of error. The OECD’s Better Life Index, by contrast, uses verifiable, comparable indicators like life expectancy, education levels, and air quality that are harder to manipulate and more directly tied to human welfare.
[P2.2] CON’s adaptation argument cuts against them. If life satisfaction barely shifts during economic downturns, that demonstrates GDP’s failure to capture welfare, not well-being’s irrelevance. Objective well-being metrics—child poverty rates, preventable hospital admissions, literacy scores—do respond meaningfully to policy. The fact that these metrics can be tracked, targeted, and improved shows they are actionable, unlike GDP which can rise while inequality, pollution, and stress all worsen.
[P2.3] This is the core failure: GDP counts cleanup costs from pollution as growth, while the pollution itself reduces welfare.
[P2.4] The resources objection rests on a false dichotomy. New Zealand’s Wellbeing Budget did not require new growth to fund mental health services and child poverty reduction; it reallocated existing resources toward outcomes that matter. The real constraint was political priority, not economic capacity. For developing nations, this shift is most urgent where rapid GDP growth has too often come with severe air pollution, child labor, and social unrest that diminish well-being.
[P2.5] Costa Rica’s achievement of life expectancy near OECD levels with a fraction of the GDP per capita proves prosperity depends on how resources are used, not merely on how many are produced. Growth that serves people builds lasting well-being; growth that consumes them erodes the very conditions for a good life. This is not anti-growth—it is growth with purpose.
