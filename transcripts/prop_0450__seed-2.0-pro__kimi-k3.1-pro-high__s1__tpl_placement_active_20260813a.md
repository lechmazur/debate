[C1.1] PRO offers a false rescue story: expensive fossil imports and power shortages supposedly leave nuclear expansion as the only exit. But the question is not whether an operating reactor emits little carbon. It is whether restarting and expanding nuclear power delivers security and emissions reductions soon enough, without creating new vulnerabilities and diverting capital from faster options.
[C1.2] The opening evidence does not establish that conclusion. High LNG prices and blackouts demonstrate exposure to fossil-fuel markets; they do not prove that new reactors, arriving many years later, would prevent the next shortage. Japan’s post-Fukushima emissions show the cost of suddenly losing a large electricity source, not that further nuclear expansion is now the best decarbonization pathway. Likewise, identifying a possible “baseload gap” assumes demand and generation choices that efficiency, storage, and grid investment can change.
[C1.3] Energy security is also more than avoiding LNG. Japan and South Korea would still depend on imported uranium and foreign fuel-cycle services. Nuclear power substitutes concentrated technical and geopolitical dependencies for diversified ones. Long construction delays and cost overruns leave countries purchasing fossil fuels while capital is locked into unfinished projects; unexpected reactor outages then remove large blocks of supply at once.
[C1.4] Passive cooling does not abolish earthquakes, flooding, spent-fuel hazards, evacuation burdens, or institutional failure. Fukushima’s deepest warning was that cascading events and overconfident assumptions can defeat supposedly robust safeguards. Nor does identifying candidate geology solve waste: neither country has an operating deep geological repository for spent nuclear fuel, and durable local consent cannot simply be announced.
[C1.5] Finally, “mountainous and dense” is not an argument against decarbonization; it is an argument for using resources intelligently. Rooftop solar requires no new utility-scale land, while offshore wind, efficiency, demand response, storage, stronger grids, geothermal where viable, and regional interconnection can be developed incrementally. This portfolio starts cutting fuel consumption and emissions before expanded nuclear plausibly arrives, while avoiding irreversible waste and disaster liabilities. PRO’s claim that no credible third path exists is not realism—it is the premise that makes the rest of the case look inevitable.
