[P1.1] GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide and tirzepatide were developed to treat obesity and diabetes, but their capacity to strip fifteen to twenty percent of body weight in months creates a precise problem for sport: they let athletes engineer body composition in ways training alone cannot replicate. In weight-class combat sports, a wrestler or boxer who drops from 80 kilograms to 68 while retaining lean mass gains a reach, speed, and power advantage that no cut-weight protocol safely achieves. In endurance events, every kilogram shed improves the power-to-weight ratio and reduces the metabolic cost of running or cycling—gains that rival what blood doping once offered, without any detectable blood manipulation.
[P1.2] The core principle of fair competition is that performance should reflect training, genetics, and effort—not pharmacology. GLP-1 drugs blur the line between therapy and enhancement because they directly alter the very variable—body mass—that governs advantage in these sports. A therapeutic-use exemption allows an athlete with a documented metabolic condition to use the drug legally, yet the performance benefit is identical whether the diagnosis is genuine or strategic. Current anti-doping frameworks were built around substances that suppress or stimulate; they were not designed for drugs that reshape physique as a primary mechanism.
[P1.3] Opponents will argue these are simply medical treatments athletes happen to use, or that scrutiny infringes on health autonomy. But sport already accepts that certain medications require scrutiny precisely because they cross into performance territory. We do not allow beta-blockers in archery because steadying the hand confers advantage, even though they treat heart conditions. GLP-1 drugs deserve the same logic applied honestly: if the substance fundamentally alters competitive capacity through body-composition change, stricter scrutiny is not punitive—it is protective of the contest itself.
