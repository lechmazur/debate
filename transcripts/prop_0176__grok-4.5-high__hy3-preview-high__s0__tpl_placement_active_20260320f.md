[P1.1] Commodity windfalls offer resource-rich developing countries a fleeting chance to escape poverty traps and build lasting prosperity. Sovereign wealth funds manage those windfalls better than direct cash distributions to citizens by securing macroeconomic stability, protecting the claims of future generations, and converting finite natural resources into durable national capital.
[P1.2] Price volatility is the core problem. Cash today floods demand, inflating prices, appreciating the currency, and gutting manufacturing and agriculture. Without a savings buffer the real exchange rate becomes a roller-coaster that punishes non-commodity exporters. The bust then brings sharp contraction and abandoned projects.
[P1.3] Sovereign wealth funds park excess revenues under clear fiscal rules and spend only a sustainable share, smoothing public investment and breaking the boom-bust cycles that have repeatedly crippled oil and mineral economies.
[P1.4] Even if prices stay high, cash transfers amount to intergenerational appropriation. Minerals and hydrocarbons belong to the nation’s entire timeline, not just the living generation. Handing out cash exhausts the inheritance for those yet unborn; a sovereign wealth fund invests the proceeds in a diversified portfolio of assets that generate income long after the wells or mines are exhausted. That permanent income stream can still fund social support, education, and infrastructure without depleting the underlying wealth.
[P1.5] Cash feels more immediate and populist, yet much of it is spent on imports and short-term consumables rather than raising the economy’s productive capacity, and it often fuels patronage politics and clientelism. A well-designed fund imposes binding spending discipline on governments while still permitting rules-based flows for genuine national needs. Transforming resource wealth into broad-based diversified growth takes decades of patient capital accumulation. Cash burns the windfall for today’s consumption; the fund invests it so that tomorrow’s citizens inherit opportunity rather than empty ground.
[P1.6] Sovereign wealth funds are therefore the better instrument.

[C1.1] Sovereign wealth funds in resource-rich developing countries are almost invariably controlled by unaccountable executive branches, with minimal transparency or citizen oversight. Unlike direct cash transfers, which flow straight to households, bypassing layers of bureaucracy and rent-seeking intermediaries, SWF assets are routinely diverted to vanity infrastructure, offshore tax havens, or personal enrichment for ruling cliques. This is not a fringe risk: it is the dominant pattern in states with weak institutional checks, where fund managers answer only to the ruling elite, not the public.
[C1.2] Even when SWFs are legally required to allocate a share of returns to social spending, those funds are still filtered through government agencies prone to leakages and corruption, unlike direct cash that reaches recipients with minimal friction.
[C1.3] The argument that SWFs protect future generations ignores the urgent, life-or-death needs of the current population. In many resource-rich developing countries, large shares of citizens live on less than $2 a day, lack clean water, or face preventable child mortality. Waiting decades for SWF returns to materialize is a moral nonstarter when cash transfers today could immediately lift families out of starvation, pay for school fees, and reduce stunting that permanently limits future productivity. Human capital built now is a more reliable intergenerational asset than a fund that may be looted before it ever pays out.
[C1.4] Claims that cash transfers drive Dutch disease and boom-bust cycles ignore that SWFs do not eliminate these risks when fund returns are eventually spent by the same volatile governments. Worse, SWFs often encourage over-saving that starves current public goods: roads, clinics, and schools needed now to support non-commodity sectors. Direct cash transfers, paired with simple, transparent spending rules, can boost demand for domestically produced goods, supporting smallholder agriculture and light manufacturing rather than fueling import consumption as often asserted. Centralizing windfall control in unaccountable fund managers prioritizes elite interests over both citizens’ immediate survival and long-term economic diversification.

Over-saving is a design flaw, not an inherent vice; under-saving is the guaranteed result of handing out the entire windfall.
[P2.5] Cash therefore fails the intergenerational test twice: it accelerates present consumption while amplifying political volatility that undermines any later recovery.
