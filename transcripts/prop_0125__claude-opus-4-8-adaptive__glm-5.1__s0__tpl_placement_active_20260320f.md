[P1.1] The carrier's complaint—"it isn't commercially viable"—is not an argument against subsidy. It is the entire reason subsidy exists. Of course it isn't profitable to string fiber to a county with eight households per square mile. Markets optimize for return on investment, not for whether a child in that county can attend school online.
[P1.2] When a service has become a precondition for ordinary economic and civic life but the market will never supply it on its own, that gap is precisely what public investment is built to close. We did this with rural electrification and rural telephone lines a century ago, and no one today argues we should have left those farms dark.
[P1.3] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, broadband is infrastructure, not a luxury product. Telehealth appointments, remote work, online schooling, applying for jobs and government benefits—all now assume connectivity. A household without it is locked out of the modern economy. Leaving rural America offline doesn't just inconvenience those families; it widens regional inequality and hollows out small towns as young people leave for connected places.
[P1.5] Second, the subsidy pays for itself. Connected regions attract remote workers, keep small businesses competitive, enable precision agriculture, and reduce the costs of delivering health care and public services to remote populations. A one-time capital subsidy unlocks decades of recurring economic activity and tax base. The "cost" the carriers cite is a private profit calculation; the public ledger looks entirely different.
[P1.6] Third, this is a market failure with a known fix. The reason private carriers won't build is high fixed costs spread over few customers. Public money lowers that fixed cost, and the line, once built, serves the community for decades. The alternative is permanent exclusion—a two-tier nation divided by zip code.
[P1.7] So the question is not whether serving sparse areas is profitable. It admittedly isn't. The question is whether we accept that profitability should determine who gets to participate in modern life. I say it should not.

[C1.3] Second, the electrification analogy collapses on contact. Electricity was a uniform technology with a single delivery model. Broadband is not. Satellite internet, fixed wireless, and mobile data are already closing the rural connectivity gap without stringing fiber to every farmhouse.

[C1.5] Third, opportunity cost is crushing. [C1.6] Fourth, subsidies create dependency and moral hazard. Carriers learn to wait for government money rather than innovate.
