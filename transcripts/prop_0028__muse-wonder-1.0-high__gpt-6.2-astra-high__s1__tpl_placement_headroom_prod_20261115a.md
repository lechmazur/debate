[P1.1] Mass university expansion promised that more degrees would mean more doors. Instead, it mostly moved the door.
[P1.2] When degrees were scarce, a bachelor's signaled distinct skills and opened distinct work. When policy pushed cohort after cohort through university, employers did not invent millions of new graduate jobs to match. They simply raised the entry ticket for the jobs that already existed. Administrative assistant, police recruit, sales manager, bank clerk — roles once filled by school-leavers or trainees now routinely demand a degree, not because the work became dramatically more complex, but because graduates are abundant and cheap to sort by.
[P1.3] That is credential inflation: the same job requiring ever-higher qualifications for roughly the same pay and prospects. The burden lands on young people, who must buy more education to stand still. A master's becomes the new bachelor's, unpaid internships become the new experience, and students from poorer families — least able to fund extra years, debts, and metropolitan living costs — are outbid by wealthier peers collecting ever-longer CVs.
[P1.4] Genuine opportunity would mean expanding the number of good jobs, raising mobility, and giving non-graduates respected alternatives. Mass expansion did the opposite. It degraded the signal of a degree, so talented graduates work in non-graduate jobs while displacing less-credentialed workers downward. It narrowed social esteem to one route, gutting apprenticeships and vocational prestige.
[P1.5] Marginal students, pushed into weak courses, often gain meagre returns after debt.
[P1.6] Expansion created access to classrooms, not access to advancement. Access without scarcity is not opportunity when employers use credentials as a filter rather than a measure of ability. More people now run faster on a treadmill that leads to the same finite set of prized jobs, only now entry costs three years and a loan. That is inflation first, opportunity second.
