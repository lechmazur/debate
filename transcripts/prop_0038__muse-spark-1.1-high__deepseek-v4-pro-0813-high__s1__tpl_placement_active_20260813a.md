[P1.1] Treating noise as a mere annoyance is a public health failure. Your body does not habituate to traffic roaring at 75 decibels outside your bedroom at 2 a.m. Your stress hormones spike, your blood pressure rises, your sleep fragments, whether you consciously notice it or not. That is not inconvenience; it is involuntary physiological exposure, exactly like breathing smog.
[P1.2] That is why health authorities rank chronic noise alongside fine particulate matter as a leading environmental threat. The mechanism is straightforward: sustained noise triggers chronic stress and sleep loss, which cascade into hypertension, heart disease, metabolic disorder, anxiety and depression. Children in classrooms under flight paths learn to read later. Night-shift workers living next to unregulated construction never get restorative sleep.
[P1.3] The damage accumulates invisibly, just like air pollution, and it hits the poorest neighborhoods hardest - those next to highways, logistics hubs, and late-night commercial strips that can afford to externalize their sound.
[P1.4] Air quality improved because cities stopped politely asking polluters to be quieter and started binding them: emission limits, continuous monitoring networks, permits that can be pulled, vehicle standards that force quieter fleets. Noise deserves the same architecture. Voluntary guidelines and complaint hotlines fail because one loud bar, one overnight concrete pour, can ruin sleep for thousands who have no individual recourse.
[P1.5] Binding limits on traffic speed and volume, low-noise road surfaces, electric buses, nighttime construction decibel caps with real fines, commercial sound limiters that trip breakers - these are proven, cheap, and enforceable. If a city can tow a car for blocking a bike lane to protect safety, it can fine a club pumping 95 decibels into apartments at midnight to protect health.
[P1.6] Air and noise are two lungs of the same livable city. Regulating one urgently while treating the other as a lifestyle issue is incoherent.
