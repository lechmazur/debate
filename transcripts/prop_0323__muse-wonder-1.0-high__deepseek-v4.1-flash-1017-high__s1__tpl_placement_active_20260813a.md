[P1.1] Every post on today's major platforms arrives with a scoreboard attached. A thoughtful photo, a joke, a grief post, a political opinion — each is instantly priced by how many strangers tapped a heart, and that number trails the author in public. The default should be reversed: like and repost counts hidden unless users choose to see or show them, with creators still able to view their own feedback privately.
[P1.2] That distinction matters because public counts do different work than private feedback. Privately, a count can tell you your friends saw a post. Publicly, it becomes a contest you are always losing to someone more viral, more attractive, or more outrageous. It invites checking, deleting posts that "underperform," and avoiding unpopular but honest views.
[P1.3] For adolescents forming an identity among peers, that constant ranking is especially corrosive — the audience is not just watching, it is grading.
[P1.4] The same default distorts what spreads. When a repost count is visible before a claim is read, people use popularity as a shortcut for truth and pile on early winners. Platforms then optimize ranking, notifications, and design around whatever raises those numbers, rewarding outrage, humiliation, and tidy tribal slogans over nuance. Hiding the counters by default does not remove sharing or liking; it forces posts to compete first on content, relevance, and trust in the source, rather than on bandwagon momentum.
[P1.5] Nor is visibility needed for discovery or accountability. Recommendation systems can still use engagement signals internally, creators can still track reach privately, and users who want public metrics can opt in. What disappears is only the compulsory spectacle — and with it, much of the value to bots, purchased engagement, and already-famous accounts whose large numbers intimidate newcomers into silence.
[P1.6] A healthier default treats popularity as information for the author, not as public identity for everyone else to rank, envy, and chase.

[C1.2] Users simply lose the ability to see what is being amplified and why. That is less accountability, not more.
[C1.3] Public metrics are also information. The user who wants context must now opt in, while everyone else is left guessing.
[C1.4] Defaults are sticky. A switch that most people never touch is not a neutral option; it is a policy that removes public metrics for most users and communities, including the fundraiser, the local artist, the moderator, the person checking whether a warning actually spread. That is not protecting ordinary users; it is giving them less information than the people whose job is to game the system.
[C1.5] The real choice is not “popularity contest versus content.” It is visible, contestable signals versus hidden, platform-controlled signals. Keep public counts available by default, pair them with controls, context, and limits, especially for minors. But do not pretend that making popularity invisible makes it disappear. The proposition offers the comfort of hiding a number while leaving the machinery that exploits it untouched.
