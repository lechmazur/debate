[P1.1] Egg freezing is marketed as insurance against a biological clock. But it is insurance whose payout depends overwhelmingly on the age at which you buy it, and the difference between freezing at 30 and at 38 is not marginal — it is the whole story. Any advertisement that sells the reassurance while burying that variable is selling a different product than the one delivered.
[P1.2] Watch the metric switch. Headlines celebrate eggs retrieved, survival after thaw, or "pregnancy rates" per transfer — numbers that flatter the clinic because they quietly drop everyone who never reached transfer. What a patient is buying is a chance of a baby, per person who starts. Those are not the same number, and the gap widens with age.
[P1.3] The remedy here is narrow: put age-banded live-birth odds, with honest uncertainty ranges, where the promise is — not three clicks deep in a footnote. Nothing is banned. No clinic must stop advertising. They already report outcomes to registries; this is a question of placement and hierarchy.
[P1.4] The reply will be that women can ask their doctor. But the doctor is the seller, and the advertisement has already set the anchor before anyone walks in. A 36-year-old who reads "extend your fertility" and waits two years has lost something no consultation can restore. Unlike a bad phone purchase, this decision cannot be repeated on better terms.
[P1.5] And if the honest answer is a wide confidence interval, that is the finding, not an excuse. "We don't know precisely, and the odds are modest at your age" is exactly what someone spending five figures and years of storage fees deserves to hear first. Uncertainty prominently stated is respect. Optimism prominently stated, with the caveats hidden, is a sales technique.

[C1.1] Requiring every egg-freezing advertisement to lead with age-banded live-birth rates sounds like transparency, but it would mandate the least reliable number in reproductive medicine as the most prominent one.
[C1.2] Unlike a drug trial, egg freezing has no clean denominator. Many women who froze eggs at thirty have not yet returned, those who do return are not a random sample, and outcomes depend on how many eggs were stored, ovarian reserve, sperm and uterine health, and laboratory technique that changes. A rate drawn from a small, self-selected group years ago does not become realistic because a regulator orders it in type. It becomes falsely precise, and a thirty-four-year-old with twenty eggs is misled in the opposite direction from a thirty-eight-year-old with five if both are handed the same age band.
[C1.3] That false precision is why counseling, not advertising copy, is the right place for prognosis. An advertisement can truthfully invite consultation, and must already avoid deceptive claims under existing consumer-protection and medical-advertising rules. What it cannot do in a banner, a fifteen-second spot, or a sponsored post is explain cumulative chances per egg, per thaw, and per transfer, confidence intervals, and why a clinic's patients differ from a national average. Forcing that compression will either frighten women away from an early consultation when timing helps, or reassure them with an average that was never theirs.
[C1.4] The mandate would also shrink information. Clinics facing liability for every mandated range will advertise less, while influencers, overseas clinics, and social media — harder to police — fill the gap with anecdote. Women would hear less from regulated providers who must give informed consent, not more truth.
[C1.5] Enforce deception where it occurs. Do not turn an uncertain, personal forecast into a compulsory headline that pretends certainty the science cannot supply.

One clinic bands 35 to 37, another 36 to 38; one reports per woman who thawed, another per woman who froze; one has twelve returnees, another two hundred treated with older methods. [C2.4] Displayed prominently, it can become the decision itself.
[C2.5] That irreversibility runs both ways. [C2.6] True-but-misleading metrics do not require this mandate. Regulators can prohibit per-transfer rates being presented as chances of a baby, require any efficacy claim to carry its denominator, and sanction “insurance” language. That targets deception without forcing every awareness advertisement to lead with a number no one can yet make realistic.
