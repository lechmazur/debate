[C1.1] The proposition asks us to give creators a blanket veto over the most transformative technology of the decade. That sounds fair, but it is a policy that would cripple the public good for a narrow slice of private interest.
[C1.2] First, the law already protects creators. Copyright gives them exclusive rights to reproductions, derivatives, and public performances. Training a model does not copy a work; it extracts statistical patterns, much like a human reader learns style. Courts have repeatedly treated large‑scale data mining as fair use when the output is not a substitute for the original.
[C1.3] Imposing a new default right would duplicate existing protections while adding a massive friction layer that benefits incumbents, not creators.
[C1.4] Second, the competition argument confuses the tool with the product. A model trained on public text does not become a rival novelist; it is a utility, like a dictionary or a spell‑checker. The real competitive threat to a writer is other writers, not a language model that can suggest a sentence. If we force opt‑in, the model’s quality collapses, and the entire ecosystem of AI‑assisted writing, translation, accessibility, and education suffers.
[C1.5] Those downstream benefits accrue to the same creators the proposition claims to protect.
[C1.6] Third, consent is already operational. Platforms provide robots.txt, API terms, and opt‑out mechanisms. The market also rewards creators who monetize their data directly—stock‑photo sites, licensed datasets—showing that voluntary arrangements work when creators see value. A blanket default right would push creators toward secrecy, paywalls, or data poisoning, exactly the dystopia the motion warns about, but now caused by the policy itself.
[C1.7] Finally, consider scale. Training a state‑of‑the‑art model requires billions of tokens. Requiring explicit permission for each token would be logistically impossible and would concentrate power in the hands of a few large platforms that can afford legal compliance, squeezing out smaller innovators.
[C1.8] The status quo—default opt‑out with existing copyright safeguards—already balances creator control with the enormous public benefits of generative AI. Strengthening the default right would tip the balance the wrong way, harming both creators and society.
