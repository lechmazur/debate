[C1.1] The proposition rests on a seductive but false distinction: that earned income is meritocratic while inherited wealth is aristocratic. In reality, the line between the two is far less clean, and the proposed remedy would cause far more collateral damage than its proponents acknowledge.
[C1.2] Consider what actually constitutes a "large inheritance." Aggressive inheritance taxation forces heirs to liquidate these enterprises to pay the bill, destroying businesses that employ people and serve communities. A family manufacturing firm worth a few million dollars is not "dynastic wealth" in any meaningful sense, yet it gets swept into the same net as the Rockefeller estate.
[C1.3] More importantly, the claim that high annual income is less threatening to opportunity than inheritance gets the causal story backwards. Dynastic wealth is not created at the moment of transfer—it is built through years of high income compounding into capital. If high earnings are the engine, taxing only the inheritance is closing the barn door after the horse has already founded a dynasty. The PRO side concedes that high earners generate income through "active, productive labor"—yet that same income, reinvested over forty years, becomes precisely the fortune they then want to tax at transfer.
[C1.4] This is incoherent: either the original earnings were legitimate, in which case families should be free to deploy them across generations, or they were excessive, in which case income taxation is the proper intervention point.
[C1.5] Finally, aggressive inheritance taxes do not work as intended. Wealthy families use trusts, lifetime gifts, valuation discounts, and jurisdictional arbitrage to minimize exposure. The practical result is a system that captures family businesses while missing the truly dynastic fortunes it targets—worse for opportunity, worse for fairness.
