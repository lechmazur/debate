[P1.1] A mass grave is not an archive that happens to contain bodies. It is a burial ground that happens to contain evidence. That order matters, and it is why descendant communities should hold substantial control over how such places are dug, shown, and narrated.
[P1.2] First is a claim of relationship, not mere interest. When archaeologists handle the remains of people enslaved on a plantation, murdered in a pogrom, or worked to death in a camp, they are handling someone's ancestors, within living traditions of mourning. Scholars and tourists will leave. Descendants must live with what was disturbed, photographed, or put behind glass.
[P1.3] Treating their consent as optional repeats the core logic of the original atrocity: that these bodies and stories are available for others to use. Substantial control — joint decision-making, consent over excavation and display of remains, and co-authorship of interpretation — breaks that continuity.
[P1.4] Second, that control produces better history, not worse. Descendants carry oral histories, languages, ritual knowledge, and family records that can identify the dead, explain burial practices, and correct misreadings that perpetrator documents leave behind. The rediscovery of the African Burial Ground in New York became more, not less, informative when Black community pressure forced a shift from salvage excavation to research shaped by descendant questions about health, origin, and resistance. Exclude those voices and you get cleaner displays built on thinner knowledge.
[P1.5] Third, descendant control is a safeguard against sanitization. States and institutions that inherited land, wealth, or prestige from forced labor have a standing incentive to turn atrocity into heritage atmosphere — a plantation as a wedding venue, a labor camp as a generic lesson in intolerance, a massacre site with no perpetrators named. The people with the strongest stake in naming what happened are the strongest check on that drift.
[P1.6] Substantial does not mean a monopoly on facts or a ban on uncomfortable research. It means the default power does not sit with distant institutions. The dead cannot consent. Their communities are the closest moral proxy we have.

Forensic necessity justifies documentation and, where prosecution is live, exhumation under law; it does not justify institutions retaining remains indefinitely, displaying skulls, or narrating a community's dead without that community deciding how.
[P2.2] Substantial control governs that “how”: notice and consent before non-urgent digging, ritual presence, limits on photography and display, return and reburial after identification, and shared authority over interpretation. That is not a veto that helps deniers [C1.2]. It is often what makes forensics work. Identifications in Bosnia and elsewhere depended on families providing DNA references, names, clothing memories, and trust that bodies would not become specimens again.
[P2.3] Refuse that partnership and graves stay unreported, witnesses stay silent, and evidence stays thinner.
[P2.4] The claim that consultation already captures descendant knowledge [C1.3] describes the problem: extraction without power. Experts listen, take the oral history that helps their label, then keep final say over the uncomfortable parts — including local complicity. Co-authorship does not mean deleting established facts. It means descendants can insist perpetrators be named, forced labor not be euphemized, and collaboration be presented with evidence rather than whispered away by a state museum protecting national pride.
[P2.5] Division among descendants [C1.5] is real, but it is not cured by handing control to scholars, ministries, or site boards, who also disagree and often answer to funders or governments. It is cured inside descendant governance: seats for families, smaller groups, women, diaspora, and dissenting relatives, with published reasons and appeal. In Eastern Europe, the danger is not that Roma voices gain power; it is that without guaranteed power they are tokenized by larger national narratives [C1.4].
[P2.6] Independent law against wedding-venue sanitization [C1.6] does not enforce itself. Descendant control is the constituency that demands, monitors, and sustains it.
