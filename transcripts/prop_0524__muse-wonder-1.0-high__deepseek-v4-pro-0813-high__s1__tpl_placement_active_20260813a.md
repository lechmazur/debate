[P1.1] Migration policy today fails institutions and communities at the same time. A university can recruit a cohort, an NHS trust or care provider can plan a rota, and then watch visa rules shift mid-cycle. At the same moment, a town can receive hundreds of new arrivals with no new homes, GP places, or school capacity, and be told the benefits are national while the congestion is local. Uncapped demand overloads places; arbitrary national caps starve them.
[P1.2] Neither is control.
[P1.3] Capped but guaranteed allocations break that cycle. Each university and qualifying employer receives a defined, multi-year allocation it can rely on — enough to plan courses, research teams, clinical rotas, and production lines — but no more than its place can absorb. The guarantee ends ministerial stop-start and the wasteful scramble for visas. The cap ends open-ended sponsorship where success for one institution means rent spikes and waiting lists for everyone else.
[P1.4] Crucially, tying the allocation to housing and local service capacity changes incentives instead of merely rationing people. An institution that wants a larger allocation next cycle must show secure accommodation, transport, and access to health and schooling, or help fund them. Universities stop treating student housing as someone else's problem and build or lease beds. Employers who need overseas engineers, nurses, or seasonal workers invest in workforce housing rather than bidding up the local private rental market.
[P1.5] Migration pays for its footprint upfront.
[P1.6] That link also restores public consent and spreads opportunity. Allocations flow not just to overheated metropolitan markets, but to places that have built capacity — smaller cities and rural employers that can house people and need staff. Locals see a visible bargain: growth arrives only with the homes and services to support it.
[P1.7] The alternative is to keep pretending a national number can fit every postcode. Guaranteed within limits, earned through capacity: that is migration that institutions can plan on and communities can live with.

[C1.1] The proposal looks like planning but is really a housing veto with extra steps. It makes a university’s or employer’s ability to operate depend on GP waiting lists, school places, transport budgets, and local planning permissions—none of which it controls. A university can build beds, but it cannot open a GP surgery, create school places, or compel a bus route. So the cap will bind; the guarantee will not.
[C1.2] That is not reliable migration policy. It is offloading public infrastructure failure onto institutions that lack the tools to fix it.
[C1.3] The guarantee is illusory because “housing and local service capacity” is not an objective threshold. It is contested, politically manipulated, and lags demand. Local actors can game or block assessments; spare capacity can be redefined downward whenever pressure grows. Institutions would spend years negotiating metrics and local vetoes while recruitment still suffers stop-start.
[C1.4] Worse, capping by current capacity rewards under-building and punishes productivity: a hospital in a high-demand city could lose nurses because a council failed to approve flats, while a low-demand area holds unused allocations. National decisions become hostage to local housing politics.
[C1.5] It also creates perverse incentives. Employers and universities stop competing on teaching, research, care, or production, and start competing as property developers and service funders. Cash-rich institutions can buy capacity and secure allocations; smaller employers, public bodies, and less wealthy universities cannot. The allocation becomes a scarce, potentially tradeable asset, inviting rent-seeking.
[C1.6] For migrants, it deepens dependence on the sponsor’s quota, reducing mobility and bargaining power.
[C1.7] The real problem is underprovided housing and services. The answer is to build homes and fund public services, not to gate migration by the consequences of failing to do so. This proposal offers cap without guarantee, rationing without consent, and infrastructure politics without infrastructure.

[P2.1] We already have a housing veto — chaotic, retrospective and political. When rents spike and GP lists close, ministers panic and slash visas mid-recruitment, so institutions and towns both lose. Tying allocations to capacity does not invent that constraint; it makes it predictable in advance and worth fixing.
[P2.2] Nor must a university run a GP surgery. It must stop pretending nurses can be recruited to a town with nowhere to live. A formula can credit what institutions can do — build or lease beds, fund extra surgery sessions, underwrite transport, partner on school places — while the state funds core services. Contribution is not control, but it is contribution the open-ended system never extracts.
[P2.3] The guarantee is illusory only if each locality redefines capacity at will. Set it nationally, on published measures, for a multi-year cycle, and it binds ministers too: they cannot halve an allocation because a headline turned. That is far stronger than today, when a rule change can strand a recruited cohort or a planned rota overnight.
[P2.4] This reverses, not rewards, under-building. Today a council can block homes yet still face unfunded pressure, or export its staffing crisis to the NHS. Here low capacity means a low ceiling, and building is the only route to a higher one. Unused allocations lapse at the next cycle and flow where need and capacity meet.
[P2.5] What gets rewarded is not hoarding paper capacity, but homes people can actually move into.
[P2.6] Universities already build halls, and employers already house clinical and seasonal staff. The question is whether that expands supply or merely bids up existing rentals. A capacity link rewards expansion. Need-weighted, non-tradeable allocations protect public services and smaller employers from being outbid — unlike the present system, where wealthy sponsors win anyway, locals get nothing, and migrants get the same sponsor dependence with no security the route will survive.

[P4.1] A1: Yes — that is precisely what guaranteed means. An allocation is fixed for the multi-year cycle on housing delivered and contributions credited at the start. If the state or council then fails to deliver its GP or school element, it cannot claw visas back mid-cycle; it must backfill the service or fund mitigation, with any recalibration only prospective at the next cycle. That puts the risk on the party that controls core services, rather than, as today, on the recruited student, nurse, or employer left stranded by a sudden rule change.
[P4.2] A2: Non-tradeable matters because the quota itself cannot be bought, sold, or hoarded, but the deeper protection is that allocations are not one auction. They are ring-fenced by sector and need-weighted to an approved workforce or student plan, not awarded in proportion to spending. A small care provider is not bidding against a university for the same beds; its ceiling reflects its own staff need and local footprint, and it can meet the contribution test through pooled housing with a council or trust, at a scale proportionate to its size.
[P4.3] A wealthy university building more halls does not consume that provider's allocation — it adds supply and eases local rents.
[P4.4] That exposes the choice. CON worries that blockers will enjoy a low ceiling, but blockers today already enjoy blocking — while staff shortages continue and arrivals still land elsewhere without funding. Under a capacity link, obstruction acquires a visible local cost: fewer university places, unfilled care shifts, and lost employer growth until homes are approved. Capacity therefore creates a pro-building constituency of institutions, workers, and residents who can see what building unlocks.
[P4.5] CON's alternative still has no answer to scarcity in the interim: uncapped sponsorship overwhelms the tightest places, or ministers ration arbitrarily. A capped guarantee at least makes the limit advance, stable, and earnable — and makes expanding homes and services the way to raise it.
