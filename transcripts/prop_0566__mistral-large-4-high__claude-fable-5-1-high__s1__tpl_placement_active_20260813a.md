[P1.1] Legacy admissions create an aristocracy of birth inside institutions that claim to be engines of merit. When a university awards a seat to the child of an alumnus or the donor’s son, it is not selecting for talent, drive, or intellectual promise. It is trading educational opportunity for lineage and cash. This is not a minor tweak at the margins; it is a structural corruption of the admissions process that determines who gets to learn, network, and launch a life.
[P1.2] The mechanism is simple and brutal. Legacy and donor preferences concentrate advantage among students who already possess it—families who can afford tuition without aid, who attended well-resourced high schools, who had access to test prep and college counseling. These applicants do not need a leg up to compete; the leg up ensures that a less-resourced student with stronger credentials loses the seat. The result is not a neutral tiebreaker but a systematic transfer of opportunity from the deserving to the connected, freezing social hierarchy into the classroom and wasting human capital that the nation cannot afford to squander.
[P1.3] This corrodes the university itself. A degree’s value depends on the belief that graduates earned their place through ability and effort. When admissions are openly rigged for the wealthy and well-connected, that belief collapses. Faculty lose moral authority, alumni giving becomes a cynical transaction rather than gratitude, and the public sees higher education as a private club for the privileged rather than a ladder of mobility.
[P1.4] The institution cannot simultaneously claim to cultivate excellence and auction seats to the highest bidder or the most nostalgic parent.
[P1.5] Sharply limiting these preferences is not an attack on universities; it is a rescue mission. It restores admissions as a competition of ideas and effort rather than a ledger of old-boy networks and donor spreadsheets. The university survives without legacy preferences; indeed, it thrives when its gates open to talent regardless of whose name is on the diploma their father holds.
