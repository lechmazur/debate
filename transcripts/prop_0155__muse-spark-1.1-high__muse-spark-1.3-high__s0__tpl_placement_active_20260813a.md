[P1.1] Real-time facial recognition and post-incident facial recognition are not the same tool used at different times. They are different powers entirely.
[P1.2] Real-time surveillance turns every camera on every street into a perpetual lineup. Every person walking to work, to a protest, to a clinic is scanned, identified, logged, without any individualized suspicion. That is a suspicionless mass search of public life. It eliminates anonymity, the breathing room that allows dissent, free association and ordinary privacy to exist.
[P1.3] No officer could lawfully stop and ID every passerby; automating that violation doesn't make it constitutional. Once people know a protest camera is identifying them in real time, many simply don't go.
[P1.4] It also fails on its own terms. Live matching must decide in seconds, on blurry, angled footage, against millions of faces. Error rates spike, and the errors are not random. Studies and lived experience show higher false positives for Black people, women, and younger people - the same communities already over-policed.
[P1.5] Detroit's false arrests are not glitches; they are the predictable outcome of using an unreliable system in an irreversible moment, where an alert becomes a gun-point stop before anyone checks the work.
[P1.6] Post-incident use with a warrant flips every one of those problems. The crime has already occurred. Investigators have a specific time, place, and offense. A judge verifies probable cause that the person in the footage committed it and that face recognition might identify them.
[P1.7] It is targeted, retrospective, reviewable, and auditable - exactly how we treat phone records, security footage, or DNA.
[P1.8] That middle path protects both safety and liberty: we don't let police track everyone just in case, but we don't blind them when evidence exists and a neutral magistrate agrees. It draws the line where the Constitution already draws it: no dragnets without cause, no investigations without oversight.
