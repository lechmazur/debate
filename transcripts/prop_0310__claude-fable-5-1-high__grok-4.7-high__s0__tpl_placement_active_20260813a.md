[P1.1] Ask what the word "core" actually means. It means: we require this because every student will need it, regardless of where they end up. By that test, cooking, basic home repair, and personal finance are more core than most of what already fills the timetable. Not every graduate factors quadratics or analyzes a sonnet again.
[P1.2] Every one will eat three times a day, live somewhere that breaks, and manage money for sixty years.
[P1.3] Making these skills optional guarantees the students who most need them never get them. Electives are self-selected. The teenager whose parents cook from scratch, own a toolbox, and talk about mortgages at dinner already has a home curriculum. The teenager living on microwave meals in a rental where nothing gets fixed, with no savings account anywhere in the family, has no reason to seek out a class in something never modeled at home.
[P1.4] "Optional" reproduces the exact inequality schools claim to exist to interrupt. Core status is the only mechanism that reaches the kid who doesn't know what he doesn't know.
[P1.5] A twenty-year-old signing a car loan without understanding interest pays for that ignorance for years. A young adult who cannot cook eats worse and pays more for it, for life. A tenant who cannot reset a breaker, unclog a drain, or recognize a leak pays someone else or lives with the damage. These skills are cheap to teach and expensive to lack; that asymmetry is the argument.
[P1.6] Two replies are coming: it's the parents' job, and the timetable is full. Parents' job it may be; the trouble is that it's a job done unevenly, and schools exist precisely for what home leaves uneven. And "the timetable is full" is a prioritization claim, not an objection. A school that requires trigonometry but treats compound interest as an extra has simply ranked its requirements wrong.
[P1.7] Finance is applied arithmetic; cooking, applied chemistry; repair, applied physics. Making them core does not dilute academics. It gives academics somewhere to land.
