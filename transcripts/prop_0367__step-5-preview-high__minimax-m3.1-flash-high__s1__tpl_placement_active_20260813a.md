[P1.1] Old cable earned its reputation through a simple formula: bundle everything, hide the true price, and make leaving painful. Streaming was supposed to end that. The proposition is not a luxury; it is the test of whether streaming remains meaningfully different from cable.
[P1.2] The core value of streaming is control. Viewers already reconfigure their lives monthly—vacations, sports seasons, a kid’s phase, a show finale. A bundle that cannot pause or split assumes attention is static. It is not.
[P1.3] When a service can be paused for a month, it competes for that month rather than for a year. That is a healthier market.

[P1.5] That distinction matters: the burden of proof should fall on the bundle to justify why a consumer cannot easily reduce, pause, or split what they pay for. When platforms make monthly changes costly or obscure the cost of each component, they are not solving a logistics problem; they are recreating cable’s information asymmetry.
[P1.6] Finally, easy reconfiguration disciplines incumbents. If leaving for one month is cheap, a service cannot coast on a back catalog. It must earn renewal. That pressure improves content and pricing for everyone.
[P1.7] So the question is not whether streaming can survive flexibility. It is whether we let digital video inherit the worst habits of the technology it replaced.

[C2.1] PRO conflates two different things: allowing consumers to change a bundle and requiring the bundle to function as a month-to-month negotiation. The proposition does not need mandatory churn, but PRO’s economic case does: easy exit is said to discipline providers only if enough households are willing to use it. That is circular. The policy succeeds only when customers repeatedly restructure their plans, so PRO cannot dismiss the resulting work as optional while making it the source of competitive pressure.
[C2.2] “Every modern subscription already requires managing logins, renewals, and prices” is not a fair comparison. Opening an app and clicking cancel is routine administration. Restructuring a discounted household bundle can require new invoices, altered entitlements, separate terms, and the loss of savings that justified combining services in the first place. Consider a family bundle that pauses its sports tier, then discovers that the remaining stand-alone services cost more than the original package.
[C2.3] PRO calls this “honest pricing,” but visibility does not make an expensive arrangement affordable. The consumer now faces both a bill and a decision.
[C2.4] Nor does cheap splitting guarantee explicit, contestable pricing, as PRO claims. Providers can still set attractive standalone prices, alter promotions, or make different tiers reflect changing assumptions about engagement. The possibility of separation constrains some pricing, but it does not establish what each component should cost. Easy monthly changes may replace one kind of opacity—hidden channel costs—with another: effective prices that depend on when and how a customer reorganizes.
[C2.5] Finally, PRO dismisses pausing as potentially a “rights trap,” yet the proposition only requires pausing to be easier, not free of conditions. Providers can preserve promised access and terms while making reconfiguration administratively available. The core complaint about old cable—bundling services consumers do not use—can be addressed through transparent component pricing and meaningful cancellation, without making every month a repricing event.
[C2.6] Thus, monthly reconfiguration is not automatically greater control. It may transfer bargaining friction to households while sacrificing bundle savings and pricing predictability. The better anti-cable standard is clear, cancellable, genuinely modular service—not mandatory instability.

[C4.5] A menu of conditional prices can be more difficult to understand than one stable package.
