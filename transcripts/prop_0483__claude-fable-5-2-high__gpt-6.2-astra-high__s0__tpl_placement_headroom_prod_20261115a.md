[P1.1] Nobody serious needs to claim that IQ-style tests measure nothing. They measure something real: how quickly and accurately a person handles abstract patterns, vocabulary, and mental juggling in a timed room, on problems someone else defined, with one right answer. The question is whether that slice deserves to act as a gatekeeper. It does not.
[P1.2] First, the slice leaves out most of what thinking is for. Real cognitive work means deciding which problem is worth solving, generating options nobody handed you, judging under ambiguity, sustaining effort across months, reading other people, and noticing when your own reasoning has gone wrong. None of that fits in a forty-minute multiple-choice sitting. Research on rational decision-making repeatedly finds high scorers prone to many of the same biases as everyone else.
[P1.3] Avoiding them is a cognitive ability, and the test is blind to it.
[P1.4] Second, the prediction is modest on its best day. Even the most generous correlations defenders cite between scores and job performance or grades leave most of the differences between people unexplained. A tool that misses most of the picture can inform a decision; it cannot be the decision.
[P1.5] Third, sorting converts a fuzzy signal into a hard wall. A child who lands a few points under a gifted cutoff at age eight—within the test's own margin of error—gets a different curriculum, different peers, and different expectations for years. An applicant screened out by a cognitive cutoff never reaches the work sample that would have shown what she can do. The score doesn't just describe opportunity; it allocates it, and the allocation then manufactures outcomes that seem to validate the test.
[P1.6] So the standard: correlation with outcomes is not enough. The other side must show this narrow band earns a gatekeeping role that is early, decisive, and hard to reverse. Proportion is the test, and the current weight fails it.
