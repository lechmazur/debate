Clinicians retain diagnosis and treatment decisions.
[P1.2] The proposition is therefore not about replacing doctors; it is about helping patients reach the right human care.
[P1.3] Patients already perform symptom checks, especially at night, when worried, or when appointments are scarce. The realistic alternative is often not an immediate clinician. [P1.4] An integrated checker can tell one person with chest pressure to seek emergency help, while reassuring another with a minor, stable complaint that routine follow-up is appropriate. That reduces dangerous delay and unnecessary urgent-care use.
[P1.5] It also expands access. A patient who struggles to describe symptoms, cannot wait on hold, or fears wasting a clinician’s time can begin privately and immediately. A structured tool asks the same basic follow-up questions every time and can offer accessible language and formats. [P1.6] That frees scarce professional attention for patients who most need it.
[P1.7] AI can make mistakes, but that is an argument for approval and integration, not discouragement. Health systems can require privacy protection, testing across patient groups, clear uncertainty warnings, emergency overrides, audit trails, and easy escalation to a human. [P1.8] Judge the policy against the actual status quo. Approved AI offers earlier contact, more consistent routing, and accountable oversight; discouragement leaves patients waiting, guessing, and using unregulated substitutes.
