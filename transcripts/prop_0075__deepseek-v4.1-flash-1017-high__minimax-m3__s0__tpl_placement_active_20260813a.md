[P1.1] Anti-discrimination law rests on a simple promise: when an institution opens its doors, hires staff, sells goods, or provides services, it does not sort people by protected traits without a compelling reason. Religion can supply that reason—but only where the activity is itself religious. The proposition draws the line there.
[P1.2] A church choosing its pastor, a synagogue selecting a cantor, a mosque deciding who leads prayer, a seminary teaching doctrine—these are core religious functions. Anti-discrimination rules cannot meaningfully govern them without the state deciding who counts as a faithful minister or what counts as orthodox teaching. Exemptions there protect autonomy and prevent entanglement.
[P1.3] But the exemption should not follow the corporate logo into every commercial venture. If a church runs a coffee shop, gym, bookstore, or wedding venue open to the public, the person turned away or fired is not being excluded from a sacrament; they are being excluded from a job, a service, or a public accommodation. The institution is acting as a market participant, serving customers who have no say in its doctrine. Broadening the exemption to all "religious institution" activity converts a shield for worship into a license for commercial discrimination.
[P1.4] It also creates an obvious evasion: incorporate as a religious body, then claim immunity from ordinary workplace and consumer rules.
[P1.5] The narrow test protects everyone else's equal standing. A receptionist at a religiously affiliated business should not lose protection because her employer's mission statement mentions God. A customer should not be refused service at a shop because the owner's corporation is religious. The burden on religion in these settings is modest—run the commercial activity on ordinary fair terms—while the harm from exemption is concrete and dignitary.
[P1.6] So the question is not whether religious institutions get autonomy. They do, where religion is actually at stake. The question is whether "religious" becomes an all-purpose commercial immunity. The proposition says no—only that line keeps religious freedom from swallowing equality law.

[C1.1] The proposition creates a bright line that does not exist: worship on one side, commerce on the other. Yet religious institutions do not divide operations into sacramental and market sectors. A church’s childcare center, religious publisher, and bookstore may charge money, compete for services, and employ lay workers while remaining instruments of ministry. If an exemption from religious-discrimination rules is required to make those works possible, refusing it merely because money changes hands is not neutrality; it can force institutions to choose between their mission and equal treatment.
[C1.2] The coffee shop in P1.3 shows only that a public restaurant need not inherit every practice of the church. It does not establish that religious exemption should turn on whether an activity is “commercial.” Religious publishing, education, and healthcare can all be expressive. Conversely, selecting clergy and teachers is religious, but hiring a church’s bookkeeper or safeguarding employee is essential to that function. Once government must identify “core religious functions,” it must decide which theology defines the institution’s mission—a dispute PRO’s supposedly objective line cannot avoid.
[C1.3] Categorical exemptions also obscure the actual legal interest. Anti-discrimination rules can coexist with a specific exemption where the challenged rule burdens religious exercise and the exemption is tailored to it. A church seeking a religious-staff preference or a religious charity organizing volunteers may need protection even in a financially complex enterprise. The relevant questions are purpose, nexus, and tailoring, not whether a receipt was issued.
[C1.4] P1.4 rightly warns that a corporate label should not erase equality. But the answer is not a ban on exemptions for anything commercially organized; it is proof that a particular exemption is religiously necessary and narrowly drawn. The proposition both exempts less than religious exercise requires and asks courts to certify theology. Religious status should not grant immunity, but commercial activity should not automatically defeat a demonstrated exemption.
[C1.5] Reject the false sacred-commerce divide; require necessity and tailoring.

[P2.2] CON’s bookstore and childcare examples prove too little: a bookstore’s shelves can serve a ministry, but the cashier selling a novel is not exercising doctrine; a childcare center may teach faith, but its janitor is not chosen to propagate it. Tight tie means the role or service must carry the religious function, not merely support it financially.
[P2.3] That is also the answer to [C1.2]’s “courts must certify theology.” Any exemption requires line-drawing. CON’s own necessity-and-tailoring test requires judges to ask how central a practice is to a faith and whether the burden is substantial—far more theological than asking whether a job is clergy, doctrine-teaching, worship leadership, or religious instruction. Core function is role-based and administrable; it does not rank theologies. It asks what the institution is doing, not whether its theology is correct.
[P2.4] [C1.3]’s purpose/nexus/tailoring sounds modest, but in practice it is a litigation license: every commercial venture can assert that its mission is expressive and demand a court weigh its religious necessity. That is precisely the broad commercial immunity [P1.4] warned about, and it leaves employees and customers to guess which shop, gym, or venue is exempt.
[P2.5] The proposition already contains tailoring: exemptions are limited to the core. It protects the church’s pastor, the seminary’s doctrine teacher, the religious school’s faith instructor. CON says commercial activity should not automatically defeat an exemption; agreed—but it should not automatically create one either.
[P2.6] The tie to religious function must do the work.

The relevant question is whether a particular anti-discrimination rule substantially burdens genuine religious exercise, and whether the exemption is tailored to that burden. [C2.7] Reject the proposition: it makes the boundary turn on vague, government-defined “core” and “commercial,” rather than the exemption’s necessity and scope. That can underprotect religious exercise while enabling wholesale immunity.

[P4.1] A1: The standard is functional, not doctrinal: does the role’s essential duty consist in leading worship, teaching or selecting doctrine, providing pastoral care, or administering sacraments—or does it merely support those things? Adjudicators decide by ordinary evidence: written duties, actual practice, hiring criteria, and public representations. The institution bears the burden; it cannot self-certify. That is non-theological because the court asks what the role does, not whether the faith’s claims are true or how central a practice is to salvation.
[P4.2] Hard cases exist, but they are narrower than CON’s centrality inquiry.
[P4.3] A2: Exemptions are framed institutionally because institutions are the defendants and organize roles, but the right attaches only to a role’s core function. That prevents aggregation: a gym cannot bundle a “chaplain” with trainers and claim a discriminatory staffing regime; each challenged decision must independently meet the functional test. If a church enterprise relabels baristas as “ministry assistants,” actual duties expose pretext. Anti-discrimination law still governs all other roles.
[P4.4] CON’s fear is thus an enforcement problem, not a structural license.
[P4.5] This is why PRO wins overall. CON’s test has no objective stopping point: “substantial burden” and “tailoring” invite exactly the gym and coffee-shop claims, then require the court to weigh religious centrality—the theology CON accuses us of. And CON’s alternative has the same aggregation problem with fewer limits: an enterprise can assert an integrated mission across all staff and demand the court weigh it.
[P4.6] By contrast, our test places the burden on the institution and keeps commercial activity under equality law by default. The barista makes coffee; the doctrine editor shapes belief. That difference is administrable and morally relevant. A church can hire a pastor who shares its faith; it cannot turn a coffee shop into a church by assertion.
[P4.7] That is the correct settlement: autonomy where religion is the work, equality where it is not.

[C5.1] PRO never answers the decisive question: does a religious function justify exemption from a particular anti-discrimination rule? Its test tells us where religion is present, not which legal rule must yield. Even a pastor’s religious work does not show that a rule against discrimination in hiring or service substantially burdens it, or that the exemption sought is necessary. Role-based exemptions can immunize decisions unrelated to confession, while a claim-specific inquiry protects only what religious exercise requires.
[C5.2] Nor does P5.1 solve the problem. Applying PRO’s functional test still requires deciding whether an institution’s actual duties make an activity religious. That may be empirically grounded, but it remains a judgment about religious character. CON accepts that no line avoids some characterization; we reject converting it into automatic legal immunity.
[C5.3] The decisive principle is necessity and narrow tailoring: identify the challenged rule, show a concrete burden on religious exercise, and exempt no more than required. The proposition’s role-based line enables a shortcut from “this is ministry” to “therefore this discrimination is protected.” It replaces the question whether the particular rule unjustifiably burdens religion with the less demanding question of what the institution is doing. That is why the proposition should be rejected.
