[P1.1] Driving is a hands-and-eyes task. Core controls must be operable without looking away. A physical knob or button can be found by touch, distinguished by shape and edge, and operated by muscle memory. That is not a preference, it is a safety architecture.
[P1.2] A touchscreen fails that architecture for core functions. Flat glass offers no tactile landmark. To change temperature or turn on defrost you must look, find an icon, tap through a menu, confirm the input. Each step pulls vision from the road and hands from a stable position.
[P1.3] In vibration, sunlight glare, or winter gloves, failure rate rises. This is why driver distraction research consistently finds touchscreen climate tasks take much longer eyes-off-road than a simple dial.
[P1.4] Consider what "core" actually means in this motion. Defrost is not comfort; when your windshield flash-fogs in rain, you need it in one second, not three taps deep under Climate. Hazard lights are legally required to be instantly accessible for a reason — you hit them when a crash is ahead of you. Routing them through software puts a life-safety control behind boot time, lag, or a frozen screen.
[P1.5] Physical buttons also work when tech doesn't. They work at -20 degrees before the LCD warms up, with wet hands, with work gloves, when the infotainment crashes. They are cheaper to operate blind and far more inclusive for older drivers or anyone with reduced visual acuity.
[P1.6] Touchscreens are great for navigation, media, and settings. For the handful of functions you use while actively trying to keep the car on the road, automakers should keep real controls. That is why safety programs are now moving back that way.
[P1.7] The reason we drifted toward all-screen interiors is manufacturing simplicity, not driver benefit. Keeping buttons for core functions corrects that misincentive and restores the hierarchy: safety first, styling second.
