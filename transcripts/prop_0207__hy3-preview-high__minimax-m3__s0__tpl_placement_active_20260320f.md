[P1.1] Universal fuel subsidies are a fiscal trap that drains national budgets to benefit the wealthy more than the poor. Because these subsidies lower prices for everyone, the lion’s share of the savings flows to urban households, industrial fleets, and middle-class drivers who consume the most fuel. Phasing them out in favor of targeted cash transfers is not just a budgetary tweak; it is a fundamental shift toward equity and economic sanity.
[P1.2] Price controls and open-ended subsidies create massive distortions. They encourage the waste of valuable resources, suppress investment in green energy, and often lead to chronic shortages or a thriving black market. When a government tries to artificially suppress the price of fuel, it inevitably faces a ballooning fiscal deficit or a mountain of debt that eventually crashes the economy, taking the poor down with it. Cash transfers, by contrast, allow the market to find its natural price while directly protecting the purchasing power of the most vulnerable citizens.
[P1.3] The "targeted" aspect is the key to social justice. Universal subsidies are effectively a regressive tax on the state, where the rich receive the largest handouts. By replacing them with direct, data-driven cash transfers, governments can provide significantly more help to the people who actually need it, while simultaneously freeing up billions of dollars for education, healthcare, or infrastructure. This approach respects the agency of the poor, allowing them to decide how to spend their money rather than forcing them to consume a specific, subsidized commodity.
[P1.4] It is a more honest, transparent, and effective way to manage public funds in a volatile global economy.

[C1.1] The motion sounds tidy on paper: swap a leaky fuel subsidy for a precise cash transfer, and the poor come out ahead. But the proposition collapses the moment we ask who actually delivers the cash, to whom, and whether it survives the next price shock.
[C1.2] First, targeting is a fantasy in most of the countries where fuel subsidies actually matter. The registries don't exist. Identity documents are incomplete. Street-level enumeration is expensive and politically charged, which is why India's biometric ID system took a decade and still excludes millions.
[C1.3] When Indonesia expanded its targeted cash program alongside fuel subsidy reform, leakage to the non-poor exceeded 40 percent in early rounds. Nigeria's fuel subsidy removal in 2023 was paired with cash transfers that, by the government's own admission, reached only a fraction of intended households. A "targeted" transfer is only as good as the targeting machine, and that machine is missing.
[C1.4] Second, cash transfers are denominated in local currency, while fuel is priced in dollars. When oil markets spike, the transfer's real value evaporates overnight. A universal fuel subsidy, by contrast, automatically scales with the price. That is not a bug; it is a built-in stabilizer for households that cannot hedge.
[C1.5] Third, the political economy runs the wrong direction. Fuel subsidies are visible, daily, and politically expensive to cut, which is precisely why they survive populist pressure. Cash transfers are invisible, bureaucratic, and the first line item squeezed in a fiscal crunch. Ghana, Kenya, and Zambia have all watched nominal transfer values stagnate while fuel prices doubled.
[C1.6] The motion asks governments to trade an automatic, universal stabilizer for an administrative miracle that has consistently underperformed. That is not reform; it is a leap into the dark.

[P2.1] Your claim that imperfect targeting dooms cash transfers ignores the abysmal baseline of universal fuel subsidies. These programs deliver nearly zero benefit to the poorest households that cannot afford vehicles or access formal fuel supply chains, even as they pay for those subsidies via higher taxes and foregone public spending. Even if a significant share of transfer funds leak to non-poor households, the remaining flow still delivers far more direct support to the bottom 40% of the income distribution than the status quo, which funnels the vast majority of benefits to wealthy urban drivers and industrial users.
[P2.2] Targeting gaps are a reason to invest in administrative capacity, not to entrench a regressive handout that excludes the most vulnerable by design.
[P2.3] You also frame universal subsidies’ automatic scaling with oil prices as a feature, but it is a fiscal trap that forces governments to divert funds from essential services every time global markets spike. Cash transfers can be indexed to either fuel prices or broader consumer inflation, protecting households against both direct fuel costs and the cascading price hikes for food and transport that hit the poor hardest, while a universal subsidy only cushions direct fuel costs and leaves households exposed to wider inflation it does nothing to address.
[P2.4] Your political economy argument relies on a flawed premise: that cash transfers are inherently more vulnerable to cuts. That only holds if transfers are not structured as statutory, indexed entitlements. Well-designed targeted programs deliver visible, direct benefits to tens of millions of low-income voters, creating a powerful political constituency universal fuel subsidies never build. Swapping regressive subsidies for targeted transfers shifts power to the poor, rather than leaving them to subsidize the consumption of the wealthy.
