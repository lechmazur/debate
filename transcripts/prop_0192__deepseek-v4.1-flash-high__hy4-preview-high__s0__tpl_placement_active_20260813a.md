[P1.1] Legal personhood is not a certificate of humanity. It is a legal shield for a being whose own interests can be wronged. We give it to corporations; we withhold it from animals who recognize themselves, mourn their dead, use tools, coordinate hunts, and form lifelong bonds. That inconsistency is the case.
[P1.2] The proposition doesn't demand voting or driving. It demands basic negative rights: not to be owned, confined, bred, traded, or forced into performances for human profit. A right against captivity is not a right to vote; it is a right not to be someone's property. Those are precisely the harms captivity and exploitation inflict.
[P1.3] Great apes, cetaceans, elephants are not borderline cases. They have complex brains, long memories, social learning, and evidence of self-directed awareness. A chimpanzee raised in a lab, an orca in a concrete tank, an elephant in a touring circus suffers not just pain but deprivation of autonomy, relationship, and range. A being capable of anticipating, choosing, and grieving is not a resource.
[P1.4] CON will warn that personhood is a slippery slope or that rights conflict with conservation. But law already handles graduated personhood: children, incapacitated adults, corporations. Guardians can represent them. Rights against captivity don't stop habitat protection or veterinary care; they stop the industries that treat them as property.
[P1.5] If we keep them as things, their interests are legally invisible.
[P1.6] Ask what justifies the line. Not intelligence alone, because humans with cognitive disabilities still have rights. Not species, because species is a biological fact, not a moral credential. The relevant capacity is a subjective life that can go better or worse.
[P1.7] These animals have it. Once we see that, captivity for entertainment is not kindness; it is imprisonment. Exploitation is not use; it is ownership. That does not make them human; it makes them rightsholders.
[P1.8] The basic minimum is personhood.

The motion asks, in effect, whether the cage is kind, but rights are trumps, not welfare balances. [C1.6] Finally, legal personhood is not a decoration; it is a bundle of standing, duties, and accountability. [P2.4] If those other species also qualify, that is an argument for considering them, not for denying these.
[P2.5] Finally, CON treats guardianship as human control. But the alternative is owner control. A guardian must act for the ward, not profit from it. [P2.6] If an orca is property, no welfare rule truly stops the tank. If an orca is a person, the tank must be justified—and it cannot be.
[P2.7] CON's fallback is regulation. But regulation without personhood leaves the fundamental status untouched: the animal is an object that can be bought, sold, and bred. [P2.8] Personhood makes exploitation the exception to be justified, not the baseline.
