[P1.1] The euro is often treated as the EU’s crowning achievement, but it is better understood as its self-inflicted fragility machine. A currency union without a fiscal union forces profoundly different economies—Germany and Greece, Finland and Portugal—to live under one interest rate and one exchange rate. When shocks hit, the exchange rate cannot absorb them, so the burden falls on wages, employment, and public budgets. That is not a defect in execution; it is the design.
[P1.2] The economic damage is clearest where the euro was supposed to deliver convergence. Greece’s debt crisis became an existential EU crisis because Athens was trapped in a currency it could not issue and could not devalue. Italy, locked into the euro, lost the ability to regain competitiveness against Germany, turning ordinary sluggish growth into a permanent debt-and-austerity standoff. These were not isolated failures; they were the predictable result of removing national adjustment mechanisms without creating a shared treasury.
[P1.3] The deeper harm is political. The euro converted economic divergence into moralized conflict between a supposedly prudent north and a supposedly profligate south. Bailouts, Troika conditions, and internal devaluation gave anti-EU movements a concrete grievance: Brussels forcing pension cuts and labor deregulation because the currency made domestic adjustments impossible. The resulting bitterness did more to weaken the Union than any trade or prestige gain from a common currency could offset.
[P1.4] The single market, free movement, enlargement, and regulation built European strength before the euro and could have deepened without it. A stronger EU is one with fewer existential crises, less nationalist resentment, and more capacity to absorb asymmetric shocks. The euro removed exactly those shock absorbers. The Union would not merely have avoided the eurozone’s serial emergencies; it would be more economically resilient and politically cohesive today.

[C1.2] France and Italy did not enjoy monetary sovereignty then—they imported Bundesbank policy with no vote and no voice. The euro did not strip these countries of a working adjustment tool; it replaced a rigged system with a shared one, giving Portugal and Finland seats at the ECB table that Frankfurt never offered.
[C1.3] Nor could the single market have "deepened without it." A genuine common market with volatile internal exchange rates invites competitive devaluation—Italy stealing German market share overnight by printing lira—which is precisely the beggar-thy-neighbor dynamic that poisoned interwar Europe and that Germany would never have tolerated indefinitely. The euro is the single market's insurance policy against its own unraveling.
[C1.4] The crises PRO catalogs prove the opposite of fragility. The eurozone absorbed a sovereign debt crisis, a banking crisis, and a pandemic, and each time built institutions the EU otherwise never would have: the ESM, banking union, the ECB as genuine lender of last resort, and—decisively—NextGenerationEU, the first common European borrowing, issued in euros and unthinkable without them.
[C1.5] Meanwhile the euro underwrites Europe's geopolitical weight: it is the world's second reserve currency, the reason EU financial sanctions on Russia had teeth, and the anchor that pulled the Baltics westward during the very crisis PRO calls existential.
[C1.6] And the political verdict is in. Greece, at the bottom of austerity, chose overwhelmingly to stay. Support for the euro across the eurozone sits near record highs. Citizens who lived through the storm judged the ship worth keeping.
[C1.7] The motion asks you to overrule them.
