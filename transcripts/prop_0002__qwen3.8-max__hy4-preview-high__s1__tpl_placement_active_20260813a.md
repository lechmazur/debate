[P1.1] Housing affordability is won and lost in the gap between how many homes people need and how many homes the law lets them build. Two decades ago, American cities were entering a long era of rising demand—job growth concentrated in metros, households smaller, migration patterns shifting, construction costs already sensitive to land scarcity. If cities had legalized denser housing then—duplexes, townhomes, mid-rise apartments near transit and jobs—they would not have produced an instant price collapse. They would have done something more important: prevented the chronic shortage that now makes every new rent increase feel like a crisis.
[P1.2] The mechanism is basic but powerful. When only a few units can be built where demand is high, those units compete for scarce land, and land prices get baked into rents and mortgages. Legalizing density spreads the opportunity. More permitted sites mean more realistic supply, less bidding frenzy, and less power for landlords to raise rents because vacant alternatives are scarce.
[P1.3] It also allows filtering: newer and denser homes absorb demand, so older homes are less relentlessly bid up. Over twenty years, those effects compound. A city that adds tens of thousands of extra units over time is not just marginally different; it has a different vacancy rate, a calmer rental market, and fewer households forced into overcrowding or long commutes.
[P1.4] The timing matters most. Housing is durable. A shortage created in the early 2000s is still being paid for today through high rents, overcrowded units, and exclusionary land values. Had density been legal earlier, the construction pipeline, local politics, and household expectations would have adapted gradually instead of facing today’s desperate backlog.
[P1.5] Opponents may say new market-rate units are expensive, but the alternative—artificial scarcity—makes everything expensive. The question is not whether every unit built would be cheap, but whether a larger supply would reduce the price pressure on millions of households. CON cannot answer how preserving scarcity makes homes cheaper.

[C1.1] Everyone agrees American cities are expensive. Legalization is ink; actual supply requires capital, labor, and a functioning credit market. Rewind to the early 2000s, the era this motion imagines.
[C1.2] If cities had upzoned then, the 2008 financial crisis would have erased the pipeline anyway. The collapse destroyed condo financing, wiped out small builders, and froze multifamily lending for years. You cannot build your way through a credit crash, so the story that upzoning would have prevented today's shortage is a counterfactual fairy tale.
[C1.3] Even in the recovery that followed, the binding constraint was never the zoning code. Ultra-low interest rates and quantitative easing channeled enormous cheap capital into real estate. That money bid up the price of land and existing apartments regardless of how many units were permitted. Worse, legalizing density raises the value of land because owners capture the right to build more, so developers pay more for lots, and those land costs are passed into rents.
[C1.4] Upzoning without capturing the land value can enrich landowners and leave market-rate rents unchanged or higher.
[C1.5] Finally, twenty years is not enough for filtering to rescue affordability. New market-rate apartments in desirable cities are built for high earners; they absorb demand at the top and often draw more high-income migrants, while the rent burden of lower-income households tracks stagnant wages and the financialization of housing. Affordability is a ratio of rents to incomes, not a unit count. Until PRO explains how extra permits in 2004 survive the 2008 crash, offset land speculation, and outpace wage stagnation, their supply-only model fails.
[C1.6] The shortage is real, but legalization two decades ago would not have delivered the affordability they promise.
