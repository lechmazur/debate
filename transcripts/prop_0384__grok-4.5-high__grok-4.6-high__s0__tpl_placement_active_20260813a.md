[P1.1] Public schools serve children of every faith and of none. The fair rule is simple: optional on-campus religious instruction belongs only when the school also offers comparable secular alternatives and when participation is unmistakably voluntary. That package protects conscience without turning the school day into a preference contest.
[P1.2] Coercion is the real danger, not belief itself. When the only released option is religious, or when peers and schedules make declining costly, “optional” becomes nominal. Requiring a genuine secular counterpart—ethics discussion, community service seminar, supervised study, or cultural literacy—removes the empty-chair stigma and keeps the choice real. Clear voluntariness means parental consent, no academic penalty, no staff pressure, and no grading that rewards attendance.
[P1.3] Under those limits, the school neither advances nor inhibits religion; it simply refuses to force families into a binary of leave-campus disruption or silent exclusion.
[P1.4] Logistics matter. Pulling children off campus for instruction fragments the day, raises safety and transportation burdens, and signals that religious practice is external to civic life. Hosting the option on campus under parity conditions treats families evenhandedly while keeping supervision continuous. A Catholic, Jewish, Muslim, or evangelical student can receive formation nearby; a secular classmate receives an equally legitimate alternative instead of idle free time that advertises second-class status.
[P1.5] This standard also blocks preferential entanglement. Unconditional access would invite one tradition to capture prime slots or official prestige. The twin conditions—comparable secular options plus clear voluntariness—enforce neutrality as a practical test rather than a slogan. Schools already manage electives, clubs, and counseling without establishment.
[P1.6] The same institutional competence can manage this arrangement.
[P1.7] Opponents who demand an absolute ban ignore accommodation that costs no one their dignity. Opponents who demand religious access without parity ignore the students left behind. The proposition draws the only line that respects both free exercise and equal citizenship: allow the option, but only when the alternative is real and the choice is free.
