[P1.1] The air inside classrooms, offices, and clinics is not a private matter. It is shared, breathed by everyone in the room, and its quality is currently governed almost entirely by voluntary guidance that building owners can ignore without consequence. That gap between what we know about indoor air and what we require of it is the case for enforceable standards.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism problem: indoor air quality is a classic collective-action failure. A landlord who skimps on ventilation saves money immediately; the costs land on tenants, students, and workers who breathe stale, pathogen-laden, or pollutant-heavy air and get sick, underperform, or both. Voluntary guidelines cannot fix this because the party bearing the cost is never the party making the decision. Only an enforceable floor changes the incentive.
[P1.3] The evidence for harm is not exotic. Elevated carbon dioxide and poor ventilation in classrooms are consistently linked to reduced cognitive performance and attention in children. Poorly ventilated offices see higher rates of respiratory illness and absenteeism. During respiratory disease outbreaks, buildings with weak air exchange become transmission hotspots; buildings with strong ventilation and filtration do not.
[P1.4] These are not marginal effects when you scale them across every school and workplace in a country.
[P1.5] Enforceability matters because building codes and safety rules already work this way for everything else we consider serious: fire exits, electrical wiring, structural load. Nobody argues fire codes should be "recommended best practice." We accept mandatory minimums precisely because the harm from noncompliance is invisible until it isn't, and because market pressure alone doesn't reliably produce safe buildings, especially in public schools with underfunded maintenance budgets or workplaces with weak bargaining power for employees.
[P1.6] This is also a straightforward equity issue. Well-resourced private schools and corporate headquarters already have excellent HVAC systems because they can afford it and want to attract families or talent. [C1.4] Finally, the PRO case ignores the existence of market-driven and voluntary improvements. [C1.5] We should empower building managers with better tools and information, not threaten them with state-mandated penalties that they cannot afford to meet.
