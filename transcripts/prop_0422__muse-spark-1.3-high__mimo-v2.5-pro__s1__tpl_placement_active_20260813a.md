[P1.1] Imagine boarding a packed flight and learning your 4-year-old is assigned row 22 while you are in row 14. That is not an inconvenience. It is a safety failure.
[P1.2] Young children cannot follow crew instructions alone, fasten oxygen masks alone, evacuate alone, or manage a medical episode alone. Separating them from a parent does not just scare the child; it transfers supervision to flight attendants who have a cabin to manage and to unwilling strangers who did not consent to be caretakers. In an emergency, seconds spent searching for a child cost lives. No business model justifies that risk.
[P1.3] This separation is not natural scarcity. It is manufactured. Airlines deliberately scatter standard seats and then charge to reunite families — often tens of dollars per person per leg. Parents face a ransom choice: pay up or gamble your toddler sits alone.
[P1.4] That is coercion, not choice, because families cannot leave a small child home or drive across an ocean. Markets work only when buyers can walk away.
[P1.5] Requiring adjacent seating at no extra charge fixes this with minimal cost. The policy need not promise first-row legroom, entire extended families together, or first-come upgrades. It means when a ticket for a small child and accompanying adult is booked together, the carrier must provide side-by-side standard seats where available, without forcing a fare jump to basic-plus or equivalent. Computers already do this in seconds; several carriers already do it voluntarily.
[P1.6] Everyone else gains too. A supervised child cries less, needs less crew time, and disturbs neighbors less. Rail is even simpler, where cars can be assigned together with no weight or evacuation penalty.
[P1.7] Safety first, extortion last: keep young children with their adults.
