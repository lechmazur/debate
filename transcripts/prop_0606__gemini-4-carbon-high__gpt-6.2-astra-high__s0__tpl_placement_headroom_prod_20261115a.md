[P1.1] Patents exist to reward innovation, not to turn human survival into a luxury good. Essential medicines—curated priority treatments like HIV antiretrovirals, vital cancer drugs, or curative Hepatitis C therapies—are not ordinary consumer products. Because demand for survival is inelastic, monopoly pricing does not merely shift consumer preferences; it rations life by wealth and drains public health budgets. Routinely, pharmaceutical firms maximize profits in unequal markets by pricing out most patients to extract massive margins from a wealthy fraction or desperate public insurers.
[P1.2] Treating access to these medicines as valid grounds for compulsory licensing whenever prices cross an affordability line restores the social contract of intellectual property: states protect private monopolies only while breakthroughs remain reachable.
[P1.3] Crucially, a defined affordability threshold fixes the fatal flaw of the status quo. International trade rules already permit compulsory licensing on paper, yet countries rarely invoke it because proving an ad-hoc emergency invites diplomatic pressure, trade retaliation, and corporate litigation. Better still, this rule disciplines prices before a single generic pill is manufactured.
[P1.4] Knowing that crossing the threshold unlocks generic competition, patent holders gain every incentive to price within reach voluntarily and keep exclusivity. When they refuse, licensed generics drive prices toward production cost while still paying the originator a fair royalty.

Scope is strictly bounded to essential medicines, not the broader pharmaceutical market, and companies still collect royalties rather than suffering confiscation. In poorer nations, high-volume royalties from newly treated millions beat zero revenue from patients priced out anyway; in wealthier systems, preventing extortion stops single drugs from cannibalizing entire healthcare budgets. Clear guardrails reward genuine progress without holding human lives hostage.
