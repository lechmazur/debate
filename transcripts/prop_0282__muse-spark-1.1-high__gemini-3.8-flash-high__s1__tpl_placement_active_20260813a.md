[C1.1] Crime is not private intellectual property; it is a public wrong against society, adjudicated in open courtrooms on the public record. [C1.2] First, the boundary between "entertainment" and public-interest journalism is legally and functionally indivisible. True-crime media—from investigative audio series that exposed prosecutorial misconduct to documentaries that mobilized public pressure to solve cold cases—serves an indispensable watchdog function. It exposes police incompetence, highlights systemic racial and socio-economic biases in policing, and questions questionable convictions. You cannot restrict the dramatization of crime without chilling the investigation of crime.
[C1.3] Second, PRO’s proposed remedies are deeply dangerous. Imposing mandatory waiting periods or requiring family consent effectively privatizes the criminal justice system. Consider the precedent: if family consent were legally required, stories involving domestic homicide, familial abuse, or cases where a family protects an influential perpetrator would never see the light of day. Grieving families deserve profound personal sympathy, but granting private citizens the power to suppress public facts creates an intolerable regime of prior restraint.
[C1.4] Third, existing legal and market guardrails already function. Defamation, intrusion upon seclusion, harassment laws, and intentional infliction of emotional distress already penalize malicious fabrications and unlawful conduct. Meanwhile, public debate and audience pushback consistently discipline creators and platforms when dramatizations cross the line of decency, prompting shifts in industry standards without inviting state censorship.
[C1.5] PRO suggests we can cleanly target tastelessness without damaging free expression. But once government or regulatory bodies dictate who may tell true stories, which public records can be depicted, or how quickly citizens can examine the justice system, we do not protect victims. We merely shield the justice system from public scrutiny.
