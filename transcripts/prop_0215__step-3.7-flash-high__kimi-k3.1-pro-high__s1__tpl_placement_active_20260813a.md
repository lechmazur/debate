[P1.1] When an institution actively designs and operates a system of forced assimilation, child abuse, and cultural erasure, it bears an unshakable obligation to fund the repair of the harms it caused—full stop. Former state churches and religious orders that ran residential schools and similar abusive systems are not passive bystanders to historical injustice: they were the primary on-the-ground operators of these atrocities, often backed by state funding, land grants, and legal authority. Take the Canadian Indian residential school system, where the Catholic Church operated roughly 60% of the 139 schools, with other Christian denominations running most of the rest.
[P1.2] Children were stripped of their languages, subjected to physical and sexual abuse, and forced into unpaid labor that lined the coffers of these religious institutions. Decades later, unmarked graves are still being found at former school sites, Indigenous communities still lack access to clean water and mental health care, and intergenerational trauma continues to devastate families. These same religious orders hold billions in global assets, while survivors and their descendants are left to pay the price for harms their institutions inflicted.
[P1.3] Critics will claim these are “old sins” that current church members should not be held responsible for, but this argument collapses on contact. These are not defunct, amorphous historical entities: they are continuous legal bodies that still exist, still hold the assets they acquired through the residential school system, and have never been held fully accountable for their role in the genocide of Indigenous peoples. Reparative funds are not punishment for individual believers: they are a demand that the institutions that caused the harm bear the cost of fixing it, rather than dumping that burden on harmed communities or general taxpayers.
[P1.4] The alternative is to let these institutions profit from their atrocities indefinitely, while the people they abused are left to pick up the pieces.

[C1.1] Survivors deserve enforceable compensation, truthful records, and sustained support. But those duties do not validate this motion’s categorical rule: that every former state church or religious order centrally involved should contribute to a reparative fund. Specific institutions should honor judgments and negotiated settlements. A general financial obligation based on institutional identity is a different—and unjust—claim.
[C1.2] First, “central role” is not a adequate principle of liability. Residential-school systems varied across countries, schools, and periods. Governments often designed assimilation policies, compelled attendance, supplied funding, and exercised regulatory authority; religious bodies’ conduct and autonomy varied. Some orders directly committed or concealed abuse; others operated within arrangements whose legal and financial responsibilities must be examined separately.
[C1.3] Fair contribution requires evidence of what each entity did, what state immunity or indemnity applied, and what obligations already exist. PRO’s demand skips that process and treats accusation by category as apportionment.
[C1.4] Second, PRO’s continuity argument proves too much. The assertion that these are simply “continuous legal bodies” holding profits from abuse cannot be assumed across former state churches, dissolved orders, merged dioceses, and legally distinct local institutions. A shared denomination does not create one treasury or one legal person. Global religious assets are not automatically available to satisfy a particular order’s obligations.
[C1.5] Taking money without tracing responsibility can mean reducing today’s food programs, shelters, or pensions for people who neither committed abuse nor inherited its proceeds. Existing liable entities should not escape; but neither should liability be manufactured by association.
[C1.6] Third, an open-ended contribution duty can obstruct rather than secure repair. It invites years of dispute over institutional boundaries and historical shares while offering no guaranteed amount, timetable, or survivor control. Worse, governments can present church contributions as evidence that their own sovereign responsibility has been discharged. The choice is not, as PRO claims, between institutional payments and abandoning survivors to taxpayers.
[C1.7] Courts, settlements, and state-guaranteed reparations can allocate responsibility without this blanket rule.
[C1.8] The motion replaces demonstrable obligations with collective institutional assessment. Justice requires the former, not the latter.

[P2.1] CON’s core objections rest on a misreading of the motion as punitive collective punishment, when it is a baseline obligation tied directly to operational culpability. The motion does not skip due process—it sets a threshold for who owes what, leaving room for case-by-case apportionment of specific amounts, which CON’s preferred ad hoc settlements have failed to deliver.
[P2.2] Look to Canada, where the Catholic Church, which ran 60% of residential schools, leveraged legal loopholes to avoid paying its agreed contribution to the national settlement, leaving survivors shortchanged for years. That failure is not an argument against the motion—it is proof that voluntary, case-by-case obligations are insufficient, and that calls for granular evidence of every entity’s role are often stalling tactics designed to delay justice indefinitely. CON’s continuity argument also falls flat: these are not dissolved, amorphous historical entities.
[P2.3] The Catholic Church in Canada is a single continuous legal body that still holds billions in assets acquired through the residential school system, assets legally separate from local parish operating funds. Redirecting those surplus holdings will not reduce food programs or shelters for vulnerable people, as CON claims—it will return wealth extracted from abused children to the communities harmed.
[P2.4] Finally, CON’s fear that church contributions will let governments off the hook is a false dichotomy. The motion does not preclude holding states accountable for designing and funding these systems; it only ensures complicit religious institutions cannot hide behind state responsibility to avoid their own debts. The alternative CON offers—relying on voluntary settlements and court judgments—has already left survivors waiting for justice for decades.
