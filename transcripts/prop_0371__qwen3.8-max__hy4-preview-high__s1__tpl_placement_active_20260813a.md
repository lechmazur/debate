[P1.1] A ticket should buy transportation for a passenger and a reasonable carry-on, not a seat plus a series of checkout surprises. The proposition requires airlines to include one baseline carry-on—say a personal item plus a small overhead bag—before any ancillary fees. That modest rule fixes a market failure, not the market.
[P1.2] Unbundled baggage pricing works only when customers can compare fares, understand what is included, and decline the extra. With carry-ons, they cannot. A passenger traveling for work, a family visit, or a weekend trip usually cannot show up empty-handed. Airlines know this, so the “basic” fare becomes a loss leader and the real price is revealed at the baggage step.
[P1.3] The result is drip pricing: advertised fares look competitive, but the ticket that actually meets ordinary needs costs more. This harms consumers and distorts competition, because carriers that rely on fragmentation can undercut carriers with honest all-in prices while charging more for the same practical trip.
[P1.4] A baseline also improves operations and dignity. When every carry-on is a negotiated upcharge or gate-check lottery, boarding slows, disputes rise, and passengers are pressured into checking bags they should be able to keep. A clear included allowance lets travelers plan, reduces last-minute gate congestion, and makes boarding more predictable.
[P1.5] This does not require free checked bags or unlimited luggage. Airlines may still charge for extra, oversized, or heavy items, and they may still differentiate products. The rule simply says the base ticket must include the minimum luggage a reasonable traveler needs. That is the line between legitimate choice and exploitative fragmentation.
[P1.6] If the price of a plane ticket is not allowed to mean what it says, consumers lose and honest competitors lose. Require the baseline, and let competition occur over real service, not over which airline can hide the cost of bringing a bag aboard.
