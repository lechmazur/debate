[P1.1] England did not stop building onshore wind because it stopped being good. It stopped because planning rules since 2015 gave a single local objection effective veto power. Across Europe the same story repeats: projects spend half a decade or more in permitting limbo while governments declare climate emergencies. If every locality can say no, nationally we always say no. You cannot keep both systems.
[P1.2] Onshore wind remains the cheapest new electricity we have in the British Isles and across much of northern Europe, and the fastest to build. When gas prices exploded after Russia invaded Ukraine, every household paid for the veto. Cheaper, homegrown electrons were replaced by imported, volatile gas. Keeping hard restrictions to appease local resistance is a direct transfer from bill-payers and taxpayers to protect a view.
[P1.3] It is the most expensive form of NIMBYism.
[P1.4] The same logic applies to the grid. The UK now has a queue for grid connection stretching years into the future, and Germany cannot move cheap northern wind to southern factories. Without new lines and substations, even approved turbines are turned off and consumers pay twice - once for constraint payments to wind farms not to generate, once for gas to replace them. Opponents of pylons talk about landscape, but the landscape is already being reshaped by climate change, by price shocks, and by energy dependence on autocrats.
[P1.5] One impact is visible and local; the others are massive and national.
[P1.6] This is not about ignoring communities. It is about who decides. Parliament sets legally binding carbon budgets and security strategy. A parish council should not be able to nullify that for the whole country.
[P1.7] Relaxing restrictions does not silence people; it stops a small, often well-organised minority from imposing blackout risk and high bills on everyone else. The answer is to remove the veto, replace it with consultation and fair compensation - community ownership stakes, bill discounts, local infrastructure - but make clear that yes means yes.

If onshore wind is as cheap and beneficial as claimed, properly negotiated terms should win support.
[C1.6] Removing the veto simply allows developers and governments to bypass negotiation and impose poor deals. That is not climate leadership; it is democratic failure dressed in green.
