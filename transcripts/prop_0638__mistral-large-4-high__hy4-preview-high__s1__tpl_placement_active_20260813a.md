[P1.1] Fundamental science operates on decadal timescales, yet our funding architecture forces it into three-year sprints. This mismatch does not merely inconvenience researchers; it systematically selects for mediocrity. When a lab must justify its existence every funding cycle, it cannot pursue the high-risk, high-reward questions that define scientific progress.
[P1.2] Instead, review panels reward safe, incremental proposals with predictable outcomes. The result is a research ecosystem optimized for grant-writing, not discovery.
[P1.3] Consider the alternative: a block grant gives a university or institute stable resources over five to ten years. This stability buys something competitive grants cannot—the freedom to fail. It allows a principal investigator to pivot when data contradicts the hypothesis, to mentor a graduate student through a five-year project, or to maintain expensive infrastructure that serves multiple lines of inquiry. The overhead costs collapse; scientists spend their time in the lab, not drafting proposals.
[P1.4] More importantly, institutions can take intellectual risks that short-cycle review panels reflexively punish as "unproductive" or "off-topic."
[P1.5] The proposition accepts a trade-off: less granular accountability per dollar. But accountability in science is not a ledger of deliverables audited annually. It is the ruthless, slow process of peer scrutiny, replication, and the long market of ideas. A block grant holds an institution accountable for its reputation over a decade; a project grant holds a researcher accountable for a milestone report every eighteen months.
[P1.6] The first produces transformative science; the second produces safe, incremental papers that nobody cites.
[P1.7] We are not arguing for a blank check. We are arguing that the unit of accountability should match the unit of discovery. When we fund people and institutions for the long haul, we stop paying the tax of constant proposal-writing and start harvesting the compound interest of sustained inquiry. The choice is between myopic oversight that strangles the goose laying golden eggs, and patient stewardship that lets science breathe.

[C1.1] The case for replacing competitive grants with block grants treats accountability as an annoyance and stability as a substitute for judgment. It is neither. The proposition asks us to accept "less accountability per dollar," yet its own premise—fundamental science is unpredictable—destroys that bargain. Uncertainty demands a portfolio, not a pension.
[C1.2] Because no one can know in advance which inquiry will matter, the system must retain the ability to move money toward emerging promise and away from stagnation. A ten-year block grant cannot do that. It locks resources into today's institutional map; short-cycle competition lets resources follow ideas across people and places.
[C1.3] Block grants do not deliver "the freedom to fail" [P1.3]; they create a freedom not to be judged. Failure is only valuable if someone can identify it and redeploy the money. In a decade-long block-funded institute, reputational accountability [P1.5] is too slow and too clubby. Reputation is backward-looking; it rewards yesterday's breakthroughs, not tomorrow's questions.
[C1.4] Institutions protect their brand by protecting senior stars and established programs, not by admitting a cherished line has died. New entrants, junior researchers, and risky interdisciplinary work lose when budgets are settled by internal hierarchy rather than external competition.
[C1.5] Nor is competition the enemy of risk. Competitive project grants are staged financing. They force a hypothesis to survive scrutiny, then let evidence unlock the next phase. That is why bold, cross-cutting work can win: panels can back a risky idea with a milestone rather than bet a decade of overhead on it.
[C1.6] If current panels are too incremental, the fix is to reform peer review—make competitive awards longer, create high-risk tracks, judge trajectories—not to abolish competition.
[C1.7] Replacing project grants with block grants does not buy serendipity. It buys inertia, and it charges taxpayers for the privilege of not looking. It mistakes a quiet backwater for a still pond where discovery can surface.

[P2.1] The opponent mistakes a false choice for a hard trade-off. They frame block grants as a "pension" that locks in today's map [C1.2], while competitive grants are a "portfolio" that follows ideas. But this inverts reality. Competitive project grants follow proposal-writing capacity and established reputations; they systematically favor researchers with preliminary data and institutional infrastructure, not the untested questions that drive breakthroughs.
[P2.2] A block grant, by contrast, is a portfolio at the institutional level—a diversified bet on human capital that lets money move internally toward whatever inquiry proves fruitful.
[P2.3] The claim that block grants create "freedom not to be judged" [C1.3] misunderstands where accountability lives. It shifts scrutiny from the proposal—a document written before the research begins—to the sustained record of discovery. Peer review does not disappear; it operates over years on published work, citations, and the institution's evolving reputation. Study sections are hardly paragons of openness: they are small, overlapping communities that reward fashionable techniques.
[P2.4] The "clubby" critique applies to both systems; the difference is that block grants judge outcomes over a decade, while project grants judge promises every two years.
[P2.5] The opponent calls competitive grants "staged financing" [C1.5] that forces hypotheses to survive scrutiny. But stage-gating is precisely what kills transformative science. A hypothesis requiring seven years to test cannot survive a three-year milestone review; researchers learn to design projects that fit the grant cycle, not the question. This is the selection effect that produces safe, incremental papers.
[P2.6] Finally, the suggestion to "reform peer review" [C1.6] concedes the structural point: as long as funding is short-cycle, the incentive to write safe proposals remains. We need not choose between accountability and stability; we need accountability matched to the timescale of discovery.
