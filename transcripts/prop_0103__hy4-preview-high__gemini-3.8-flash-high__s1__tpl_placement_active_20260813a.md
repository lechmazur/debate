[P1.1] A background check at the cash register asks one question: does this buyer show up in a database today? That is a checkpoint, not a standard. Point-of-sale screening is reactive, instantaneous, and full of holes. It leaves transfers between private sellers—online, at gun shows, among "friends"—largely unchecked.
[P1.2] It can only search records that states and agencies actually uploaded; missing domestic-violence or mental-health records can let a dangerous buyer be approved, and an unresolved check can become a default sale. Most importantly, it never asks whether the buyer knows how the firearm works, how to store it safely, or how to prevent a tragedy at home.
[P1.3] A federal firearm license fixes the flaw by licensing the person, not just scanning the moment. To get it, an applicant clears a thorough background check, completes hands-on training on safe handling, storage, and suicide prevention, and receives an objective, renewable credential. Every purchase—public or private—requires presenting that license, so all transfers move through the same qualification. This does not ban guns or create discretionary denial for ordinary citizens: it sets a clear, uniform benchmark that responsible owners can meet.
[P1.4] The key contrast is automobiles. We do not let someone buy a car and test competence only after a crash; we require a license, training, and renewal to operate a dangerous machine in public. Firearms deserve at least that logic. Training lowers accidents, theft, and impulsive harm; renewal means a later felony, restraining order, or severe mental-health adjudication can suspend eligibility instead of waiting for the next sale.
[P1.5] Point-of-sale screening cannot do that.
[P1.6] Opponents defend the status quo by pretending a point-of-sale form is the whole answer. It isn’t. A license with training and checks turns gun ownership into a verified, continuing responsibility rather than a one-time background ping. That is why the United States should require it.

[C1.1] PRO’s comparison between driving a car and exercising a fundamental right collapses at the starting line. Subjecting a core constitutional guarantee to a federal pre-licensing scheme transforms a fundamental freedom into a conditional government permission slip. No other civil right—whether voting, speech, or due process—requires passing an administrative course, paying fees, and waiting on bureaucratic approval simply to be exercised.
[C1.2] Beyond the constitutional barrier, a federal licensing mandate fails the test of practical efficacy. The primary driver of gun violence is illicit acquisition: street-level black markets, straw purchases, and theft. Individuals willing to commit violent felonies will not hesitate to bypass an administrative licensing scheme.
[C1.3] Instead, the burden falls entirely on law-abiding citizens. Mandatory training, application fees, and bureaucratic backlogs create severe economic and logistical hurdles—disproportionately disarming low-income and minority Americans seeking self-defense. When someone facing an imminent threat must wait weeks for federal paperwork and class availability, administrative delay becomes practical disenfranchisement.
[C1.4] Finally, PRO constructs a false choice between federal licensing and an imperfect status quo. If records are missing from current background check databases, the remedy is enforcing reporting mandates on state and military authorities, not erecting a massive, centralized licensing bureaucracy. A federal license inherently requires compiling a national registry of gun owners, creating serious civil liberties concerns while inviting administrative bottlenecks.
[C1.5] We can improve firearm safety through rigorous enforcement of existing criminal prohibitions and better data integration without turning a constitutional right into a federal privilege. Replacing point-of-sale screening with a burdensome licensing regime punishes the law-abiding while leaving criminal supply chains untouched.
