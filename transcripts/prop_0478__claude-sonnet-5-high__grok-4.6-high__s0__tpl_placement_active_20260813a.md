[P1.1] A driver logs forty hours a week across two rideshare apps. A designer strings together contracts from three platforms and clears real money doing it. Neither has a single employer to point to, and neither has a single day of paid sick leave, a retirement contribution, or coverage if a car accident sidelines them for a month. That gap is not an accident of the market.
[P1.2] It is a design flaw in how we define "employer," and it is the thing this debate is actually about.
[P1.3] The traditional benefits system attaches protections to a single employment relationship: one boss, one paycheck, one set of withholdings. Gig and short-term contract work breaks that model by design, spreading income across multiple platforms so that no single company can be pinned with full employer obligations under current law. The result isn't freedom from burden, it's the disappearance of the floor entirely. A portable benefits system fixes the actual mismatch: instead of asking "who is the employer," it asks "how much income came through covered platforms," and requires proportional contributions from each one into benefits the worker carries with them, no matter how many apps they use or how often they switch.
[P1.4] This matters because the stakes are not abstract. Workers earning substantial income this way are often not casual side-hustlers; they're primary earners, sometimes supporting families, absorbing risk that a single-employer worker doesn't have to absorb. One bad injury, one slow month, one health crisis, and there is no unemployment insurance, no employer health plan, no safety net at all. Society still ends up paying that cost, just later and less efficiently, through emergency rooms, bankruptcy, and public assistance.
[P1.5] Portable benefits don't require calling every gig worker an employee. They don't destroy flexibility, because contributions follow the worker across platforms rather than trapping them at one company. What they do is close a hole in the labor market that platforms have built their profitability partly around ignoring. [C1.1] The case for a required portable benefits floor starts from a false diagnosis. Gig and contract work did not break the employer model. It offered an alternative to people who did not want, or could not get, a single-employer package. The missing sick day is not a design flaw.
[C1.2] It is the bargain: more cash now, more control over hours, no claim on any one firm's benefit budget.
[C1.3] Force platforms to fund a proportional floor and you do not conjure free insurance. You raise the cost of every ride, delivery, and short contract. Platforms recoup it through commissions, prices, or fewer hours. The worker ends up with less take-home and a benefit they did not choose.
[C1.4] A parent covering rent this Friday is not more secure because part of this week's earnings was diverted into a pot she cannot touch.
[C1.5] Heterogeneity kills the one-floor story. Some platform earners are primary breadwinners. Many are students, moonlighters, retirees, or specialists who already have coverage. A statute keyed to "substantial income across platforms" treats them as if they share one risk profile and one taste for deferred pay.
[C1.6] They do not.
[C1.7] Then government must draw lines it cannot draw cleanly. Substantial compared with what? Which apps? Which short-term contracts?
[C1.8] Cover marketplaces but not a designer who invoices directly, and you invite evasion. Cover both, and independent contracting becomes a contribution scheme. That is not closing a hole. That is annexing a labor market.
[C1.9] Society already absorbs uncovered risk through emergency rooms, public insurance, and tax-advantaged accounts that travel with the person. If those tools are thin, thicken them for every worker, not only those whose income happens to route through an app. A special levy on one way of earning will be paid by workers and customers, and it will shrink the work it claims to civilize.
