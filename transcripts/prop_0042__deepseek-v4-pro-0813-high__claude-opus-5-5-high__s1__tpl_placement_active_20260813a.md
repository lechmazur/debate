[P1.1] Meat is artificially cheap. The checkout price omits the largest costs of producing it: greenhouse gases, deforestation, water pollution, manure runoff, antibiotic resistance, and diet-related chronic disease. That omission is not neutral. It is a continuous public subsidy to harmful production, paid invisibly by everyone else.
[P1.2] A serious country should tax meat production according to the harm it actually imposes.
[P1.3] The principle is familiar. When a factory dumps waste into a river, we do not call the cleanup cost an unfair tax on the poor; we make the polluter internalize it. Meat production is a larger version of the same problem. Its environmental footprint is massive, and its health costs arrive later in public health budgets, lost work, resistant infections, and higher healthcare spending.
[P1.4] Exempting meat from that logic means lower-income households often pay twice: once for cheap meat at the store, and again through polluted water, worse air, climate damage, and a strained health system.
[P1.5] The food-price objection is real, but it cuts the other way. Lower-income households already bear the worst environmental and health burdens with the fewest resources to adapt. Treating artificially cheap meat as “affordable” ignores the deferred bill. The right response is not to abandon the tax; it is to use the revenue.
[P1.6] A meat externality tax can fund targeted rebates, nutrition assistance, and support for affordable plant-based alternatives. That protects real incomes while preserving the price signal.
[P1.7] The decisive question is simple: who should pay for the harm a product creates? Today, the answer is the public, and disproportionately the vulnerable. A full externality tax shifts that burden toward producers and consumers of the harmful product, where it belongs.
