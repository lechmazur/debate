[P1.1] A tax on land value targets something whose supply is fixed: the land itself. Transaction taxes and rent caps instead punish decisions housing markets need people to make—selling, moving, building, maintaining, and letting. That difference gives land-value taxation a decisive advantage on both supply and public finance.
[P1.2] Under land-value taxation, the taxable base is the location’s value, not the value of the building placed on it. An owner holding a vacant lot near jobs or transit therefore faces a recurring cost for leaving it idle, while an owner who adds apartments does not receive a higher tax bill merely for adding useful floor space. The pressure runs toward development, redevelopment of underused sites, and fuller use of expensive locations. It is not a substitute for sensible planning permission, but it stops the tax system from rewarding speculation and penalizing construction.
[P1.3] The fiscal base is also unusually durable. Land values reflect schools, transport, safety, and access to employment; taxing part of that value lets the community recover some of the benefits its own spending creates. Revenue can then support those services without depending on people repeatedly buying and selling homes. Transaction taxes do the opposite: they raise money most readily when markets boom, discourage otherwise beneficial moves, and leave local budgets exposed when sales freeze.
[P1.4] A household that would move closer to work, or an older resident who would downsize and release a family home, is told to pay a toll before making the efficient move.
[P1.5] Rent caps are weaker still as a supply and finance policy. They can protect sitting tenants from sudden increases, but they generate no broad revenue stream for local services. If binding and durable, they squeeze maintenance and new rental investment; if heavily exempted, their protection becomes patchy while allocation problems remain. They also encourage tenants to stay in units that no longer fit, reducing the effective supply available to everyone else.

[C1.1] PRO treats land as fixed, so taxing it is supposedly costless. But the binding constraint on housing is not the tax on land; it is the legal right to build. A vacant lot in a zone that forbids apartments stays vacant under land-value taxation no matter how high the annual bill. Unless LVT is paired with immediate zoning reform, it merely raises costs on owners who cannot build, delivering neither apartments nor durable revenue.
[C1.2] Supply follows permission, not tax theory.
[C1.3] PRO calls accurate assessment a design task. In reality, separating location value from building value requires contested residual calculations. Because land value is inferred—sale price minus construction cost—errors are systematic. Overestimate the land share and you penalize construction exactly as the old property tax did; underestimate it and the efficiency vanishes.
[C1.4] That uncertainty raises development risk and discourages the building PRO wants.
[C1.5] The fiscal claim is overstated too. Land values rise and fall with local booms, reflecting the same amenities PRO praises. When a factory closes, the base shrinks. Transaction taxes are narrow but cheap to collect, and a targeted levy on rapid turnover can avoid taxing ordinary movers while raising money.
[C1.6] Rent caps fund nothing, but LVT’s fiscal edge depends on an assessment apparatus that can consume much of the gain.
[C1.7] The comparison is skewed. Transaction taxes can be narrowed to flippers and second homes. Rent caps address displacement, which LVT does not. The proposition demands LVT beat both on supply and finance.
[C1.8] It does not, because it ignores zoning, assessment friction, and base volatility.

[P2.2] Transaction taxes obstruct sales and moves; rent caps weaken returns to providing rental homes. Zoning reform may be necessary, but it is no comparative advantage for CON.
[P2.3] The assessment objection confuses valuation error with a tax on buildings. Constructing apartments does not itself enter the LVT base. Even an imperfect land assessment therefore does not recreate the old property tax’s marginal penalty on construction. Assessment affects revenue and distribution, and warrants comparable evidence, appeals, and phase-ins; it does not erase the incentive to use valuable land productively.
[P2.4] CON simply asserts that administration “can consume much of the gain,” without comparing those costs with a recurring revenue base.
[P2.5] On volatility, durability never meant immunity to local decline. If a factory closes, land values and transaction activity can both weaken—but transaction revenue also depends on turnover, precisely what that tax discourages. Narrowing it to flippers and second homes reduces harm to ordinary movers only by shrinking the base further. It cannot match a recurrent levy on land as a foundation for local services.
[P2.6] Finally, rent caps’ displacement benefit is not a fiscal benefit and produces no revenue for services or tenant relief. Caps may help incumbent tenants while reducing mobility and, when binding, maintenance and investment. Protecting some existing occupants does not answer a motion about housing supply and public finance.
[P2.7] That comparative point decides the clash: LVT is the only option here that can press permitted land into use while funding the amenities that create its value. CON identifies implementation work, not an alternative that performs both tasks better.
