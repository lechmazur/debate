[P1.1] The right question is not whether meat becomes more expensive, but who is currently paying for its hidden costs. Cheap meat is not cheap: its price tag omits methane-driven warming, deforestation, manure runoff, antibiotic overuse, and diet-related disease. Those bills do not disappear; they are paid through disaster recovery, health insurance, lost farm yields, and polluted water. The proposition simply says the producer should pay at the point of production rather than passing the costs to everyone else, including the poor.
[P1.2] A full-externality tax is the most honest price correction. It targets production, not individual consumption, so it creates a continuous incentive for producers to cut emissions, improve waste treatment, reduce antibiotic misuse, and shift toward lower-impact proteins. The tax scales with harm: beef and industrial pork pay more than less damaging products. That is not a moral panic; it is standard cost accounting, like taxing carbon or tobacco.
[P1.3] Our opponent will say this is regressive. But the status quo is more regressive. Low-income communities are more likely to sit near feedlots and processing plants, to suffer asthma from ammonia and particulates, to face food-price shocks from climate-damaged crops, and to have less access to care when diet-related illness strikes. Asking them to keep buying artificially cheap meat means asking them to subsidize harms that hit them hardest.
[P1.4] Even if compensation is imperfect, they gain from less pollution and fewer climate shocks. If the checkout price rises, the state can and should recycle revenue through child nutrition benefits, food subsidies, or rebates. But the remedy for regressivity is compensation, not preserving a false price that externalizes suffering.
[P1.5] Vote PRO because it aligns incentives with truth: producers pay for damage, consumers see real costs, and governments can protect the poor without pretending harm is free.
