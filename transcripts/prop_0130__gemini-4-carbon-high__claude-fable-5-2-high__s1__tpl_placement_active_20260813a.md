When museums refuse repatriation by claiming superior preservation capacity, they launder imperial plunder through paternalism, casting the beneficiaries of theft as permanent trustees and the victims as perpetual wards unfit to steward their own past.
[P1.2] Superior resources never override stolen title.
[P1.3] Even practically, the preservation excuse is a self-serving trap. Museums making this claim act as judge and jury in their own financial and reputational interest. [P1.4] Meanwhile, Western custody is hardly infallible: countless looted objects languish unseen in museum basements, even as nations across Africa and Asia steward vast collections in modern facilities. More fundamentally, possession is not required for conservation. If wealthy institutions truly care about physical survival rather than prestige and ticket sales, they can return looted artifacts while funding climate control, security, and conservation training in the country of origin. Refusing to separate assistance from possession proves the goal is hoarding, not protection.
[P1.5] Finally, keeping plundered heritage abroad misunderstands what preservation is for. An artifact is not merely intact bronze, wood, or stone; it is living memory, identity, and historical continuity. When British forces sacked Benin City and scattered its royal bronzes across European galleries, the metal survived, but its role as a living archive for Nigerians was severed. Locking a nation's history behind foreign glass, accessible only to descendants wealthy enough to secure visas and airfare, preserves physical shells while hollowing out cultural meaning.
[P1.6] Museums must return what colonial violence took.
