[P1.1] Every return-to-office mandate is a tax: on commutes, on childcare, on the employee who relocated during the pandemic and now faces a de facto pay cut. This motion asks one modest thing — that before a large employer levies that tax, it show a reason specific enough to be checked. "Culture" is not that reason.
[P1.2] First, culture claims are unfalsifiable, and an unfalsifiable justification is no justification at all. If presence can be demanded because culture needs it, presence can be demanded for anything, forever, with no possible test. A role-specific reason — this lab shares equipment, this onboarding cohort learns by shadowing, this trading desk runs live deal reviews — can be examined, weighed, even refuted. That is the whole difference between governance and whim.
[P1.3] Second, jobs genuinely differ, so a blanket mandate is guaranteed wrong in both directions. The analyst who codes alone all day gains almost nothing from a badge swipe; the apprentice who learns by watching a senior engineer loses real development at home. One rule treating them identically overburdens the first and underserves the second. Role-specific justification simply forces the policy to match the work — which is what management is supposed to do anyway.
[P1.4] Third, the requirement protects the employer itself. Broad mandates drive attrition, and attrition is not random: the most marketable workers leave first, while caregivers and disabled employees are pushed out disproportionately. Research on hybrid arrangements across many firms finds performance holding steady while quits fall. A company forced to write down why a given role needs presence will often land on the honest answer — two days a week for collaboration, not five for vibes — a better policy reached precisely because someone had to justify it.
[P1.5] Notice what this motion does not do. It bans no mandate; if a real reason exists, writing it down costs an afternoon. The only mandates this rule blocks are the ones nobody can explain — and those are precisely the ones worth blocking.

[C1.2] Consider the analyst who supposedly codes alone all day. That description confuses a job description with a workday. That analyst answers a junior's quick question, overhears a product problem, gets pulled into a whiteboard fix, models urgency for interns. None of those moments belong to his role, yet all disappear if only supposedly collaborative roles return.
[C1.3] Culture is not vibes; it is the transmission belt for standards, speed, and trust. Mentorship, onboarding, crisis response, and cross-team innovation require co-presence as a precondition, not as a line item. An office at 30% occupancy provides neither quiet home focus nor real collaboration — just commuting to Zoom.
[C1.4] Role-by-role justification also fails administratively. Large employers have hundreds of titles that blur and evolve monthly. Forcing managers and lawyers to draft bespoke defenses invites endless internal appeals — why does sales need three days and support two? — plus grievances, discrimination claims, and managerial paralysis. A clear uniform rule is more transparent and fairer than a patchwork of exemptions that looks like favoritism.
[C1.5] And mandates need no outside permission slip. Employment is an offer, not a tax. Commutes and childcare are real costs, but workers weigh them against pay, promotion, and stability, and leave if the bargain is bad. If a blanket mandate truly wrecks retention, the firm pays in quits and missed hires.
[C1.6] That market discipline is faster and more accurate than any justification paperwork.
[C1.7] Broad culture reasons are business reasons. Let leaders lead, and let workers decide.
