[P1.1] The proposition is not a blanket license for coercion; it is a narrow, principled defense of institutional pluralism. Religious schools and employers exist because communities deserve the freedom to embody their convictions in shared life. When a teacher, nurse, or cafeteria worker chooses to join a Catholic school, an Orthodox Jewish hospital, or a Muslim academy, they are opting into a community with known values and a coherent mission. Many actively seek out these environments because the shared ethos makes the workplace feel like home.
[P1.2] The dress expectations at issue flow directly from the religious character that gives the institution its reason to exist. Strip away that room, and the institution is forced to choose between betraying its identity or closing its doors entirely.
[P1.3] The critical safeguard is built into the motion itself: participation must be genuinely voluntary, and alternatives must remain realistic. No one is trapped. A teacher who objects to a modesty policy at a religious school can teach elsewhere, just as a worker can decline a role at a faith-based clinic and seek employment at a secular one. The law already distinguishes between conditions imposed on a captive workforce and expectations accepted by those who freely associate.
[P1.4] The proposition asks us to respect that distinction, not erase it. Workers who find the expectation burdensome are free to leave; workers who find it meaningful are free to stay.
[P1.5] Opponents of this view often treat every difference as discrimination, but that framing collapses the crucial distinction between exclusion and self-definition. A religious institution that asks for head coverings or modest attire is not targeting outsiders; it is preserving the coherent way of life that attracted members in the first place. If we deny this space, we do not create neutrality—we create homogeneity, punishing religious communities for daring to maintain boundaries that secular institutions take for granted.
[P1.6] The motion gets the balance right. It says yes to faith-based dress expectations where choice is real, and no where choice is illusory. Religious liberty without institutional room to breathe is just private [clipped]

[C1.1] The motion is a permission slip wearing the costume of a limit. “Genuinely voluntary” and “realistic alternatives” do not restrain faith-based dress rules in schools and workplaces. They launder them.
[C1.2] Employment is not a club. A nurse at the only hospital in reach, a teacher whose pension sits inside a diocesan system, a cafeteria worker whose hours fit the bus and the rent: these people are not shopping a menu of equal secular jobs. Exit costs references, childcare, seniority, housing, and sometimes the only community that will hire them. Calling that voluntary because a job exists somewhere else is how pressure gets a polite name.
[C1.3] The institution holding the leverage will be the one declaring the alternative “realistic.”
[C1.4] Schools expose the fiction faster. Students do not opt into a mission statement. Parents often “choose” a religious school because it is nearby, affordable, safer, or what a voucher will cover. The code then lands on adolescent bodies, overwhelmingly girls’: hair, hems, sleeves, coverings.
[C1.5] That is not the community defining itself. It is a hierarchy imposed on the people least able to walk out.
[C1.6] The identity dilemma does not rescue this. A congregation can vest its liturgy as it wishes. What the motion protects is different: an employer or school making someone else’s clothing a term of the paycheck or the diploma. Secular hospitals and ordinary schools already keep a coherent mission without prescribing faith costume.
[C1.7] An identity that requires control over subordinate bodies was never a liberty a free society owes.
[C1.8] And the wording will not stay narrow. “Some room,” “genuinely,” and “realistic” are elastic. A chapel covering becomes a ban on another faith’s symbols, a gendered trouser rule, a beard policy, a mark on dissenters as disloyal. Objectors are told they knew the culture when they signed. That is the exclusion being denied, rewritten as consent.
[C1.9] Applied honestly, the motion’s own tests would sink nearly every contested mandate in a real school or workplace. It survives only if read loosely. Reject it.
