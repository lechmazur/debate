[P1.1] A background check at the counter answers only one question: does this buyer have a disqualifying record at this exact moment? It says nothing about whether they know how to store a gun, handle it safely, or whether they will still be eligible next year. And it doesn't even reliably answer its one question. Roughly a fifth of gun transfers in America occur without any check at all—private sales, online listings, gun shows in many states.
[P1.2] Point-of-sale screening is a fence with the gate propped open.
[P1.3] A federal license flips the architecture. Instead of a seller running a rushed query—one that can default to approval if the FBI can't finish in three business days, the loophole that armed the Charleston church shooter—the buyer must demonstrate eligibility and competence up front. Every subsequent purchase, from any seller, requires showing that license. That closes the private-sale gap without deputizing every citizen as an amateur background-checker, because the license itself carries the verification.
[P1.4] The evidence behind this model is real and consistent. Connecticut's permit-to-purchase law was followed by substantial declines in gun homicides and suicides; Missouri repealed its permit requirement and saw gun homicides rise sharply afterward. Researchers who compare policies consistently rank licensing among the most effective interventions we have—stronger than checks alone, precisely because licensing adds fingerprinting, in-person application, training, and a waiting period that cools impulsive violence, especially suicide.
[P1.5] Nor is this alien to American life. We license drivers, pilots, and hunters. Training requirements for concealed carry exist across red and blue states and have survived constitutional scrutiny. A shall-issue license—objective criteria, no bureaucratic discretion—respects the Second Amendment while ensuring that the person exercising it can do so safely.
[P1.6] The question in this debate is not licenses versus liberty. It is a verified, trained, accountable system versus a leaky checkpoint that criminals simply walk around. Choose the system that actually checks.

[C1.2] It transforms lawful gun ownership into a credentialed privilege.
[C1.3] PRO’s central promise is that licensing closes gaps. But licensing targets only people who seek lawful purchases. The criminal who steals a firearm, buys from a black-market dealer, or uses a straw purchaser does not stop at a federal licensing office. If a prohibited person can evade a counter check, he can evade a wallet card.
[C1.4] What licensing does add is a national permission structure—and likely a registry of licensed owners—creating privacy and security risks, while doing little to touch illegal markets.
[C1.5] The training requirement sounds prudent, but it is a blunt and unequal gate. Courses cost money, require travel, and take time off work. Rural owners, low-income buyers, and people needing a firearm for protection may be delayed or priced out. [C1.6] PRO leans on before-and-after stories from Connecticut and Missouri, but those comparisons are contested. Many factors move homicide and suicide rates; attributing shifts mainly to permit laws assumes away enforcement, poverty, neighboring states, and existing gun markets. [C1.7] The better path is to make the existing check more accurate and immediate: improve record reporting, fix errors, reduce delays, and focus enforcement on prohibited possessors and traffickers. A federal license is a rights-invasive solution searching for a problem it cannot solve. Vote CON.
