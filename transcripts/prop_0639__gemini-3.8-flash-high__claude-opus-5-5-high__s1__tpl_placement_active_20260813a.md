[P1.1] We already trust randomly selected citizens with life and liberty in the jury system. We do this because everyday people, insulated from political ambition and exposed to rigorous evidence, can deliver impartial judgment. Yet on policy, we treat citizen deliberation like a glorified focus group. Advisory assemblies are routinely shelved the moment their recommendations challenge party leadership or entrenched donors.
[P1.2] To fulfill the democratic promise of sortition, citizens' assemblies must hold binding authority on specific policy questions.
[P1.3] First, advisory status is a recipe for co-optation. Politicians eagerly commission assemblies for democratic cover, but retain an absolute veto. When citizens recommend ambitious, necessary action—as seen in climate assemblies across Europe—executives dilute or discard the proposals. Power without the force of law is not democracy; it is political theater.
[P1.4] Second, representative legislatures suffer from unavoidable conflicts of interest. Politicians are structurally incapable of impartially deciding issues tied to their own survival: drawing electoral boundaries, capping campaign donations, or taking near-term costs to prevent long-term crises. Sortition dissolves the perverse incentives of the permanent campaign. Citizens chosen by lot have no party whips to obey, no wealthy donors to court, and no re-election to engineer.
[P1.5] Third, binding sortition restores genuine democratic legitimacy. Modern legislatures are dominated by wealthy elites, career partisans, and specialized interests. Sortition builds a true descriptive mirror of the public—bringing everyday perspectives directly into governance. When granted access to balanced expert testimony and real decision-making power, ordinary citizens consistently demonstrate the capacity for thoughtful, public-spirited compromise that polarized parliaments fail to achieve.
[P1.6] Representative democracy is failing under polarization and self-dealing. On specific, vital questions where elected institutions are hopelessly gridlocked or conflicted, binding citizens' assemblies return sovereign authority directly to the people.

[C1.1] Juries are PRO's best analogy, and it breaks on inspection. A jury applies a law it did not write, to facts about one case, under a judge who can set aside a perverse verdict and an appeals court that can reverse it. PRO wants randomly selected citizens to write the law itself, for everyone, with no one to answer to. That is the opposite of a jury.
[C1.2] My case rests on one problem: binding power without accountability.
[C1.3] First, nobody can be held responsible. When a legislature passes a bad policy, voters can remove the people who did it. When a sortition assembly passes a bad policy, its members go home. They cannot be voted out, recalled, or questioned next year.
[C1.4] A hundred or so people would bind millions who never chose them and can never sanction them. Democracy is not only representation. It is the ability to punish power. Binding assemblies remove that.
[C1.5] Second, binding power does not end capture. It moves it somewhere less visible. PRO says assemblies are free of whips and donors. But someone writes the question, picks the experts, designs the briefing materials, and runs the facilitation.
[C1.6] Once the outcome becomes law, controlling those levers becomes the most valuable prize in politics. Lobbyists stop courting legislators and start courting organizers. Ordinary citizens would struggle to name those organizers, let alone remove them. PRO's complaint about co-optation in [P1.3] gets worse under its own model, because the stakes of framing rise from a report to a statute.
[C1.7] Third, the best success story for sortition is advisory. Ireland's assemblies on marriage equality and abortion produced real change because their recommendations went to the whole electorate in a referendum. Millions of voters ratified what a hundred citizens proposed. Here the deliberation informed the decision, and the voters made it.
[C1.8] PRO's model cuts out exactly the step that gave Ireland its legitimacy.
[C1.9] Finally, policy is trade-offs, not isolated questions. A binding climate measure collides with budgets, other laws, and future governments. Elected bodies can be blamed for balancing those things. [clipped]

[P2.3] Next, CON warns of invisible capture by organizers and facilitators [C1.5-C1.6]. This is an extraordinary reversal. [P2.5] CON then praises Ireland’s assemblies [C1.7], but misunderstands why they matter. Ireland succeeded because the government made a political commitment that gave the assembly's work genuine legal consequence: a direct path to the ballot. In contrast, purely advisory assemblies—such as France's climate assembly—saw their comprehensive recommendations systematically gutted by executive veto. Advisory status guarantees that politicians take credit when citizens agree with them, and bury the findings when they do not.
[P2.6] Finally, the motion specifies binding authority on *specific policy questions*, not abolishing representative government [C1.9]. Constitutional limits, legal review, and legislative frameworks remain.
