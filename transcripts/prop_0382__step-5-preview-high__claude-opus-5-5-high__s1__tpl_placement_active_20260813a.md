[P1.1] The principle is simple: if a product is safe, it should compete on the same shelf. Governments do not maintain separate legal regimes for bread because it was baked in a solar oven rather than a gas oven, nor special licenses for milk because a robotic milker drew it. The regulatory question is safety, not biography. Cultivated meat—real animal cells grown outside the animal—meets that test once safety standards are met.
[P1.2] Treating it as conventional meat is not a loophole; it is the logical consequence of ending the review where the law already ends it: at harm. Naming a product by what it is, not by how it was made, is the ordinary way markets work.
[P1.3] CON must explain why an identical product requires an unequal regime. If the cell-culture process yields muscle and fat chemically and nutritionally analogous to slaughtered meat, then special taxes, segregating labels beyond standard safety and allergen disclosures, or moratoriums are not precaution. They are protectionism dressed as caution. Those barriers do not protect consumers; they protect incumbents from competition and deny citizens the freedom to choose a product that carries no land-use footprint, no slaughter, and a fraction of the antibiotic load.
[P1.4] Equal treatment is also better regulation. It forces cultivated meat into the same inspection, hygiene, and traceability frameworks as conventional meat, subjecting the newcomer to the full weight of existing food-safety law rather than an ad hoc patchwork. Consumers benefit from clarity: one standard, one market, one set of enforceable rules. Farmers and processors gain a predictable path to diversify.
[P1.5] Governments gain a tool for protein security without building a parallel bureaucracy.
[P1.6] The motion asks only for parity after safety is established. That is the minimum a rational food policy owes to any new technology. Anything less substitutes the politics of the old product for the science of the new one.
