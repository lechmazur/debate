[P1.1] The euro was a political gamble that welded together economies as different as Germany and Greece under a single interest rate, with no shared treasury, no fiscal transfers, and no exit door. [P1.2] The core defect is mechanical, not ideological. A monetary union without fiscal union strips member states of their two natural shock absorbers—devaluation and independent interest rates—while providing nothing in their place. When the 2008 crisis hit, countries like Poland and Sweden, outside the euro, let their currencies adjust and recovered quickly. Greece, Spain, and Portugal were forced instead into brutal internal devaluation: mass unemployment, youth joblessness approaching half a generation, and depressions in some cases deeper and longer than the 1930s.
[P1.3] Italy's economy has barely grown since it joined the euro—a stagnation almost unprecedented for a major developed democracy.

The euro handed populists across the continent their most potent grievance and made "Brussels" synonymous with imposed austerity. That is corrosion of the political trust on which everything else—the single market, enlargement, collective foreign policy—depends.
[P1.5] The counterfactual is not chaos. It is the pre-1999 architecture: the single market, coordinated exchange-rate management, and national currencies that flex when economies diverge. That system delivered decades of deepening integration without a Troika, without bailout ultimatums, without a lost decade in the south.
[P1.6] The test in this debate is "stronger today." A Union with converging economies, adjustable currencies, and no euro-crisis scar tissue is stronger by any honest reading of that word.

[C1.1] The euro is not the EU’s original sin; it is the main reason the Union acts as one economic power rather than a loose trade area with many currencies. Without it, the EU would be weaker today in internal market depth, crisis capacity, and global influence.
[C1.2] The proposition’s counterfactual romanticizes pre-1999. That system was unstable: the ERM crises of 1992-93 showed separate currencies under free capital movement invited speculation and abrupt devaluations. The real alternative to the euro was not a serene single market but recurring currency turmoil, competitive devaluations, and permanent exchange-rate risk.
[C1.3] By removing that risk, the euro deepened cross-border trade, investment, and supply chains. That makes cooperation harder to reverse and the Union more cohesive. A Europe of many small currencies would have thinner capital markets and more fragmentation—hardly strength.
[C1.4] The eurozone crisis was real, but it exposed the euro’s incomplete surrounding architecture, not the currency itself. The EU responded by building a rescue fund, common banking supervision, and an ECB able to act as lender of last resort. Those are capacities a euro-less Union would not have needed or gained.
[C1.5] PRO’s Poland and Sweden examples cut the other way. Their floating currencies are national escape valves, not collective strength. Inside the single market, the ability to depreciate is a standing invitation to beggar-thy-neighbor policy, the exact dynamic that once nearly wrecked the internal market. Meanwhile, the euro gives Europe a major global currency and monetary weight it would otherwise lack.
[C1.6] The question is comparative: for all its scars, the Union today is more integrated, better equipped, and harder to break apart with the euro than it would have been without it.
