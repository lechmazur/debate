[P1.1] When a fan pays for a ticket, they buy a seat, not a digital leash. Yet platforms increasingly impose blanket non-transferability, trapping buyers when illness, emergencies, or schedule conflicts arise. The law should establish a default right to peer-to-peer transfer, because a ticket that cannot be given to a friend, sold at face value, or donated is not property—it is a contingent privilege revocable at the organizer's whim.
[P1.2] The opposition will invoke scalping, but this motion is not a free-for-all. It explicitly permits organizers to block transfers through narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits: price caps, account transfer ceilings, or identity verification for high-risk events. What it forbids is the lazy nuclear option of total transfer bans that punish legitimate fans to avoid the administrative work of precision. If an organizer can show a real scalping threat, they retain the power to stop it; they simply cannot use that threat as a pretext to annihilate consumer autonomy.
[P1.3] The cost of blanket bans is concrete. A parent falls ill the day of the show; a student's exam is moved; a worker is called in. These are not speculators, yet they are locked into non-transferable digital wallets and forced to swallow the loss, while the venue often resells the empty seat itself. Peer-to-peer transfer at face value is consumer protection, not commerce.
[P1.4] Organizers already monopolize supply, set fees, and deploy dynamic pricing. Removing transfer rights completes their control from purchase to turnstile. A legal right to transfer, bounded by narrow anti-scalping safeguards, does not destabilize the market. It prevents organizers from converting a purchased ticket into a non-negotiable personal obligation.

[C1.2] A default legal right to peer-to-peer transfer lets scalpers scoop up these discounted tickets en masse and resell them for 10 times face value on secondary markets, with no recourse for organizers unless they can prove a specific, imminent scalping threat to a court’s satisfaction. The “narrowly tailored” exception is an after-the-fact litigation hurdle, not a preventative tool.
[C1.3] Second, it creates unavoidable safety risks for vulnerable community gatherings. PRO frames this as a fight against lazy blanket bans, but the real choice here is not between total bans and unfettered transfer: it is between letting organizers set reasonable, context-specific access rules, and letting scalpers and bad actors weaponize a vague legal right to exploit fans and terrorize vulnerable communities.
[C1.4] The so-called “narrowly tailored” exception is not a workable guardrail—it is a litigation booby trap that will force even well-intentioned organizers to choose between hosting inclusive events and facing waves of fraudulent secondary sales or harassment at their doors.

That is not a booby trap; it is the minimum standard for any market that pretends to sell property rather than peddle revocable privileges.
