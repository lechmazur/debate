[P1.1] When a state removes an asylum seeker to a third country, it does not end its obligations — it hands them to someone else. What makes that handoff legitimate? Not a label on a map. Safety is not a geography; it is a set of conditions a person can live under.
[P1.2] A destination without these three rights offers a holding pen, not protection.
[P1.3] Start with work. Safety without a livelihood is destitution on a delay. Barred from legal work, a person does not become self-sufficient; they become exploitable or they move on — the very secondary movement these rules exist to prevent. A policy that manufactures the problem it claims to solve fails on its own terms.
[P1.4] Next, appeal. Every asylum system errs. If a removed person cannot challenge the removal or a later rejection, "safe" becomes unfalsifiable — a designation no evidence can disturb. That is how refoulement happens: not through malice but through uncorrectable paperwork.
[P1.5] Britain's Supreme Court blocked the Rwanda scheme for exactly this: deficiencies in the receiving asylum system meant assurances could not be trusted.
[P1.6] Finally, family unity. Splitting families does not keep people in place; it gives them a reason to pay smugglers — the very networks these policies claim to starve. For children especially, separation is the injury, not an inconvenience.
[P1.7] Note the load-bearing word: enforceable. Rights on a statute book but unreachable in practice — Syrians in Turkey spent years in informal work despite nominal access — do not count. Enforceable means usable: a permit obtainable, an appeal heard, a reunion processed.
[P1.8] The likely objection is that this standard kills most existing deals. Perhaps — but that concedes the argument. A transfer scheme that works only by warehousing people in rightless limbo buys efficiency with their rights; deterrence by making conditions worse than flight is cruelty with a spreadsheet. The other side must explain why a state may outsource protection to a place that cannot deliver it.
