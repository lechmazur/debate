[P1.1] Gene-editing therapies represent a historic leap in medicine: the power to rewrite fatal or debilitating genetic conditions, such as sickle cell disease or severe inherited blindness, with a single treatment. Yet because a one-time cure eliminates a lifetime of repeat prescriptions, pharmaceutical monopolies price these breakthroughs in the millions of dollars per patient. Without a legal counterweight, countries face a brutal choice: ration actual cures to a privileged few, or watch a handful of therapies bankrupt public health budgets.
[P1.2] Requiring a public-interest licensing pathway solves this crisis at its root. Traditional patent flexibilities were built for simple chemical pills that generic factories can easily reverse-engineer. Gene editing is different; production relies on proprietary delivery vectors, cell-processing protocols, and regulatory data. A dedicated pathway updates law to match science: when manufacturers refuse affordable terms, governments can compel licensed access not just to patents, but to the technical know-how and manufacturing capacity required to deliver the therapy.
[P1.3] Crucially, this pathway works primarily through leverage, not routine intervention. When a monopoly knows the state holds no credible alternative, price negotiation is theater: health systems either pay ransom prices or let citizens suffer. Putting an enforceable licensing trigger into law transforms bargaining overnight, pressuring drugmakers to strike voluntary, high-volume, affordable agreements before compulsion is ever invoked.
[P1.4] Nor does this break the innovation engine. First, public-interest licensing is not confiscation; originators receive reasonable royalties that reward clinical development while curbing pure price-gouging. Second, the foundational tools of gene editing grew heavily out of taxpayer-funded academic laboratories. The public already underwrote the steepest early scientific risk; families should not be priced out of the resulting cures, especially in lower-income nations where genetic diseases hit hardest.
[P1.5] A cure locked behind an insurmountable paywall is clinically indistinguishable from no cure at all. Requiring a public-interest licensing pathway ensures essential gene editing saves lives rather than merely maximizing monopoly rents.

[C1.5] Innovation is adaptive. A credible compulsory-access regime means the payoff from successful development is capped by political judgment, while failures remain fully costly. That discourages risky, first-in-class programs for rare disorders. [C1.6] If voluntary licensing, advance-purchase commitments, nonprofit manufacture, public R&D, and ordinary patent and competition law can secure access, why add this risk?
[C1.7] Compulsory licensing is a price mechanism, not an access guarantee. The decisive issue is not whether monopoly pricing is offensive—it often is—but whether this broad, vaguely triggered power improves access enough to justify its predictable costs. The proposition has not met that burden.
