[P1.1] The question is not whether unemployment hurts. It does. The question is which mistake costs more over three years: a central bank that tolerates inflation drifting above target, or one that accepts a modest rise in joblessness to bring it home. It turns on three points.
[P1.2] First, credibility is the cheapest tool a central bank owns, and it is easy to lose. When households and firms believe two percent is coming back, they set wages and prices accordingly, and inflation falls with little pain. When they stop believing, every contract builds in higher inflation, and the only cure is a far deeper recession. That is the lesson of the 1970s: repeated "stop-go" retreats to protect jobs produced both high inflation and high unemployment, and ended with the brutal tightening of the early 1980s.
[P1.3] A modest rise in unemployment now is insurance against a severe one later.
[P1.4] Second, inflation is the most regressive tax there is. Wealthy households own assets that reprice; low-income workers spend everything on food, rent, and energy, and their wages lag. A cashier whose grocery bill rises faster than her paycheck loses ground every month, whether or not she keeps her job. Unemployment, by contrast, falls on a smaller group and can be cushioned by unemployment insurance, retraining, and fiscal support.
[P1.5] Inflation reaches everyone and has no targeted remedy except the central bank.
[P1.6] Third, the trade-off is smaller than CON will claim. After the post-pandemic surge, major economies brought inflation down without the mass layoffs many predicted, because policy stayed committed and expectations held. Commitment is what keeps the cost modest.
[P1.7] So the burden on CON is specific: they must explain why accepting persistent above-target inflation, and risking unanchored expectations, beats a modest, cushionable rise in unemployment. That is a gamble with everyone's wages.
