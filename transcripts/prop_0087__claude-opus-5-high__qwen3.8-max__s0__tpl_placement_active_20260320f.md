[P1.1] The right unit of measurement in gambling policy is not the size of a bet but the number of bets per hour. That single shift decides this debate.
[P1.2] A weekly lottery ticket gives you one decision, one outcome, and six days to reflect. An online slot resolves in two or three seconds, all night, with autoplay and turbo modes to remove even the pause of a finger press. In-play betting does the same to sports: a match that used to offer one bet before kick-off now offers hundreds of micro-markets — next point, next throw, next corner — each with a cash-out button designed to keep you transacting.
[P1.3] The product has not just become faster. It has become continuous.
[P1.4] That matters because the mechanisms of harm are speed-dependent. Rapid, unpredictable reinforcement is the schedule that produces the most compulsive behaviour in every species we've studied. Losses arrive before the brain can register them as losses, so chasing happens within seconds rather than after reflection. Players describe entering a dissociative "zone" — something nobody reports while buying a scratchcard at the corner shop.
[P1.5] And because money velocity scales with speed, a £2 stake repeated every three seconds destroys a household budget faster than a £50 weekly wager ever could. Identical stake limits produce wildly different exposure.
[P1.6] We already legislate this way everywhere else. Nicotine is regulated by delivery speed, not just dose. Credit is regulated more tightly the faster and more frictionlessly it is extended. Speed limits differ by road.
[P1.7] Treating a two-second slot spin and a fortnightly football pools entry as the same regulated object is the anomaly requiring justification, not the reform.
[P1.8] And targeting speed is the *liberal* option. [P1.9] The alternative to proportionate regulation is not freedom. It is blunt, blanket restriction later — after the harm.
