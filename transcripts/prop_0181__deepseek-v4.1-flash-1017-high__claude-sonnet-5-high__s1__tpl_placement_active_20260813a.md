[P1.1] Consumer genealogy databases are not crime labs. They are private spaces where people spit into a tube to learn about ancestry or health, often without understanding that police may later search their DNA and, by extension, their parents, children, and cousins. The proposition draws a clear line: law enforcement may not turn those commercial repositories into a genetic dragnet. That line is necessary because the harm is structural, not speculative.
[P1.2] When police search a genealogy database, they do not just test a suspect. They test everyone related to that suspect. A person who never committed a crime, never opted into forensic use, and may not even know a relative uploaded DNA becomes a lead. The widely publicized Golden State Killer breakthrough came through a consumer genealogy site and family-tree inference.
[P1.3] Even those who celebrate the outcome should see the method: innocent relatives were swept into a criminal investigation without their consent. Cold cases are tragic, but tragedy is exactly when institutions face pressure to ignore limits.
[P1.4] Terms of service are not democratic consent. A checkbox buried in an ancestry product cannot authorize police access across generations, and it cannot be undone for relatives who never clicked anything. Commercial firms also have incentives to cooperate, and voluntary policies can change overnight. Strict prohibition is the only rule that does not depend on corporate goodwill or the next urgent case.
[P1.5] Once police can access these databases for cold cases, the category expands: violent felonies, then property crimes, then identifying protesters or immigration targets. DNA is uniquely powerful and uniquely permanent; a sample uploaded today can implicate descendants decades from now. The chilling effect is real, and it falls hardest on families and communities already over-policed.
[P1.6] Prohibition still leaves police free to build their own databases with informed consent, use conventional genealogy, seek court-authorized testing of suspects, and make direct appeals. The question is not whether cold cases matter. It is whether solving some justifies conscripting private genetic information from millions who never agreed to be searched. A free society should say no.
