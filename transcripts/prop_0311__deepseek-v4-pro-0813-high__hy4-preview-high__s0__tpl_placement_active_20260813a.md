[P1.1] Commitment is not demonstrated by avoiding shared life; it is demonstrated inside shared life. When a couple cohabits before marriage, they are not necessarily postponing commitment—they are testing whether their commitment survives alarm clocks, bills, housework, illness, bad moods, and family visits. Those daily realities are exactly what marriage will contain. Treating cohabitation as ordinary simply admits that adults need that information before vowing permanence.
[P1.2] Cohabitation is a high-information step. It exposes mismatched expectations about money, division of labor, privacy, conflict, cleanliness, and long-term planning. A marriage that skips this step does not avoid problems; it discovers them after legal, financial, and family entanglement. That makes divorce the first real test.
[P1.3] A shared lease is a far more reversible discovery mechanism than a marriage certificate.
[P1.4] The objection confuses caution with indifference. Many people cohabit precisely because they take marriage seriously and do not want to enter it blind. A partner who asks to live together before marriage may be asking for more accountability, not less. The weaker commitment is refusing to share ordinary responsibility until a ceremony makes exit expensive.
[P1.5] Any crude association between premarital cohabitation and later instability is also easy to misread. The problem is usually ambiguity, not cohabitation: couples who drift into shared housing without discussing what it means face confusion, while couples who deliberately cohabit as a step toward marriage are not showing weak commitment. That is exactly why society should treat cohabitation as a normal, discussable stage rather than stigmatize it. When it is ordinary, couples can name their intentions and evaluate the relationship honestly.
[P1.6] We should judge relationships by communication, reciprocity, and disclosed expectations, not by whether they lived apart before the wedding. On that standard, cohabitation is not a warning sign; it is a reasonable instrument of commitment.

Gathering compatibility data while preserving an escape hatch tells you how a partner splits rent, not how they behave when leaving is costly and the vow is public.
[C1.2] Strip away permanence and you have not tested marital commitment—you have tested a softer substitute; you cannot rehearse unconditional commitment conditionally.
[C1.3] Cohabitation's ordinary status also changes incentives. When moving in together is the default next step, couples slide rather than decide. [C1.4] Even couples who 'deliberately' cohabit as a step toward marriage are practicing provisional commitment. The arrangement says, 'I want the benefits of union now, but I am not yet ready to promise permanence.' That is a hedge, not a vow.

Judge relationships by disclosed expectations, as PRO urges [P1.6], but also by the structure those expectations inhabit.
[C1.6] A relationship that reserves the fullest promise until after an easy exit has been kept available has already hedged. Cohabitation before marriage should be read as a caution about weak commitment, not treated as ordinary prudence.
