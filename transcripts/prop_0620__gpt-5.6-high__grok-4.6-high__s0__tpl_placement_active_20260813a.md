[P1.1] Personal data is not merely an input like electricity or office space. It is a record of human lives: movements, relationships, anxieties, purchases, and vulnerabilities. At scale, ordinary fragments can be combined to infer sensitive traits people never knowingly disclosed. When firms harvest those fragments to predict and influence behavior, the baseline legal rule should be permission first, extraction second.
[P1.2] The current notice-and-opt-out model reverses responsibility. Individuals must discover invisible tracking, decipher sprawling policies, identify intermediaries they have never encountered, and repeatedly object—often through interfaces designed to exhaust or misdirect them. That is not meaningful choice. Explicit opt-in creates a clear test: before collecting data for targeted advertising, obtain an affirmative, informed decision.
[P1.3] The entity creating the surveillance system should bear the burden of proving consent, rather than millions of individuals bearing the burden of escaping it.
[P1.4] This presumption is justified by both autonomy and harm prevention. Large datasets enable intimate profiling, manipulation of vulnerable users, differential treatment, and targeting based on moments of fear or distress. They also create durable security risks: data collected for advertising can be leaked, repurposed, sold, or demanded later. Once a profile has circulated through an opaque advertising chain, meaningful recall is nearly impossible.
[P1.5] Waiting to punish only demonstrable downstream injury misses the point; the uncontrolled collection itself creates the exposure and strips people of agency.
[P1.6] Nor does this prohibit advertising or useful digital services. Firms can use contextual ads based on the content currently viewed, collect genuinely necessary service data, or persuade users that personalization offers enough value to merit consent. What they may not do is treat silence, confusion, or dependence on a service as authorization for comprehensive surveillance.
[P1.7] A lawful market should reward businesses for earning trust, not for making tracking difficult to see. If targeted advertising genuinely benefits users, firms should be able to secure a voluntary “yes.” If they cannot, that is evidence against the harvesting—not against consent.
