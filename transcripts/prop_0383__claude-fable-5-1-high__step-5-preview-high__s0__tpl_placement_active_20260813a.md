[P1.1] A foam clamshell holds a sandwich for about fifteen minutes. It then sits in a landfill, a storm drain, or a river for centuries, because expanded polystyrene is nearly impossible to recycle, breaks into fragments that never disappear, and is almost never collected at all. That fifteen-minute convenience is paid for by everyone except the person who bought the sandwich: by taxpayers funding litter cleanup, by fishing communities, by water utilities straining out microplastics, by whoever lives next to the incinerator.
[P1.2] The motion asks only one thing: should that bill finally be sent to the people creating it, even though some of them will grumble? Yes.
[P1.3] Notice how narrow the proposition is. It targets the most wasteful packaging, not all packaging, not plastic generally. Foam containers, single-use plastic cutlery handed out unasked, and multi-layer cups that cannot be recycled anywhere are the worst offenders because the alternatives already exist and already work. Paperboard boxes, fiber trays, wooden cutlery, and deposit-based reusable cup schemes are stocked by every mainstream packaging supplier today.
[P1.4] The transition cost is real but bounded: a few cents per unit, a changed order form, a short adjustment period. The cost of inaction is unbounded, because the waste accumulates every year and nobody is paying to remove it.
[P1.5] The inconvenience argument proves less than it seems. Every rule that corrects an externality inconveniences the people who were previously getting something for free. Plastic bag charges across many countries cut bag use by very large margins within months, and the predicted collapse of retail never arrived; shoppers adapted within a season. Markets respond to clear rules.
[P1.6] What actually hurts small businesses is uncertainty and uneven competition, where the cafe that does the right thing voluntarily pays more than the one across the street that doesn't. A phase-out, with a lead time and a level playing field, removes exactly that disadvantage.
[P1.7] So the comparison is clear: modest, temporary, concentrated costs for a transition we already know how to make, against permanent, diffuse, growing costs dumped on everyone else. Phasing out [clipped]
