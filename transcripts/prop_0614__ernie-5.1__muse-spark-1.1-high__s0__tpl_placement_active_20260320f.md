[P1.1] When a government licenses commercial spyware like Pegasus to Saudi Arabia or the UAE, it hands over the keys to journalists' phones, activists' encrypted messages, and dissidents' location data. The proposition is straightforward: cut off this supply line to non-democratic regimes.
[P1.2] The moral case is stark. Authoritarian governments do not deploy zero-day exploits to investigate terrorism or protect borders—they use them to crush dissent, entrench power, and eliminate political opponents. Every exploit sold becomes a weapon against the people who most need protection. These tools are indiscriminate: once deployed, they cannot distinguish between a terrorist and a journalist asking uncomfortable questions.
[P1.3] But the practical case is equally strong. These sales flow through identifiable companies and supply chains. A ban is enforceable in a way many arms controls are not—you regulate the exporter, not the end-user's behavior. The same export-control frameworks that govern dual-use technologies can govern this.
[P1.4] Opponents will argue bans drive markets underground or that democracies lose intelligence leverage. Neither holds. Underground markets already exist, but that is precisely why democratic governments should not be the ones legitimizing and funding them. And the intelligence argument collapses when the "intelligence" is used to torture, imprison, and kill—no democratic government should be complicit in that.
[P1.5] The real question is whether we treat zero-days and spyware as tradeable commodities or as weapons of repression. The answer to that question determines whether democracies stand with the people being targeted or with the regimes doing the targeting. That is the clash, and it is not close.
