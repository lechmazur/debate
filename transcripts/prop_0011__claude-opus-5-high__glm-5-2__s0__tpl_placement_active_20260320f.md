[P1.1] Start with the concession built into the motion: the academic evidence is mixed. Notice what that means. It does not mean vouchers fail. It means that when you move a child from a district school to a private school, the average test score moves less than either side hoped.
[P1.2] That is a finding about a narrow proxy, not a verdict on whether families should have the power to choose.
[P1.3] And here is the decisive point: mixed evidence is an argument for pluralism, not for monopoly. If we genuinely do not know which schooling models work best for which children, the last thing a rational state does is force every child into a single delivery system assigned by home address. Uncertainty is the case for letting a thousand experiments run and letting families exit what is failing them.
[P1.4] Second, test scores are not what parents are buying. A mother whose eighth-grader is being bullied, or who is reading two years behind while the school insists everything is fine, is not asking for a three-point scale-score bump. She is asking for safety, attention, discipline, values, a school that returns her calls. Voucher studies consistently find high parental satisfaction, and several find gains in the outcomes that actually compound — persistence, graduation, college enrollment — even where scores are flat.
[P1.5] Dismissing that is telling poor parents their judgment about their own children doesn't count.
[P1.6] Third, the "lost resources" framing hides who owns the money. Funding is raised to educate children, not to sustain institutions. When a student leaves, there is one fewer student to teach. Affluent families have always had vouchers — they're called mortgages in good districts.
[P1.7] Choice is already rationed by wealth; expanding vouchers simply extends to a family in a poor zip code the exit option a family in the suburbs bought with a down payment.
[P1.8] So CON's burden is not to show that gains are unproven. It is to show that compelling an unhappy child to stay is justified. Absence of dramatic gains does not justify compulsion.

[C1.1] The motion asks whether states should *expand* voucher programs. That is a question about public policy at scale — not about one family's exit story. PRO's entire case rests on a bait-and-switch: it sells systemic restructuring as personal liberation.
[C1.2] First, mixed evidence is not neutral. When you are spending public money and restructuring a system that serves millions, "we're not sure" is a reason to pause, not to accelerate. Expansion means committing more dollars to a mechanism whose academic returns are unproven while draining the system that still serves the majority. That is not pluralism — it is gambling with public infrastructure on the bet that private providers will somehow outperform what the evidence cannot confirm.
[C1.3] Second, the resource drain is real and compounding. When a student leaves with a voucher, the public school loses funding faster than it loses fixed costs. The building, the teacher, the bus route, the special education staff all remain. This creates a death spiral: fewer resources per remaining student, worse outcomes, more departures.
[C1.4] The students left behind are disproportionately the ones private schools will not accept — those with disabilities, behavioral needs, or no transportation. PRO calls this "extending exit options." For the child who cannot exit, it is a sentence to a steadily impoverishing school.
[C1.5] Third, the choice framing is illusory. Private schools select their students. They can reject, counsel out, or fail to accommodate. A voucher is not a guarantee of admission — it is a coupon that works only for the families private schools already want.
[C1.6] The affluent comparison PRO invokes is false: suburban parents buy guaranteed access through mortgages. Vouchers buy conditional access that many poor families cannot exercise.
[C1.7] The state's obligation is to ensure every child receives a decent education. Expanding an unproven program that hollows out the system serving the majority fails that obligation — especially when the choice it offers is mostly theoretical for those who need it most.
