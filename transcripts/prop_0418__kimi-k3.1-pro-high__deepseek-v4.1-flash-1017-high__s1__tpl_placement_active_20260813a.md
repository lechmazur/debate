[C1.2] Platforms can verify identity, license, background, vehicle, and trip history; a women-only toggle verifies almost none of that. Worse, it can create false assurance: the rider relaxes because the match is “safe,” while the same gaps in vetting, route monitoring, and emergency response remain.
[C1.3] The operational cost is not a software checkbox. Ride-hail depends on two-sided liquidity. Splitting the driver pool by sex shrinks supply for both products, especially at night and in low-density areas. The predictable results are longer waits, more cancellations, higher surge, and fewer completed trips.
[C1.4] The woman who cannot get a women-only car either waits alone on the curb, pays more, or takes a standard ride she distrusts—none of which is safer. A feature that works only where supply is already abundant is least useful exactly where safety concerns are greatest.
[C1.5] Enforcement adds another layer: who counts as a woman, how drivers are verified, what happens to trans drivers or documents that do not match, and who bears liability when the promise of a “women-only” ride is breached. Separate matching queues, pricing, and support are real costs, passed to riders or drivers.
[C1.6] Trust matters, but it should be earned with safeguards that protect everyone: background checks, route sharing, panic buttons, verified pickups, and easy cancellation. Those do not fragment the market. PRO calls faster matching a hollow victory, but a match that never arrives is no victory for safety either. Reliability is safety.
[C1.7] The motion must show a net safety improvement, not merely an option some riders prefer. It has not.
