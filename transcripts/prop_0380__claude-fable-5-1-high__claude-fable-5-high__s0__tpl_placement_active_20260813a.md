[P1.1] The franchise line at eighteen is not a scientific finding. It is a leftover. Most democracies picked eighteen mid-century because it matched conscription age, and nobody has re-examined the fit since. Meanwhile, we ask sixteen-year-olds to work, pay income tax on those wages, consent to medical treatment, drive a car, and in many places stand trial as adults.
[P1.2] We already trust them with responsibility; we just deny them the one tool that lets them hold the people making the rules accountable. Taxation without representation is an odd principle to defend in a democracy.
[P1.3] The competence objection collapses on inspection. Developmental research distinguishes "cold" cognition — deliberate, informed reasoning — from "hot" cognition, the impulsive choices made under peer pressure or emotional stress. Cold reasoning is essentially adult-level by mid-adolescence. Hot reasoning matures later, into the mid-twenties.
[P1.4] Voting is the coldest civic act there is: you read, you think, you mark a paper in private with weeks to prepare. If we drew the line where hot cognition matures, we would disenfranchise every twenty-three-year-old. Nobody proposes that, because the standard is being applied selectively.
[P1.5] Here is the argument that should decide the debate: voting is a habit, and eighteen is the worst possible age to start it. Eighteen-year-olds are moving out, starting jobs or university, changing addresses, cut loose from the routines that make registration easy. Sixteen-year-olds live at home, attend school, and can be registered and taught the mechanics in a classroom. Where this has been tried — Austria for over a decade, Scotland since its independence referendum, Wales, Malta, several German states — sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds have turned out at higher rates than eighteen-to-twenty-four-year-olds, and studies found their choices tracked their stated values as coherently as adults' did.
[P1.6] First votes stick. Catch citizens at sixteen and you get lifelong voters; miss them and you get the chronic youth-turnout gap every democracy laments.
[P1.7] Finally, on climate, pensions, and public debt, sixteen-year-olds will live with today's decisions longest. The people with the biggest stake deserve a say.

[C1.1] Democracies do not hand out the ballot as a prize for passing a cognition test. The vote marks full civic adulthood — the point at which society holds you completely responsible for yourself. At sixteen you are, in nearly every legal system, a minor: you cannot sign a binding contract, sit on a jury, or make major decisions without a guardian's consent. Your parents remain legally answerable for you.
[C1.2] Giving a legally dependent person the supreme instrument of self-government inverts the basic bargain of citizenship: full rights arrive with full responsibility, together, at eighteen.
[C1.3] That dependency is not a technicality — it is the practical problem. Sixteen-year-olds live under their parents' roof, on their parents' money, and increasingly under the influence of teachers and school administrators who would now be registering and mobilizing them. The secret ballot protects the moment of marking a paper; it cannot protect a minor from the household and institutional pressures that shape everything before that moment. We would not be enfranchising independent citizens so much as multiplying the votes of the adults around them and politicizing classrooms in the process.
[C1.4] Notice, too, which direction every other age line is moving. Societies have been raising thresholds — for tobacco, for alcohol, for trying children as adults — precisely because we now recognize adolescence as a protected stage, not a shortened one. Lowering the single most consequential threshold while raising all the others is incoherent.
[C1.5] The cognition argument proves too much anyway. If "cold reasoning by mid-adolescence" settles it, why sixteen and not fourteen? The science offers no bright line at sixteen; only adulthood does. And modern elections are not cold environments — they are conducted amid viral misinformation and intense peer signaling, exactly where adolescent judgment is weakest.
[C1.6] The real youth-turnout problem is a registration problem, fixable with automatic registration at eighteen — not a reason to hand the franchise to children.
