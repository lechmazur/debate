[P1.1] The choice is not between a treaty and a lunar free-for-all. It is between conditional, accountable legality and an indefinite legal limbo in which activity can still proceed, but with fewer incentives to disclose or clean up. Recognition means permission under enforceable conditions, not ownership of the Moon, immunity from international law, or a permanent settlement of benefit-sharing.
[P1.2] Existing space law already supplies important guardrails: states must authorize and supervise their operators, avoid harmful interference, register space objects, and answer for damage. Conflating resource use with territorial conquest would also condemn activities that could support sustainable exploration. A later comprehensive treaty may be desirable, but desirability is not a legal prerequisite.

Consider a company extracting water ice for propellant: under this proposal, legality is the leverage that makes transparency and debris control the price of doing business.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes an absence of explicit permission for a legal vacuum that needs filling. In reality, the Moon remains a global commons where the principle of non-appropriation and the lack of any treaty authorizing commercial title create a deliberate check against unilateral extraction. Declaring sale legal before a full treaty does not merely clarify ambiguity; it resolves that ambiguity in favor of whoever moves first. That is not a procedural shortcut but a substantive transfer of authority from the international community to individual licensing states and their chosen operators.
[C1.2] The proposed safeguards are weaker than they appear. Transparency and debris-control duties are only as strong as the state willing to enforce them. Without a treaty to harmonize standards, licensing states face a straightforward incentive to offer light-touch regulation to attract capital. A company can shop for the most permissive flag, disclose the minimum required, and claim compliance while independent verification remains thin.
[C1.3] The proposition leaves enforcement to the very actors who benefit from laxity.
[C1.4] Early recognition also produces irreversible economic facts. Once billions are sunk into extraction infrastructure, operators will possess overwhelming political leverage to shape any later treaty, turning equitable benefit-sharing and access into afterthoughts. The logical order is to establish distributive rules before commercial stakes mature, not to hope that entrenched interests will voluntarily accept retroactive constraints.
[C1.5] Finally, the Moon is designated as the province of all mankind. Unilateral legality grants a handful of wealthy operators the right to convert a shared heritage into private inventory, freezing out states that never consented. A treaty should not be treated as a formality that follows market fait accompli; it is the mechanism by which the many remain owners of the commons. Premature recognition does not govern the future—it auctions it.
