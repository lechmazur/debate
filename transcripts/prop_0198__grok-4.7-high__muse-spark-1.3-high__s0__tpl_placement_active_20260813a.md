[P1.1] The Constitution assigns tariff power to Congress, not the president. Article I gives Congress the authority to lay duties and to regulate commerce with foreign nations. A sweeping tariff is both a tax and a trade regulation. Emergency powers that let one official impose that tax across broad categories of imports invert the constitutional design.
[P1.2] Reclaiming sole authority over broad tariff policy, and eliminating those emergency powers, returns the decision to the branch that must vote in public.
[P1.3] Emergency statutes were built for genuine, temporary crises, not as a substitute trade code. A persistent deficit, industrial decline, or a demand for negotiating leverage is ordinary policy, not a sudden emergency. Once "emergency" becomes the password for economy-wide tariffs, every president has reason to declare one. The country then gets legislation without a legislature: no hearings that bind, no amendments, no recorded vote whose authors must defend higher prices to their own voters.
[P1.4] Those prices, and the retaliation that follows, are the point of the clash. Broad unilateral tariffs raise costs for households and manufacturers, punish exporters when partners strike back, and make supply chains a political football. Because one person can announce, expand, or cancel them, firms and allies plan for whim rather than for law. Congress is slower.
[P1.5] That slowness is the safeguard. Economy-wide taxes should require a majority willing to own the costs. Gridlock is not a license to transfer the taxing power; it is evidence that no such majority yet exists.
[P1.6] Limited authority for a true, narrow security threat can remain. What cannot remain is a blank check to rewrite broad tariff policy under an emergency label. Speed does not justify a durable tax imposed without a vote. The branch that legislates in daylight should decide a policy that functions as both a nationwide tax and a diplomatic weapon.
[P1.7] Close the emergency shortcut.
