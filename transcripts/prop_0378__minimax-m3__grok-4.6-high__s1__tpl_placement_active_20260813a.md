[P1.1] Friendship isn't dying from neglect. It's dying from structural pressure, and that's exactly why treating it as a private matter has already failed. Surveys over the last generation show Americans report fewer close friends than they used to, with a striking share admitting they have no one to confide in. We know what this costs: loneliness reliably shortens lives on a scale that has moved public health bodies to act, depresses workplace productivity, weakens civic participation, and dumps more demand onto publicly funded mental-health services.
[P1.2] A problem that large, and that externalized, is not a private one.
[P1.3] Consider what has collapsed around us. The "third places" — cafés, lodges, bowling leagues, parish halls, civic clubs — have been hollowed out by consolidation, remote work, screen-mediated leisure, and the simple math of two-earner households with no surplus evening. No individual fixes that; no private choice restores a parish hall. When the infrastructure of connection disappears, telling lonely adults to "just make more friends" is like telling a stranded driver to "just find a road."
[P1.4] The policy response is already familiar in miniature. Public libraries host conversation hours and friendship benches. Some cities fund community-center programming rather than letting buildings rot. Employers experimenting with cross-team social time have reported gains in retention and engagement.
[P1.5] None of this is coercive; it leaves the content of friendship to individuals while rebuilding the scaffolding. Compare the alternative: a society that treats friendship as strictly private has produced the very decline we now lament.
[P1.6] The opposing frame treats friendship as a taste, like preferring tea to coffee. But taste creates no externality. Friendship does. A friendshipless adult is more likely to need public mental-health care, less likely to vote, less likely to help a neighbor.
[P1.7] The private-choice model has had a generation to show it works. It hasn't.
