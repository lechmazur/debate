[P1.1] Universities should allow AI-assisted drafting in most coursework if students disclose their use and remain accountable for accuracy and reasoning. This is not a surrender to technology but a pragmatic recognition: AI, like the calculator before it, can serve as a cognitive catalyst. AI can help students overcome barriers in expression—such as grammar, clarity, or organization—without relieving them of the responsibility to think critically. For example, an AI might help a history student refine a thesis statement, but the student must still select and interpret primary sources, construct an argument, and ensure its historical accuracy.
[P1.2] Disclosure ensures instructors know the context of the work, allowing fair assessment without the deception that fuels academic misconduct concerns.
[P1.3] Permitting disclosed AI use also advances equity. Students with dyslexia, ADHD, or non-native English proficiency often face disproportionate struggles with the mechanics of writing, which can obscure their grasp of course material. AI-assisted drafting helps them demonstrate their knowledge without being unfairly disadvantaged by expression challenges. The accountability requirement preserves the integrity of the assignment: the student remains responsible for the substance of the work, so the cognitive rigor is maintained.
[P1.4] Outright prohibition is impractical and counterproductive. AI writing tools are already integrated into many platforms and are trivial to access; enforcement would require intrusive monitoring that poisons the student-faculty relationship. Worse, it pushes AI use underground, where students learn neither the tool’s potential nor its pitfalls.
[P1.5] By contrast, allowing disclosed use enables universities to teach responsible AI literacy—how to prompt effectively, verify outputs, and cite sources. This prepares students for professional environments where AI is standard and ethical use is a competitive advantage.
[P1.6] The alternative is a policy that ignores reality, wastes resources on detection, and denies students the chance to learn with the very tools shaping their future careers. Such a ban would leave students ill-prepared for an AI-augmented world.

[C1.1] The proposition's fatal flaw is buried in one word: "drafting." Drafting is not a mechanical chore that sits beside thinking—it *is* the thinking. Anyone who has written seriously knows that you discover what you believe by struggling to put it into words: the false start that exposes a weak premise, the paragraph that collapses and forces you to rethink your thesis. Outsource the draft and you outsource precisely the cognitive work coursework exists to build.
[C1.2] The calculator analogy fails for exactly this reason: a calculator automates arithmetic so students can focus on mathematical reasoning. AI drafting automates the reasoning itself. It is not a calculator; it is someone else doing the proof.
[C1.3] Second, the safeguards in this motion are decorative. "Disclosure" tells an instructor nothing useful—a checkbox saying "AI was used" cannot distinguish a student who polished their own prose from one who prompted, lightly edited, and submitted. And "accountability for accuracy and reasoning" is hollow when the student never generated the reasoning. You cannot be meaningfully accountable for an argument you didn't construct; you can only be accountable for proofreading it.
[C1.4] The motion asks universities to certify competence students may never have developed. A degree is supposed to warrant that its holder can think and write. Under this policy, it warrants that they can supervise a chatbot.
[C1.5] Third, notice the scope: "most coursework." Not capstone projects with explicit AI components, not designated digital-literacy modules—*most* assignments across the curriculum, including first-year courses where foundational skills are formed. That is the worst possible place to permit it, like letting novice pilots fly on autopilot before they've learned to land.
[C1.6] The real choice isn't between this policy and a futile blanket ban. It's between universities that protect the formative struggle of learning and universities that quietly abandon it while keeping the tuition. Targeted, course-specific permission is defensible. Default permission across most coursework is institutional surrender dressed as pragmatism.
