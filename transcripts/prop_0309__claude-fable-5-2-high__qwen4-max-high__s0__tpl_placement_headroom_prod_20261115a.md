[P1.1] A hospital's balance sheet measures volume. A heart attack measures minutes. That gap is the whole debate.
[P1.2] Market logic says consolidate: more patients per bed, lower unit cost. All true, and all beside the point, because the market is pricing the wrong thing. A rural emergency room is not valuable because of how many patients it bills; it is valuable because it is there when the stroke, the farm accident, or the hemorrhage after childbirth happens. That is standby capacity, and nobody pays for standby.
[P1.3] We do not ask a rural fire station to turn a profit on fires fought. We fund it because the alternative is watching the house burn while a truck drives ninety minutes from the city.
[P1.4] These hospitals rarely fail because nobody needs them. They fail because their patients are older, poorer, and more often publicly insured or uninsured, and because fixed costs do not shrink with population. A closure is a signal about payment, not about need. Treating it as proof of worthlessness mistakes a revenue problem for a value judgment.
[P1.5] And consolidation does not erase costs; it relocates them onto the people least able to bear them. The pregnant woman who now drives two hours for delivery. The county's only ambulance, gone for half a day on a single transfer while the next call waits. Then the second-order damage: the largest employer goes, then the doctors, then the families.
[P1.6] Nobody is defending brain surgery in every village. Complex elective care belongs in regional centers, where volume improves outcomes. The claim is that emergency stabilization, obstetrics, and basic inpatient care must exist within reach, and that government should pay for that when revenue will not.
[P1.7] Efficiency that works only by making distance lethal is not efficiency. It is cost-shifting onto rural lives.
