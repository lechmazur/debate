[P1.1] Peer review is the backbone of scholarship, yet it limps along on unpaid labor and secrecy. This undermines quality and accountability. The fix is simple: pay reviewers and publish their reports alongside the papers they evaluate.
[P1.2] Uncompensated review creates a systemic moral hazard. Journals charge thousands in publication fees yet expect expert evaluation for free, effectively subsidizing profits with volunteer academic labor. This devalues the work and discourages participation, especially from early-career researchers who can least afford the unpaid hours. Payment would transform this: even modest per-review fees would recognize peer review as serious, skilled labor.
[P1.3] The result would be more qualified reviewers, more time invested per review, and higher quality feedback. Currently, overworked academics often rush reviews between other duties; compensation would make thorough evaluation the rational choice rather than an altruistic burden.
[P1.4] Anonymity is the other critical flaw, enabling irresponsibility. It allows reviewers to be vague, harsh, or biased without consequence. Publishing reviews alongside papers forces accountability. Signed critiques must be justified rigorously, not hidden behind anonymity’s veil.
[P1.5] Readers gain full context: they see not just the final paper but the dialogue that shaped it, including disagreements among reviewers and their reasoning. This transparency deters conflicts of interest and lets the community assess evaluation fairness. Open review models in computing, medicine, and social sciences show public critiques can be as candid and more constructive than anonymous ones.
[P1.6] The status quo is unsustainable. It treats a function vital to scholarly integrity as an afterthought, expecting expert labor for free and in darkness. Paid, published review would professionalize peer review, improve quality, and restore trust in academic publishing.

[C1.1] Peer review has one job: to tell the scientific community what a paper's claims are actually worth. Judge this motion by that standard — not by which side sounds fairer — and both of its pillars collapse.
[C1.2] Start with payment. A per-review fee is piecework, and piecework pays for completion, not care. The rational response to a flat fee is to review faster and accept more invitations, not to spend another weekend checking the statistics. Meanwhile the fee journals can realistically offer — a few hundred dollars at most — will not move the senior expert whose hour is scarce; it will disproportionately attract reviewers whose time is cheapest.
[C1.3] Crowding out is real: turn a professional duty into a small transaction and you convert "I owe this to my field" into "is this worth my rate?" — often the answer becomes no. Nor can money fix the actual bottleneck. Expert hours are fixed. Paying reviewers bids up the price of the same hours and passes the bill to authors through publication fees, hitting unfunded fields and researchers in poorer countries hardest.
[C1.4] Now publication. Anonymity isn't a shield for laziness; it's the only reason a junior scholar can write "this analysis is fatally flawed" about a lab that might sit on their hiring committee, review their grant, or referee their next paper. Sign the reports and honesty becomes career risk. Predict the results: softer reviews, more declined invitations, and in small fields where everyone knows everyone, quiet log-rolling — you praise mine, I'll praise yours, in public, verifiably.
[C1.5] Published reviews also become performances written for an audience, not candid assessments written for an editor.
[C1.6] The accountability PRO wants already exists: editors read reviews, weigh them, and discard biased ones. Publishing reports adds theater, not scrutiny.
[C1.7] Worst is the combination: payment rewards saying yes, publicity punishes saying anything sharp. That is a machine for fast, bland, conflicted review — the opposite of better.

[P2.6] Published reviews are not performances; they are essential components of the scientific record, providing the accountability and context the current broken system lacks.
