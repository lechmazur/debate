[P1.1] An influencer with a large audience is not merely a private person sharing an opinion; they are a paid media personality operating in the attention economy. When a beauty creator reviews skincare, a streamer promotes an energy drink, or a fitness personality endorses supplements through affiliate links, viewers reasonably understand the statement as personal testimony, not as a paid advertisement. That gap between perception and economic reality is exactly the harm disclosure rules exist to prevent. The medium may be a phone rather than a television studio, but the persuasive function is the same.
[P1.2] The case for strictness gets stronger, not weaker, as audience size grows. Large creators enjoy parasocial trust: followers feel they know them, so endorsements carry more credibility than conventional ads. Reach and revenue scale together, while hidden commercial relationships can shift purchasing decisions at mass-market scale. A short, clear, unavoidable disclosure imposes almost no burden compared with the deception it prevents.
[P1.3] Strict rules therefore need not restrict speech; they correct an information asymmetry so audiences can weigh the message as advertising.
[P1.4] There is also no principled reason to allow regulatory arbitrage. If a magazine ad or television segment must disclose payment, a creator should not escape the same duty by moving the same endorsement to Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube. Lax influencer rules would simply push advertising dollars toward the least transparent channels. Traditional-media standards are administrable, widely understood, and compatible with commercial media; applying their substance to large creators closes a loophole rather than inventing a novel burden.
[P1.5] Equal rules follow from equal function. Large influencers already possess media power, monetize audience attention, and shape consumer choices at scale. They should bear the same transparency obligations as traditional media personalities: disclose payment, free products, affiliate relationships, and material connections in clear, conspicuous, hard-to-miss terms.

[C1.1] The proposition fails at its central equation: a large audience does not make someone a traditional media personality, and “as strict as” is not a neutral extension of existing law. Traditional disclosure regimes assume identifiable ads—a sponsored segment, a paid placement, a host reading a script. They can require labels at the point of broadcast because the commercial unit is separated from editorial content. Influencer speech is continuous, personal, and mixed: a creator may mention a product because she uses it, because a friend works there, or because a brand paid her.
[C1.2] Strict parity would either miss that distinction or force creators to label opinion as advertising, which is compelled speech and drains the label of meaning.
[C1.3] Second, strict parity is a compliance trap. Traditional media personalities operate inside institutions with legal review, standardized formats, and production schedules. Individual creators do not. Importing broadcast-style duties—pre-clearance, fixed disclaimers, recordkeeping—advantages established agencies and punishes independent voices, especially those outside legacy media.
[C1.4] That is not closing a loophole; it is protecting incumbents.
[C1.5] Third, the “equal function” claim proves too much. If every large creator with commercial relationships is a media personality, then every monetized post becomes regulated advertising. But audiences do not read a cooking video as a magazine ad, and they do not need the same warning architecture. The real harm—hidden payment—can be addressed by clear, targeted disclosure rules keyed to material connections, not by treating influence as broadcast.
[C1.6] Strictness is not effectiveness. A blunt label on everything becomes wallpaper. The better regime distinguishes paid promotion from genuine editorial judgment, enforces against concealed payment, and preserves room for ordinary speech. Large influencers should disclose material connections, but the proposition’s demand for parity with traditional media is a category error, not consumer protection.
