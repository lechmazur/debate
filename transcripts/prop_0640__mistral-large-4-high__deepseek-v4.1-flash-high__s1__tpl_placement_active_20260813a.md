[P1.1] Agricultural land is becoming a financial asset class. Pension funds, insurance giants, and private equity firms treat farmland as a stable, inflation-hedged investment—buying thousands of acres not to work them, but to park capital and extract rent. This financialization hollows out rural communities and threatens the resilience of our food systems.
[P1.2] When an institutional investor acquires farmland, the land rarely passes to a working farmer. Instead, it is leased back to operators under short-term contracts, converted to monocultures for maximum yield, or left fallow while the fund waits for appreciation. The result is a one-way transfer of wealth from the countryside to distant balance sheets. Young farmers—facing crippling debt and volatile prices—bid against entities with hundreds of billions in assets.
[P1.3] They cannot compete. Family farms are priced out, breaking the intergenerational transfer of knowledge and severing ties that keep small towns alive.
[P1.4] This matters for resilience because absentee ownership severs the link between stewardship and profit. A fund manager in Manhattan or a pension trustee in Toronto has no stake in next year’s soil health, no relationship with the local watershed, and no commitment to keeping food production domestic during a crisis. When returns dip, land is sold to the highest bidder—often another fund or foreign buyer—creating instability. We have watched this devastate housing; farmland is the next frontier for extractive capital.
[P1.5] Governments recognize that concentrated, non-productive ownership of strategic resources poses systemic risks. New Zealand restricts foreign purchases of sensitive rural land. Several U.S. states bar corporate farming. These are not anti-market gestures; they are circuit breakers preventing the commodification of the ground beneath our food supply.
[P1.6] A cap on institutional ownership preserves farming as a vocation rather than a speculative instrument. It ensures that those who work the land have a path to own it, that rural communities retain their economic anchor, and that food security remains with those who produce food—not those who merely trade it.

[C1.1] The motion asks a cap to do something ownership caps rarely do: change behavior. The problem PRO describes is not that a particular investor holds title. It is how farmland is used and controlled: short leases, monoculture, speculation, absentee decisions. But a cap on non-farming institutional ownership can be evaded while leaving those practices untouched.
[C1.2] An investor blocked from owning land can finance it, lease it, manage it through a subsidiary, take an option, or write contracts that capture the same returns and control. If control, not title, is the threat, the cap is a fence with a gate wide open.
[C1.3] Worse, it bans useful capital alongside extractive capital. A local pension fund, a university endowment, or an insurance pool can provide patient capital that helps a retiring farmer cash out while a younger operator leases with an option to buy. That is not necessarily a hollowing out. A cap can collapse the buyer pool, reducing farmland values and making succession harder for exactly the rural families PRO wants to protect.
[C1.4] Land becomes less liquid, credit tightens, and the next buyer is often the already-wealthy neighboring operation—not a young farmer.
[C1.5] Resilience is built by rules attached to land: conservation covenants, long-term tenancy protections, limits on fallowing or speculation, right of first refusal for local producers, and antitrust scrutiny of actual consolidation. Those target the harm directly. An ownership cap targets a label. A leased farm run by a local family under a conservation easement can be resilient; an owner-operated farm can still drain the aquifer and degrade the soil.
[C1.6] It can punish good actors, reward evasion, and leave the real drivers untouched. PRO must show that banning a class of owners improves soil, food production, and rural livelihoods. It cannot, because the mechanism doesn't connect. The cap is symbolic, evadable, and costly.
[C1.7] Vote CON.

[C2.1] PRO's rebuttal rests on one sentence: "control follows title." That is the claim to defeat, because the motion caps title, not control. In agriculture, operational control routinely travels through debt covenants, management agreements, offtake contracts, water rights, and options. An institution barred from the deed can finance an operator who must plant specified crops, sell to a specified buyer, and farm to a lender's yield target.
[C2.2] Conversely, a local family can hold the deed and still be a passive landlord while a corporate integrator makes every real decision. So the cap changes who signs the closing papers, not who bears risk or dictates soil practices. A fund can own just below the cap in every county and still control far more land through leases and management deals.
[C2.3] PRO's celebration of the "wealthy neighboring operation" confirms the problem. The motion does not cap large local farmers. It hands scarce ownership rights to incumbents—often the best-capitalized neighbors—who can consolidate further, lease land out, or strip it just as easily as a fund. "Local" is not a synonym for "steward."
[C2.4] A neighbor can drain the aquifer; a distant owner can fund cover crops. The relevant variable is land-use rules and accountability, not the owner's mailing address.
[C2.5] The patient-capital point also cuts against PRO. If the cap binds, it shrinks the buyer pool for retiring farmers. Their farm is usually their pension. A thinner market can mean a lower sale price, weaker collateral, and tighter credit for the next generation.
[C2.6] PRO says capital can still lend or hold minority stakes; but if the cap is meaningful, it reduces the ownership capital available exactly when succession is most fragile. That harms rural communities.
[C2.7] PRO calls the cap structural. It is not. It is a title test that leaves the lease, the debt, and the management contract—the actual machinery of control—untouched. A covenant or tenancy rule can require stewardship on any farm.
[C2.8] A cap cannot. Vote CON.
