[P1.1] Legislators face a rigged payoff: spending increases and tax cuts win votes today, while the debt bill arrives long after they leave office. Left to discretion, governments borrow in booms as well as busts. That is not cynicism, it is the arithmetic of elections. Only a higher law can stop today's majority from picking tomorrow's pocket.
[P1.2] Unlimited discretion mortgages people who cannot yet vote. Interest payments quietly devour budgets, forcing higher future taxes or cuts to pensions, health and education. Heavy debt pushes up borrowing costs for families and firms and leaves the state weakest precisely when crisis strikes, with no fiscal room to respond. Freedom without a rule becomes freedom to bankrupt successors.
[P1.3] A constitutional rule requiring structural balance, with automatic stabilizers exempted, is discipline with lungs. When recession hits, tax collections fall and unemployment support rises, producing a timely deficit without a political fight. When recovery comes, the same mechanisms automatically produce surpluses to pay down debt. What is banned is permanent red ink: funding everyday promises with borrowed money and pretending growth will cover it.
[P1.4] This is workable because markets and politicians treat constitutions differently from statutes. A statute promising restraint is broken whenever convenient. A constitutional commitment lowers risk premiums, preserves central bank independence, and forces honest debate: if a program is worth having, find the taxes to pay for it.
[P1.5] Dozens of American states live under balanced-budget requirements while still building roads, schools and rainy-day funds. European countries with debt brakes entered the pandemic with lower debt and greater capacity to act because restraint in good years bought freedom in bad years. Discretion promises flexibility and delivers indebtedness. A constitutional rule with stabilizers delivers both responsibility and resilience.

[C1.1] A constitutional balanced-budget rule cannot tell a necessary deficit from a convenient one, and it writes that confusion into the one place a country cannot easily correct. Higher law does not repeal electoral arithmetic. It relocates it.
[C1.2] Structural balance is an estimate, not a fact. Output gaps are revised long after budgets pass, and ministries already shade them. Constitutional text does not make the shading honest; it makes the fight judicial. Defer, and the rule is theater.
[C1.3] Enforce a stale forecast, and government cuts while unemployment is still rising.
[C1.4] The stabilizer exception does not save the design. Automatic revenue losses and jobless benefits are not a crisis program. Rescues, pandemics, wars, and rebuilding are discretionary. A tight reading forbids action until the damage is done.
[C1.5] A loose reading lets any majority rename its program a stabilizer. The exception either swallows the rule or bites when flexibility matters most. Suspending the constitution in each real emergency admits discretion was doing the work.
[C1.6] State rules are the wrong model. States lack a currency of their own and often must cut or tax into a downturn, amplifying the recession. Europe's debt brakes, praised as stored-up freedom, were suspended or litigated when crisis actually arrived. A bad statute can be rewritten.
[C1.7] A constitutional mistake outlives the parliament that made it.
[C1.8] Nor does the rule stop today's majority from spending. Costs migrate off the books: guarantees, underfunded pensions, mandates instead of appropriations. Voters can fire a reckless legislature. They cannot fire a formula without a constitutional crisis.
[C1.9] Roads, research, and schools can enrich the heirs. A structural-deficit ban treats that investment as a sin defined by whoever owns the forecast.
[C1.10] Discipline belongs in elections and revisable law, where errors can be punished and reversed. A constitutional clause does not cheapen debt; markets price whether it will be honored, suspended, or gamed. It locks in bad measurement and the wrong answer to the next crisis.
