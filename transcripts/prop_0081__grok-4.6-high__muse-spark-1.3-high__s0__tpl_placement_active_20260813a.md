[P1.1] Dating apps put strangers in each other's lives at a scale no bar or classified ad ever could. That is the service. Identity verification, real block tools, and scam-response are not luxuries layered on top of that service. They are the minimum conditions that make the service something other than a trap.
[P1.2] When the best versions of those tools are reserved for paying users, the company is rationing protection by ability to pay. Scammers, catfish, and harassers do not need a subscription. The people they target often cannot afford one. A free-tier account is the largest hunting ground on the platform, and the app knows it.
[P1.3] Weak free blocking lets a blocked user reappear under a new account. Unverified photos let a scammer wear someone else's face. A gated fraud desk leaves the victim reconstructing the paper trail alone.
[P1.4] Paywalling safety also reverses the incentive. Fake profiles generate matches. Matches generate engagement. Engagement generates revenue.
[P1.5] A company that charges for verification has a reason to leave the unverified majority noisy and unsafe, then sell the badge as status. A company that charges for better blocking has a reason to let harassment persist until the victim upgrades. A company that gates scam help has a reason to treat fraud as the user's problem until a credit card appears.
[P1.6] This is not a cost argument that can win. Dating apps already monetize attention, visibility, and impatience. Those features can fund baseline safety. What they cannot fund ethically is a two-class system in which the person most at risk—the broke student, the recently single parent, the older user lonely enough to be groomed—gets the weakest lock on the door.
[P1.7] Safety delayed until checkout is safety denied. If the app created the room, it does not get to charge extra for the exits.

[C1.1] Safety sounds simple until you have to build it. A blue check is not safety. A database of driver's licenses, selfies, and live scans for tens of millions of strangers is a burglary kit, an extortion file, and a single breach away from catastrophe. Mandating that the strongest identity verification be free by default means pressuring every broke student, undocumented immigrant, or teenager experimenting with identity to hand over the most sensitive documents they own to stay equally visible.
[C1.2] That does not equalize safety. It concentrates risk and creates a false halo: once everyone can get verified for free with a low-cost automated check, scammers will too, using stolen faces, and victims will trust the badge that betrayed them.
[C1.3] Paid tiers are not just greed; they are friction with a paper trail. A scammer can spin up twenty free verified accounts in an afternoon. He cannot as easily spin up twenty accounts tied to distinct credit cards, app-store histories, and chargeback identities without tripping fraud systems and leaving police something to follow. The payment wall does what a free selfie check cannot: raise the cost of predation.
[C1.4] And human scam response is expensive. Real investigators, trauma-trained reviewers, rapid takedowns — that labor scales with reports. Make the most intensive version unlimited and free and you flood the queue with revenge reports, jealous exes, and bots, burying the lonely older user PRO rightly worries about under a longer wait.
[C1.5] Basic blocking and reporting must be free. But funding advanced defenses through willing subscribers is what keeps the lights on for everyone else. Take that away and apps do not magically fund the same protection; they cheapen it, mine more data, or raise base prices — locking out exactly the vulnerable user this motion claims to save.
