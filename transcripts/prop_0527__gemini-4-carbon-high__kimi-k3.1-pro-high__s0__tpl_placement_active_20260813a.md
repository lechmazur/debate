[P1.1] A breakthrough antibiotic is the only medicine we hope almost nobody uses. When researchers discover a drug that kills resistant superbugs, doctors rightly lock it away as a last resort so bacteria cannot evolve resistance. Medically, that restraint saves lives; economically, it is fatal. Under standard market rules, tiny sales volume means innovators cannot recoup massive development costs.
[P1.2] Biotech firms have won approval for urgently needed antibiotics only to go bankrupt, driving capital out of the field while resistant infections grow deadlier.
[P1.3] Advance market commitments solve this failure by guaranteeing a viable reward for drugs that hit critical clinical targets. Yet forbidding these commitments unless unit prices drop misunderstands how antibiotics differ from other medicines. With vaccines, low unit prices maximize uptake. With reserve antibiotics, maximizing volume destroys the drug.
[P1.4] Cheap unit prices tempt overstretched hospitals to prescribe powerful new agents empirically where older generics still work, breeding resistance. High unit prices reinforce stewardship: hospital formularies require diagnostic confirmation before unlocking a costly reserve drug.
[P1.5] Allowing high unit prices also keeps commitments workable. Because clinical volumes must stay tiny, capping unit prices either leaves returns too small to attract researchers, triggers foreign reference-pricing cuts that wreck revenues abroad, or forces taxpayers to fund the entire R&D bill upfront. Permitting commitments alongside high unit prices lets countries pair guaranteed revenue floors with unit sales, mobilizing private research without derailing global pricing.
[P1.6] Finally, unit-price sticker shock is misleading. Unlike lifelong biologics, an antibiotic course lasts days for a small number of critically ill patients. Public reimbursement and tiered global access can shield bedside patients at a fraction of the cost of prolonged intensive care when untreatable infections win. Blocking these commitments over unit price optimizes for the wrong number: the deadliest outcome in medicine is not an expensive fire extinguisher, it is an empty cabinet when the ward catches fire.

Reference pricing is a reason to structure commitments as explicit availability payments and coordinate contributors—not a reason to make every rescued infection generate an expensive sale. [C2.2] One country’s imperfect leverage cannot establish that countries generally should accept high prices; at most, it describes a transition problem.
[C2.3] PRO also attacks a straw alternative. Post-approval subscriptions and milestone payments can leave development risk with private capital just as an AMC does; delinkage does not mean paying for failed molecules. Strict entry targets do not eliminate lock-in: they establish eligibility, not enduring real-world value.
[C2.4] Most importantly, PRO’s volume argument concedes my central distinction. Low prices attached to ordinary sales can encourage volume; high prices attached to sales can restrict access. Delinked payments remove the need to chase either. If contracts can restrict promotion and enforce stewardship, as PRO says, those controls—not the invoice—are doing the work.
[C2.5] A high price adds no scientific ability to diagnose sepsis.
[C2.6] The reimbursement answer is circular. Dedicated funding releases the drug when protocols or diagnostics justify it; stewardship therefore comes from the protocol, while reimbursement neutralizes the price. Where funding is delayed, capped, or absent, that price rations care. Nor does profitable pricing in wealthy countries guarantee voluntary licenses abroad; access and supply should be binding conditions of the public reward, not incidental hopes.
[C2.7] PRO finally compares one drug course with weeks of intensive care, but patients can incur both, alongside the guaranteed revenue. The relevant comparison is an identical clinical reward under two payment designs: one preserves bedside tolls and sales dependence; the other pays openly for availability, supply, and appropriate access. The second fills the cabinet without making hospitals pay again to open it.
