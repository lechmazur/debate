[P1.1] Incremental density bonuses are a political compromise that fails in practice. They treat the symptom of low housing supply while preserving the disease of artificial scarcity. When cities rely on bonuses—like allowing an accessory dwelling unit or a modest density bump in exchange for affordability set-asides—development remains patchwork, expensive, and easily blocked by discretionary reviews. NIMBY opposition simply shifts from fighting the underlying zoning to fighting the specific bonus applications, stifling the very supply the bonuses aim to create.
[P1.2] Banning new single-family-only zoning fundamentally reshapes the land market. It removes the artificial scarcity premium that inflates land prices. When developers and homeowners know they can build a fourplex, courtyard apartment, or townhome row by right on any residential lot, land values adjust to reflect actual housing capacity rather than speculative exclusion. This unlocks missing-middle housing at scale.
[P1.3] Incremental bonuses cannot achieve this because the underlying zoning still legally restricts the highest and best use of the land, keeping the baseline supply artificially choked and forcing builders to navigate costly approval processes just to add a second unit.
[P1.4] Furthermore, we must address the structural inequity of the status quo. Single-family-only zoning was historically designed to enforce economic and racial segregation by pricing out lower-income households. Incremental bonuses leave this exclusionary skeleton intact. [P1.5] A blanket ban on creating any new single-family-only districts ensures that future growth is distributed organically across the entire city. It prevents the hoarding of high-opportunity neighborhoods and guarantees that working-class families are not legally walled out of the best schools, parks, and transit corridors.
[P1.6] We cannot bonus our way out of a crisis created by absolute prohibitions. Only a complete ban on new single-family-only zoning restores a functional, equitable housing market.

[C1.4] Fourth, the equity argument cuts the other way. The exclusionary harm lives in *existing* wealthy single-family districts. [C1.5] The choice is not ban-versus-bonus. It is categorical posturing versus reforms that pass, survive, and actually deliver units.
