[P1.1] Cities can protect parks, sidewalks, and public safety without punishing people for having nowhere else to go. The difference between legitimate regulation and criminalizing poverty is whether there is somewhere else to go.
[P1.2] A just law must be possible to obey. An anti-camping order tells a person: do not sleep here. That command is only fair if sleeping somewhere safe and lawful is actually available. When shelters are full by 8 p.m., bar couples from staying together, refuse people with dogs or possessions, demand instant sobriety, or expel everyone at dawn back onto the same sidewalk, the offer is a fiction.
[P1.3] The person is not choosing the tent over a bed; there is no bed. Punishing that choice punishes status, not misconduct.
[P1.4] Enforcement without alternatives also fails on its own terms. Sweeps do not end camping; they move it a block away. Each clearing costs police and sanitation hours, fills jails and emergency rooms, and destroys the very things that allow exit from homelessness: IDs, medications, phones, tools, and trust with outreach workers. Taxpayers pay for disappearance, not solutions, while shopkeepers and neighbors see the same tents return weeks later.
[P1.5] Visible poverty is hidden briefly, then reproduced.
[P1.6] Provide genuine shelter, storage, and services first, and the moral and practical calculus flips. A low-barrier bed through the night, a locker where belongings survive the day, a caseworker who can replace documents and treat addiction make compliance both possible and attractive. Then a rule against tents in a playground or doorway is no longer banishment. It is management of shared space with an exit ramp attached.
[P1.7] Public order and compassion are not opposites here. Order lasts only when people have somewhere to go.

[C1.2] It is a veto that can always be invoked.
[C1.3] A law need not erase every hardship before it may forbid conduct that injures other people. Cities already bar blocking fire lanes, open fires, and fouling streams when apartments are scarce. Scarcity explains wanting a tent on a playground. It does not deed that playground to the first person who pitches one.
[C1.4] Sleep is a necessity. Building structures, storing waste, and shutting the public out of whole blocks is taking space. Regulating that taking punishes conduct, not the status of being poor.
[C1.5] "Genuinely available" never closes. A bed can be refused because it splits couples, bars dogs, sits across town, asks for sobriety, or opens too late. If every refusal proves the law unjust, the person who declines writes the rules for everyone else. [C1.6] Those harms land on workers, children, disabled residents, and shops with no other street.
[C1.7] Shelter and outreach are duties, not a key that must turn before any other duty counts. A ban can run alongside a bed, a locker, and a caseworker. Making those offers a precondition means years of open-ended occupation whenever supply lags, which it will. Order is owed to the people who must use the street tomorrow, not only to whether tonight's shelter matches every preference.

[P2.1] The veto charge misreads the standard. 'Genuinely available' does not mean shelter matching every preference. It means an order that can actually be obeyed: a bed open when people need it, reachable, safe enough to use, and not conditioned on abandoning a partner, a service animal, essential possessions, or entering treatment overnight. That is administrable.
[P2.2] Police and outreach teams apply versions of it every night when they ask: is there a real place for this person to go? A standard that excludes sham offers is not endless; endless is what the other side defends — endless punishment for failing to do the impossible.
[P2.3] Their fire-lane analogy collapses on that point. Anyone can refrain from parking in a fire lane without having an apartment. No one can refrain from sleeping. When a city tells a person with no bed 'do not sleep here, or there, or anywhere visible,' it is not regulating how space is taken.
[P2.4] It is punishing the condition of having no private space, which is status punished as conduct.
[P2.5] Nor does every refusal write the rules. A bed across town with no transport, full by evening, or barred to a mother with a teenage son is not refused; it is unavailable. The city retains authority to set reasonable rules — no open fires, no blocking ramps, no playground tents once a usable alternative exists. What it cannot do is call sobriety tonight, separation, or surrender of a dog a reasonable price for obeying a sleep ban, then clear the camp anyway.
[P2.6] Harms to neighbors, workers, and disabled residents are real, which is exactly why criminalization without exits fails them. Sweeps do not stop overdoses, fires, or blocked sidewalks. They relocate them a block away after destroying IDs, medicines, and contact with caseworkers.
[P2.7] Order that lasts requires somewhere to go. Enforcement alongside a real bed works. Enforcement instead of one does not.
