[P1.1] Copyright is a means to promote culture, not a command to let culture disappear. Digital objects are unusually fragile: websites vanish, file formats become unreadable, servers close, software requires obsolete hardware, and DRM can lock away material even when no viable market remains. Once those objects disappear, later permission is worthless.
[P1.2] Preservation itself requires acts copyright law regulates: copying files, migrating formats, bypassing obsolete access controls, building redundant backups, and sometimes emulating old systems. At archival scale, clearing every right is impossible. A single collection may contain millions of items with unknown authors, defunct publishers, fragmented ownership, or no responsive owner. Traditional enforcement makes one mistaken inclusion potentially ruinous through injunctions and accumulated damages.
[P1.3] The rational institutional response is therefore under-preservation: save less, discard uncertain material, and avoid public access.
[P1.4] That failure is irreversible. A temporarily over-accessible copy can be restricted later; a deleted archive cannot be reconstructed. And the benefits are diffuse but enormous: historians tracing public discourse, families finding local records, researchers examining obsolete software, and future creators building on forgotten culture. Private licensing systematically undervalues those future users because they cannot bargain in today’s market.
[P1.5] The exemption should attach to the institution and its archival mission: nonprofit digital libraries preserving and providing cultural access, not commercial actors repackaging works for profit. Exemption from traditional copyright enforcement does not mean exemption from governance. Law can require genuine nonprofit status, preservation practices, transparency, security, and remedies for conduct outside the archival mission. What must disappear is the threat that ordinary copying rules and catastrophic copyright liability can destroy the archive itself.
[P1.6] Copyright’s ordinary model assumes an identifiable owner, a functioning market, and replaceable goods. Digital heritage often has none of those features. Protecting every theoretical claim at the cost of permanently losing humanity’s record mistakes the tool for the purpose.
