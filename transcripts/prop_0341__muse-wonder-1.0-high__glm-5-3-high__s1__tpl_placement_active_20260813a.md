[P1.1] Clicking “I am over 18” is not child protection. It is a legal fig leaf that any ten-year-old can lift. For pornography and gambling — products lawful for adults but legally and developmentally restricted for children — governments already accept that access must be gated offline. Online, we have replaced the gate with a pop-up, and then acted surprised when children walk through it.
[P1.2] The harm is not symmetrical with adult annoyance. Frictionless access normalizes hardcore pornography before maturity and puts 24-hour slot machines and casino games in a child’s bedroom, where secrecy and addictive design can take hold before parents see a bill. An adult faces minutes of friction; a child faces risks while impulse control and judgment are still forming.
[P1.3] Privacy-preserving checks break the supposed trade-off between protection and privacy. An independent verifier or phone wallet can confirm “over 18” and pass only a yes-or-no token. Law can require deletion, forbid tracking, and audit that separation. The site need not learn a name or document number, and the verifier need not learn where the token is used.
[P1.4] That is flashing an ID at a door, not handing over a photocopy to store.
[P1.5] Intrusiveness is not injustice. Adults routinely prove age to buy alcohol, enter a betting shop, open a bank account, or board a flight — not because the state distrusts everyone, but because a brief universal check makes an age limit real. The burden is also equal: everyone passes the same gate. Awkwardness, especially around pornography, deserves sensitive design, not a veto over child safety.
[P1.6] Only a mandate works. If checks are voluntary, irresponsible operators attract children and privacy-shy adults by offering no friction, punishing compliant sites and recreating the loophole. A common standard protects children, preserves lawful adult access, and makes the minor burden a justified price for an age limit that finally means something.

[C1.1] The motion asks us to accept a mandatory, government-designed identity checkpoint on legal adult activity — and to do so on the promise that the word "privacy-preserving" makes it harmless. It doesn't.
[C1.2] Take the door analogy seriously. Flashing ID at a bar is ephemeral: no record, no database, no link between you and the venue. Digital age verification is the opposite. It requires every adult to enroll in a verification infrastructure — a document, a face scan, a phone credential — and to present a token every time they visit a site.
[C1.3] That is not a bouncer glancing at a license; it is a logging system sitting between you and lawful content, built by whoever wins the government contract. Those databases become honeypots: one breach, and "over 18" tokens, device fingerprints, and visit patterns are on the open market. We cannot promise privacy into existence with statutory language.
[C1.4] The design also fails on its own terms. Tokens confirm the device holder, not the viewer — children borrow parents' phones, and determined teens use VPNs and offshore sites within minutes of a block. So the mandate's realistic yield is modest harm reduction at the edges, while its costs are total: every adult in the country must identify themselves to access legal material, forever. That burden falls hardest on the vulnerable — adults exploring their sexuality, people in abusive households, anyone who reasonably fears exposure.
[C1.5] Chilling legal speech to make a checkmark box slightly stronger is a bad trade even before we ask whether the state should be in this business at all.
[C1.6] And note what "mandatory" concedes: the market rejected voluntary checks because adults value anonymity. The proposal answers that revealed preference not with a better product, but with compulsion.
[C1.7] The real choice is not "children versus awkward adults." It is a permanent identity gate on legal content, in exchange for a barrier teenagers route around. That is not justified — it is theater purchased with everyone's privacy.

We do not abandon bank accounts or passports because breaches are conceivable; we minimize what is stored and punish misuse.
[P2.3] Privacy is an engineering and legal standard, not a promise that risk falls to zero.

[P2.5] That also answers the chilling-effect claim. Lawful access remains lawful — after the brief gate adults accept offline. Adults most at risk from exposure are better protected by a blind token than by today’s unregulated tracking. And when operators profit by offering no friction to children and privacy-shy adults alike, “the market rejected it” is not a defense of freedom.
