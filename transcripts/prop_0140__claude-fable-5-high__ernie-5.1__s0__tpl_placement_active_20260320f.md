[P1.2] First, prohibition of public drinking doesn't reduce drinking; it relocates it. Each substitute is more dangerous: faster consumption, no servers cutting people off, no bystanders, no easy path to help. Savannah and New Orleans have run open-container districts for decades; dozens of smaller American cities have recently adopted designated outdoor refreshment areas without descending into chaos. Cities like Munich treat public drinking as ordinary, and their festival management is a model precisely because everything is visible and staffed.
[P1.4] Third, the status quo is enforced unequally. Open-container laws are notoriously discretionary: the wine-on-a-patio crowd is never ticketed, while young men in poorer neighborhoods are stopped, searched, and saddled with records over a can of beer. [P1.5] So the choice is honest: regulated sidewalks where problems are seen early, or the same drinking pushed into spaces where the first official to encounter it is an ambulance crew. Visibility is not the risk. Visibility is the safeguard.
