[P1.2] That is the whole case: the cheapest available supply of skilled labor is the skilled labor we have already admitted and then deliberately wasted.
[P1.3] Recognition is the intervention with the shortest distance between action and result. No new arrivals, no political fight over numbers, no housing pressure — just an assessment body that reads a foreign transcript in three months rather than three years, offers bridging exams instead of full re-qualification, and publishes what it actually requires. Every month of delay is deskilling: clinical skills atrophy, references go stale, and a doctor becomes permanently a driver.
[P1.4] Labor-market testing, by contrast, is a machine that produces paperwork and calls it protection. It asks an employer to prove a negative — that no domestic worker could be found — through job ads written to be unanswerable, or refused because the ad ran on the wrong website. It is inherently backward-looking: shortage lists describe the labor market of two years ago, while the employer needs a welder in April. And it screens for the wrong thing entirely.
[P1.5] Testing asks *how many* foreigners; recognition asks *is this person actually competent*. The second question is the one the public genuinely cares about when the anaesthetist walks in.
[P1.6] Note also what tighter testing does to wages. Block the qualified nurse from the register and she does not leave; she works below her training, in the grey economy, undercutting the very workers testing claims to shield. Recognition pushes people up into regulated, taxed, unionised, licensed employment.
[P1.7] Priorities are choices about scarce administrative capacity and political will. Spend it on the filing cabinet, not the paperwork factory.
