[P1.1] Our legal systems currently treat major rivers and ecosystems as property, resources to be divided, exploited, and destroyed whenever the economic math favors a new dam or pipeline. This property-based framework is failing. We must grant legal personhood to ecosystems when Indigenous and local communities request it because it is the only mechanism that corrects this fatal undervaluation of nature, and the resulting complications to infrastructure are a necessary feature of sustainable development, not a bug.
[P1.2] First, legal personhood shifts the paradigm. It grants ecosystems standing in court. It does not mean a river can vote; it means the river has a legally enforceable right to exist, flow, and regenerate. When a corporation wants to build a pipeline, they can no longer simply pay a fine for pollution as a cost of doing business.
[P1.3] They must prove their project does not violate the fundamental rights of the ecosystem.
[P1.4] Second, Indigenous and local communities are the most qualified guardians. They possess deep, generational ecological knowledge and bear the immediate, devastating brunt of environmental collapse. When these communities request personhood, they are leveraging their intimate understanding of the watershed to protect it. We have seen this work: the recognition of the Whanganui River in New Zealand and the Atrato River in Colombia demonstrates that empowering local guardians with legal standing creates holistic, enforceable protection that top-down state management consistently fails to achieve.
[P1.5] Finally, we must embrace the friction. The proposition explicitly states we should do this even if it complicates infrastructure development. This complication is the entire point. Our current model externalizes the cost of ecological destruction.
[P1.6] If a hydroelectric dam must be redesigned, delayed, or canceled because it violates a river’s legal right to flow, the legal system is finally pricing in the true cost of that project. Short-term infrastructural convenience must not outweigh the permanent, irreversible collapse of a vital watershed.
