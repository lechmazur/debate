[P1.1] Fragmented service industries do not fail because one statutory wage floor is too low; they fail because a single flat floor treats radically different enterprises as if they were identical. Sectoral bargaining fixes that mismatch. By negotiating wages, classifications, and job content across cleaning chains, logistics platforms, construction subcontractors, or hospitality networks, it reaches the actual conditions that determine whether a minimum standard means anything.
[P1.2] Repeated minimum-wage hikes look decisive, but they invite avoidance. In a supply chain, a prime contractor can squeeze the entity that actually pays the worker. Platforms can relabel employees or push scheduling costs onto workers. Low-margin subcontractors can collapse, prompting churn rather than genuine improvements.
[P1.3] Sectoral bargaining follows the contracting relationship: one agreement can establish floor standards for work performed under a brand, network, or framework, regardless of which small entity appears on the payslip.
[P1.4] It also produces better standards, not merely higher numbers. Bargaining can regulate unpredictable scheduling, unpaid waiting time, safety risks, training, and workload, which a wage-only instrument cannot reach. For a home-care worker or delivery rider, the decisive issue may be whether they are paid for the hours they must actually be available. Sectoral bargaining can answer that question in industry-specific terms.
[P1.5] Nor does sectoral bargaining require uniform national coverage from day one. It can begin with sectors where fragmentation is severe and expand as institutional capacity grows. The comparison is therefore not perfect institutional complexity versus simple market correction. It is a blunt, repeatedly contested wage adjustment versus a mechanism that understands the structure of the industry and the scope of the work.
[P1.6] Minimum-wage hikes are not useless; they are a blunt floor. But in fragmented service industries, a blunt floor invites gaming, displacement, and false compliance. Sectoral bargaining sets standards where the standard actually operates.

[C1.1] Bespoke bargaining sounds precise, but in fragmented services it cannot reliably deliver coverage. A minimum standard works because it is universal, legible, and enforceable on every payslip. A wage hike gives one rate, one date, one obligation, whatever the subcontractor, franchisee, app, or agency is called. Sectoral bargaining must first define the sector, worker and employer representatives, and every bound entity.
[C1.2] In cleaning chains, platforms, and hospitality networks, those boundaries are what firms dispute and redesign.
[C1.3] Fragmentation does not make bargaining more targeted; it makes it less complete. A hotel using an agency, a laundry contractor, and an app partner can re-cut contracts around the covered box. The wage floor follows the worker; bargaining follows the table. High-turnover, variable-hour, small-worksite workers are least likely to have sustained representation there.
[C1.4] The claim that flat hikes invite avoidance [P1.2] is backwards. Avoidance is an enforcement problem, and a simple public rate is easier to audit than negotiated classifications, availability rules, workload terms, and scope disputes. A cleaner paid through three layers still has hours and a paycheck; a sectoral board must chase those layers before it can state the rate. Complexity moves enforcement upstream.
[C1.5] Sectoral detail confuses the purpose of a minimum. Scheduling and waiting time matter, but each extra term narrows the coalition, slows revision, and creates margins to contest. Repeated hikes are transparent and repeatable: too low, raise it again; conditions change, the legislature answers. Sectoral minima depend on institutional capacity, employer assent, union leverage, implementation, and litigation over scope.
[C1.6] Where those fail, the worker has no floor, only a process.
[C1.7] For minimum standards, breadth beats elegance. Sectoral bargaining may add protections after a solid statutory floor exists, but as the better instrument in fragmented services it leaves the most vulnerable waiting. A repeated hike is crude only as a foundation is crude: visible, general, and hard to contract around.

Sectoral bargaining does not move enforcement upstream; fragmentation does.
[P2.4] By making the network responsible for work performed under its framework, it shifts the burden to the entity that actually controls the margin.
