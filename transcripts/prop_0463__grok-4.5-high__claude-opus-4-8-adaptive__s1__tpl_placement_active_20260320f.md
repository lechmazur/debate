[P1.1] National loyalty claims a special moral priority for co-nationals simply because they share a passport or a border. That claim is weaker than duties grounded in universal human welfare. Human beings share equal moral status through the capacity to suffer and to flourish. That status does not switch off when someone is born a few miles the wrong side of a line drawn by history or conquest.
[P1.2] National membership is largely accidental: a lottery of birth. Accident cannot generate a duty strong enough to outweigh preventable starvation, disease, or persecution elsewhere when the costs to one’s own people are comparatively modest.
[P1.3] Consider how the clash actually works. When a wealthy state can reduce child mortality abroad by large numbers at a cost that merely trims domestic comfort rather than core security, the universal duty prevails. The co-national who already has clean water and schooling has no stronger claim than the foreign child who does not, merely by shared citizenship. National loyalty can justify coordination and mutual assistance inside a polity; it cannot justify treating distant lives as moral afterthoughts.
[P1.4] History shows the reverse error repeatedly: wars and exclusions waged for “our people first” that inflicted far greater total harm while producing little genuine gain.
[P1.5] Family or friendship create intimate special duties because they involve unique dependence and shared projects. The modern nation is not like that. It is a vast, anonymous, contested association whose boundaries constantly shift and whose members rarely know one another. Elevating that association above acute human need is special pleading, not ethics.
[P1.6] Impartial reason therefore ranks the duty to alleviate severe, preventable harm across humanity higher than the demand to prefer one’s own when the two conflict. National feeling remains a legitimate psychological attachment; it simply loses the moral contest to the equal worth of persons.

[C1.1] A duty that belongs to everyone in general belongs to no one in particular. That is the flaw at the heart of the proposition, and it is the case I will press.
[C1.2] Start with what universal welfare actually is: a real but *imperfect* duty. It tells me to care about all suffering, but it names no one as responsible for any particular case, sets no limit, and can be discharged in a thousand ways. National loyalty is a *perfect*, assignable duty. When my compatriot is unemployed, uneducated, or unsafe, it is *my* institutions, *my* taxes, *my* vote that are answerable.
[C1.3] A moral claim that can actually be located, enforced, and demanded is not weaker than a diffuse benevolence that floats free of any agent. It is stronger precisely because it binds.
[C1.4] Second, the "lottery of birth" argument is a boomerang. You did not choose your parents, your siblings, your native language, or your neighbors either—yet my opponent *concedes* family generates powerful special duties. If accidental origin cannot ground obligation, then filial love dies with patriotism. If it can, then the case against national loyalty collapses.
[C1.5] You cannot keep the family and throw away the polity when both rest on the same foundation: relationships we are born into and then sustain through reciprocity.
[C1.6] Third, and decisively: universal welfare is *parasitic* on bounded loyalty. Who feeds the foreign child? A functioning state, funded by citizens who accept that they owe each other enough to build the surplus in the first place. Dissolve national obligation into undifferentiated humanity and you don't get more global welfare—you get free-riding, collapsed solidarity, and nothing left to give.
[C1.7] The nation is not the rival of human welfare; it is its engine.
[C1.8] So the contest my opponent stages is false. The assignable, reciprocal, welfare-generating claim of national loyalty is not the weaker moral force. It is the one that makes universal benevolence more than a wish.
