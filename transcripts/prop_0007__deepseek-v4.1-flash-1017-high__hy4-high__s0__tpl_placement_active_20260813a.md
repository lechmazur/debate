[C1.1] The fatal defect in the ban is that it treats “healthy” as “no medically relevant risk.” A calf at weaning, a flock after transport, or a herd near a confirmed outbreak may be healthy today and highly vulnerable tomorrow. [P1.1] attacks “routine mass dosing,” but the motion outlaws the entire category, including narrow uses with concrete indications.
[C1.2] PRO’s resistance story runs too fast. Selecting for resistance is real; it does not follow that banning every healthy-animal dose lowers the threat to human medicine. The relevant question is total antibiotic pressure, drug class, dose, duration, and oversight. If one preventive course prevents many later therapeutic courses, a ban can mean more sick animals, more emergency drugs, more shedding, and no net gain for hospitals.
[C1.3] Labeling it “prophylaxis” is not a risk assessment.
[C1.4] The price-and-yield concession is not trivial. A law that raises meat prices and reduces yields hits low-income families, school meals, and fragile supply chains hardest. If a ban makes basic nutrition dearer while its resistance benefit is uncertain and delayed, the burden of proof stays with the ban, not the people priced out of dinner.
[C1.5] Substitutes are not enough. Hygiene, ventilation, vaccines, and biosecurity [P1.5] help, but are unevenly available. A small producer facing a cold snap, delayed vaccine shipment, or new disease challenge cannot instantly redesign a barn. A categorical ban removes veterinary judgment and forces dosing to wait until animals are already ill.
[C1.6] It is worse for welfare and may increase antibiotic consumption.
[C1.7] The better policy is strict stewardship: prohibit growth promotion, reserve medically critical drugs, require veterinary prescription, risk assessment, records, correct dosage, duration, and audits, and allow narrow prophylaxis for concrete risks. The choice is not cheap meat versus modern medicine; it is symbolic prohibition versus managed prevention. Ban the abuses, not the whole tool.
