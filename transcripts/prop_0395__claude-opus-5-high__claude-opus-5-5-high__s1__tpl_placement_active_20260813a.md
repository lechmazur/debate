[P1.1] A mandatory retirement age does something we tolerate almost nowhere else in employment law: it terminates a competent worker for a fact about their birth certificate. Not for failing a test. Not for a safety incident. For a birthday.
[P1.2] The whole case for it rests on one move — treating age as a workable proxy for decline — and that proxy is bad precisely where it matters most, because variation within any age cohort dwarfs the average difference between cohorts. A fit, sharp 68-year-old engineer and a 68-year-old in visible decline are treated identically, while the disengaged 45-year-old down the hall is untouchable. If the concern is capacity, measure capacity.
[P1.3] And we can. Every serious employer already runs appraisal systems, competency reviews, medical clearances, licensing renewals, error audits. Aviation and medicine assess skills directly all the time. The claim that we need a birthday rule because individualized assessment is too hard is really a claim that we would rather not look — and organizations that would rather not look tend to carry plenty of underperformers of every age.
[P1.4] Mandatory retirement doesn't fix that problem; it lets managers avoid the one conversation that would.
[P1.5] The costs are concrete. You lose institutional memory exactly when a firm needs it — the person who remembers why the 2008 workaround exists, the mentor who trains three juniors a year. With longer lifespans and shrinking working-age populations across most rich countries, pension systems increasingly depend on people who *want* to keep contributing being allowed to. Forcing them out converts taxpayers into claimants by statute.
[P1.6] Note what "most occupations" concedes. Where an unpredictable, unmonitorable failure kills people instantly — the airline captain, the frontline firefighter — a bright line may survive scrutiny, and those jobs are a sliver of the labor market. The real question is the accountant, the professor, the civil servant, the pharmacist.
[P1.7] For them, CON must defend firing capable people by calendar, as a routine tool, for convenience. That is not workforce management. It is discrimination with a spreadsheet attached.
