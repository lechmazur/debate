[P1.1] The proposition is a necessary guardrail against a practice that eviscerates genetic privacy, bypasses constitutional protections, and disproportionately harms marginalized communities, all while offering no proven public safety benefit. Millions of Americans upload their DNA to commercial genealogy services to trace family roots, never imagining their genetic code will be shared with law enforcement without explicit, informed consent. Terms of service bury this access in dense legalese, meaning most users have no idea their data is usable for criminal investigations.
[P1.2] This is a transparent end-run around the Fourth Amendment: police cannot compel a suspect to provide a DNA sample without a warrant and probable cause, but they can instead obtain identical genetic data from a private database the suspect never agreed to share with state investigators. Even when deployed for serious violent crimes, this practice routinely ensnares innocent bystanders. Genetic matches are often based on distant familial relationships—people who share less than 1% of their DNA with a suspect, with no connection to the crime.
[P1.3] There are documented cases of innocent people being interrogated for hours, publicly named as persons of interest, and forced to prove their innocence solely because a distant relative uploaded their DNA to a genealogy site. These databases are overwhelmingly white, meaning Black and Latino communities are systematically excluded from the narrow set of “solved” cases this tool produces, while also facing heightened surveillance risk if their data enters the system via a relative’s upload. The marginal public safety benefit of solving a small number of cold cases does not justify a permanent, unregulated erosion of genetic privacy for every person in the country, plus the recurring risk of wrongful harm to innocent people.
[P1.4] Strict prohibition is the only way to protect fundamental rights from this unchecked expansion of state power.
