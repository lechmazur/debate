[P1.1] Peer review works better when scrutiny is timely, careful, and legible to the people asked to trust it. Today a core quality check is built on uncompensated, invisible labor. Editors chase overcommitted referees; rushed after-hours reports are common; early-career scholars and those outside wealthy institutions donate expertise; and the published article carries an approval badge while objections, concessions, and unresolved doubts disappear.
[P1.2] Payment fixes the supply problem. A flat honorarium, journal-set and independent of accept/reject, turns reviewing into commissioned professional work: editors can demand deadlines, decline thin reports, and pay statistical or methodological specialists. This is not paying for a favorable verdict; it is paying for reading, checking, and explaining. The present “free” system already costs editorial time, delay, and fatigue; it merely hides the bill.
[P1.3] Publication fixes the accountability problem. A reviewer whose signed report is public and citable has a reputational stake in precision. “Not novel enough,” “cite my group,” or “the result seems implausible” are harder to hide behind when the reasoning appears beside the paper. Good reviewing earns credit; careless or self-serving reviewing carries a cost. Editors can redact confidential material; the argument remains public.
[P1.4] Published reports also improve the paper. Readers can see which robustness checks were demanded, which limitations were conceded, and which disputes remained after acceptance. For a fragile medical or social-science claim, that record tells us what “peer-reviewed” actually meant. It trains reviewers and preserves standards now passed by rumor.
[P1.5] The status quo must defend two odd claims: that the central filter should depend on spare time, and that its reasoning should be least visible to readers. Secrecy may protect comfort; it does not protect quality: editors still know identities, power still operates, and errors still surface later. Paid, published review aligns the system with its public function: accountable expert judgment, not a private ceremonial hurdle.

[C1.1] Peer review is the one place in science where a junior scholar can tell a senior one, without consequence, that the result does not hold. Both halves of this proposal attack that protection, and together they are worse than either alone.
[C1.2] Start with publication. Anonymity does not shield the comfortable; it shields the critic. A postdoc reviewing a famous lab's paper, knowing the report will carry her name beside that lab's work forever, faces an obvious calculation: hedge, soften, or decline. Journals that have tried signed open review have generally found more reviewers refusing and reports growing more polite, not more rigorous.
[C1.3] The sharpest objections migrate into private emails to the editor or simply go unsaid. PRO promises accountability; the predictable product is diplomacy. Meanwhile the senior reviewer with nothing to fear becomes a public performer, writing for an audience of future citers rather than for the author who needs the hard truth.
[C1.4] Now payment. PRO calls it "commissioned professional work," but no journal honorarium approaches what a competent statistician or clinician charges per hour. A flat fee therefore selects not for expertise but for availability: people who need the money, people who review in volume, and eventually review-mills. Worse, the fee crowds out the collegial obligation that currently gets serious scientists to spend an unpaid weekend on a stranger's manuscript.
[C1.5] Once reviewing is a transaction, "I already did my share" becomes "the rate is too low." And the bill lands somewhere: higher article charges that fall hardest on authors from poorer institutions, the very people PRO claims to help.
[C1.6] Combine the two and you have produced a consultant: paid, named, writing in public. That is not scrutiny; it is a second publication with its own career incentives, including the incentive to be seen agreeing with winners.
[C1.7] PRO's real diagnosis is editorial overload and opacity about what review checked. Those have cheaper remedies: better editorial triage, public reviewer credit without disclosure, and journals stating what review covered. Neither requires dismantling the one shelter candor has.

[P2.1] The objection romanticizes anonymity. A junior is not telling a senior “without consequence” [C1.1]: editors know, authors infer, and senior labs can retaliate privately. Anonymity hides not hierarchy, but review quality and the editor's use of it. A published, journal-certified report gives the junior critic a record: the objection was made, answered, or ignored.
[P2.2] If a powerful author objects, the journal owns the decision, not an isolated postdoc.
[P2.3] So the claim that signed reports become “more polite” [C1.2] measures the wrong thing. A softer refusal matters less than a checkable report that cannot be quietly discarded. Public performance has costs: a senior's glossy endorsement stays on record if the result collapses. If sharp objections “migrate into private emails” [C1.3], that is an argument for making the official file authoritative: the article should not receive a clean peer-review badge while the real dispute is off-record.
[P2.4] Secrecy preserves deniability, not candor.
[P2.5] Payment answers the supply objection. The comparison to hourly professional rates [C1.4] misses the purchase: a defined report, not a verdict, on time, to an editor's standard. Unpaid review attracts whoever has a spare weekend, not the best methodologist. Published names make review-mills self-defeating: boilerplate and volume work are visible; payment lets editors reject thin reports and commission missing expertise.
[P2.6] “Collegial obligation” is not a fair funding model. It means those with spare time subsidize those who decline, and poorer scholars donate expertise without citable credit. The bill is paid in delay, chasing, rushed reports, and invisible labor; charging authors from poorer institutions is a design choice, not inherent.
[P2.7] The “cheaper remedies” [C1.7] omit the missing object: reasons. Credit without text is reputation without accountability; a journal summary is the editor reporting on the editor; triage may reject more papers but still hides what review found. The choice is an unpaid private veto or paid public reason-giving. Only the latter is demandable by editors, creditable for reviewers, and legible to readers.
