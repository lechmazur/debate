[P1.1] An ordinary software project sells code or digital tools; it does not take billions of dollars from the public, promise instant redemption at par value, and invest the pool to pocket the yield. Stablecoin issuers do exactly that. Because their core promise is financial—turning private liabilities into dollar substitutes—they must be regulated like banks or money-market funds, not like tech startups shipping an app.
[P1.2] Consider the central vulnerability: the classic run. When standard software has a bug, users suffer a glitch. When a stablecoin issuer faces doubts about its reserves, panicked holders rush to redeem tokens before liquid assets run dry. This danger is real.
[P1.3] Terra’s collapse wiped out billions in savings because an unbacked financial scheme masqueraded as software innovation. Even major reserve-backed stablecoins have temporarily broken the buck when partner banks holding their cash failed. [P1.4] Moreover, the fallout will not stay inside crypto. Large stablecoin issuers function as shadow money-market funds, holding massive portfolios of short-term government debt and bank deposits. A run on a major issuer forces a fire sale of those reserves, threatening to freeze short-term credit markets and destabilize traditional banks. That is the exact contagion risk that required strict liquidity and portfolio rules for money-market funds.
[P1.5] The law must regulate financial activities by their economic substance, not their technological wrapper. Applying banking or money-market guardrails—strictly liquid reserves, verified audits, capital buffers, and legally enforceable par redemption—does not outlaw blockchain code. Open-source developers writing pure software can still be regulated like software creators.
[P1.6] But corporate issuers that manufacture private money and court systemic runs must obey the rules built to prevent financial panics.

[C1.3] Run risks are dramatically overstated. For a fully reserved stablecoin, redemption demands are by definition matched by liquid assets. [C1.5] Most critically, bank-style regulation would strangle stablecoins’ core value: open, global, permissionless access. Existing oversight—anti-fraud statutes, money transmitter licensing, and securities laws—already addresses genuine risks. The better solution is strict reserve transparency and auditing, not a misguided attempt to regulate code as if it were a savings account.
