[P1.1] A continent that cannot prove who its people are cannot deliver what it promises. Hundreds of millions of Africans still lack reliable legal identity. That is a mother who cannot register a birth, a pensioner who cannot collect what is owed, a worker who cannot move under continental trade, a displaced family that cannot be counted for aid. Service delivery fails first at the door of identity.
[P1.2] A continent-wide digital ID framework is how the African Union opens that door at scale. Interoperable credentials let a person carry proof of personhood across borders, ministries, and programs. They cut ghost payrolls, duplicate beneficiaries, and the leakages that swallow social budgets before they reach the poor. They make vaccination drives, disaster cash, school enrollment, and land records cheaper and faster to administer.
[P1.3] Paper files and siloed national systems already fail at this scale. Fragmentation is itself exclusion.
[P1.4] Yes, a digital backbone can increase surveillance risks. That is the motion’s tradeoff, and it should not be denied. Local officials already watch, extract, and deny. Unaccountable paper lists and patronage registers are surveillance too—just slower, more corrupt, and more arbitrary.
[P1.5] A continental framework at least creates common standards, audit trails, purpose limits, and political contestation at Union scale. Fifty-five incompatible systems offer the same risks with none of the service gains.
[P1.6] The clash is not privacy in the abstract versus convenience. It is whether the Union should keep millions outside the state while waiting for a risk-free identity architecture that will never arrive. Exclusion is the more immediate, more widespread harm. Digital ID will not be perfect.
[P1.7] It should still be pursued, because analog failure is already a daily catastrophe, and a shared framework is the only instrument large enough to reverse it.
