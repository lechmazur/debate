[P1.1] The five-day, forty-hour workweek is a legal artifact from the 1930s, not a law of nature. Since then, output per hour in advanced economies has multiplied several times over, yet the statutory standard never followed. Recalibrating it to four days at unchanged base pay simply hands workers a share of productivity growth they already produced.
[P1.2] The mechanism is not mysterious. Across the four-day week pilots run in recent years — in the UK, Iceland, and firms across Europe and North America — the consistent pattern is that companies restructure meetings, cut low-value coordination, and hold output roughly constant while hours fall. Most participating firms chose to keep the shorter week afterward. That is what we would expect from decades of research showing that long hours generate fatigue, error, and diminishing marginal output.
[P1.3] The fifth day of most office jobs is not where the value is created; it is where the value leaks — into status meetings, slow email chains, and presenteeism.
[P1.4] The legal redefinition matters precisely because voluntary adoption stalls. Individual firms that shorten hours fear being undercut on price and coverage; employees cannot bargain for a structural change they have no right to demand. A statutory standard solves the coordination problem the same way the weekend itself did in 1932 and the Fair Labor Standards Act did in 1938: employers competed on the same field, and the sky did not fall. Redefining "full-time" also cleans up a thicket of modern problems at once — overtime thresholds, benefits eligibility, and part-time misclassification all key off a full-time definition frozen in another century.
[P1.5] Critically, "no reduction in base pay" is not charity. When output per worker holds and hours fall, hourly productivity rises; the wage bill is not rising relative to the value workers create. Firms retain the flexibility to staff genuine five-day or shift coverage — hospitals, logistics, customer service — by scheduling across people, exactly as they already do for weekends. The burden falls on work design, not on workers' income.
