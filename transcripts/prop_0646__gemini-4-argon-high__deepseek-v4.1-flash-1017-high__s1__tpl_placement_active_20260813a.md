[P1.1] Private equity is built to extract maximum cash in minimum time. Elder care requires steady, labor-intensive investment in fragile human beings. When these models collide, seniors pay with their health, and taxpayers foot the bill. Governments must restrict private-equity ownership of elder-care facilities because the buyout business model is structurally incompatible with safe care.
[P1.2] Look at the mechanics of a buyout. Firms typically acquire nursing homes using high leverage, saddling the facility itself with the debt used to buy it. Next comes asset stripping: selling off the underlying real estate and forcing the nursing home to pay inflated rent. Because elder-care revenues are largely fixed by public health programs, a facility suddenly loaded with debt service, management fees, and newly invented rent has only one place to squeeze out rapid returns: the care floor.
[P1.3] In elder care, labor is the primary cost, and cutting it is lethal. To hit aggressive profit targets within a short flip window, buyout-backed operators slash registered nurses and front-line aides. For a frail resident, losing those staff hours means untreated bedsores turning into sepsis, unassisted falls, or being sedated with chemical restraints because nobody has time to walk with them. Unlike ordinary consumers, immobile eighty-five-year-olds with dementia cannot comparison-shop and walk out when quality collapses.
[P1.4] Routine health inspections cannot fix this. Buyout firms splinter facilities into webs of shell companies, separating lucrative real estate and management contracts from the licensed operating entity. Public reimbursements flow upward to investors, while legal liability stays trapped in a cash-starved shell. When regulators issue fines or families sue over neglect, the operator can simply declare bankruptcy, leaving the state to rescue a hollowed-out facility.
[P1.5] Governments should not wait to police the wreckage after the assets are stripped. Restricting private-equity ownership—through buyout limits and ownership caps—stops the extraction engine before it reaches the bedside.
