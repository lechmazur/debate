[P1.1] Latin America has a once-in-a-generation opening as American firms look to shorten supply chains away from Asia. Geography, time zones, trade agreements and cultural ties give Mexico, Central America and parts of South America a natural advantage. But that advantage stays potential without deliberate state action.
[P1.2] Markets alone will not capture nearshoring. Multinationals choose locations based on ready industrial parks, reliable power and logistics, trained technicians, and legal certainty. Latin American economies largely lack those on a turnkey basis. A focused industrial policy — targeted tax incentives, special economic zones, supplier development programs, technical training, and infrastructure co-investment — bridges that last-mile gap and turns interest into factories, jobs, and exports.
[P1.3] That is how Querétaro built an aerospace cluster from almost nothing.
[P1.4] The costs invoked against this strategy misunderstand development math. Yes, incentives cost revenue and local-content rules raise some prices. But the alternative is far costlier: continued dependence on volatile commodities, mass informality, and migration driven by lack of formal work. A semiconductor packaging plant in Costa Rica or an auto parts cluster in northern Mexico does not just create assembly jobs; it forces upgrades in electricity, roads, vocational schools, and small-business suppliers that benefit the whole economy.
[P1.5] Fiscal outlays today expand the future tax base tomorrow.
[P1.6] Nor is selective protection a retreat from openness. Done for nearshoring, it is bargaining power: requiring technology transfer, domestic sourcing, and wage standards ensures foreign investment roots itself instead of operating as an enclave. Without such conditions, countries compete only by offering cheaper labor and larger tax holidays — a race Latin America cannot and should not win.
[P1.7] Seizing this window requires accepting short-term fiscal and trade friction for long-term structural transformation. Waiting for perfect free-market conditions means watching the investment go to Southeast Asia instead.

[C1.1] The proposition does not ask whether Latin America should welcome factories nearer the United States. It asks governments to prioritize industrial policy even when that means more protection and heavier fiscal burdens. That permission is the error. Nearshoring is real; using it to revive import substitution is how the opening gets wasted.
[C1.2] Firms leaving Asia are not shopping for tariffs, local-content rules, or technology-transfer mandates. They want rules that hold, power that stays on, ports that clear, security, and clean access to the U.S. market and to imported parts. Mexico’s manufacturing pull has come through proximity and trade agreements, not a new wall of protection. Domestic-sourcing conditions raise the cost of the plants being courted and risk friction with the agreements that make the region worth choosing.
[C1.3] The fiscal side is worse. These states already face narrow tax bases, debt, and weak spending control. Incentives and co-investment do not pay for themselves. They become rents: holidays for firms that would have come anyway, and checks for those that leave when the subsidy ends.
[C1.4] Richer governments are already bidding with industrial subsidies Latin America cannot match. A poorer treasury that enters that auction mortgages schools, grids, and courts—the public goods that actually decide location.
[C1.5] The region already ran this experiment. Protect the industry, spend public money, hope linkages appear, and call the pain developmental. The yield was high-cost production, fiscal crisis, and firms that never competed. One aerospace cluster is not a warrant to repeat it.
[C1.6] Where such clusters work, they track skills, logistics, and market access, not a blank check for protection.
[C1.7] The alternative is not passivity. Build energy, logistics, and courts; keep trade open; spend on bottlenecks, not winners. A plant lost to Southeast Asia costs less than a high-cost enclave that taxpayers fund and households overpay for. That is the development math this strategy gets backward.

[P2.5] Well-designed incentives with sunsets, job targets, and clawbacks avoid paying firms that would have come anyway, and turn temporary holidays into permanent suppliers and taxpayers.
[P2.6] History proves the point against them. The costly enclaves of the past lacked export discipline and were allowed to live behind protection indefinitely. Today’s successful nearshorers in Southeast Asia win because they pair openness to the U.S. with aggressive industrial steering. [P2.7] A plant lost today is not savings; it is another decade of commodities, informality, and migration.

[P4.1] A1: Neither aim is dropped. Anchor does not mean maximum protection on day one. It means phased, export-tested conditions: a grace period using duty-free imported inputs, then rising domestic sourcing for simpler parts, plus contributions to worker training and supplier qualification rather than seizure of technology. That cost is modest against tariff-free U.S. access, same-time-zone operations and freight savings, so a U.S.-bound auto or medical-device maker still prefers northern Mexico or Costa Rica to Vietnam, which imposes its own performance demands.
[P4.2] A2: The targeted fix comes first and pays for the general one. No one proposes rebuilding the national grid before the first factory. Governments co-finance a park substation, industrial water, road spur and fast customs lane alongside a committed anchor tenant, often through development banks and public-private deals repaid from that project's payrolls and exports. That island of reliability lands the plant; its jobs and suppliers then widen the tax base for broader power and ports.
[P4.3] Together those answers collapse the opposition's dilemma. They want turnkey sites, reliable power and trained technicians without the incentives, zones and co-investment that create them, and open access without any sourcing or training conditions to spread gains beyond the fence line. That is industrial policy without instruments. A plant that pays no training levy and buys nothing locally may arrive slightly cheaper, but it leaves no suppliers, no skills, and no reason to stay when the next holiday expires elsewhere.
[P4.4] Their Vietnam warning therefore reverses. Vietnam wins precisely because it pairs openness to America with aggressive steering, not because it offers clean hands. If Latin America offers only generic courts someday while rivals offer ready factories now, the window closes, leaving exactly what they claim to fear: narrow budgets, informal work, and commodity dependence. Short-term friction is the price of structural transformation; refusing it is not thrift but surrender.
