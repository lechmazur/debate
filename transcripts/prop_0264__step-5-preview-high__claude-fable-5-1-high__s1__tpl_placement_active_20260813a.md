[P1.1] Taxpayers should not underwrite the foreseeable risks of a profession that already knows those risks exist. When a city pays a seven-figure settlement because an officer injured someone, the money comes from schools, sanitation, and road repair—not from the actor whose conduct created the bill. Requiring officers to carry personal professional liability insurance moves the cost back to where the behavior was, and it gives the public a practical remedy instead of an empty judgment against an officer with few assets.
[P1.2] The strongest objection will be that this is unfair to officers and will hollow out policing. It won’t. The burden falls only on officers whose conduct is egregious or negligent; the overwhelming majority will never pay a deductible. And insurance markets already price risk: officers with repeated complaints can face higher premiums or become uninsurable, creating a professional incentive that internal discipline and tort immunity often fail to produce.
[P1.3] Departments could even subsidize the base premium, as many cities do for certain public-facing roles, so the requirement targets misconduct rather than ordinary policing.
[P1.4] This is not exotic. Doctors, nurses, contractors, and rideshare drivers routinely carry malpractice or professional coverage because their work carries elevated risk to the public. Policing carries far greater authority over liberty and bodily safety, so the burden of predictable harm should sit with the professional and the insurer, not the taxpayer. The proposition also improves municipal solvency: cities facing repeated misconduct settlements currently borrow or cut services, meaning residents pay twice—first in lost trust, then in deferred infrastructure.
[P1.5] The central question is not whether policing is difficult. It is whether the people who exercise state power should internalize some of the costs when they misuse it. PRO says yes, because public safety is strengthened when officers, employers, and insurers all have a reason to prevent harm.

Officers are indemnified by state statute and union contract for acts within the scope of employment; that indemnity does not vanish because a policy exists.
[C1.3] A personal policy with a six-figure or low-seven-figure cap sits beneath a multi-million-dollar verdict, and the city still pays everything above the cap.

Any mandatory premium gets negotiated back into salary within one contract cycle—and the opponent concedes the point in advance by proposing that departments "subsidize the base premium" [P1.3]. The "practical remedy" promised to victims becomes a denial letter, and the city steps in anyway.
[C1.7] The malpractice analogy in [P1.4] cuts the wrong way. Medical malpractice insurance didn't make doctors eat the cost of harm; it raised fees that patients pay. It also did nothing to fix the systemic errors that kill most patients, because individual policies don't reach hospital staffing or protocols. Police misconduct is likewise systemic: training, supervision, culture.
[C1.8] Individualizing the bill lets the institution that controls those levers off the hook, and hands the question of who may police to actuaries rather than voters.

[P2.2] The officer with repeated complaints watches premiums climb or coverage disappear; that is feedback no internal discipline file has ever reliably produced. Even if a subsidy or bargaining recycles some cost, the marginal incentive remains personal: the officer with claims pays more.
[P2.3] CON says municipal liability and indemnity absorb the bill. That describes the problem, not a defense. When the city pays, the officer’s balance sheet is untouched; a reprimand can be grieved away. Indemnification is also not a blanket—jurisdictions routinely exclude intentional or bad-faith conduct, and coverage disputes force facts into the open.
[P2.4] A personal policy puts a private actor with its own money in the file.
[P2.5] The exclusion point is CON’s best one, but it proves too much. If a chokehold or fabricated report is truly intentional, insurance should not cleanse it; decertification should. Much liability, however, is pleaded and often resolved as excessive force, failure to intervene, or negligence—not adjudicated intent. Insurers investigate and defend those claims.
[P2.6] The prospect of denial or exclusion is itself a discipline mechanism.
[P2.7] The malpractice analogy does not rescue CON. PRO never said individual insurance replaces training or supervision. It says professional risk-bearers should not socialize foreseeable harm. Charging departmental budgets is useful, but departments are conflicted; insurers are not.
[P2.8] The choice is not insurance versus systemic reform. It is a system where taxpayers and victims absorb predictable risk, or one where the officer and carrier have reason to prevent it.
[P2.9] CON calls insurance a toll booth. Better: it is a copay for state power. The difficulty of policing is not an argument for making residents underwrite its mistakes.
