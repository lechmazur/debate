[P1.1] A 37 year old woman sees a fertility clinic ad: "Freeze your eggs, keep your options open." It shows a smiling professional, mentions safety, mentions convenience. What that ad does not show, front and centre, is that for women her age, the vast majority who pay tens of thousands of dollars to freeze eggs will never take home a baby from those eggs. Right now that number is buried three clicks deep, in tiny text, if it is broken down by age at all.
[P1.2] This is not a ban on advertising. This is not telling anyone what choice to make. This is the most basic standard of fair communication for a high-stakes medical decision. Opponents will argue this is unnecessary paternalism, that people can look up the numbers if they care.
[P1.3] But every basic principle of decision science tells us people do not dig for fine print when they are scared, hopeful, and making a choice that feels like it is running out of time. No clinic can afford to be honest under the current rules.
[P1.4] This requirement fixes that broken market dynamic. It protects people from wasted savings and wasted years of false hope. It does not restrict any choice. It only ensures that when people choose, they actually know what they are choosing.
[P1.5] This is not an unreasonable burden. This is decent treatment for people making one of the hardest decisions of their lives.

Most frozen eggs are never thawed; outcome data lags the technology by a decade; results vary wildly by lab quality, egg yield, and individual ovarian reserve. A 35-year-old with high AMH who banks twenty eggs faces a completely different prognosis than her same-age neighbor who retrieves six.
[C1.2] Stamping one "age-based number" prominently across every ad is not transparency — it is government-mandated false precision, dressed up as honesty.
[C1.3] And the mandate targets the wrong moment. Nobody freezes eggs off an advertisement. Between the ad and the procedure stand consultations, bloodwork, ultrasounds, and formal informed consent — the actual place where individualized prognosis belongs, and where professional standards already require it. Meanwhile, genuinely deceptive advertising is already illegal under ordinary consumer-protection law.
[C1.4] The motion doesn't fill a gap; it duplicates consent law badly, in a medium structurally incapable of nuance.
[C1.5] Worse, prominence mandates create the very race to the bottom PRO fears. We've seen this with IVF league tables: when clinics must compete on headline success rates, they cherry-pick patients, quietly turning away the older and harder cases to protect their advertised numbers. The 37-year-old in PRO's opening doesn't get better information under this regime — she gets a closed door.
[C1.6] Finally, weigh the chilling effect. Faced with compliance risk over contested statistics, clinics will simply advertise less. Then women hear about egg freezing later — at 38 instead of 33 — which is the single worst outcome possible, because age at freezing is the one variable that matters most. A policy sold as protecting women would, predictably, push them toward older, worse decisions.
[C1.7] Good intentions don't redeem a mandate that forces fake certainty, duplicates real safeguards, incentivizes patient-dumping, and delays the information it claims to spread. That is the case to beat.
