[P1.1] A culture that preserves only what can sit on a shelf will leave a false record of the digital age. Software is not merely a consumer product: games embody storytelling and visual design; apps shape communication, work, and politics; digital art often exists only through executable code. Their historical value lies not just in screenshots, but in code, assets, documentation, versions, and operating environments. Storefront delisting, server shutdowns, obsolete formats, and corporate failure can erase them rapidly.
[P1.2] Once those materials disappear, faithful reconstruction may be impossible.
[P1.3] Voluntary preservation predictably fails. Rights holders have little commercial incentive to maintain unprofitable old versions, while users often possess only revocable licenses, encrypted files, or incomplete copies. Hobbyist archives can help, but they operate under legal uncertainty and cannot reliably obtain source materials or server components. Legal deposit fixes this coordination failure: when a work is officially designated culturally significant, its custodian must provide a preservation package to a national archive, including the materials reasonably necessary to preserve and study it.
[P1.4] Deposit need not mean immediate public release. Archives can use embargoes, secure research access, and safeguards for copyright, trade secrets, personal data, and cybersecurity. Requirements can be proportionate to the creator’s resources, limited by technical feasibility, and governed by transparent significance criteria with appeal rights. That answers the burden objection without surrendering the principle.
[P1.5] Copying existing digital materials is generally a modest obligation compared with the irreversible public cost of losing them.
[P1.6] Nor is imperfect preservation a reason to accept guaranteed disappearance. National archives offer continuity, professional stewardship, redundant storage, and migration or emulation expertise that no storefront or publisher can promise indefinitely. The right to commercially control a work should not become a private power to erase part of society’s memory.
[P1.7] Governments already recognize that culturally important books and films outlive the firms that sold them. Software now carries equal cultural weight but is often more fragile. Future generations should inherit the works themselves—not merely trailers, advertisements, and anecdotes about vanished digital worlds.

[C1.1] The proposition's weakness is hidden in its analogy. Legal deposit for books works because a book is a finished, self-contained artifact: hand one copy to the archive and it survives. Software is not like that. A game or app is a living system—dependent on servers, third-party engines, licensed middleware, operating systems, and authentication infrastructure.
[C1.2] Even a "preservation package" of code and assets cannot make a decade-old multiplayer title run again, because the parts that made it culturally alive were social and commercial, not just technical. The mandate promises preservation it cannot deliver, while imposing costs it cannot justify.
[C1.3] And the costs are severe. To make deposit meaningful, governments must compel creators to hand over source code and internal documentation. That is not "copying a published work"—it is forced disclosure of trade secrets, the core competitive asset of an entire industry, deposited with institutions that have suffered breaches. Once archived, that code becomes a permanent target for attackers and, in less trustworthy states, for competitors and political rivals.
[C1.4] No book deposit regime ever required the manuscript, the printer's proofs, and the author's business records.
[C1.5] Then comes the definition problem. "Culturally significant" is unknowable at deposit time. Flappy Bird seemed disposable; obscure utilities turned out to shape history. Significance emerges over decades, so any designation scheme will either be hopelessly under-inclusive or sweep in nearly everything—turning every app store submission into a compliance obligation.
[C1.6] Guess who bears that burden? Not the storefront giants with legal departments, but independent developers, students, and hobbyists for whom registration, packaging, and appeals are real costs and real chilling effects on what they dare to build.
[C1.7] Preservation is a genuine good, and it is already happening through the channels suited to it: voluntary source releases, corporate archives, academic emulation projects, and enthusiast communities that solved this for MUDs and dead MMOs without compulsion. Culture is being saved by consent and cooperation. Mandates would add coercion, secrecy risk, and bureaucratic gatekeeping to a problem that cooperation handles better. Reject the proposition.
