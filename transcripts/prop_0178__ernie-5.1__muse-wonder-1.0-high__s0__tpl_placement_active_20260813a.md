[P1.1] Population health is shaped not by what people know, but by what they are offered. Ultra‑processed foods dominate modern diets, delivering excess salt, sugar and fat in every bite. Mandating caps on these nutrients changes the food environment itself, guaranteeing that every product on the shelf meets a healthier baseline. Labels and education, by contrast, ask individuals to decipher information, resist marketing, and alter habits—tasks that consistently falter in the real world.
[P1.2] Consider the United Kingdom’s voluntary salt‑reduction programme. When the government set incremental targets for manufacturers, average daily salt intake fell by roughly a gram, a shift linked to measurable drops in blood‑pressure‑related strokes and heart attacks across the whole population. The effect was achieved without any consumer action; it was the product that changed. Similar reforms in trans‑fat bans in Denmark and in several Latin American countries produced rapid declines in cardiovascular events, again because the risk factor was removed from the food supply rather than left to personal choice.
[P1.3] Front‑of‑pack traffic‑light labels, the most studied labeling scheme, produce modest shifts—on the order of a few percent in purchasing patterns—primarily among already health‑conscious shoppers. Nutrition education campaigns, even when intensive, typically generate short‑term knowledge gains that fade without supportive environments. Both approaches rely on literacy, time, and motivation, resources that are unevenly distributed. Low‑income families, children, and the elderly—the groups most burdened by diet‑related disease—are the least able to translate information into healthier plates.
[P1.4] A mandated reformulation standard sidesteps these inequities. By capping salt, sugar and fat at the source, it delivers a universal, automatic reduction in exposure, reaching every consumer regardless of education or income. It also eliminates the industry’s ability to game labels or market “healthy‑looking” products that remain nutritionally poor. When the default is healthier, the aggregate health gain dwarfs what any campaign of information can achieve.

[C1.1] Capping salt, sugar, and fat does not make an ultra-processed product healthy. It makes it slightly less bad on three metrics while leaving intact what drives harm: energy density, refined starch, low fibre, soft texture, and engineered palatability that encourages fast, excessive eating. A biscuit meeting a sugar cap with sweeteners and starch, or a ready meal meeting a fat cap with extra sugar, still displaces real food. Marketed as “now healthier,” it may even be eaten more often, cancelling the theoretical saving.
[C1.2] People do not consume nutrients in fixed doses. If mandated products taste blander, many add salt or sauces at home, switch to unregulated bakery, takeaway, or imported foods, or eat more to feel satisfied. Manufacturers can game thresholds, shrink servings, or cut one nutrient while raising another. Factory-gate gains therefore shrink by the time food is eaten, while reformulation and compliance costs are passed to shoppers least able to afford higher prices.
[C1.3] Labelling and education attack the larger problem: how often these products are eaten at all. A clear warning label does not celebrate a marginally improved crisp; it signals a product to limit, and gives manufacturers strong reason to improve recipes to avoid that stigma. School education, cooking skills, and sustained campaigns change whole patterns — drinks, snacks, breakfasts, portions — rather than trimming a gram inside an otherwise unchanged diet. Eating ultra-processed foods less often, and replacing them with water, vegetables, fruit, and home-prepared staples, improves calories, fibre, micronutrients, and satiety together.
[C1.4] The familiar success stories do not prove the motion. Britain’s salt reduction was largely voluntary and gradual, built on targets, monitoring, and pressure, not rigid caps across ultra-processed foods. Trans-fat removal was exceptional: a needless artificial ingredient with easy substitutes. Those narrow cases cannot show blunt mandates on ubiquitous nutrients will beat every label or education campaign.
[C1.5] A slightly reformulated crisp eaten daily is still worse than an informed diet in which such products are chosen rarely.
