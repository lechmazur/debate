[P1.1] Public decision-making exists to serve the people subject to those decisions, not to optimize abstract metrics of efficiency. The core purpose of a democratic system is to ensure that collective choices reflect the will of the governed, which is the only source of legitimate authority for any public action. Without that consent, even the most brilliant policy is nothing more than an imposition by a self-appointed elite. Technocratic competence is a useful adjunct to this process, but it is never a substitute for the consent that makes a decision morally and practically enforceable.
[P1.2] Consider a municipal government that hires world-class transit experts to route a new rail line through a working-class neighborhood without any public consultation. The experts confirm the route is the most cost-effective, fastest to build, and will reduce regional congestion by a wide margin. Yet if the neighborhood opposes the project, the decision is illegitimate: residents will protest, delay construction, or refuse to use the line once built. Even perfect technical competence cannot overcome the resistance that follows a lack of democratic buy-in.
[P1.3] By contrast, a less technically optimal but legitimate decision—say, a town that votes to allocate funds to a community-requested library instead of an expert-recommended stormwater upgrade—will see far higher compliance and long-term success. The library is used, maintained, and valued by the people who chose it. The stormwater upgrade, if forced on a reluctant public, may face neglect, sabotage, or weakened trust that undermines future public initiatives.
[P1.4] Prioritizing competence over legitimacy collapses public decision-making into paternalism. Technocrats can model traffic flows or budget projections, but they cannot know the lived priorities of a community better than the people who live there. Legitimacy is not a nice-to-have add-on; it is the foundation that makes any public decision worth following at all.
