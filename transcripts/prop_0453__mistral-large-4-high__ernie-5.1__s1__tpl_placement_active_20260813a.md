[P1.1] Housing inflation is not a peripheral concern—it is the engine of modern wealth inequality and a structural threat to economic stability. When home prices detach from incomes, entire generations are locked out of wealth-building, cities become segregated by income, and household balance sheets become dangerously leveraged against a single asset class. This is not merely a cost-of-living grievance; it is a fundamental distortion of how economies allocate capital, where people can live, and who holds economic power. Shelter costs dominate household budgets, meaning housing inflation transmits directly into reduced consumption elsewhere, depressing aggregate demand in ways that compound over time.
[P1.2] A recession, painful as it is, is a cyclical correction with a known endpoint and policy tools to mitigate its depth. Housing inflation, left unchecked, compounds irreversibly. Every year of delay makes the eventual adjustment steeper, concentrates ownership among the already-wealthy, and entrenches intergenerational poverty while inflating financial fragility. Central banks cannot claim to be guardians of price stability while ignoring the largest asset market in the economy, which drives both measured inflation through shelter costs and inflation expectations through highly visible price signals that anchor public psychology.
[P1.3] The proposition asks us to weigh a temporary downturn against permanent structural damage. That calculus is unambiguous: a recession costs jobs and output for a few quarters; runaway housing inflation costs social mobility, financial stability, and democratic legitimacy for decades. When a housing bubble threatens to collapse under its own weight—as history repeatedly demonstrates—the resulting recession is far deeper and more chaotic than one engineered deliberately to cool prices gradually. Central banks should accept the risk of recession to prevent the certainty of a housing crisis that would make any downturn look mild by comparison.

[C1.1] Central banks exist to maintain price stability and, in most jurisdictions, to support maximum employment. Prioritizing housing inflation above all else flouts that dual mandate and turns monetary policy into a blunt instrument aimed at a single, slow‑moving price component. Shelter costs enter consumer‑price indices with a lag of twelve to eighteen months, so by the time they signal overheating the market is already adjusting. Raising rates aggressively to chase a lagging figure risks over‑tightening the whole economy, triggering a recession whose scars last far longer than a few quarters of elevated rent growth.
[C1.2] The costs of a recession are not merely cyclical. Hysteresis effects mean that prolonged unemployment erodes skills, depresses lifetime earnings, and inflates public‑debt burdens as fiscal stimulus is deployed. Historical episodes illustrate the danger: in the mid‑2000s the Federal Reserve’s rapid rate hikes, intended to cool an overheated housing market, helped precipitate the 2008 financial crisis rather than prevent it. The recession that followed was far deeper and more destructive than the housing correction that might have occurred with a more measured approach.
[C1.3] Moreover, central banks already have targeted tools—loan‑to‑value caps, counter‑cyclical capital buffers, stress‑testing—that can cool mortgage credit without dragging down the broader economy. Using blunt rate policy to fight housing inflation sacrifices those precision instruments, raises mortgage defaults, and threatens financial stability. Furthermore, higher rates raise borrowing costs for existing homeowners, increasing arrears and forcing sales that depress neighbourhood values, creating a feedback loop that worsens the very problem central banks aim to solve. Supply‑side reforms—zoning liberalisation, increased construction—address the root of price pressure without sacrificing growth.
[C1.4] If the goal is to protect households, a recession that throws millions out of work, forces evictions, and depresses wages is a far graver threat than a gradual moderation of house‑price growth. Central banks should therefore reject the proposition: the mandate to avoid catastrophic downturn outweighs a narrow focus on housing inflation.
