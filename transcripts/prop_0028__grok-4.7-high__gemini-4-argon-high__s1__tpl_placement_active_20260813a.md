[P1.1] Mass university expansion did not chiefly open genuine new opportunities. It mainly inflated the credentials required to reach the opportunities that already existed.
[P1.2] A university degree once marked a relatively scarce set of skills and aptitudes. When enrollment surged, the supply of graduates rose far faster than the number of jobs whose actual content demanded degree-level training. Employers responded as any rational screener would: they raised the paper threshold. Clerical work, sales supervision, and many public-sector roles that high-school leavers once entered now demand a bachelor’s degree.
[P1.3] The work did not become more skilled. The credential did. Graduates did not climb into better jobs so much as non-graduates were locked out of the old ones. That displacement is the signature of credential inflation.
[P1.4] Genuine opportunity would mean additional high-productivity employment, skills that employers actually use, and mobility that schooling creates rather than merely redistributes. Instead, large numbers of graduates occupy roles that do not require their qualifications, while the earnings premium on an ordinary degree has been squeezed by oversupply and by the time and debt required to obtain it. The students pulled in at the margin often buy a signal that no longer distinguishes them, at a cost their parents’ generation never faced.
[P1.5] Some graduates—those in selective institutions or scarce technical fields—did gain real advantages. Those gains are real but narrow. They are outweighed by the positional race that compels everyone else to spend extra years merely to hold their place. An opportunity that disappears the moment it is widely claimed, or that exists only by excluding someone else from a job that has not changed, is not an expansion of opportunity.
[P1.6] It is inflation of the ticket. Mass university growth produced more of the second than the first.

Mass university expansion transformed both the productive capacity of the economy and who could participate in it, expanding genuine opportunity far more than it inflated credentials.
[C1.2] First, the work did become more skilled, and the economic pie grew. [P1.2] and [P1.3] claim that graduate supply outran real skill demand, leaving employers to slap degree requirements onto unchanged clerical or supervisory roles. Basic economics refutes this: if degrees were mostly paper signals chasing a static pool of jobs, multiplying the supply of graduates several times over would have collapsed the college wage premium.
[C1.3] Instead, a substantial graduate earnings and employment advantage persisted even as enrollment surged. That happened because a broad pool of educated workers allowed high-productivity sectors—software, modern healthcare, biotechnology, engineering, and complex services—to scale. [C1.4] Second, expansion broke an elite monopoly. When university degrees were scarce [P1.2], opportunity was not open; it was rationed by class and gender. Medicine, law, science, and executive leadership were largely preserves for affluent men. Mass higher education brought tens of millions of women, working-class students, and first-generation graduates into professional life, creating real mobility where exclusion once stood.
[C1.5] Yes, some employers use degrees as a blunt filter at the margin. But screening friction in a subset of office jobs is a byproduct, not the headline. A transformation that fueled the modern knowledge economy and opened the professions to the broad public expanded genuine opportunity far more than it inflated credentials.
