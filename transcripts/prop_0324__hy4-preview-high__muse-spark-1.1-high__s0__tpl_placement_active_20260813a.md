[P1.1] Place identity is not a slogan launched at a press conference; it is the story a place tells about itself through repeated practice. If public institutions want that kind of identity, they should prefer creator-led local festivals and recurring community events to one-off prestige mega-events.
[P1.2] The first reason is recurrence. Identity is habit, not hype. A neighborhood arts walk, a seasonal food and makers market, or an annual street-theatre festival becomes part of the local calendar. Each edition trains volunteers, grows local suppliers, and deepens shared memory.
[P1.3] Over years, the event stops being something the city hosts and starts being something the city is. A mega-event, by contrast, arrives, peaks, and disappears. Its attention curve is a spike; place identity needs a pulse. Visitors may photograph a stadium, but they remember a street where locals gather every summer.
[P1.4] The second reason is authorship. Creator-led festivals are made by local musicians, chefs, designers, and community groups, so their content is specific: the sounds, tastes, and rituals of that place. A global championship or Expo is a franchise. The ceremonies, sponsor villages, and broadcast visuals are designed to look the same everywhere, because the rights-holders and sponsors demand consistency.
[P1.5] Winning the right to host proves logistical competence, not distinctiveness. A city that defines itself by a two-week global spectacle is renting a brand, not building one.
[P1.6] Third, the investment case is just as clear. Mega-events concentrate cost and risk: overruns, underused venues, and attention that leaves with the closing ceremony. Local recurring events are cheaper, iterable, and leave behind organizational capacity and small-business networks. If the goal is a durable identity rather than a short-term visitor spike, the choice is clear.
[P1.7] A city known for one huge party is like a person known for one viral video—memorable for a moment, but not a character.
