[P1.1] A high school student discovers that classmates are circulating sexually explicit images of her—images she never made, generated by an app in seconds. These are not edge cases; they are the daily reality of non-consensual deepfake abuse, and existing law leaves victims largely unprotected.
[P1.2] The harm here is not abstract. Sexually explicit deepfakes strip real people of bodily autonomy and dignity, inflict severe psychological injury, and can destroy livelihoods. Yet when victims seek justice, they hit structural gaps: defamation law requires specific reputational falsity, harassment statutes demand patterns of conduct, and copyright claims fail when the victim does not own the original image. Prosecutors decline cases or lack resources.
[P1.3] Platforms remove content slowly, if at all. The result is a practical immunity for abusers.
[P1.4] An explicit private right of action closes this gap. It gives victims direct access to courts, allowing them to seek damages and injunctions before material spreads irretrievably. More importantly, it creates genuine deterrence: creators of non-consensual sexual imagery face concrete financial liability rather than the distant threat of criminal prosecution that rarely materializes. It shifts power to the person actually harmed.
[P1.5] Yes, this narrows some speech protections, but the expression at stake has minimal value. Non-consensual sexual depiction is not political speech, satire, or art—it is exploitation. Free expression guarantees were never meant to shield the unauthorized sexual use of a real person's body. Any chilling effect falls on conduct society has every reason to suppress, while consensual content, journalism, and artistic works remain untouched.
[P1.6] We are not sacrificing meaningful discourse for safety; we are removing a tool of abuse.
[P1.7] Countries should enact this right now. The technology is outpacing the law, and every day of delay means more victims.
