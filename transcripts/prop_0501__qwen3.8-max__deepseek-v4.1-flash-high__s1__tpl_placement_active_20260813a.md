[P1.1] The strongest reason to adopt this mandate is that modern central banks learned the wrong lesson from the 2008 crisis. They can keep consumer inflation low while credit inflates housing and other asset markets until the damage becomes systemic. CPI measures what households buy today; it does not capture the price of shelter as an asset, the leverage behind it, or the wealth effects that follow when valuations collapse. If the policy framework waits for consumer-price inflation to respond, it is usually responding after the boom has already distorted construction, household balance sheets, and bank lending.
[P1.2] Housing deserves special treatment because it is where money, credit, and everyday life intersect. A stock-market correction can hurt wealth holders; a nationwide housing spiral hits collateral values, mortgage lending, construction employment, local government finance, and consumption at once. When prices rise too fast, younger and lower-income households are forced into riskier borrowing or priced out entirely. When they fall abruptly, negative equity and forced sales amplify recessions.
[P1.3] Explicitly targeting housing-price stability gives central banks a duty to lean against these dynamics early, instead of claiming that bubbles are only visible in hindsight.
[P1.4] This does not mean central banks should peg every asset price or keep housing costs frozen. It means adding a clear second objective: monitor and respond to unsustainable housing credit-price spirals using interest rates, loan-to-value limits, debt-to-income rules, and communication. Critics will say policy is too blunt.
[P1.5] But macroprudential tools are often incomplete, pro-cyclical, and vulnerable to lobbying; if the central bank cannot say why housing stability matters, it will defer until inflation arrives—too late. The core issue is simple: consumer-price stability alone is necessary but not sufficient for economic stability. After repeated housing-driven crises, the burden belongs on those who would leave central banks watching the wrong dashboard.

Housing “stability” has no agreed definition: real prices? price-to-income? rent yields? credit growth? Choose wrong and policy becomes arbitrary.
[C1.2] A central bank told to stabilize housing prices must decide what a house should cost. That is not monetary policy; it is central planning for the balance sheets of the largest asset most households ever own.
[C1.3] Second, identification is impossible in real time. A price surge may reflect restrictive zoning, population growth, low real rates, or speculative credit. The first two need supply reform and fiscal policy, not higher interest rates. If the central bank tightens because prices look frothy, it may crush construction, worsen the shortage, and make affordability worse, while doing nothing about the actual cause.
[C1.4] If it loosens because prices fall, it may validate leverage. PRO says target early; in practice early is exactly when you cannot distinguish a bubble from a boom.
[C1.5] Third, rate policy is too blunt, and macroprudential tools are better aimed. Loan-to-value caps, debt-to-income limits, and countercyclical capital buffers can cool dangerous mortgage credit without setting a national price target or punishing cash buyers and renters. The motion would subordinate those tools to an unmeasurable asset-price objective.
[C1.6] Finally, an explicit housing target corrupts the primary mandate. Central banks gain credibility by anchoring expectations around consumer prices. Add a volatile second target and every rate decision becomes a tradeoff between inflation and home values. Politicians will demand that rising prices be called stability and falling prices be called failure.
[C1.7] The 2008 lesson isn’t that central banks needed a housing target; it’s that they needed better regulation and a willingness to act on financial stability. Consumer-price stability plus a clear financial-stability mandate can do that. An asset-price target cannot.
