[P1.1] A platform should not tell a child, before that child has chosen anything, that their face needs correction. Face-slimming, skin-smoothing, and age-shifting filters do exactly that when enabled by default: they establish a manufactured face as the starting point and the child’s real face as the deviation. The proposition does not ban filters. It requires platforms to stop making appearance alteration the automatic condition of participation.
[P1.2] These tools are not neutral decorations like cartoon ears or colored backgrounds. They target socially sensitive traits: weight, skin texture, wrinkles, and youthfulness. For minors, whose identities and body images are still forming, repeated exposure can turn ordinary features—round cheeks, acne, pores—into perceived defects. The harm is not only that an edited image looks unrealistic.
[P1.3] It is the constant before-and-after lesson: “you are more presentable when thinner, smoother, or differently aged.” That lesson becomes especially powerful when peers post similarly altered faces, making the artificial standard appear normal.
[P1.4] Defaults matter because they shape behavior without requiring conscious agreement. A young user opening a camera may not understand what has been changed, may overlook a subtle filter, or may fear that disabling it will make them look worse than everyone else. Platforms design defaults precisely because defaults increase adoption. They cannot dismiss their influence when the same mechanism affects children’s self-perception.
[P1.5] Nor is parental control an adequate substitute: parents cannot inspect every camera setting, while the company knows exactly when an effect is activated.
[P1.6] The solution is modest and workable: begin with an unaltered face, clearly label appearance-changing effects, and require an intentional opt-in appropriate to the user’s age. Creative expression remains available; casual novelty remains available; even age transformation remains available where suitable. What disappears is covert pressure disguised as convenience. When the choice is between a child having to request alteration and a child having to undo an imposed “improvement,” the safer and more respectful default is obvious: their real face should be enough.
