[P1.1] In 2015, fires set to clear land for plantations burned across Sumatra and Kalimantan's drained peatlands. Schools closed for weeks, airports shut, millions breathed toxic smoke from Riau to Singapore, and Indonesia's own economic losses were widely estimated in the billions of dollars. That is the real ledger of loosely governed palm-oil expansion: the profits are private, and the bill is sent to everyone else in the region.
[P1.2] The motion concedes that tighter rules cost some income and some export growth. Accept that. The question is whether that cost is outweighed, and three arguments say it is.
[P1.3] First, the harms of unchecked expansion land on Southeast Asians, not distant environmentalists. Drained peat burns, subsides and floods; cleared watersheds silt up rivers that rice farmers and fishers depend on; concession boundaries drawn over customary land produce long-running disputes. The rural poor are the first victims of weak land-use rules, not their beneficiaries.
[P1.4] Second, expansion is the laziest route to growth. Smallholder yields typically run far below what well-managed estates achieve on the same soils. The income gap can be closed by replanting with better seedlings, fertiliser and secure titles on land already cleared. [P1.5] Indonesia's moratoria on new forest and peat permits coincided with falling deforestation while output kept rising. The trade-off is real but small.
[P1.6] Third, regulation protects the export market itself. Major buyers and importing governments increasingly demand deforestation-free, traceable supply. Producers who cannot prove clean land lose premium access; smallholders without legal, mapped plots are cut out first.
[P1.7] So weigh it: a modest, compensable slowdown at the frontier against haze, lost forests, burned peat and a tainted brand. Regulate harder; grow up, not out.
