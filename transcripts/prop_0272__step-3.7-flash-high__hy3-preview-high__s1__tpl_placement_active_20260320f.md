[P1.1] The core of this proposition is a choice about what public schools owe students first: the in-person, embodied, social experiences that build foundational human capacity, or screen-based digital enrichment that often prioritizes narrow technical skills over holistic development. Prioritizing after-school sports, arts, and unstructured play is the right call, because these activities build competencies no digital program can replicate, while also closing equity gaps that digital enrichment widens.
[P1.2] Unstructured play, sports, and arts teach skills critical to long-term success that no coding app or educational video can deliver. When kids negotiate the rules of an impromptu playground game, they practice conflict resolution and collaborative problem-solving without adult intervention. Team sports build resilience, teach kids to handle loss, and reduce rates of anxiety and depression—a critical benefit as youth mental health crises worsen. Arts give students an outlet for emotional expression and build the creative, non-linear thinking that drives innovation in every field, from engineering to medicine.
[P1.3] These are not "extras": they are the building blocks of the socio-emotional and physical health that makes all other learning possible.
[P1.4] This priority also advances equity in ways that expanding digital enrichment first cannot. Low-income students are far less likely to have access to safe yards, community sports leagues, or paid art classes outside of school. Even low-cost digital enrichment requires reliable internet, working devices, and often parental support to navigate—resources many low-income families lack. Expanding school-based sports, arts, and play programs levels the playing field, giving all students access to the developmental opportunities their wealthier peers often take for granted.
[P1.5] This is not a rejection of digital learning. Digital tools can enhance these activities: students can use free design software to create playbills for a school play, track soccer stats for their team, or document their outdoor play projects. But prioritizing purely digital enrichment first puts screen time ahead of the in-person experiences that build the core capacities kids need to thrive in school and beyond.
