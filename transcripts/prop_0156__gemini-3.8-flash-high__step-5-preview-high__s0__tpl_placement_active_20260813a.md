[P1.1] The eurozone’s post-2010 crisis response did not preserve European solidarity; it institutionalized an antagonistic creditor-debtor hierarchy that poisoned political trust across the continent. Instead of acting as a community of shared fate, the eurozone operated as a debt-collection mechanism, subordinating democratic legitimacy to financial discipline.
[P1.2] First, the response fractured trust between European publics. The bailouts were framed to northern voters through toxic caricatures of southern profligacy, obscuring how emergency loans primarily insulated core northern banks from peripheral default. In debtor nations, the Troika’s punitive austerity was experienced not as solidarity, but as foreign subjugation. When the Greek electorate rejected bailout terms in the 2015 referendum, only to see harsher conditions immediately imposed, the message was clear: national democracy was subordinate to creditor diktat.
[P1.3] Mutual trust—the foundational premise of European integration—was replaced by resentment and humiliation.
[P1.4] Second, this asymmetry fueled a durable populist revolt that permanently damaged European cohesion. The Alternative for Germany was founded explicitly to oppose eurozone bailouts, while across southern Europe, traditional governing parties collapsed under anti-austerity anger. Scapegoating neighbors became a mainstream electoral strategy, entrenching political division into national party systems.
[P1.5] Preserving the currency's technical architecture is not the same as preserving solidarity. Solidarity requires voluntary mutual support, not coercive conditionality enforced through the threat of financial asphyxiation. By choosing to protect balance sheets at the expense of democratic agency, European leaders saved the euro’s mechanics while shattering the political goodwill necessary to sustain it. Distrust did not merely increase; it became the defining fault line of contemporary European politics.

[C2.2] That is solidarity, however unpopular.
[C2.3] Greece 2015 is the strongest example, but it proves preservation, not subjugation. The referendum did not produce Grexit; it produced a negotiation in which Greece kept the euro, avoided chaotic default and retained full membership. Greeks subsequently accepted the terms Tsipras negotiated while retaining the option to leave; they chose Europe. That may be humiliating politics, but it is not democratic erasure.
[C2.4] On backlash, PRO over-reads AfD. Born around euro rescue, its breakthrough came with the 2015 migration and identity conflict, a Western-wide pattern visible in Britain, France and the United States. Anger at policy is not the same as rejection of mutual commitment.
[C2.5] Nor does the unfinished banking union prove that trust died. In 2010 there was no common supervision or resolution regime; afterwards there are both. Deposit insurance waits for risk reduction in every federation; delay is caution, not collapse. More decisively, the same eurozone later agreed joint borrowing and grants for NextGenerationEU, with northern creditors accepting shared liability for southern recovery.
[C2.6] PRO's metric would count every quarrel inside a family as proof the family no longer exists. But the eurozone's most existential test ended with membership intact, markets reopened and the architecture repaired. The response preserved solidarity.
