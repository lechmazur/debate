[P1.1] Apex predators are not decorative. They are functional. Remove them and herbivores proliferate unchecked, vegetation is stripped, stream banks erode, birds lose nesting cover, and the ecosystem that everyone downstream depends on quietly degrades. Wolves and lynx suppress mesopredators, reshape deer and boar behaviour, and push browsing pressure off vulnerable ground.
[P1.2] This is the mechanism of restoration, not a symbol of it. Where predators have returned, we see woody recovery in over-browsed valleys and healthier riparian strips — and where they are absent, no amount of fencing and culling has halted the slide. Deer overabundance in predator-free landscapes is the proof: decades of managed hunting have not substituted for predation, because hunting does not do what predators do — it does not create landscapes of fear that redistribute grazing pressure across whole watersheds.
[P1.3] The motion's second half is where this debate is really decided. Rural objections matter; they are not a veto. Ecosystems are public goods. The water regulation, flood mitigation, biodiversity, and carbon storage that restored predator populations produce are enjoyed by millions who never see a pasture.
[P1.4] A policy that lets a small, concentrated group block a diffuse, nationwide benefit is not a fair weighing — it is a transfer of decision rights over shared nature to whoever lives nearest to it. Democracies routinely proceed over local objection on flood defences, rewilding, protected areas, and infrastructure, because "some people object" cannot be the stopping rule for public goods.
[P1.5] And the harms are real but tractable: livestock losses can be reimbursed, prevention subsidised — fladry, guardian animals, night enclosures, early-warning systems. Countries with longer predator experience show that compensation and prevention sharply reduce conflict. Compare that cost to the cost of permanent ecosystem degradation, which no one reimburses anyone for.
[P1.6] So the case is simple: reintroduction delivers a benefit no substitute provides; the objectors' losses are compensable; the alternative is unpaid-for decline. Restraint is not neutrality — it is choosing degradation on behalf of everyone else.

[C1.1] The motion is not a modest conservation proposal. It is a command that one of the most consequential land-use decisions in a region be imposed on the people who bear its daily risks. That is where it fails.
[C1.2] Apex predators can change ecosystems, but the proposition treats reintroduction as a restoration switch. It is not. Outcomes depend on prey density, habitat quality, land use, weather, and how a specific landscape is already managed. Predators may suppress some herbivores while creating new pressures elsewhere; benefits are slow, diffuse, and hard to attribute, while costs arrive immediately and locally.
[C1.3] A wolf or lynx does not respect property lines. Livestock losses, injured animals, disrupted grazing, guard costs, and the constant uncertainty of checking stock fall on individual farms. Compensation rarely covers the non-lethal costs: stress, lost breeding stock, extra labour, and the simple loss of control over one’s own land.
[C1.4] The proposition’s decisive flaw is the phrase “even when rural communities and livestock farmers object.” That is not a fair weighing of public goods; it is treating local consent as an obstacle to be overridden. Public goods can justify compensation, consultation, co-design, and careful piloting. They do not justify handing a concentrated minority an ongoing burden and calling the resulting diffuse benefit democracy. Conservation imposed over sustained opposition invites poaching, sabotage, non-cooperation, and political backlash.
[C1.5] Legitimacy is not sentiment; it is a practical ecological resource.
[C1.6] And the burden is backwards. If predators are to be imposed over objection, proponents must show they are necessary, not merely beneficial, and that less coercive alternatives have failed. Habitat protection, riparian fencing, controlled grazing, targeted culling, and wildlife corridors are less charismatic but more accountable and reversible. The proposition converts a contested ecological experiment into a mandate.
[C1.7] That is the wrong decision rule.

If those tools restored degraded ecosystems, the motion would be unnecessary. CON concedes these systems need restoring and that predators can change them.
[P2.4] "Beneficial but not necessary" is a distinction without a difference when the alternative on offer is the one already failing.
