[P1.1] The five-day week is not a law of nature. It is a law. Before the 1930s, six days was normal; then legislatures in the advanced economies set forty hours as the standard and made anything beyond it expensive. Employers predicted ruin.
[P1.2] Instead, the following decades produced the fastest growth and broadest prosperity in industrial history. The question here is not whether a shorter standard week is possible — we have already done this once — but whether we will do it again after eighty years of productivity gains that have shown up almost nowhere in the clock.
[P1.3] That is the heart of the case. Since roughly 1980, output per hour in rich countries has risen enormously while working time has barely moved. Workers got the productivity; they did not get the time. A legal standard is the mechanism that converts efficiency into life, and we simply stopped turning it.
[P1.4] Second, this must be law rather than corporate goodwill because no single firm can move first without risk. A company that offers four days while rivals offer five bears the cost of experimentation alone; a company that keeps five while rivals move loses staff. That is a textbook coordination failure, and coordination failures are precisely what statutes exist to resolve. Redefining the standard week — thirty-two hours as the threshold beyond which overtime premiums attach, with base pay protected — changes the default for everyone simultaneously, which is why no one is competitively punished for humane scheduling.
[P1.5] Third, the trials point the same direction. Iceland's large public-sector experiments and the British pilot organised a few years ago produced a consistent pattern: output held steady, burnout and sick leave fell, and the overwhelming majority of participating employers chose not to go back. Fatigue is not free. The eighth and ninth hour of knowledge work are the cheapest hours to buy and the least valuable hours produced.
[P1.6] We already legislate the workweek. The only real question is which number, and forty is a number chosen for a factory economy that no longer exists.

[P4.1] A1: Someone pays — I've never pretended otherwise. But CON is comparing a new line item to a status quo whose costs are already being paid, just not itemised. Health systems in every advanced economy currently pay enormous sums for agency and locum cover, for overtime premiums, for re-recruiting and retraining nurses who quit within five years, and for the beds that sit closed because no one will staff them. That is the burnout budget, and it is real money.
[P4.2] A shorter standard week converts an unbudgeted attrition cost into a budgeted staffing cost. If a residual gap remains, then yes: the public purse pays it, the same way it pays for the weekend, for statutory holidays, for the eight-hour day. Rest has always been a thing societies bought deliberately, not a windfall they waited for.
[P4.3] A2: Some have — smaller firms and a scattering of well-known employers run four-day weeks right now, which is itself evidence the gains exist. But universal adoption never follows, and the reason is structural. A firm capturing only its own retention gain does not capture the public health gain, the reduced burnout externality, or the effect on its rivals' labour pool. And it faces clients, suppliers and courts operating on a five-day rhythm; Friday absence is costly precisely because everyone else is present.
[P4.4] Note the precedent: Ford adopted the five-day week unilaterally and profitably in the 1920s. Most employers still did not follow until statute made them. Unilateral proof of concept plus non-diffusion is exactly what a coordination trap looks like.
[P4.5] So the clash resolves like this. CON says costs are certain and benefits speculative. But the status quo's costs are equally certain — they are simply borne by workers, in exhaustion, in caregivers dropping to part-time, in early exit from nursing and teaching. The question is not whether society pays for the time.
[P4.6] It is whether workers pay for it alone, invisibly, or whether the law makes eighty years of productivity growth show up, at last, in the calendar.
