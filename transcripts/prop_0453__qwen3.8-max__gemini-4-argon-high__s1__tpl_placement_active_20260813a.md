[P1.1] Housing inflation is not just another price increase; it is the most socially corrosive form of inflation and the one most likely to become entrenched. Shelter costs are a huge share of household budgets and of standard inflation measures, and once rents and house prices run hot, they feed wage demands, inflation expectations, and financial fragility. A central bank that lets housing inflation persist is buying a short-lived calm in output at the price of a harder stabilization problem later.
[P1.2] The strongest reason to prioritize housing inflation is that the damage it causes is less reversible than the damage from a moderate recession. A recession can be cushioned by unemployment insurance, fiscal transfers, and later rate cuts. Housing inflation, by contrast, redistributes wealth upward, locks renters into unaffordable leases, delays household formation, and can inflate mortgage leverage until a correction becomes far more dangerous. Monetary policy cannot build homes, but it can prevent demand and credit from chasing scarce housing.
[P1.3] When housing costs outpace incomes for years, the result is not merely a statistic: it is crowded homes, longer commutes, strained family budgets, and a generation priced out of ownership.
[P1.4] Central banks also have a special responsibility here because credibility is their key asset. If markets and workers conclude that the bank will flinch whenever tightening threatens growth, expectations can de-anchor. That risk is especially acute with housing, because housing inflation is visible, emotionally salient, and often financed with debt. Aggressive action today reduces the chance that the bank must induce a deeper recession tomorrow.
[P1.5] Opponents will say recession hurts ordinary workers. True, but housing inflation hurts renters, younger households, and future homebuyers first and hardest. The better tradeoff is to accept a higher probability of a contained downturn than to normalize a cost-of-living crisis that compounds inequality and eventually forces a harsher reckoning.

[C1.1] The case for aggressive tightening concedes its own fatal flaw: "Monetary policy cannot build homes" [P1.2]. In fact, it does something worse—it stops homes from being built. Housing inflation is overwhelmingly driven by structural supply shortages, zoning barriers, and demographics. When a central bank prioritizes crushing housing inflation with high interest rates, it attacks that supply.
[C1.2] Builders rely on financing; when rates surge, new housing starts and apartment projects are canceled. Simultaneously, homeowners with older, cheaper mortgages refuse to move, freezing resale inventory. By choking off new construction and listings to suppress short-term demand, rate hikes deepen the housing shortage and guarantee worse affordability once the economy recovers.
[C1.3] Worse, prioritizing housing inflation invites policy disaster because shelter metrics lag reality. Official inflation gauges track all existing leases, which renew gradually, meaning reported shelter inflation reflects market conditions from up to a year ago. A central bank fixated on bringing down housing prints is driving while looking in the rearview mirror—hiking rates long after real-time demand has cooled and turning a soft landing into a needless recession.
[C1.4] Finally, claiming that recessions are more reversible than housing inflation [P1.2, P1.5] gets the human cost backward. Rate hikes do not help young families buy homes; trading a high purchase price for a punitive mortgage rate still locks out first-time buyers, handing the market to cash-rich investors. And when tightening triggers a recession, younger, lower-income renters are the first to lose their jobs. A renter cannot pay even a stabilized lease without a paycheck, and unemployment checks do not prevent eviction or the permanent wage scarring inflicted on young workers during a downturn.
[C1.5] Central banks must balance broad price stability and employment, leaving mortgage leverage to macroprudential regulators and supply bottlenecks to housing policy. Sacrificing jobs and crushing homebuilding to chase a lagging sectoral index makes both the economy and the housing crisis worse.

Prioritizing housing inflation means watching the leading edge, not mechanically hiking stale numbers.
[P2.5] Their recession objection [C1.4] is real but misses the tradeoff. A downturn can be offset by unemployment insurance, fiscal aid, and later rate cuts. A housing-cost regime cannot be undone by a quick cut: it reshapes wealth, delays families, and embeds high rents for years. Yes, high mortgage rates hurt first-time buyers, but entrenched prices hurt them longer, and a mild recession preserves their future income and down-payment capacity.
