[P1.1] When algorithms decide who gets a job interview, a mortgage, an apartment, or covered care, the person shut out deserves to know why. Courts should recognize a limited right to explanation precisely for those automated decisions that significantly control access to housing, credit, employment, or healthcare.
[P1.2] Opacity is not neutral. A tenant-screening score tanks because of a mismatched identity or outdated eviction record no one will confirm. A credit model quietly weights zip-code density or purchase patterns that track protected class. An applicant is auto-rejected and told only “system decision.” Without an explanation, the individual cannot find the error, surface the proxy discrimination, or prepare any real challenge.
[P1.3] Silence entrenches power and turns judicial review into theater: judges cannot test fairness when the basis remains hidden.
[P1.4] The right is deliberately limited. It does not force disclosure of source code, training data, or proprietary weights. It requires a clear statement of the principal factors and logic that produced the adverse outcome—comparable to the reasons human officers have long been expected to give. That is enough for the affected person to spot mistakes, correct inputs, or appeal on substance.
[P1.5] Courts already police arbitrary public and private power that gates essential opportunities. Extending that tradition to automated systems simply updates due-process and equal-protection principles for the tools now in use. Recognition gives a concrete remedy: compel the usable explanation first, then litigate the merits. The alternative leaves life-altering gatekeeping permanently black-boxed, error-prone, and unaccountable.
[P1.6] Limited explanation restores basic dignity and error correction without freezing useful technology. That is the floor courts should set.
