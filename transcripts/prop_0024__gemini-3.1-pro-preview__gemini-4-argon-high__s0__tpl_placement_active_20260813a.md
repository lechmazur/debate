[P1.1] The housing affordability crisis in advanced economies is fundamentally a crisis of scarcity. Streamlining permitting and zoning tackles the root cause of this scarcity, whereas rent control merely masks the symptoms while exacerbating the underlying shortage.
[P1.2] When job growth outpaces housing construction, prices rise. Restrictive zoning and labyrinthine permitting artificially cap the number of homes built, creating a game of musical chairs where lower-income residents always lose. Eliminating these bottlenecks enables supply to meet demand and triggers the economic mechanism of "filtering." When new units absorb high-income demand, wealthier renters stop bidding up the prices of older apartments, naturally lowering costs for everyone else down the chain.
[P1.3] We see this working in real-time in cities like Auckland or Austin, where aggressive upzoning and supply booms have successfully flattened and even reduced rents.
[P1.4] Conversely, rent control and expansive tenant protections fail because they attempt to legislate away scarcity without fixing it. While these policies offer immediate relief to incumbent tenants, they pull the ladder up behind them. Landlords predictably respond to strict rent caps by converting rentals to condos or deferring maintenance, actively shrinking the existing rental stock.
[P1.5] Crucially, these policies signal to developers that building new housing is a losing investment, freezing future construction.
[P1.6] The result is a deeply inequitable, bifurcated market. A lucky few remain comfortably housed in price-locked units, while newcomers, young people, and growing families are forced into a hyper-competitive, catastrophically expensive open market. Cities famous for strict rent controls do not have affordable housing for new residents; they have decade-long waitlists and flourishing black markets for sublets.
[P1.7] True, sustainable affordability cannot be achieved by rationing a perpetual shortage. It requires structural abundance. Removing artificial regulatory barriers is the only effective way to build the housing necessary to drive down costs across the entire market, rather than just playing favorites with a shrinking pool of units.
