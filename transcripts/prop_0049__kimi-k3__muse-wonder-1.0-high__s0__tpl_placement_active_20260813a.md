[P1.1] A fourteen-year-old scrolls at 11 p.m., and an ad appears because an algorithm has inferred she is anxious about her body. That is behavioral targeting: not a billboard, but a one-to-one psychological intervention built on harvested data. It should be prohibited for under-sixteens, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, consent is impossible. Behavioral targeting runs on continuous surveillance — clicks, location, dwell time, inferred mood. Meaningful consent requires understanding what is collected and how it will be used against you, a judgment the law already doubts adolescents can make: under-sixteens generally cannot sign contracts, and data-protection regimes like the GDPR peg digital consent ages as high as sixteen. An industry founded on data a child cannot validly surrender has no legitimate foundation.
[P1.3] The profile compiled at thirteen can follow a person for life, and no child can bargain away that future.
[P1.4] Second, the harm is structural, not incidental. Developmental psychology tells us children and younger teens recognize advertising poorly — especially when it is personalized, native to the feed, or delivered by a trusted influencer — and their impulse control is still maturing. Targeting converts that vulnerability into a business model: junk-food ads timed to cravings, gambling-style game mechanics, appearance products aimed at freshly measured insecurity. We already ban cigarette and alcohol marketing to minors on exactly this logic.
[P1.5] Personalized targeting is the same exploitation at industrial scale.
[P1.6] Third, note what the motion does not do. It bans no advertising to children. Contextual ads — the cereal commercial in the cartoon break, the banner matched to the page rather than the person — remain legal and keep funding children's content, as they did for decades before tracking existed. So the objection that prohibition destroys free content or speech fails on its own terms.
[P1.7] The central clash is simple: may corporations build psychological dossiers on children to sell to them more effectively? A society that will not let a fifteen-year-old sign a phone contract should not let an ad network auction her insecurities.

Ban behavioral targeting, and she gets the same generic ads as everyone else — more perfume, more fast food, more noise. Relevance is not exploitation, and prohibition mistakes a useful tool for an inherent abuse.
[C1.2] First, a ban is self-defeating on privacy. To stop targeting under-sixteens, platforms must first know exactly who is under sixteen. That means stronger age checks, identity documents, facial estimation, or even more behavioral inference to detect children lying about their age. We would expand surveillance of everyone, push teenagers onto adult accounts with fewer safeguards, and create valuable age-verified databases — all in the name of collecting less data.
[C1.3] Second, it treats a fifteen-year-old like a six-year-old and parents like bystanders. Development is gradual, not a cliff at sixteen. Families already can use consent rules, device controls, ad preferences, and limits on sensitive categories such as gambling, alcohol, and weight loss. A blanket state ban overrides those judgments even when targeting is plainly beneficial: dyslexia tools for a struggling reader, mental-health support, scholarships, sports equipment sized for the sport a teen actually plays.
[C1.4] Contextual advertising cannot do that well in a social feed or app, where the 'context' is just scrolling.
[C1.5] Third, the ban harms the very content children use. Targeted ads let small creators, educational channels, games, and local youth organisations reach a niche audience cheaply without giant budgets. Replace them with lower-value contextual ads and revenue falls; the likely results are paywalls that exclude poorer families, more ads overall to compensate, or content designed for advertisers rather than children.
[C1.6] Abusive practices deserve hard rules: no targeting based on health or insecurity, short data retention, clear labels, and real parental controls. But prohibit the practice regardless of purpose, consent, or benefit, and we get more surveillance, less useful information, and poorer free content.
