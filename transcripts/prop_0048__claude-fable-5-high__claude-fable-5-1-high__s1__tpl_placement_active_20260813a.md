[P1.1] A patient bleeding internally at 3 a.m. does not get to choose how many nurses are on the ward. Someone chooses for her — and the question is whether that choice should be constrained by a legal safety floor or left to the budget pressures of hospital administrators. Every other high-stakes industry answers this question the same way: airlines have mandated crew minimums, mines have ventilation standards, daycares have adult-to-child ratios. Hospitals should not be the exception.
[P1.2] The case rests on three pillars. First, understaffing kills, and it kills invisibly. A large body of research, most famously the work associated with Linda Aiken's team, has consistently found that each additional patient added to a nurse's workload is linked to a meaningful increase in mortality risk after surgery. Missed deterioration, medication errors, delayed responses — these deaths never appear on a spreadsheet labeled "staffing decision," which is precisely why administrators keep making it.
[P1.3] Second, this is a textbook market failure. The savings from cutting a nurse are immediate, concrete, and credited to the administrator. The costs — deaths, readmissions, burned-out nurses quitting the profession — are diffuse, delayed, and borne by others. When incentives are structurally misaligned, "trust management's judgment" is not a policy; it is an abdication.
[P1.4] That is why we regulate, rather than merely advise, in every domain where lives hang on cost decisions.
[P1.5] Third, mandates work in practice. California implemented minimum ratios two decades ago; predicted hospital closures did not materialize, nurse retention improved, and nurses reported safer workloads. Queensland, Australia followed with similar reported gains. A ratio is a floor, not a ceiling — administrators keep full flexibility to staff above it, adjust skill mix, and manage acuity.
[P1.6] The law simply removes the option of staffing below the point where care becomes dangerous.
[P1.7] The burden tonight is simple: if flexibility for administrators requires the freedom to understaff below safe levels, that flexibility is not worth defending.

[C1.8] The real choice is not law versus reckless administrators. [C1.9] Understaffing is a problem. A number in a statute is not the solution to it.
