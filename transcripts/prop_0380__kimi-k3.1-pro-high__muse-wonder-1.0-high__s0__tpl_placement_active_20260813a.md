[P1.1] Sixteen-year-olds can work, pay taxes, and live under laws governing their education, healthcare, and future. Yet at eighteen, adulthood is suddenly treated as the moment political judgment appears. That is not a principled boundary; it is a delay imposed on citizens whose interests are already being decided.
[P1.2] Voting is not a certification of flawless maturity. It is the basic means by which people subject to government acquire a voice in it. We do not require older voters to pass tests of knowledge, impulse control, or independence. CON therefore needs more than the observation that sixteen-year-olds are still developing: everyone is.
[P1.3] The relevant question is whether they can understand political choices, form preferences, and cast a ballot according to them. Sixteen-year-olds routinely perform far more demanding reasoning in school and make consequential decisions about their lives. If immaturity justified exclusion, age would be an extremely crude and selectively applied competence test.
[P1.4] Lowering the age also creates participation at the right moment. Many eighteen-year-olds first encounter voting while moving, starting work, or entering higher education—precisely when registration and a new polling routine are easiest to miss. At sixteen, most are still in stable communities and schools, where civic education can connect directly to a real election rather than remain an abstract lesson. Voting earlier can establish the habit before life becomes more disruptive.
[P1.5] This is not merely about adding ballots; it is about building citizens.
[P1.6] The experience of places including Austria, Scotland, and Wales shows that sixteen-year-old voting is administratively feasible, not a radical experiment with unknowable mechanics. Extending it to all elections removes the incoherence of telling young people they are competent to choose some representatives but not others.
[P1.7] CON’s predicted dangers are speculative, while the exclusion is concrete and recurring. Young citizens inherit public debt, climate policy, education systems, and international commitments, often for longer than anyone else voting. A democracy serious about consent should stop making them wait until those decisions are already locked in.

[C1.1] The ballot is not a megaphone. It is binding power over other people: to tax, punish, commit public money, and choose governments that can wage war. Because a vote governs others, not just expresses the voter, democracy may require a threshold of independent judgment before granting it. Eighteen is not magic, but any line must be set where mature judgment and legal autonomy normally converge: adulthood.
[C1.2] Sixteen-year-olds often reason well in calm, structured settings. Elections are not calm or structured. They are emotional, social, compressed campaigns designed to exploit identity, peer pressure, outrage, and short-term reward. Adolescent development is precisely uneven here: abstract reasoning arrives earlier than impulse control, resistance to social influence, and weighing long-term consequences.
[C1.3] Not testing older voters proves nothing; a minimum age is a general safeguard, as licensing is, and we do not abolish it because some adults drive badly.
[C1.4] Independence matters as much as cognition. Most sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds live with parents, depend on them financially, and spend their days under teachers' authority. Formal ballot secrecy cannot remove dinner-table pressure, classroom cues, or the desire to conform. The likely result is not a fresh youth voice but an echo, giving already-enfranchised adults extra influence through their children and making schools campaign terrain.
[C1.5] Being affected by law cannot be the test. Fourteen-year-olds can work and pay tax on purchases, younger children are affected even longer by climate and debt, and visitors pay taxes too, yet none of that settles political membership. Society bars minors from binding contracts, jury service, and other irreversible legal acts because responsibility is graduated until majority. Voting for all elections — including the highest-stakes national choices — would make it the great exception.
[C1.6] Starting the habit earlier does not fix this. A habit formed under parental roofs and school supervision is not evidence of autonomous participation, and tying civic education to live partisan contests risks unequal mobilization by schools and campaigns. Keep eighteen: rights, duties, and full political power arriving together is coherence, not delay.
