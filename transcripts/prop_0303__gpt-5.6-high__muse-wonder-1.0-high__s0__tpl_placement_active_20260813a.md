[P1.1] Modern meritocracy confuses competition with equal competition. Elite universities and professions may no longer reserve places by explicit birthright, but removing formal barriers does not erase the advantages accumulated before the contest begins. A system can reward genuine talent while still exaggerating how much outcomes reflect talent alone.
[P1.2] Consider two equally capable students. One grows up with stable housing, excellent schools, private tutoring, extracurricular fees, informed parents, and time to build an impressive application. The other navigates weaker schools, paid work, family responsibilities, and little guidance through admissions. When the first wins the place, the decision may appear meritocratic because both submitted the same forms.
[P1.3] But the résumé being judged was partly purchased through years of money, time, security, and institutional knowledge. Treating unequal preparation as evidence of unequal merit simply launders class advantage into achievement.
[P1.4] The same process continues after graduation. Affluent families can finance prestigious degrees, relocation to expensive cities, unpaid or poorly paid internships, professional clothing, exam preparation, and months spent searching for the right job rather than taking the first available one. Their networks provide referrals, advice, and familiarity with elite manners and expectations. Employers then label the resulting polish “fit,” “confidence,” or “leadership potential.” Once an elite credential and first prestigious job are secured, advantage compounds through stronger networks, higher pay, and greater freedom to take career risks.
[P1.5] Scholarships, exceptional upward mobility, and open examinations do not disprove this pattern. They show that the gates are permeable, not that the road to them is level. Indeed, celebrated exceptions can legitimize the wider structure by making inherited advantages look universally attainable.
[P1.6] Successful people often emphasize effort because their effort was real. Yet real effort and structural privilege can coexist. The crucial question is not whether elites worked hard, but whether similarly talented people had comparable chances to convert effort into recognized success. They plainly do not.
[P1.7] Modern elite pathways therefore select some ability, but they also reproduce class privilege—and then rename the combined result “merit.”
