[P1.1] Treating a seventeen-year-old like a seven-year-old until the stroke of midnight on their eighteenth birthday is not protection—it is developmental malpractice. Adolescence is the period when young people must practice self-governance, and the family is the training ground. When parents retain indefinite, child-style control over a teenager's private communication and movement, they do not make the teen safer; they make the adult they are becoming less competent.
[P1.2] The case for graduated privacy rests on a simple developmental truth: autonomy is how maturity is built. A teenager who is allowed to text friends without surveillance, to walk to a library alone, or to close their bedroom door is practicing the judgment calls they will need at nineteen, twenty-five, and beyond. Strip that practice away, and you produce not a protected child but a dependent adult who panics when a parent is not managing their schedule.
[P1.3] Privacy also functions as a safety valve, not a threat. A teenager who fears that every message will be read, every search logged, learns to hide problems rather than solve them. The teen contemplating self-harm, the one being groomed online, the one struggling with sexuality or substance use—these are precisely the young people who need confidential channels to reach counselors, hotlines, or trusted adults. Indefinite monitoring does not stop danger; it drives it underground.
[P1.4] When a phone is locked down, a teenager in crisis does not stop being in crisis; they stop being visible.
[P1.5] We already trust teenagers with lethal responsibility elsewhere. Sixteen-year-olds drive two-ton vehicles. Seventeen-year-olds work, pay taxes, and in many places make their own medical decisions. The law recognizes that competence grows gradually.
[P1.6] The family should mirror that reality. A growing right to private communication and movement is not a privilege extracted from parents; it is the necessary apprenticeship for the independence that adulthood demands.
