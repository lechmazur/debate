[P1.1] Retailers should remain free to change prices with inventory, demand, time, or costs. What they should not be allowed to do is estimate how desperate, affluent, constrained, or poorly informed a particular customer is, then use that estimate to set that customer’s price. The motion targets that practice—not discounts, ordinary dynamic pricing, or price uniformity.
[P1.2] First, this pricing monetizes vulnerability rather than creating value. Consider two customers buying the same baby formula at the same moment. One has time to compare sellers; the other is shopping late, has no nearby alternative, and whose purchase history signals urgency. An algorithm that charges the second customer more has not improved the product or reduced the cost of serving them.
[P1.3] It has simply discovered how little choice they have. The same logic applies when browsing behavior suggests a customer can be pushed further before walking away. That is extraction through information asymmetry, and the burden is especially likely to fall on people with urgent needs, limited mobility, or fewer retail options.
[P1.4] Second, personalized prices undermine the conditions on which fair markets depend. Advertised prices let people compare, budget, and switch. A hidden individual price makes comparison unreliable: a customer cannot know whether a higher quote reflects costs, a genuine offer, or a profile built from their device, location, purchases, or proxies for protected characteristics. Meaningful consent is also unrealistic when the inference and its pricing effect are invisible.
[P1.5] A retailer should not acquire an ever-expanding license to infer sensitive circumstances merely because someone once clicked “accept.”
[P1.6] Third, the ban is narrow and workable: prohibit willingness-to-pay and ability-to-pay profiles as inputs to individualized retail prices. Retailers can still offer public promotions, transparent loyalty schemes, verified needs-based discounts, and prices based on product or transaction conditions. If personalization genuinely benefits consumers, those benefits can be delivered through visible, voluntary discounts rather than secret price discrimination.

[P5.1] The decisive question is not whether a secret model can sometimes produce a coupon. It is whether the chance of that coupon justifies giving retailers standing permission to infer how constrained or affluent each customer is and allocate prices accordingly.
[P5.2] CON’s posted-price scheme does not answer that question. Checking receipts against a displayed price can detect a surcharge; it cannot reveal why one shopper received an offer and another did not, or whether the nominal baseline was strategically maintained. Their safeguard polices the visible outcome while leaving the motion’s target—the covert willingness-to-pay judgment—intact.
[P5.3] Nor is there a genuine dilemma over published offers. A rule customers can know and qualify under is an offer; a model’s private estimate of what a particular person can be induced or compelled to pay is not made fair by calling its output a discount. The difference is not paperwork. It is whether the customer can understand the terms or must submit to an unchallengeable judgment.
[P5.4] A possible coupon is not worth a standing license to monetize vulnerability. Retailers retain public sales, ordinary dynamic pricing, and knowable offers. Consumers should not have to surrender invisible judgments about their means or desperation to obtain them. That is the power this ban removes, and none of CON’s safeguards removes it.
