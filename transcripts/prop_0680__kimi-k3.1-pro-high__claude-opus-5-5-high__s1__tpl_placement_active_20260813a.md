[P1.1] The decisive fact is that these systems do not simply predict crime; they predict what police records will look like next. Arrests reflect where officers were sent, whom they stopped, and which offenses they chose to pursue. Incident records also depend on who reports, how police classify complaints, and whether residents trust them enough to call. Treating those records as a neutral map of danger builds yesterday’s enforcement policy into tomorrow’s.
[P1.2] That creates a self-reinforcing loop. Send more patrols to one block and officers discover more drugs, warrants, and minor offenses there. Those discoveries become fresh evidence that the block needs still more patrols, while an unpatrolled block looks quiet partly because nobody was looking. Extra patrols are not harmless observation: they bring stops, searches, surveillance, and sometimes violence.
[P1.3] Residents inherit heightened scrutiny because of their address, with no meaningful way to inspect or contest the data trail behind it.
[P1.4] The likely defense is efficiency: crime is concentrated, so why ignore forecasts? But accurately predicting recorded police activity is not the same as preventing harm. Deployment must be judged by crimes avoided, not arrests generated or familiar hotspots reproduced. Added patrol may displace offending, intensify low-level enforcement, or discourage residents from cooperating with police.
[P1.5] Forecast accuracy alone cannot distinguish those outcomes. Human oversight is also weak when officers receive an apparently objective map whose distortions are already baked in.
[P1.6] This is a narrow prohibition, not a ban on statistics, emergency response, or evidence-based prevention. Cities can respond to current calls, investigate crimes, evaluate outcomes transparently, and invest in lighting, housing, violence interruption, and other measures that do not automate coercive deployment. Audits and promises of “better data” leave the central defect intact: the system’s primary fuel remains records shaped by unequal enforcement and reporting. When a tool turns contested administrative history into recurring police contact, the responsible rule is to prohibit it—not keep experimenting on neighborhoods least able to refuse.
