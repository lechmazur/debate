[P1.1] AI code may be compiled anywhere on Earth, but its harms land in specific hospitals, police precincts, and hiring offices. When an algorithmic tool wrongfully denies an EU citizen welfare benefits, racially discriminates against a job applicant in Paris, or misdiagnoses a patient in Milan, the injury occurs inside the European Union. Regulating AI based on where its impacts occur—rather than where a provider happens to be headquartered—is the only coherent way to govern this technology.
[P1.2] First, anchoring jurisdiction to corporate headquarters creates an immediate blueprint for regulatory evasion. If high-risk duties apply only to EU-headquartered entities, global tech firms can deploy untested, dangerous systems across Europe with zero accountability simply by maintaining their headquarters in Silicon Valley, Shenzhen, or a regulatory haven. Worse, it perversely penalizes domestic European developers, saddling them with strict oversight while foreign competitors flood the single market unburdened. Jurisdiction based on impact levels the playing field: if you touch the European market and affect European lives, you play by European rules.
[P1.3] Second, the entire premise of high-risk classification is risk mitigation and the defense of fundamental rights. The risk of an AI system is not determined by corporate paperwork; it is determined by the context, scale, and vulnerability of the population exposed to it. Biometric mass surveillance or critical infrastructure control carries the exact same potential for catastrophe whether the controlling board sits in Munich or Mumbai.
[P1.4] Third, impact-based governance is the bedrock of proven European doctrine. From consumer product safety to competition law and data protection under the GDPR, Europe has consistently asserted jurisdiction where effects materialize. A car cannot bypass brake safety standards simply because the manufacturer is based overseas; algorithmic systems with the power to deny employment or liberty cannot be treated with greater leniency.
[P1.5] To protect citizens and prevent regulatory flight, high-risk rules must follow the harm.
