[P1.1] Right now, two people can be arrested for the exact same nonviolent shoplifting charge, same criminal history, same assessed risk of skipping court. One has $500 in the bank, walks out that night, keeps their job, keeps their kid with them, and shows up for their hearing. The other has $12, sits in jail for three weeks, loses that job, gets evicted, and ends up pleading guilty just to get out—even if they are innocent. This is not an anomaly.
[P1.2] This is how cash bail operates, every single day.
[P1.3] Cash bail does not separate dangerous people from safe ones. It separates people with money from people without. For nonviolent offenses, the overwhelming majority of people held pretrial are locked up only because they cannot post a bail amount under one thousand dollars. Every real world jurisdiction that has ended this system found exactly the same thing: when you switch to supervised check ins and court reminders, court appearance rates do not drop.
[P1.4] They stay identical, and in some places rise slightly. There is no loss to public safety, no loss to court functioning.
[P1.5] This does not eliminate accountability. People who skip court still face warrants. Supervision can include curfews, required check ins, or monitoring where appropriate. What it eliminates is the punishment you receive before anyone has proven you committed any crime at all.
[P1.6] Opponents will attempt to frame this as leniency. It is not leniency. It is ending a two-tier justice system that destroys ordinary people’s lives for absolutely no justifiable public purpose. There is no good reason to keep this system.

[C1.1] Two defendants facing the same shoplifting charge are not divided by cruelty and kindness. They are divided by whether the system has any real leverage to get them back into court.
[C1.2] Cash bail is not punishment before proof. It is collateral for release. A text reminder tells you when court is. Bail gives you and your family a financial reason not to ignore it.
[C1.3] That difference matters most for the people most likely to miss court — those with unstable housing, addiction, prior warrants, or a strong motive to avoid a guilty verdict. A missed reminder disappears. Lost money does not.
[C1.4] Calling offenses “nonviolent” hides the harm. Repeat retail theft that shutters neighborhood stores, car break-ins and auto theft, drunk driving that did not kill someone this time, fraud that drains a retiree’s savings, drug distribution fueling overdoses — all count as nonviolent. Releasing repeat defendants on a promise and a phone call tells shop owners, drivers, and victims that their losses do not count, while inviting the same person to reoffend before trial.
[C1.5] Supervised release sounds humane and cheap until you run it. It demands caseworkers, check-ins, drug testing, electronic monitoring staff that most counties simply do not have. Overloaded agencies become voicemail supervision. And when someone on supervised release skips, a warrant is largely paper.
[C1.6] Police departments do not launch manhunts for nonviolent failures to appear.
[C1.7] So cases stall, victims wait, defendants pick up new charges, and jails fill anyway — only later and with more victims behind them.
[C1.8] The answer is reform, not elimination: ability-to-pay hearings, non-monetary conditions where they work, bail that fits means. Keep the tool that makes release credible. Eliminating it trades a flawed safeguard for no safeguard at all.
