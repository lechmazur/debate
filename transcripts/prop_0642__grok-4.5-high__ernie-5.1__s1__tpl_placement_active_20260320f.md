[P1.2] Minimums assume every new unit needs a fixed quota of stalls, ignoring location, transit access, or resident choices. [P1.3] Developers build only the parking that pencils out, freeing space for more units or better amenities. Housing becomes cheaper and denser without government-mandated car storage.
[P1.4] In car-dependent areas the usual objection is that demand is high and predictable. Yet markets already signal that demand: if residents need cars, they will pay for spaces or use nearby lots. Caps still allow ample parking; they simply stop speculative overbuilding.
[P1.5] Lower parking loads make denser mixed-use projects feasible, which in turn support better bus service, ride-share hubs, and local retail—gradually eroding car-only culture without bans.
[P1.6] Continuing minimums treats parking as a public good paid privately, subsidizing cars at the expense of affordability and land.
