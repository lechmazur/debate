[P1.1] Bodily autonomy is not a gift the state grants when convenient. It is a baseline. When a competent adult faces unbearable suffering from a terminal or degenerative condition, denying them the option to end that suffering on their own terms is a profound violation of that baseline. No one is forced to use this option.
[P1.2] The proposition asks only that the option exist, gated by strict safeguards.
[P1.3] The word "unbearable" does real work here. Terminal and degenerative conditions do not merely shorten life. They can dismantle it piece by piece. Consider motor neuron disease, where a fully lucid mind may become trapped in a body that can no longer move, speak, or swallow independently.
[P1.4] Consider late-stage Huntington's, where personality itself erodes. For some patients, palliative care manages pain. For others, it cannot touch the loss of dignity, agency, and selfhood. Suffering is not always soluble.
[P1.5] The status quo already forces brutal outcomes. People who are denied medical assistance in dying do not simply stop wanting it. Some take their own lives in ways that are violent, solitary, and traumatic for those who find them. Some endure against their will, surviving in conditions they experience as torture.
[P1.6] The question is not whether suffering ends by itself. It is whether the law will offer a humane, supervised pathway or force people into darker corners.
[P1.7] Strict safeguards are the framework, not a concession. Requiring multiple medical assessments, psychological evaluation, mandatory waiting periods, and documented voluntary consent ensures this is not a tool for coercion or despair. The proposition explicitly demands safeguards. The debate is not between regulation and chaos.
[P1.8] It is between regulated compassion and enforced suffering.
[P1.9] The core moral claim is simple. If we trust competent adults to refuse treatment, to donate organs, to make end-of-life decisions, then we must trust them with this. A law that compels someone to endure agony they find unbearable, when they are of sound mind and have every safeguard available, does not protect them. It controls them.
[P1.10] That is not a position worth defending.

So the question is not whether the lucid, well-supported, articulate patient in my opponent's examples deserves relief. It is whether any legal regime can reliably tell a free choice from a manufactured one — and what happens to everyone else when it cannot.
[C1.2] Start with the word doing the work in the motion: "unbearable." It is unverifiable by design. No doctor can measure it, contradict it, or audit it. Which means the gate the proposition leans on is not a gate at all; it is a self-report.
[C1.3] The same is true of "competent." Competence assessment is a coarse instrument, and the states that most often drive a wish to die — demoralization, depression, isolation, the belief that you are a burden on your children — do not remove capacity. They just reliably make death look rational.
[C1.4] Then watch what happens to "strict safeguards" over time. Everywhere assistance in dying has been legalised, the boundary has moved: from terminal to chronic, from physical to psychiatric, from adults to minors, from years of prognosis to none required. That is not a smear on any country. It is a logical inevitability, because once relieving unbearable suffering is the principle, every safeguard becomes an arbitrary cruelty to somebody, and courts and legislatures duly strike it down.
[C1.5] Safeguards are the price of passage, not a permanent feature.
[C1.6] Meanwhile the cheapest option in any strained health system is the one that ends the need for care. Canada has already produced reported cases of disabled people and veterans who could not get housing, home support, or a ramp — and were offered death instead. That is the real choice architecture. A right to die inside a system that will not fund living is not autonomy; it is abandonment with paperwork.
[C1.7] Wrong refusals of death are reversible tomorrow. Wrong deaths are not.
