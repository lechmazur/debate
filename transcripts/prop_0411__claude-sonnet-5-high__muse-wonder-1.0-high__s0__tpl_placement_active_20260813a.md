[P1.1] Picture a wheelchair user and an able-bodied friend standing on the same corner, opening the same app, trying to get to the same job interview. The friend's ride comes in six minutes. The wheelchair user's ride comes in forty-five, if it comes at all, because the one accessible van in the fleet is across town. That gap is not a minor inconvenience.
[P1.2] It is the difference between keeping a job and losing it, between living independently and depending on someone else to drive you everywhere. That is the gap this motion exists to close.
[P1.3] The core argument for PRO is simple: mobility is not a luxury good, and when it is rationed by disability, every other right that depends on mobility gets rationed too. You cannot show up for work, see a doctor, vote in person, or attend your kid's school event if you cannot reliably get there. A transit system that works for 95% of riders but strands the other 5% with hour-long waits isn't providing "transit for all," it's providing transit for the able-bodied and a lottery for everyone else.
[P1.4] Equal access to transportation is the precondition for equal participation in everything else society offers.
[P1.5] This is also not a hypothetical fix in search of a problem. Paratransit systems across major cities are notorious for requiring next-day booking and multi-hour windows, while standard buses and ride-hail cars show up in minutes. That disparity exists not because accessible service is impossible to deliver quickly, but because operators have never been required to treat it as a core service obligation rather than an afterthought bolted on to satisfy minimum legal compliance.
[P1.6] The comparison the judge should carry through this debate is this: when convenience is optional, it gets deprioritized, underfunded, and shrugged off as "too expensive." When it's a requirement, companies find the engineering and operational solutions, the same way they did for curb cuts, captioning, and accessible building codes once those became mandatory rather than aspirational. Requirement is what turns accessibility from charity into infrastructure.

[C1.1] The friend arriving in six minutes and the wheelchair user in forty-five is a real failure, but requiring the second wait to be "close" to the first will not fix it — it will make service scarcer, more expensive, and often worse for wheelchair users themselves.
[C1.2] Parity sounds simple until you price the fleet needed to deliver it. A standard ride-hail car can be any sedan already on the road; a wheelchair-accessible vehicle is a converted van costing far more to buy, fuel, insure, and maintain, with a lift, securements, and longer loading on every trip. Demand at any given corner at any given minute is sparse and unpredictable. To guarantee a six-minute pickup everywhere a standard car can, an operator must keep many expensive vans circulating empty, waiting for a call that may not come.
[C1.3] Public agencies pay for that idling with route cuts and fare hikes; private platforms pay by raising prices or leaving smaller cities entirely. Either way, total mobility shrinks.
[C1.4] That shrinkage hits disabled riders first. An agency forced to chase minute-by-minute parity downtown will have less to run reliable evening, weekend, and suburban paratransit — the trips to dialysis, work, and home that matter most. A ride-hail company that cannot profitably staff a parallel accessible fleet will cap trips, delay expansion, or exit, leaving a formally equal right with no car behind it. Mandated sameness produces compliance on paper and abandonment in practice.
[C1.5] Equal participation does not require identical wait times; it requires dependable, dignified access that can actually be sustained. That is better delivered by funded dedicated fleets, trip subsidies, vouchers usable across providers, and enforceable reliability standards — guaranteed pickup windows, no next-day-only booking — tailored to density and demand. Those tools expand real trips. This motion instead locks operators into the costliest possible metric, sacrificing more access for more people to satisfy it.

[C2.2] The curb-cut analogy in [P2.2] fails on the distinction that matters. A curb cut is built once, then serves everyone at near-zero marginal cost. Wait-time parity is a recurring operating expense: every minute shaved off the wait must be bought again, all day, with a driver, fuel, and a specialized van. Captioning scales with software; dispatch cannot scale past the location of the nearest available vehicle.
[C2.3] Algorithms cannot repeal density. With low, dispersed demand, an operator faces a trilemma: keep vans idle nearby, pool riders and make trips longer and pickups less predictable, or send the nearest van farther and let the wait rise. Better routing in [P2.3] only chooses which cost to impose; it does not eliminate the cost. Pooling can reproduce the indignity PRO condemns, turning a direct ride to work into a shared circuit while a sedan goes straight there.
[C2.4] When PRO says “close” in [P2.4] is merely a floor, not parity, it creates a dilemma. If it means a dependable absolute standard — arrive within a promised window, without next-day-only booking — that is our alternative, not this motion. If it means tracking standard service, the benchmark moves with sedan supply: minutes downtown, longer in suburbs, worse in bad weather. That moving target is unrelated to what a dialysis or shift-work trip actually needs, yet it dictates spending.
[C2.5] A guaranteed trip riders can plan a job around beats a relative benchmark that consumes the fleet budget chasing sedans.
