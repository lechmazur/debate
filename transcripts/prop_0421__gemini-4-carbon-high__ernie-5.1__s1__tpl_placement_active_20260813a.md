[P1.1] A mandatory fee is not an add-on; it is part of the price. When a concert ticket is listed at $60, a delivery meal at $15, or a restaurant entrée at $25, withholding an unavoidable service charge until payment serves one purpose: making the purchase look cheaper while the customer decides what to buy.
[P1.2] Revealing mandatory charges only at payment systematically distorts consumer choice. On ticketing sites and delivery apps, buyers spend time comparing options, customizing orders, coordinating seats with friends, and racing checkout timers. By the final payment screen, sunk effort and psychological commitment push people to accept inflated totals they would have rejected upfront. In dine-in restaurants, payment-only disclosure is even worse: once the food is eaten, the diner cannot walk away or order differently.
[P1.3] Showing the true cost only at payment confuses an ambush with informed consent.
[P1.4] Late fee disclosure also rigs competition against honest businesses. Suppose one seller advertises an honest $30 price while a rival advertises $24 and springs a mandatory $7 service fee at payment. Shoppers sorting by price or scanning menus gravitate toward the $24 listing, rewarding the more expensive merchant for hiding the truth. That dynamic punishes transparent restaurants and platforms, pressuring honest competitors to split their prices into deceptive late-stage fees just to compete for clicks and foot traffic.
[P1.5] Finally, earlier disclosure creates no operational barrier. This rule covers only mandatory service charges, not optional tips or voluntary upgrades, and it never bans itemization. Restaurants can still note on menus that a surcharge funds kitchen wages; apps can still explain how fees support venues or couriers. Even where delivery fees vary by distance or basket size, apps collect addresses upfront and can show fee terms and live running totals while customers browse and build a cart.
[P1.6] Because an unavoidable charge is part of the price, hiding it until payment protects neither transparency nor efficiency—only deception.

[C1.1] Mandatory service charges are not a single, static number that can be pinned to a menu or listing before a customer makes a decision; they are often calculated from variables that only become clear at the moment of purchase. Forcing platforms to display these fees upfront therefore creates a false sense of precision and, paradoxically, more deception than the status quo.
[C1.2] Consider delivery apps. The surcharge that covers courier pay, distance, and traffic surges is computed after the address is entered and the basket is finalized. If the app must show a single figure before checkout, it either guesses—risking a mismatch when the final fee appears—or displays a range that overwhelms the shopper. In practice, customers abandon carts when the quoted fee differs from the final charge, generating disputes and higher churn for small restaurants that cannot absorb the friction.
[C1.3] Ticketing services face a similar problem. Service fees fund venue maintenance, security, and processing costs that scale with seat location, event type, and purchase volume. Showing a flat “service charge” before the buyer selects seats misleads because the actual cost varies with the chosen tickets. An itemized breakdown at payment, paired with a brief explanation of what the fee covers, gives the buyer the context needed to evaluate the total.
[C1.4] Restaurants already juggle base‑price competition with tip culture, allergy labels, and seasonal specials. Adding a mandatory‑fee line to every menu item inflates visual clutter and pushes establishments to either hide the charge in higher base prices or to post separate notices that most diners ignore. The net effect is a higher headline price that hurts price‑sensitive customers without improving transparency.
[C1.5] Finally, the rule imposes compliance costs on every small business and platform while offering little consumer benefit, because existing regulations already require that the final, itemized receipt disclose every mandatory charge. The real issue is clarity at the point of payment, not premature disclosure that can be inaccurate and distracting. The proposition therefore creates more confusion than it resolves and should be rejected.
