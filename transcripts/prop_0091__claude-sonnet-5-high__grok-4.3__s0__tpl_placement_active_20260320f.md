[P1.1] When you buy a car, you buy the metal, the wiring, the sensors, and the chips bolted into it. That purchase should mean something. The proposition is simple: if the hardware is already sitting in the vehicle, physically capable of doing the job, the owner should not have to pay again and again just to unlock what they already own.
[P1.2] Start with the clearest case: heated seats. BMW tried gating heated seats behind a monthly fee, even though the heating element, the wiring, and the control unit were already installed at the factory. The marginal cost of "delivering" that feature after the sale is close to zero. There's no ongoing service, no server load, no consumable being replenished.
[P1.3] The subscription isn't paying for anything real — it's a toll booth placed on a road the owner already paved and paid for.
[P1.4] That distinction matters and should anchor this whole debate. Software that requires continuous provision — live traffic data, cloud navigation updates, connected security monitoring — has an ongoing cost, and reasonable subscription models can be defended there. But locked hardware is different in kind. The company already manufactured, shipped, and installed the part.
[P1.5] Charging recurring fees to "activate" it is not a service, it's a rent-seeking toggle switch on physical property the customer paid for at purchase.
[P1.6] Think about what this does to ownership itself. A car is a five, ten, fifteen year asset. If manufacturers can retroactively decide which already-installed features you get to use based on your willingness to keep paying, they've converted a purchase into a lease in disguise — without giving you the lower upfront price a lease would come with. You paid full hardware cost, but you don't get full hardware access.
[P1.7] That's the core injustice: paying twice for one thing.
[P1.8] This isn't anti-innovation or anti-subscription. It's anti-fake-scarcity. Software subscriptions should fund real, ongoing services. They should not be used to manufacture recurring revenue out of parts the owner already bought and installed in their own car.

[C1.1] The claim that hardware already installed creates an automatic right to permanent free access ignores how vehicles are actually designed and financed. Manufacturers include advanced components like seat heaters, adaptive suspension modules, or enhanced sensors only because they can recover part of the development and certification costs through optional activations over time. Without that path, the parts either disappear from lower trims or the entire vehicle price rises to cover the expense immediately.
[C1.2] This changes buyer economics in a concrete way. A customer who rarely uses heated seats pays less at purchase when the feature remains optional via subscription. Forcing permanent inclusion for every unit raises the base price for everyone, including those who would have preferred a cheaper car without it. The hardware exists conditionally on a viable revenue model, not as an unconditional gift of ownership.
[C1.3] Subscriptions also sustain updates that physical ownership alone cannot guarantee. Security patches, performance refinements, and compatibility fixes for new regulations require continued engineering effort long after the car leaves the factory. Treating installed hardware as permanently unlocked removes the funding mechanism for that work, leaving vehicles to degrade in capability while the owner still bears full replacement cost when problems accumulate.
[C1.4] The deeper issue is that the proposition converts a purchase into an open-ended claim on future company resources. A car buyer acquires a specific vehicle, not a perpetual right to every software toggle a manufacturer might later develop or withhold. Allowing activation fees preserves the flexibility to differentiate products, control quality, and price features according to actual demand rather than mandating one-size-fits-all inclusion at the point of sale.
