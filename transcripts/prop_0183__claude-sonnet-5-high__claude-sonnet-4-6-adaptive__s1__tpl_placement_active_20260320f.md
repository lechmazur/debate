[P1.1] Copyright exists to serve one purpose: to give creators enough incentive to keep creating. It was never meant to be a tool for erasing the past. Yet that is exactly what strict enforcement against nonprofit digital libraries does. When a game studio dissolves, when a publisher lets a book go out of print, when a website vanishes because a hosting bill wasn't paid, the copyright holder isn't losing income they would otherwise have earned — they're often gone, indifferent, or unreachable.
[P1.2] What's lost isn't a sale. What's lost is the work itself, permanently.
[P1.3] This is the core asymmetry that should decide this debate: traditional enforcement assumes every unauthorized copy is a market substitute causing real economic harm. That assumption collapses when the copy is held by a nonprofit archive with no commercial exploitation, serving preservation and research rather than competing in any marketplace. The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine has preserved hundreds of billions of web pages — journalism, government records, early internet culture — that no publisher was ever going to "sell" again because there was no commercial afterlife to protect.
[P1.4] Enforcing copyright there doesn't defend an author's livelihood; it just guarantees that history disappears when a server goes dark.
[P1.5] Digital decay is not hypothetical and not slow. Software rots when license servers shut off. Formats become unreadable in a decade without conversion. Orphan works — where no living rights-holder can even be found to ask permission — make "get a license first" a practical impossibility, not a principle.
[P1.6] Traditional enforcement treats every one of these cases identically to commercial piracy, when the actual harm profile and the actual social value are opposite.
[P1.7] A narrow, mission-limited exemption for nonprofit digital libraries — no commercial use, no substitution for the current market, preservation-first — costs rightsholders little because in the overwhelming number of these cases there is no functioning market left to protect. What it buys is exactly the sort of cultural continuity copyright itself claims to serve.
