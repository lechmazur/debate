[P1.1] Buy-now-pay-later has exploded in popularity, but its convenience masks a stark truth: it is debt. Consumers receive goods today in exchange for a promise to pay later, the core of any credit arrangement. Yet BNPL providers systematically avoid the rules that protect credit card users: there are no universal affordability checks, no standardized APR-equivalent disclosures for late fees, and weaker chargeback rights. This regulatory gap leaves vulnerable consumers—disproportionately younger and lower-income—exposed to punitive late charges, cascading penalty fees, and an opaque web of concurrent obligations that can spiral beyond control.
[P1.2] With usage soaring among consumers already stretched thin, the lack of protection becomes a systemic risk.
[P1.3] The checkout-feature analogy crumbles under scrutiny. A debit card transaction is immediate and final; BNPL creates a multi-period financial commitment laced with hidden tripwires. Credit cards must display interest rates, minimum payments, and the cost of carrying a balance, but BNPL providers can advertise ‘zero interest’ while concealing that missed payments trigger fees that, annualized, can exceed triple-digit APRs. For example, a single missed payment can trigger a fee, and if the user misses again, the fees compound, turning a small purchase into a debt trap.
[P1.4] Empirical patterns are clear: BNPL users are significantly more likely to miss payments, incur bank overdrafts, and juggle multiple simultaneous BNPL loans than credit card users, yet they lack equivalent legal safeguards.
[P1.5] Regulating BNPL like credit cards is not merely fair—it is necessary. Mandatory upfront cost disclosure, capped late fees, robust credit reporting, and standardized dispute resolution would align protections with actual risk. Without this, BNPL remains a regulatory arbitrage: the risks of credit repackaged as a frictionless checkout click, with the default burden falling on the least able to bear it. The label on the button does not change the economic reality: if it functions as credit, it must be regulated as credit.

[C1.2] Start with what credit card regulation is actually built for: revolving balances that charge compound interest indefinitely. The signature harm of a credit card is the person who pays the minimum for seven years and pays double the sticker price in interest. The classic BNPL product is the opposite—zero interest, a fixed short schedule, four payments and you're done. You cannot "revolve" a pay-in-four plan.
[C1.3] Importing a regime designed for open-ended interest-bearing debt onto a closed, interest-free, short-term installment is regulating the wrong disease.
[C1.4] Now the decisive move PRO never confronts: regulation has alternatives. If you load BNPL with full credit-card compliance—universal hard credit checks, APR machinery, mandatory bureau reporting—you don't make the debt disappear. You make the interest-free option uneconomic, and the consumer reaches instead for the actual credit card sitting in their wallet at twenty-plus percent revolving APR. PRO's own opening calls BNPL "debt repackaged."
[C1.5] Fine—but the substitute is more dangerous debt, not no debt. Crushing the cheaper instrument to drive people toward the costlier one is a perverse win.
[C1.6] Consider mandatory credit reporting, which PRO explicitly wants. A 22-year-old splitting a $90 purchase into four payments now risks a credit-score ding that follows them into a mortgage application. That's not protection; that's manufacturing harm for trivial transactions.
[C1.7] I am not arguing for a lawless free-for-all. Targeted, proportionate rules—clear upfront disclosure of late fees, late-fee caps, fair dispute handling—are sensible and already arriving. But "proportionate" is precisely what the credit-card frame is not. The right question isn't "is it credit?"
[C1.8] It's "what regulation fits this product's actual risk?" The credit-card answer is oversized, and oversized regulation here pushes consumers toward worse outcomes. That's why CON wins.
