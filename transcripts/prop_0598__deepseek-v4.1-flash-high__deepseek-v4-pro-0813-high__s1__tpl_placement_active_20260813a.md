[P1.1] Irregular migration is not a mystery. It is what happens when the demand for labor is real and the legal supply of workers is closed. Gulf states run on migrant labor—construction, care, hospitality, logistics—yet many workers arrive through brokers, overstay, or change jobs illegally because the legal system is rigid, expensive, and employer-tied. North African states are both transit and destination, with informal construction, agriculture, and domestic work.
[P1.2] Mass deportation attacks the visible person while leaving that demand untouched. It is a revolving door: remove one worker, the job remains, and another migrant pays a smuggler to fill it. The result is predictable: visa overstays, informal intermediaries, debt bondage, and repeated cycles of detention and re-entry.
[P1.3] Legal labor corridors change the equation at the source. Bilateral or regional agreements can match workers to verified employers before departure, with standard contracts, wage protection, portable residency, and sectoral quotas. That shrinks the smuggler's market because the product—legal access to work—becomes available at lower cost and lower risk. It also gives states data: who is entering, for what job, for how long, and with what rights.
[P1.4] Enforcement then targets abusive recruiters and criminal networks, not entire nationalities.
[P1.5] Prioritizing corridors does not mean open borders or amnesty. It means sequencing: build legal routes first, then return those with no claim, using readmission agreements and reintegration support. Deportation-only systems are expensive, generate abuses, and push workers further underground, making exploitation harder to detect. Corridors make migration governable.
[P1.6] If a Gulf state needs ten thousand seasonal hospitality or farm workers, a corridor can bring them lawfully, register them, insure them, and rotate them home. A deportation campaign can only chase the ones who already came.
[P1.7] The choice is not perfect control versus chaos. It is a strategy that reduces irregularity by draining its labor-market fuel, versus mass deportation that removes people and leaves the pump running. Vote PRO.

[C1.1] The motion’s core error is treating irregular migration as merely unmet labor demand. In the Gulf and North Africa, irregular flows include visa overstayers, unregistered border crossers, asylum-seekers, trafficked people, and families—not just workers who could be neatly placed in a construction or farm corridor. A legal labor corridor addresses only the easiest slice. Making it the priority leaves most irregular migration untouched while consuming scarce administrative capacity.
[C1.2] Corridors cannot discipline migration without credible removal. The region already has large temporary-worker programs, and irregularity persists because sponsorship rules are gamed, documents are weak, and overstaying often carries no real cost. Add new corridors without enforcement and the same brokers, sponsors, and employers will arbitrage them: quotas are sold, contracts ignored, workers leave the corridor job, and the smuggling market continues. A corridor is not a substitute for deterrence; it is an invitation unless the state first shows that illegal entry and overstay end in removal.
[C1.3] The sequencing is backwards. The PRO position says build legal routes first, then return those with no claim. But return is the harder, more foundational task. If a state cannot credibly remove people, it cannot enforce the wage, stay, and return conditions attached to any corridor.
[C1.4] Mass deportation is not mere “chasing”; it sets the rule that makes legal channels meaningful rather than an amnesty in disguise.
[C1.5] Finally, capacity matters. Many North African states lack the labor inspection, border data, and readmission systems that managed corridors require. Prioritizing corridor design over removals diverts those resources while enforcement backlogs grow. Control presence first, then open the new door.
