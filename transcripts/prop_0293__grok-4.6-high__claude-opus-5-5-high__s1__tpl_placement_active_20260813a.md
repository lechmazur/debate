[P1.1] A statue on a public square is not a history book. It is a civic endorsement. It tells children walking to school and citizens approaching a courthouse: this is the kind of person we hold up as worthy of honor. When that person is primarily associated with slavery, colonial domination, or mass repression, the endorsement is a political choice, not a neutral archive.
[P1.2] History belongs in museums, classrooms, and archives, where context is the point. Honor belongs to those whose primary legacy we would actually recommend. Relocating a monument does not delete a life; it withdraws a public blessing. We already know the difference.
[P1.3] No serious city keeps a dictator in bronze outside city hall and then claims it is merely remembering the twentieth century. The bronze is doing work: it naturalizes power, sanitizes cruelty, and invites the living to identify with the perpetrator rather than the people he ruled.
[P1.4] These monuments often entered civic space as weapons, not as scholarship. Confederate statues rose with Jim Crow, not Appomattox. Imperial heroes were planted to make domination look like destiny. Keeping them in places of honor continues that work for generations who never consented to it.
[P1.5] A democracy of equal citizens cannot treat the public square as a private club of old victors. If residents must pass daily under the gaze of the man who enslaved their ancestors or crushed their country, the state is not preserving history. It is ranking whose dignity counts. Equal citizenship is not a mood.
[P1.6] It is incompatible with state-sponsored veneration of people whose power was built on bondage, conquest, or terror.
[P1.7] Leaving them up with a plaque keeps the honor architecture intact and demotes the harm to a caption. Captions do not cancel pedestals. Pedestals are the argument. Move the statues to museums or contextualized sites, and the record remains.
[P1.8] What ends is the lie that these men are still our civic models.

[C1.2] First, the rule cannot be applied honestly. "Primarily associated with" sounds precise, but it is the whole fight. Is Churchill primarily the man who stood against Hitler, or the man who defended empire in India and presided over brutal repression in Kenya? Is Washington primarily the founder, or an enslaver?
[C1.3] Ask different citizens and you get different answers. PRO's motion does not settle that dispute; it hands it to whoever controls the council this year and calls the result principle. Regimes have always toppled rivals' monuments with righteous language. A standing mandate to remove makes the square hostage to shifting majorities rather than a place where a nation argues with its past.
[C1.4] Second, removal hides the evidence at the scene of the crime. PRO says history belongs in museums [P1.2]. But most people rarely visit museums, and those who do chose to. The square is where the unwilling and uncurious actually meet history.
[C1.5] A slave trader's statue beside the port he enriched, answered by a memorial to the people he shipped, teaches more than a crate in a warehouse. PRO says "captions do not cancel pedestals" [P1.7]. Fine: then change more than the caption. Add counter-monuments, alter the site, place a new figure in dialogue with the old one.
[C1.6] When Richmond commissioned a Black rider in the heroic Confederate style, it turned the genre into an answer. That is transformation, not endorsement.
[C1.7] Third, removal is cheap absolution. Cities can crate a bronze, declare the past handled, and change nothing about housing, schools, or wealth gaps that slavery and colonialism actually left behind. A statue that stays, reframed, keeps the debt visible.
[C1.8] PRO offers a clean square. I offer an honest one: keep the evidence where it happened, and make it confess.
