[P1.1] International shipping accounts for roughly 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions, a share on track to grow rapidly as global trade expands, even as power generation, road transport, and buildings cut their carbon footprints. Left unregulated, shipping could single-handedly consume a large share of the remaining global carbon budget for 1.5C of warming, erasing hard-won progress made in every other sector. Decades of voluntary industry pledges and loose, non-binding IMO targets have failed to shift behavior: ship operators consistently opt for the cheapest available high-sulfur, high-carbon bunker fuel, register vessels under flags of convenience to avoid patchwork national rules, and delay fleet retrofits because no single country can enforce standards for craft that cross dozens of jurisdictions on a single voyage.
[P1.2] A binding, IMO-set timetable for low- and zero-carbon fuel adoption fixes that free-rider problem by creating a single, global level playing field, where no operator can undercut competitors by clinging to dirty fuels. The clear, enforceable deadline also gives fuel producers and shipbuilders the long-term certainty needed to scale green methanol, ammonia, and wind-assisted propulsion technologies, driving down their costs over time just as binding clean energy rules helped slash solar panel costs by roughly 90% in two decades. Critics will point to higher freight costs, but those costs are trivial against the stakes: for most finished traded goods, shipping makes up a tiny fraction of final consumer price, meaning even a meaningful jump in bunker costs adds mere pennies to the cost of electronics, clothing, or packaged food.
[P1.3] Those small, predictable costs are vastly preferable to the unpriced, catastrophic costs of unmitigated warming: flooded coastal ports that shut down trade entirely, more intense storm systems that disrupt voyages, and crop failures driven by emissions that fall heaviest on low-income communities that barely participate in global freight markets.

[C1.1] The IMO cannot decree fuels into existence, and a binding timetable will not make ammonia or green methanol safe, scalable, or available in the ports where ships actually need them. Today those fuels are produced in tiny quantities, cost multiples of conventional bunker fuel, require new engines, storage, and bunkering infrastructure, and ammonia is acutely toxic to crews. Mandating them before that system exists does not accelerate innovation; it creates scarcity, lets few suppliers charge monopoly rents, and forces owners to scrap workable vessels early or pay for fuel that is not there.
[C1.2] That cost is not 'pennies' for the trade that matters most. Finished electronics can absorb freight, but bulk shipping cannot. Grain, fertilizer, iron ore, coal substitutes, and fuel itself move on razor-thin margins where freight is a large share of delivered price. Raise it sharply and you raise bread prices in import-dependent countries in Africa and the Middle East, raise construction costs, and push poor states toward cheaper, dirtier overland or short-sea alternatives.
[C1.3] The proposition accepts that harm, then outsources the decision to the IMO, a consensus body dominated by flag states and industry lobbying, with weak enforcement against flags of convenience. A rule it cannot police uniformly will reward paperwork compliance, biofuel accounting tricks, and reflagging, not real cuts.
[C1.4] Finally, a rigid fuel mandate is worse climate policy than a flexible emissions limit. It locks in today's favored fuels, discourages slow steaming, hull efficiency, wind assistance, and operational savings that cut emissions now, and diverts capital from retrofits that work with existing fleets. Hold firms to shrinking carbon intensity over time, with freedom in how they meet it, under national enforcement that can actually inspect, fine, and detain ships. A binding IMO fuel timetable gets the sequence backwards: punishment first, infrastructure later, regressivity throughout.

Nor does predictability make regressivity fair: scheduled grain and fertilizer freight rises are paid now by import-dependent buyers, with no compensation in this motion, for diffuse benefits decades away achievable more cheaply without raising food prices this year.
