[P1.1] Long-term care is not a private preference. It is a catastrophic risk of living long: years of help with bathing, dressing, eating, or dementia. A large share of people will need it. Private savings cannot price that risk.
[P1.2] Families cannot absorb it without wrecking other lives. Means-testing waits until people are already ruined. Governments should therefore build universal or near-universal long-term care systems.
[P1.3] Private markets fail for familiar reasons. People underestimate late-life disability. Healthy buyers drop out, premiums rise, and remaining policies stay thin, expensive, and exclusion-heavy. That is why private long-term care insurance never became a mass product, and why most households still face unpaid family labor or a financial cliff.
[P1.4] Leaving the residue to families is not freedom. It is a hidden tax on daughters, daughters-in-law, and partners, often forcing them out of paid work. Aging makes that tax heavier: fewer children, later births, dual-earner households, longer widowhood. Informal care produces caregiver poverty and injury while delivering uneven quality to the old.
[P1.5] Means-testing is the worst hybrid. It punishes savers, encourages spend-down, and still leaves the middle class exposed until they qualify by becoming poor. Money goes to eligibility policing instead of aides and home support.
[P1.6] A universal or near-universal system treats care like a pension complement: contributions in working life, a claim when need is certified. Risk is pooled. Families stay involved without being residual insurers. Women's employment is protected.
[P1.7] Hidden private costs become an honest public budget. Countries that took this path did not abolish family; they stopped treating unpaid women as the default care plan.
[P1.8] The alternative is a lottery of lucky assets, lucky relatives, and lucky health. Societies rejected that lottery for old-age income. They should reject it for old-age care.
