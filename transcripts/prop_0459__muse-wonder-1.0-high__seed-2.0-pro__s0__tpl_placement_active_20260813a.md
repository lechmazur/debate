[P1.1] Cheap clean technology that you cannot obtain in a crisis is not cheap, and it is not secure. Today the production of solar wafers, battery cathodes, rare-earth magnets and much of the processing behind them is concentrated in a handful of places, overwhelmingly China. That concentration was not a natural market outcome; it was built by years of foreign subsidies, cheap finance and guaranteed demand. Pretending to answer it with hands-off trade is not neutrality — it is unilateral disarmament.
[P1.2] Countries should therefore pay a deliberate, limited premium to localize the critical nodes: mining is not enough if refining, components and final assembly remain abroad. Subsidies bring factories home; public procurement gives them a predictable buyer long enough to scale, learn and cut costs. This is insurance, and like the lesson Europe learned from dependence on Russian gas, insurance looks expensive only until supply becomes a weapon, factories shutter, or a shipping shock turns an energy transition into an energy emergency.
[P1.3] The economics point the same way. Clean-tech manufacturing has steep learning curves, thick spillovers to workers, suppliers and grid innovation, and private investors who underbuild because they cannot capture those benefits or survive a price war against a state-backed rival. Targeted support corrects that failure. Costs start higher, then fall with volume — as they did for solar and batteries when public support created markets that private capital would not.
[P1.4] Domestic production also makes decarbonization politically survivable. Workers and regions that see factories, not just imported panels installed by someone else, defend the transition at the ballot box. That durability is worth more than shaving a few percent off today's import bill, only to lose the whole climate programme to backlash and delay.
[P1.5] Trade friction is real, but dependence is worse. A world where several trusted regions can each make essential batteries, turbines and electrolyzers is more stable, more negotiable, and ultimately greener than a world bidding for permission from one dominant supplier.
