[P1.2] Clinical indication already narrows who qualifies: documented obesity, related disease, and unsuccessful first-line measures. This is not recreational coverage.
[P1.3] Cash-pay patients get the drugs. An insured patient with a qualifying BMI and prediabetes still gets a denial. The complications land on the same plan: advancing diabetes, heart failure, sleep apnea, joint failure. Coverage already pays for the sequela.
[P1.4] The question is whether it will also pay for indicated treatment that can reduce some of that harm.
[P1.5] This is not a blank check. The requirement pairs coverage when clinically indicated with value-based pricing or outcomes-based contracts. Manufacturers get a covered population. Insurers get rebates, refunds, or lower net prices if agreed results—weight, glycemic control, cardiovascular events—do not materialize.
[P1.6] List-price free-for-all is not the policy. Payment tied to benefit is.
[P1.7] We already cover other high-cost chronic therapies: insulin, antiretrovirals, hepatitis C antivirals, rheumatology biologics. We did not make "try harder" a permanent substitute for indicated drugs. Obesity should not be carved out because the disease is visible or culturally blamed.
[P1.8] Optional coverage distorts care. Clinicians delay. Patients cycle through weaker options. Healthier employer groups keep access; sicker and lower-income groups wait.
[P1.9] A mandate with outcomes contracts stops that selection and prices the drug against what it actually delivers.
[P1.10] The clash is whether indicated treatment for a high-cost chronic disease belongs in the benefit, with spending tied to results—or whether insurers may exclude it while still billing for later complications. One treats obesity as medicine. The other pretends it is not.
