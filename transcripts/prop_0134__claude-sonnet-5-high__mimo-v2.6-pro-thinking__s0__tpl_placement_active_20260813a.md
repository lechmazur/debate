[P1.1] Start with the mechanism, because it decides everything else. In a high-demand neighborhood, housing units are a fixed, scarce stock. Every unit converted from a long-term rental into a short-term rental (an Airbnb, a "boutique" unlisted apartment) is a unit pulled out of the pool that residents compete for. When landlords can earn a nightly tourist rate instead of a monthly resident rate, they rationally exit the long-term market.
[P1.2] That is not a hypothetical; it's the basic arbitrage that made short-term rental platforms attractive to investors in the first place. Restricting that arbitrage pushes those units back into the resident pool, increasing supply exactly where demand is highest, which lowers rents and slows displacement.
[P1.3] The scale of the effect tracks the scale of the distortion. In dense, tourist-heavy neighborhoods, short-term rentals aren't a marginal sliver of housing stock, they can be a meaningful double-digit percentage of units in the most affected blocks. When a regulation removes even a fraction of those units from the tourist market, the resident-facing supply increase is concentrated precisely in the neighborhoods where every additional unit matters most for rent-setting. Displacement isn't just about price, it's about the destabilizing churn of long-term tenants losing renewal leases because owners find nightly guests more profitable.
[P1.4] Restricting short-term rentals directly removes that incentive to churn people out.
[P1.5] Weigh this against the harms CON must lean on: reduced income for hosts and reduced visitor spending. Those costs are real but diffuse and substitutable. A displaced host can rent long-term instead, still earning income, just less. A tourist who can't find an Airbnb books a hotel instead, spending in the same neighborhood, often at the same restaurants and shops, just channeled through a different lodging type.
[P1.6] Displacement of a resident, by contrast, is not substitutable, it means lost schools, lost community ties, and often a forced move to a cheaper, farther neighborhood with worse job access. The asymmetry between a shifted spending channel and a broken housing lease is the core of this debate, and it favors restriction decisively.
