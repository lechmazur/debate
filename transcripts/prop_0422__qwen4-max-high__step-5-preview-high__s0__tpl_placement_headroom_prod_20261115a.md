[P1.1] Every ticket carries an unstated promise: that a small child will be supervised by the adult travelling with them. A three-year-old cannot buckle her own belt, find an oxygen mask, or follow an evacuation command through smoke. So when a carrier puts a mother in row 9 and her toddler in row 26, it hasn't sold two seats. It has manufactured a hazard and put a price on removing it.
[P1.2] That is why the usual market answer — if you value sitting together, pay for it — collapses here. This isn't a preference like a window or a hot meal. No parent can decline it, and no parent can comparison-shop once the booking is half-completed and the seat map is already selling. Charging for adjacency is charging for something the customer has no ability to refuse.
[P1.3] Notice how narrow the obligation is. Nothing here bans extra-legroom pricing, business class, or preferred-location upgrades. Carriers keep every product they sell today; they simply may not sell the separation itself. Operationally this is solved by holding a block of standard rows out of paid selection and assigning families at booking — rail reservation systems already seat a shared booking together as default.
[P1.4] Open-seating airlines manage it without charging anyone.
[P1.5] And the cost of separation is already being paid — just off the books. Gate agents reseat families, pushback slips, strangers are conscripted into supervising someone else's frightened child. The fee doesn't eliminate that cost; it transfers it onto crews and other passengers while the airline keeps the revenue.
[P1.6] So the opposing case must name a number: what price makes it acceptable to leave a four-year-old among strangers? If no such price exists, the fee was never a product. It was a penalty for travelling with a child.

Guaranteeing row adjacency is not a safety regulation; it is a convenience preference dressed as moral necessity.
[C1.2] The market answer does not collapse. Unlike an evacuation or a life vest, adjacency is a discrete service with real inventory costs. Holding blocks of standard rows, as PRO proposes [P1.3], is not free. It strands seats that could otherwise be sold to solo business travellers or connecting passengers.
[C1.3] On a 180-seat aircraft, protecting clusters for families means fewer seats available to maximize load factor. That lost yield does not vanish; it reappears in higher base fares for everyone. PRO may imagine a narrow fee ban, but the revenue must come from somewhere.
[C1.4] Rail is not the model here. Rail operators run deterministic schedules and fixed seat maps on owned track; airlines face weather cascades, aircraft swaps, and constant re-optimization. A policy that works for a fixed rail inventory cannot simply be pasted onto dynamic air operations.
[C1.5] Nor is the fee a penalty for travelling with a child. Parents who book early, choose open-seating carriers, or accept a middle seat across the aisle already manage without paying. What PRO calls a refusal is really a scheduling choice: families who wait until late in the booking window pay for the privilege of guaranteed adjacency that early planners secured for free. Open seating is not a universal fallback but a specific business model.
[C1.6] That is not a penalty. It is the ordinary price of scarcity.
