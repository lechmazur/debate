[P1.1] Climate change is not an ordinary policy file. For low-lying atoll states, failure is not lost GDP; it is loss of habitable territory. Maldives, Tuvalu, and Kiribati face sea-level rise, saline intrusion, and stronger storms that can make a homeland uninhabitable. Yet voting power in climate negotiations is calibrated to population or GDP, as if a state with a minuscule fraction of another’s population has a minuscule fraction of a national survival interest.
[P1.2] That is the flaw PRO corrects. Population and GDP measure administrative footprint and market size. They do not measure exposure. Sea-level rise does not spare a country because its economy is small; storm surges do not discount themselves by GDP.
[P1.3] When a decision determines whether a sovereign state has land on which to exercise sovereignty, its interest in binding mitigation and adequate adaptation finance is not marginal.
[P1.4] Risk-weighting is not hand-waving. It can be grounded in physical exposure: land below storm-surge levels, dependence on climate-stressed freshwater and fisheries, and lack of territory to retreat inland. These are precisely the conditions that have made small island states among the strongest voices for 1.5°C ambition and real loss-and-damage support.
[P1.5] Weighted voting by existential risk is not charity; it is stake-sensitive democracy. Those who would bear catastrophic, non-compensable harm from a collective decision should have influence commensurate with that harm. Weighted voting is already familiar in international institutions; the real question is which principle the weights embody. PRO says the answer is the risk of national extinction, not tax base or demographic size.
[P1.6] Votes shape deadlines, finance, liability, and the boundary between adaptation and disappearance. A system that gives Tuvalu and Germany formally equal power despite radically unequal stakes is equal in procedure but unjust in substance. Vote weight should track what a bad climate decision can destroy.

[C1.1] Weighted voting by existential risk sounds compassionate, but it replaces one form of unfairness with a far worse one. Climate negotiations already rest on sovereign equality: every nation, large or small, gets a seat at the table. That principle is not a relic; it is the scaffold that holds a fragile consensus together. Giving Tuvalu a vote weight proportional to its risk of submergence would hand a handful of micro‑states disproportionate leverage over decisions that affect the emissions of billions.
[C1.2] The result is not justice but gridlock, or worse, the erosion of the very cooperation that climate action requires.
[C1.3] The biggest practical objection is measurement. Existential risk is not a single, observable number; it is a bundle of sea‑level projections, storm‑surge models, freshwater stress, and migration capacity—all riddled with uncertainty and open to politicised interpretation. Who decides the weight? A panel of climate scientists?
[C1.4] A political committee? Any answer invites accusations of bias and opens the door to strategic manipulation. By contrast, population and GDP are transparent, routinely verified, and hard to dispute.
[C1.5] Moral hazard is another danger. If a state’s bargaining power rises with its vulnerability, the incentive to invest in adaptation, relocation planning, or resilient infrastructure diminishes. Why build sea walls when a larger vote can compel richer nations to pay? This creates a perverse feedback loop that punishes preparedness.
[C1.6] Existing mechanisms already address disproportionate stakes without distorting voting. The UNFCCC grants small island states special negotiating status, dedicated funding streams, and a voice in loss‑and‑damage discussions. These tools can be strengthened. What they cannot replace is the institutional stability that comes from equal sovereign representation.
[C1.7] The proposition therefore trades a workable, transparent system for an opaque, destabilising one that threatens the collective action the climate crisis demands.

[P2.2] When the alternative to weighted voting is a system where physical disappearance of sovereign states can be carried by a majority indifferent to that outcome, the cooperation CON protects is already hollow.
[P2.3] C1.3 and C1.4 argue risk is too uncertain to weight. This is special pleading. Population and GDP are also politically constructed, routinely revised, and verified by partisan bodies; no one demands we abandon them because measurement is imperfect. Physical exposure is concrete: land elevation, storm-surge reach, freshwater stress, absence of inland retreat.
[P2.4] Risk-weighting is ordinary practice in insurance and financial regulation, where uncertain future harm is priced daily. Transparency is not an argument against risk weights; it is an argument against any metric that would inconvenience the status quo.
[P2.5] C1.5’s moral hazard is speculative. Vote weight does not remove the need for sea walls; it corrects the incentive structure that lets major emitters externalize costs. The true moral hazard is the current status quo: a rulebook that lets states with the largest carbon footprints retain the largest voting power, ensuring that those least exposed to harm decide how much harm is tolerable.
[P2.6] C1.6 confuses voice with power. Small-island caucuses, dedicated funds, and loss-and-damage talks are what a system provides when it wants to be seen listening but not obliged to act. C1.7 has it backwards: the destabilizing choice is not risk-weighting; it is pretending equal procedure delivers justice when the stakes are terminal.

Weighted voting does not guarantee survival; it jeopardises the collective action that makes survival possible.
[C2.2] [P2.1] and [P2.2] treat the status quo as morally bankrupt, but they ignore that the current system already gives small‑island states disproportionate moral influence through the Alliance of Small Island States and dedicated loss‑and‑damage funds. When a coalition of vulnerable nations can shape the agenda without breaking the one‑state‑one‑vote baseline, the “hollow cooperation” PRO decries does not exist.
[C2.3] On measurement, PRO dismisses uncertainty as “special pleading” [P2.3]. In reality, sea‑level projections, storm‑surge models and freshwater stress carry far larger confidence intervals than census data or GDP accounts, and they are already weaponised in geopolitical disputes. Assigning a numeric weight to existential risk would invite endless challenges, lobbying for favourable risk assessments, and a new arena for political manipulation that population or GDP never provokes.
[C2.4] [P2.5] calls the status‑quo a moral hazard, but reversing the incentive creates a larger one. If a state’s vote grows with its vulnerability, governments may postpone costly adaptation in favour of lobbying for higher weights, entrenching dependence rather than resilience. The existing framework—transparent, verifiable metrics, and targeted finance streams—already corrects the externality problem without destabilising the negotiating table.
[C2.5] [P2.6] equates voice with power, yet the very purpose of sovereign equality is to prevent power from being seized by the most desperate. Weighted voting would erode the trust that keeps the climate regime functional, and the islands would lose more than they gain.

[P4.4] Risk-weighting changes who must be persuaded, not the atmosphere by itself.

[P4.6] Sea level rewards neither market size nor population. Weighted voting makes Tuvalu’s stake count because Tuvalu itself is at stake.
