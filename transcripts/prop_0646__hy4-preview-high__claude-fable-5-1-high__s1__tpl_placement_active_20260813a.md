[P1.1] Societies are judged by how they treat people who cannot protect themselves. Elder-care residents are not shoppers who can walk away; they are often frail, cognitively impaired, and dependent on staff for eating, bathing, medication, and safety. When a private-equity fund buys the facility, the governing objective changes. PE does not buy nursing homes to hold them for thirty years; it buys with borrowed money on a three-to-seven-year clock and a duty to maximize investor returns.
[P1.2] Extraction, not care, becomes the operating imperative.
[P1.3] The mechanism is predictable. In a typical leveraged deal, the chain takes on debt and often sells its buildings to an affiliated landlord, then pays rent and cuts overhead. Labor is the largest controllable cost, so staffing is cut first: fewer aides, thinner supervision, more agency temps. The results are missed bed turns, untreated infections, falls, and avoidable hospitalizations.
[P1.4] Residents suffer, and public insurance pays. Profit is largely a transfer from fragile bodies and taxpayers to investors.
[P1.5] Markets discipline firms only when buyers can exit and information is clear. In elder care, they cannot. Families cannot verify overnight staffing, and moving a frail parent is dangerous. Picture a 120-bed facility post-buyout: management cuts aide shifts and defers roof, generator, and HVAC repairs.
[P1.6] A resident with limited mobility develops pressure sores; another wanders and is hurt; a storm knocks out power and the building cannot safely shelter patients. Saved dollars become human harm.
[P1.7] The question is not whether private capital can build buildings—it can—but who should control care for people who cannot defend their interests. Restriction need not mean expropriation or banning all investment. It can bar leveraged acquisitions, prohibit sale-leaseback rent extraction, and require resident-care obligations to outrank debt service. Ownership should fit the resident's time horizon—nonprofits, public bodies, mission-locked providers.
[P1.8] Because foreseeable harm to a captive population is the predictable output of PE ownership, governments should restrict private-equity ownership of elder-care facilities.
