[P1.1] Integration is not a favor a host society does for newcomers; it is the terms on which strangers become fellow citizens. The proposition does not demand cultural sameness. It demands a shared floor: commitment to core civic norms—rule of law, nonviolence, democratic resolution of conflict, equal rights regardless of sex or belief—and functional competence in the common public language. A shared public language does not erase mother tongues; it adds a second key.
[P1.2] Private identity, religion, cuisine, dress, and family language remain free. What cannot be optional is the public grammar of equal citizenship.
[P1.3] Optional integration sounds generous. In practice it is abandonment. If the common language is merely optional, newcomers can be parked in linguistic enclaves, shut out of decent jobs, parent-teacher meetings, local politics, and the wider public conversation. That is not multiculturalism; it is segregation with a kinder name.
[P1.4] If core civic norms are optional, the state has no principled answer when cultural practice is invoked to justify coercion, domestic abuse, or denying children an equal education. An expectation does not stigmatize immigrants; it gives institutions a clear basis to protect the vulnerable inside every community and to say that rights are not culturally negotiable.
[P1.5] Reciprocity matters: a society that expects integration must fund language provision, enforce anti-discrimination law, and open institutions to newcomers. But expectation and support are complements, not opposites. The real choice is between a shared civic floor and a hands-off model that leaves newcomers to sink or swim in parallel worlds. Diversity is compatible with unity; it is not compatible with making the very terms of public cooperation optional.
[P1.6] A country that treats integration as optional does not become more tolerant—it becomes more unequal, more distrustful, and less able to govern itself democratically.
