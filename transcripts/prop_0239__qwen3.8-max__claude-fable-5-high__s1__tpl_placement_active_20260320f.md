[P1.1] Consent is necessary, but it is not the whole of ethics. It tells us someone agreed; it does not tell us whether the agreement was free in any meaningful sense, whether the terms were decent, or whether the deal preys on weakness. If a starving person “agrees” to work fourteen-hour days in a locked factory for less than survival pay, the problem is not lack of a signature. The problem is that desperation has done the coercing.
[P1.2] Calling that ethical because it is consensual mistakes absence of protest for presence of justice.
[P1.3] The first reason is background conditions. Consent only carries moral weight when people have reasonable alternatives, sufficient information, and real power to walk away. In many transactions, those conditions are missing: a tenant facing eviction, a patient confused by fine print, a worker told the take-it-or-leave-it contract is standard, a borrower offered a loan they cannot understand under immediate financial threat. The weaker party may choose the deal, but choice under siege is not moral approval.
[P1.4] Second, consent cannot cure exploitation by itself. A person can consent to sell a kidney, to waive basic safety protections, or to accept a wage below what their labor is worth because their options are terrible. The ethical question is not only “did they agree?” but “is this a fair use of another person’s vulnerability?” If consent alone were enough, every predatory contract, every usurious loan, every dangerous job accepted under threat of hunger would become ethically clean the moment it was signed.
[P1.5] Finally, transactions affect third parties and social norms. A bribe, a contract to harm others, or a market in basic rights may be consensual among participants while still corrupting public trust. Ethics therefore looks beyond the parties’ yes to the structure, fairness, and consequences of the exchange. Consent matters.
[P1.6] It is not enough.

[P2.6] If the only standard is signature plus capacity, the strongest party gets to define freedom for the weakest.
[P2.7] Finally, CON says third-party harms are solved by extending consent to everyone affected [C1.3]. But public harms are not so easily consented to. A bribe, vote-buying, or sale of public office may satisfy both immediate parties while corrupting institutions everyone depends on.
