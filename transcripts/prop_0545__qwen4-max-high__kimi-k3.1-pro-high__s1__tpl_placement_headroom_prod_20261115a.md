[P1.1] The gap this motion closes is between what people want and what paperwork records. In opt-in countries, large majorities say they would donate; far fewer ever register. That gap is not a moral decision. It is a form nobody got around to.
[P1.2] Presumed consent stops treating procrastination as refusal, and that is where the measurable gain comes from.
[P1.3] The model to defend is the one actually implemented: soft presumed consent. Spain has sat near the top of world deceased-donation rates for decades on that legal foundation, paired with hospital coordinators and family conversations. Wales and England moved to deemed consent while keeping families in the room. Anyone can register a refusal, free and simply, and that refusal binds.
[P1.4] The change is what silence means, not whether choice counts.
[P1.5] That is why the ethics and distress objections collapse into each other. Autonomy is preserved by an easy, publicised exit, not by a bureaucratic hurdle most people never clear. And the anguish families report at the bedside is so often the anguish of guessing: would she have wanted this? A default gives them an answer they can lean on instead of a decision they must invent while grieving.
[P1.6] Ambiguity is the distress engine; presumed consent removes it.
[P1.7] So the burden is to show a net new harm, not a squeamishness about defaults. We already use defaults where inertia costs people dearly — automatic pension enrolment lifted participation among exactly the people who never signed up. Organs are the highest-stakes case for the same insight, and the exit is easier than any pension opt-out. The gain is measured in transplants performed, and the mechanism is fewer families left without guidance.
[P1.8] The baseline is not universal deliberation. It is usable kidneys buried because a healthy twenty-year-old never filed a card.

[C1.1] The motion makes a causal promise and a harm-free promise. A change in the legal meaning of silence can deliver neither by itself. A default is not a transplant system.
[C1.2] Presumed consent operates only after a possible donor has already been identified, referred, and kept medically suitable. It does not create viable organs, intensive-care capacity, retrieval teams, coordinators, or transplant capacity. Those constraints can leave transplant rates almost unchanged even when the legal pool on paper expands. Spain’s high performance therefore does not isolate the effect of its default: its coordinated donor-detection and hospital systems are plausible explanations for why legal form alone is not the lesson.
[C1.3] The family safeguard creates a dilemma. If clinicians still defer to distressed relatives, the same bedside barrier remains, now with relatives asked to accept a decision the patient never actually expressed. If clinicians override them, PRO buys additional donations by risking precisely the violations and distress the motion excludes. A legal default tells a family what the state will permit; it does not tell them what their mother believed. “She never opted out” is compatible with “she never knew.”
[C1.4] That is why the easy-exit defence fails. Simple registration is not universal awareness, comprehension, or access. People with limited literacy, unstable addresses, language barriers, or little contact with public messaging are least likely to turn silence into a considered choice. Their organs cannot ethically be treated as available merely because bureaucracy is easier to navigate for others.
[C1.5] Explicit registration supplies clearer evidence of wishes and better guidance for families; investment in referral, coordination, and consent conversations addresses actual bottlenecks without converting ignorance into consent. PRO must show both additional transplants and no additional harm. The soft model weakens the gain; the hard model breaks the safeguard.
