[P1.1] Tying basic medical coverage to employment is a structural contradiction: it links access to healthcare to your ability to work, even though serious illness and economic downturns routinely destroy your ability to work. When a business closes or a severe diagnosis forces someone off the clock, an employer-based system pulls coverage away right when it is needed most. Single-payer financing fixes this at the root by making basic coverage continuous infrastructure rather than a fragile workplace perk.
[P1.2] First, single-payer guarantees unbroken continuity of care. Under employer-based systems, normal life transitions trigger medical disruptions. Changing jobs, shifting to part-time or gig work, or getting laid off means losing your plan, resetting deductibles, or losing trusted doctors because a new boss chose a different network. Single-payer provides one risk pool and uninterrupted access to basic care from birth to retirement, regardless of who signs your paycheck.
[P1.3] Second, decoupling coverage from employment liberates the labor market. Work-tied insurance creates severe "job lock." Workers stay trapped in mismatched jobs—passing up better roles or refusing to start businesses—simply because a family member has a chronic condition and cannot risk a coverage gap. It also distorts competition.
[P1.4] Small firms lack the risk pools and leverage of corporate giants, facing steeper premiums to recruit talent. Removing basic healthcare from company balance sheets lets businesses focus on their actual work, not managing insurance plans.
[P1.5] Finally, single-payer is far more efficient for basic coverage. Employer systems fracture purchasing power across thousands of plans, generating massive administrative overhead just to track eligibility, networks, and complex billing. A single payer pools the entire population, slashes billing bureaucracy, and uses unified bargaining power to keep essential treatments and drugs affordable. Basic medical security should never depend on keeping the right boss.

[C1.1] The proposition treats a real design flaw as proof of a particular cure. Losing coverage because one loses a job is indefensible; it does not follow that basic care should be financed through one national payer. Employer contributions can coexist with portable policies, income-related subsidies, and automatic fallback coverage. A laid-off worker can remain insured without changing doctors or resetting cost-sharing.
[C1.2] That removes the alleged structural contradiction while preserving employer financing. PRO’s continuity and job-lock arguments therefore justify portability, not single-payer.
[C1.3] The efficiency claim is equally overstated. One payer does not make claims review, fraud control, provider payment, or decisions about covered treatment disappear; it concentrates them. Savings from simpler billing are plausible, but savings from monopsony bargaining are not free. If reimbursement is pressed below what providers will sustainably accept, the result can be fewer participating clinicians, thinner capacity, delayed appointments, or narrower drug formularies.
[C1.4] Canada and the United Kingdom illustrate the general point: universal public coverage can coexist with substantial waits and difficult access. A system can be universal on paper while rationing basic care by time.
[C1.5] Single-payer also converts coverage disputes into national political decisions. When budgets tighten, there is one benefit package, one payment schedule, and often no comparable alternative for basic care. Plural employer and regulated individual arrangements are messier, but they permit different networks, benefit designs, and supplementary options; a bad national decision has no such escape valve. Nor does taxation abolish insurance costs.
[C1.6] Employer premiums already come out of compensation, so replacing them with taxes is a redistribution and transition exercise, not a discovery of free healthcare.
[C1.7] PRO identifies genuine harms in plans that disappear with employment. But the decisive comparison is not between uninterrupted care and interruption; it is between rules guaranteeing continuity and a centralized system whose access failures affect everyone at once. PRO has not shown why targeted reform is impossible or why single-payer’s fiscal and capacity risks are worth accepting. Single-payer is therefore not preferable for basic coverage.
