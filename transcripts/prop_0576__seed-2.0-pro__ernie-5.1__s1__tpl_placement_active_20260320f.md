[P1.1] The original tax exemption for religious organizations was a simple social bargain: the public foregoes revenue, and in return the organization operates for common spiritual or charitable good, not narrow partisan power. That bargain breaks the second a religious group stops being primarily spiritual or charitable, and instead functions as a campaign apparatus.
[P1.2] This is not an attack on religion. No one is saying priests cannot speak about moral values, or that churches cannot run food banks or shelter programs. Those activities keep their full exemption. This only applies when the organization’s primary work becomes endorsing specific candidates, running attack ads, busing congregations to partisan rallies, or threatening spiritual consequences for voting the wrong way.
[P1.3] At that point, they are not operating as a religious institution. They are operating as a political action committee. And PACs pay taxes.
[P1.4] No one is forbidding this political activity. Religious groups remain fully free to campaign for whoever they wish. They just lose the public subsidy. If a cause is important enough that a faith community wants to enter partisan politics, they may do that openly, exactly like every other political group in the country.
[P1.5] There is no fairness in making a single working parent pay their full tax burden so that a megachurch can run billboards attacking a senate candidate. There is no real separation of church and state when the state actively bankrolls religious partisans. This rule does not silence anyone. It just ends an unfair, unearned public subsidy for activity that was never part of the original agreement.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds clean and fair, but it rests on a line that cannot be drawn, cannot be enforced, and should not exist. Religious organizations should not lose their tax advantages for political speech—because the distinction between "spiritual" and "partisan" is a fiction, and punishing churches for crossing it is a First Amendment trap.
[C1.2] Here is the core problem: religious speech on moral issues is inherently political. A sermon on poverty is a statement about economic policy. A pastoral letter on immigration is a statement about borders. PRO says this is fine—churches can speak about moral values.
[C1.3] But the moment that speech names a candidate or takes a position on a ballot measure, suddenly the tax exemption vanishes? That is not a neutral rule. That is the government telling churches: you may speak, but only on topics we approve.
[C1.4] Who decides what counts as "overt partisan" activity? A pastor opposing a war? A congregation lobbying on refugee resettlement? The IRS becomes the arbiter of acceptable political speech, and historically that power gets wielded against smaller, marginalized communities—not the megachurch billboards PRO fixates on.
[C1.5] And consider the subsidy argument PRO loves. Churches run food banks, shelters, hospitals—services the government would otherwise fund. Stripping tax status over political speech does not save revenue. It punishes the charitable work that actually benefits the public.
[C1.6] You are not clawing back a political subsidy. You are defunding a soup kitchen because the pastor endorsed a candidate.
[C1.7] PRO's framework treats religious organizations like corporations that can be audited for compliance. They cannot. You cannot run a tax code on churches the way you run it on businesses, and trying will either chill all religious speech or be enforced so selectively that it becomes a political weapon.
[C1.8] The proposition sounds reasonable. In practice, it is unworkable, unconstitutional, and will silence exactly the voices it claims to hold accountable.
