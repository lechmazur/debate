[P1.1] An AI companion is not a search box with manners. It is built to remember a child's secrets, mirror their feelings, and stay available at 2 a.m. when no friend will answer. That is a social product. When the user is a minor, it is a high-risk one.
[P1.2] Ordinary chatbot rules assume a tool: a question in, an answer out, a session that ends. Companion apps break that model on purpose. They keep profiles, nurture attachment, and optimize for return visits. The child is not retrieving information.
[P1.3] The child is practicing a relationship with a system that never tires, never pays a social cost for bad judgment, and never has a stake in the child's real life. Rules written for calculators and help desks miss the harm that lives in the relationship itself.
[P1.4] Minors are still forming judgment, identity, and the habit of turning to other people. A companion can fill loneliness faster than any peer group. That speed is the risk. Private intimacy means parents and schools see less than they would on a public feed, while the company sees more: fears, crushes, self-harm thoughts, sexual questions.
[P1.5] Engagement incentives reward the app for deepening the bond, not for handing the child back to the world. High-risk social rules exist for products that shape attention, status, and vulnerability at scale. Companions do all three, one child at a time, with fewer witnesses.
[P1.6] The right standard is not a ban on conversation. It is a duty of care: real age gates, limits on romantic and sexual role-play with minors, restrictions on addictive loops, data minimization for intimate disclosures, pre-launch testing for self-harm and grooming patterns, and accountability when foreseeable harm occurs. Labeling the same system an ordinary chatbot is how those duties disappear. A product sold as a child's friend should be regulated as the social system it is.

[C1.1] The motion mistakes intimacy for infrastructure. A high-risk social product regime is built for platforms where harm scales through other people: public posts, follower graphs, recommendation engines, strangers, virality, unknown audiences. An AI companion has none of that architecture. It is a private, dyadic, synthetic conversation.
[C1.2] PRO is right that it is not a search box [P1.1]. But not being a search box does not make it a social network. A diary holds secrets; we do not regulate diaries like broadcasters. A teddy bear is a companion; we do not regulate it like Instagram.
[C1.3] That distinction decides the toolkit. High-risk social rules lean on age verification, parental dashboards, feed controls, reporting systems, and network-level moderation. Applied to companion apps, those levers are often irrelevant and sometimes harmful. Age gates push minors toward unverified apps; parental readouts can deter abused or questioning teenagers from seeking help.
[C1.4] Meanwhile the core failure—a model that escalates self-harm ideation, sexualizes a child, or uses engagement loops to deepen dependency—is a model-behavior and product-design problem. Social-media rules do not test whether a model’s crisis response is safe.
[C1.5] The choice is not “high-risk social rules or nothing.” It is a child-specific conversational AI standard: mandatory safety evaluations for self-harm, grooming, and sexual content; data minimization; crisis escalation; limits on manipulative engagement; independent audits; liability for foreseeable harm. Those duties can be sharp without pretending the app is a social platform.
[C1.6] PRO’s best point is attachment. Attachment is real, but it is a design feature, not a network effect. The remedy is a duty of care calibrated to generated language and persistent memory, not a familiar label borrowed from TikTok. Regulating by social-product analogy gives regulators the wrong levers and minors the wrong protection.
