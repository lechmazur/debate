[P1.1] Picture a seventh-grade classroom where every student has a laptop open. The teacher asks a question. Half the room is looking at a screen she cannot see. One tab holds the assignment, another holds a game, a third holds a group chat about who sat with whom at lunch.
[P1.2] That is not a failure of discipline. It is what happens when you hand a twelve-year-old a device engineered to capture attention and ask him to ignore it for six hours.
[P1.3] This debate turns on one question: what should schools protect first for students aged roughly eleven to fourteen? Our answer is the two things no device can supply, the ability to focus and the ability to relate to other people.
[P1.4] First, attention is the precondition for every other kind of learning. Early adolescence is when students move from being managed by adults to managing themselves, and self-control is still under construction. Research on multitasking consistently finds that switching between tasks degrades comprehension and memory, and students routinely overestimate their ability to do it. A one-to-one program places a permanent distraction on every desk, then asks teachers to police screens they cannot see.
[P1.5] International assessment data broadly suggest that heavy classroom device use is associated with weaker performance, not stronger. Several systems, Sweden prominently, have begun walking back aggressive digitization toward printed books and handwriting.
[P1.6] Second, early secondary school is where students learn to disagree, collaborate, read a face, and repair a friendship. Those skills are built through practice: talking across a table, working a problem out loud, sitting through awkward silence. Every hour spent side by side with screens is an hour of that practice lost, at exactly the age when anxiety and isolation are rising.
[P1.7] Prioritizing does not mean banning technology. Shared devices, labs, and targeted access for students who need them all remain available. The burden on CON is heavier: to show that putting a personal screen in front of every child, all day, is worth more than attention and human connection. That trade is the wrong one.
