[P1.1] Science has a foundational problem: we do not actually know which published findings are true. The psychology replication crisis found that only around a third to a half of prominent published effects held up when independent labs tried to reproduce them. Cancer biology labs have reported similar failure rates trying to replicate landmark preclinical studies before building expensive drug pipelines on top of them. This is not a minor asterisk on an otherwise healthy system — it means a large fraction of the "novel" findings we currently fund follow-on work with, cite in policy, and teach as fact, are noise dressed up as discovery.
[P1.2] The reason this keeps happening is structural, not accidental. Grant committees reward novelty because novelty is what gets cited, what builds reputations, and what wins the next grant. Nobody's career is built on confirming someone else's result. Left to itself, the incentive gradient always tilts toward original, exciting, surprising claims — exactly the claims most likely to be false positives, since surprising results are disproportionately produced by small samples, flexible analysis choices, and publication bias.
[P1.3] A market that only pays for novelty will never self-correct, because self-correction is a public good that no individual researcher profits from producing. That is precisely why it must be reserved for by policy, deliberately and by fixed share, rather than left to hope that curiosity-driven grants will occasionally include a replication.
[P1.4] A fixed share matters, not just enthusiasm or good intentions, because otherwise replication funding evaporates the instant any given panel decides a "sexier" proposal exists. Every year the temptation is the same: fund the flashy new idea now, defer the boring verification later. Later never comes. Only a protected floor guarantees that verification survives contact with the incentive structure that keeps trying to starve it.
[P1.5] The comparison the rest of this debate should turn on is this: what does a novel grant dollar actually buy if the foundation under it is unverified? An unreplicated finding is not scientific progress — it's a bet. PRO is arguing we should insure [clipped]

[C1.1] The replication crisis is real. The motion's answer is still wrong. A fixed share is not a scientific judgment; it is a budget quota, and quotas allocate by political arithmetic rather than by where unreliable findings actually threaten knowledge.
[C1.2] Replication value is radically uneven. In some fields, an independent rerun is cheap and decisive; in cancer biology or social psychology, materials, samples, and analysis choices can make replication expensive and contested. A meaningful fixed share forces every funder to fill replication slots whether or not the best replication targets exist. The predictable result is quota-filling: safe, convenient replications of low-stakes findings, while the shaky, field-defining claims remain untested.
[C1.3] PRO's public-good argument [P1.3] shows replication may need public support; it does not show it needs a permanent percentage of every budget. Public goods are normally funded by targeted programs, not by an inflexible reserve that must be spent even when no valuable replication proposal is waiting.
[C1.4] Second, the opportunity cost is not just fewer novel projects. Novel projects are the raw material of future replication. Starve the discovery pipeline and you get fewer important findings to check. And if the share is meaningful, the cut to novel work is meaningful too; the motion cannot wave that away.
[C1.5] The choice is not "new versus true"; it is whether verification should be built into high-stakes projects, targeted at dubious claims, and funded competitively, or imposed as a set-aside regardless of scientific need.
[C1.6] Third, protected floors are vulnerable to capture. Panels under a quota must spend the money; institutions learn to supply replication-shaped proposals; metrics reward volume, not correction. If the aim is reliability, funders should require independent validation for high-stakes claims, support registered reports and multi-site replication, and run competitive replication calls. That funds replication where it matters.
[C1.7] The motion's fixed share guarantees replication spending, not replication value—and buys that guarantee by cutting the discoveries we most need to verify.
