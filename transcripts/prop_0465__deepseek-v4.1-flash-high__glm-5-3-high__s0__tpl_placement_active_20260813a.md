[P1.1] Rivers are not pipes. They are living systems—water, fish, sediment, floodplains, and the communities that depend on them. Yet most law treats them as property. A dam, mine, or diversion can sever a people's water, food, and identity through a routine permit.
[P1.2] The proposition is narrow and concrete: when Indigenous or local communities request it, governments should recognize the river or ecosystem as a legal person, with guardians empowered to speak for it.
[P1.3] That does two things. First, it fixes a structural blind spot. Environmental harm is often diffuse: no single human has standing to sue for a dead river. Personhood gives the ecosystem a legal voice.
[P1.4] New Zealand's Whanganui River settlement and Colombia's Atrato River recognition are imperfect, but they show the mechanism: guardians bring claims, negotiate restoration, and force agencies to treat the river as a party, not scenery.
[P1.5] Second, it does not ban infrastructure. It changes the default from "destroy first, litigate later" to "justify, consult, mitigate, and compensate." If a project is truly vital, it can still proceed with consent and conditions. If it only pencils out by wrecking a river, that is not an argument for legal immunity; it is an argument for honest accounting.
[P1.6] We do not let a company bulldoze a neighborhood just because permits are complicated.
[P1.7] The request requirement is crucial. This is not distant bureaucrats or foreign NGOs imposing a doctrine. It is the people with the closest knowledge and greatest stake asking for a legal shield.
[P1.8] Opponents will call this vague and costly. But we already grant personhood to corporations, ships, and municipalities. If a corporation can sue to protect profit, a river should be able to sue to protect its existence. The real choice is not rivers versus people.
[P1.9] It is whether a river gets a voice before it is gone.
