[P1.1] Professional sports franchises have perfected a shakedown: pay for our billion-dollar playground or we move to the next eager city. Municipalities routinely surrender hundreds of millions in tax abatements, free land, and infrastructure upgrades for stadiums that deliver fantasy economic returns while developers pocket the real profits. This racket continues only because there is no credible alternative on the table. The proposition changes the calculus: public money comes with ironclad conditions, or it does not come at all.
[P1.2] The economic evidence against blank-check stadiums is overwhelming and consistent across decades of urban analysis. These projects generate negligible net new economic activity; they primarily shuffle existing entertainment dollars from local restaurants and theaters into the team's coffers. They occupy prime real estate that could support permanent mixed-use development or affordable housing, and they frequently saddle municipalities with hidden maintenance debt that lingers long after the ribbon-cutting photos fade. When a city commits half a billion dollars to a billionaire owner, that represents half a billion dollars not spent on transit infrastructure, public safety, or schools.
[P1.3] The asymmetry is stark: teams capture naming rights, luxury suite revenue, and broadcast windfalls, while taxpayers absorb cost overruns, traffic congestion, and neighborhood displacement. The promise of "revitalization" usually translates to speculative real estate booms that price out legacy residents to make room for sports bars they cannot afford, leaving the municipality holding the bag for infrastructure strain that benefits only the franchise.
[P1.4] The proposition offers a simple corrective. Revenue-sharing agreements treat public subsidies as investments requiring measurable returns to the treasury, not unilateral wealth transfers. Neighborhood-benefit requirements—legally binding community land trusts, affordable housing set-asides, and local hiring quotas—ensure the surrounding community actually gains rather than suffers from the project. If owners reject these enforceable terms, the market has spoken clearly: they can finance their own luxury venue.
[P1.5] We must stop subsidizing private profit while socializing risk, or at minimum ensure the public gets paid back. There is no third option that serves the city's fiscal health or its residents' welfare.
