[P1.1] A light-rail stop surrounded by single-family zoning is a public investment nobody is allowed to use. That is the status quo the other side defends.
[P1.2] Start with the causal claim at the heart of this debate: blocking new housing does not stop displacement — it is displacement. When a neighborhood near transit adds jobs and amenities but no homes, new residents don't disappear; they outbid existing tenants for the same aging stock. That is how you get the evictions and rent spikes CON fears, without building a single unit. Cities that froze development didn't freeze in place; they got San Francisco's outcome — among the least affordable housing markets in the country, plus the exodus of exactly the working families the freeze was meant to protect.
[P1.3] Where supply has been legalized — Auckland's broad upzoning, Minneapolis ending single-family-only zoning — early evidence points to rents growing more slowly than in comparable cities. Scarcity, not construction, is the eviction machine.
[P1.4] Second, "by-right" is doing the real work in the motion. Discretionary approval is where housing dies: years of hearings, legal appeals, and veto points that favor incumbent homeowners over future renters. By-right converts a political fight into a building permit. It makes infill projects small and fast enough for local builders, and it lets transit investments actually carry riders.
[P1.5] Third, weigh the conceded risk honestly. The motion accepts that short-term, localized displacement can rise near new construction. But that harm is visible, concentrated, and addressable — cities can pair by-right zoning with tenant protections, right-to-return policies, and affordability set-asides. The alternative's harm is invisible and permanent: thousands of households quietly priced out each year, region-wide, with no remedy because nothing was ever approved or denied.
[P1.6] One displacement ends in new homes and eventually lower rents; the other ends in nothing.
[P1.7] The choice is not displacement versus stability. It is displacement with a path to affordability, or displacement without one.

[C1.6] Third, the supply story is not as automatic as the other side claims. Upzoning near transit has, in many U.S. cases, produced exactly the luxury product that accelerates displacement of the very households the policy was meant to help. [C1.7] The motion puts the cart before the horse. Build the protections first, then upzone. Not after.
