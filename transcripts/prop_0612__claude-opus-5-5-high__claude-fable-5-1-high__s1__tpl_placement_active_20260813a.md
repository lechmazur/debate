[P1.1] A university can survive a bad donor. What it cannot survive is a donor it cannot afford to offend. That is the core of this case: when a foreign government becomes a major funder, independence erodes quietly, without anyone ever issuing an order.
[P1.2] First, foreign governments are not ordinary donors. A private alumnus funding a chemistry building wants his name on the wall. A state has strategic interests: its reputation, its territorial claims, its dissidents, its access to sensitive technology. When that state funds a centre, an endowed chair, or a partnership campus, the university inherits an incentive not to host the Uyghur activist, not to publish the awkward paper, not to hire the critic.
[P1.3] No one needs to make demands. Self-censorship does the work.
[P1.4] We have seen this pattern. Confucius Institutes operated on Western campuses for years under agreements that gave a foreign state influence over staffing and programming. Many universities closed them only once scrutiny arrived. The London School of Economics accepted money linked to Gaddafi's Libya, and the resulting scandal cost its director his job.
[P1.5] In the United States, disclosure of foreign gifts has been legally required for decades, yet repeated reviews found widespread underreporting. Voluntary transparency fails because the institution that benefits from silence is the one deciding whether to speak.
[P1.6] Second, disclosure is the minimum condition for accountability. Students choosing a programme, faculty deciding what to research, and taxpayers who already fund these institutions all deserve to know who else is paying. Sunlight costs honest partnerships nothing.
[P1.7] Third, limits prevent dependency. Disclosure tells you a department relies on a foreign state; a cap stops that reliance from becoming a hostage situation. Limits can be proportionate: thresholds by share of budget, stricter scrutiny for sensitive fields, and no restrictions at all on foreign students, researchers, or ordinary scholarly collaboration.
[P1.8] So the question for CON is simple. If this money is benign, why should it be secret? And if a single foreign government can become indispensable to a university, who is actually protecting that university's independence?
