[P1.1] An employer buys your labor, not your life. Yet under at-will employment, a worker can be fired for a lawful blog post, a protest sign, a romantic partner, or a drink after hours—none of which changes job performance. When conduct is lawful and does not materially affect performance, firing serves no legitimate business interest; it serves power.
[P1.2] That is the case in one line: the employer’s legitimate interest ends where the job is done well. If a nurse is sober and competent, her lawful glass of wine on Saturday is not patient care. If a coder ships clean code, an off-hours tweet may annoy clients but not alter output. Only if the employer can prove a real work-related harm—safety risk, harassment, conflict of interest, or a substantial reputational hit tied to the role—should dismissal be allowed.
[P1.3] That safeguard preserves every tool a real business needs.
[P1.4] The burden should be on the employer to show that material effect, not on the worker to guess which private choices will cost a paycheck. Without that protection, termination becomes private control. Losing a job can mean lost health coverage, income, and bargaining power, so workers self-censor: they avoid unpopular views, lawful associations, or medical choices because a boss might disapprove.
[P1.5] It also launders bias. A manager barred from saying “fired for being gay, Muslim, or Black” can fire for attendance at a certain church, a same-sex wedding, or a cultural event. Discrimination law is evaded because the stated trigger is conduct, not status.
[P1.6] Consider a warehouse employee photographed at a legal rally. The photo goes viral, one customer complains, and he is fired for “conduct.” His punctuality, safety, and output are untouched. That dismissal is often lawful today. It should not be.
[P1.7] Stronger protection is simple: justify off-duty dismissals by demonstrable work-related harm, or leave private life alone. The motion does not protect bad performance; it protects paychecks from being used to purchase obedience.
