[P1.1] The global supply chain is restructuring. Pandemic disruptions, US-China decoupling, and corporate risk diversification are driving a structural shift toward nearshoring—and Latin America is sitting on the geographic edge of the world's largest consumer market. The question is not whether this shift is happening, but whether the region will capture it or cede it to Southeast Asia and other competitors. The answer requires deliberate industrial policy, including protectionist levers and fiscal investment, precisely because the market alone will not deliver it.
[P1.2] Consider Mexico's recent trajectory. Auto manufacturing, electronics assembly, and aerospace components have surged as firms relocate production closer to North American demand. That growth did not happen through laissez-faire. It required tariffs on competing imports, tax incentives for foreign direct investment, and public spending on infrastructure and workforce training.
[P1.3] Brazil, Colombia, and other economies can replicate this model—but only if they accept the short-term costs the proposition acknowledges.
[P1.4] The opposition will call these measures distortionary. They are, in a narrow sense. But the distortion corrects a far deeper one: decades of commodity dependence that left Latin America vulnerable to price swings, deindustrialization, and brain drain. Protectionist tools used strategically—temporary, performance-linked, sunset-claused—build domestic manufacturing ecosystems that generate tax revenue, formal employment, and technology spillovers.
[P1.5] Fiscal burdens today become fiscal returns tomorrow as the tax base widens and import bills shrink.
[P1.6] The real risk is inaction. Waiting for market forces to organically deliver industrial upgrading has been tried for forty years. It produced stagnant productivity and persistent inequality. Nearshoring is a narrow window of opportunity.
[P1.7] Industrial policy, even with its imperfections, is the mechanism to convert geographic advantage into structural transformation. The cost of getting it wrong is manageable. The cost of missing the window is permanent.

[C1.2] That is exactly what was promised then. In the region's political economy, protection does not sunset; it lobbies, entrenches, and becomes a right.
[C1.3] Argentina's Tierra del Fuego regime shows the equilibrium: decades of tax breaks and tariff walls to assemble electronics at the end of the earth, yielding devices far costlier than imports, surviving only because the state keeps paying. Brazil's national-champions strategy funneled cheap public credit to politically connected conglomerates and ended in scandal, not competitiveness. These are not aberrations—they are what discretionary protection becomes under weak institutions.
[C1.4] The fiscal point compounds it. These states tax little, borrow expensively, and still carry pandemic-era debt. A subsidy race against Washington or Hanoi is lost on day one, and [P1.5]'s "fiscal burdens today become fiscal returns tomorrow" is the same accounting that produced the lost decade. When the returns don't materialize, the adjustment falls on the poorest—through inflation, austerity, or both.
[C1.5] [P1.2] also misreads its own example. Mexico's boom rests on USMCA market access and geography—openness, not tariffs. Firms nearshore to cut risk and cost; walls on intermediate inputs raise both, and protection invites retaliation against the very access that makes the region attractive. What nearshoring actually requires is reliable energy, functioning ports, security, customs efficiency, and macro stability—public goods, not protection.
[C1.6] The clash is simple: PRO needs fragile institutions to run sophisticated, self-liquidating industrial policy. The region's record says the tools get captured, the bills come due, and the window closes anyway—leaving less money and more distortion than before.
