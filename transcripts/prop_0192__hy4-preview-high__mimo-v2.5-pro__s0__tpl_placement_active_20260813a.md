[P1.1] Personhood is not a metaphor for “almost human”; it is the law’s recognition that a being has a life of its own. Great apes, cetaceans, and elephants cross that line: they are individuals with memories, relationships, and futures that can be harmed.
[P1.2] Consider what they are. Apes make tools, cooperate in hunts, navigate alliances, grieve their dead, and pass on local traditions. Dolphins and whales live in societies with signature calls, learned dialects, cooperative hunting, and deep maternal bonds. Elephants remember drought-era water sources, coordinate through vocal and seismic cues, mourn their dead, and transmit ecological knowledge through matriarchies.
[P1.3] These are not reflexes; they mark subjective, social, future-oriented lives.
[P1.4] Now consider captivity and exploitation. An orca in a concrete tank is not merely “housed”; its ranging, family life, and acoustic world are nullified. An elephant in a circus or logging camp is severed from the herd that carries its knowledge. An ape in a lab or zoo loses the social and spatial world its mind needs.
[P1.5] The resulting chronic stress, stereotypies, and broken families are not side effects; they are the destruction of what makes life go well for that individual.
[P1.6] The predictable objection is that personhood requires rationality, language, or moral agency. That test fails. Human infants and people with advanced dementia lack full rationality or moral agency, yet we do not treat them as exploitable. Corporations are legal persons with no moral agency at all.
[P1.7] The consistent principle is not species membership or contractual ability, but being a subject-of-a-life whose basic interests can be frustrated.
[P1.8] Recognizing non-human persons would not make them citizens. It would grant a presumption against captivity: confinement only to serve the individual, never for commerce, entertainment, sport, research, or forced labor. They would remain protected animals, but their deepest interests would count in their own right. If suffering and autonomy matter, excluding these beings is not prudence; it is arbitrary moral luck.
