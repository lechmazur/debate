[P1.1] Fragmented service industries are not one labor market. A hotel housekeeper, a home-care aide, a cleaner, a fast-food worker and a delivery courier may all sit near the wage floor, but they face different problems: split shifts, unpaid travel between clients, cancelled hours, misclassification, unsafe solo work, no training ladder. A repeated minimum-wage hike turns one dial—the hourly rate—and leaves those standards untouched. Sectoral bargaining sets a package for the whole industry, negotiated by worker and employer representatives, with wages by role and skill plus rules on scheduling, travel time, training, safety and benefits.
[P1.2] That is the better tool for fragmented services.
[P1.3] The mechanism matters. Fragmentation means thousands of small workplaces, franchised outlets and subcontractors, each with weak HR and little union presence. A statutory minimum is enforced mainly through individual complaints, so the worker with the most to lose must challenge her boss. Sectoral agreements can be extended across a sector, create joint enforcement funds, require registration and make lead firms or franchisors responsible for compliance down their chains.
[P1.4] That attacks the business model that undercuts good employers.
[P1.5] Repeated hikes also invite evasion and compression. Raise the floor and firms reclassify workers as self-employed, cut hours, intensify workloads, or shave differentials so experienced staff earn barely more than entrants. A sectoral scale can preserve skill premiums and tie pay to training, giving workers a ladder rather than a single step.
[P1.6] The comparison is not minimum wages versus nothing. It is a single, blunt, politically volatile wage floor versus an industry-specific standards regime that can cover pay and the terms that actually shape service work. A home-care worker paid the minimum but not paid for travel between clients is still underpaid. A hike changes her hourly rate; sectoral bargaining changes the rules of her industry.

[C1.1] The proposition fails because it compares an idealized sectoral system with a caricature of minimum-wage hikes. In fragmented service work, the decisive question is not which tool sounds more comprehensive, but which can actually reach the most workers, quickly and reliably. Repeated minimum-wage hikes win that test.
[C1.2] Fragmented industries are defined by churn: small firms, franchises, platforms, subcontractors, short-lived businesses, and workers moving between jobs. A universal minimum wage is portable. It follows the worker across a restaurant, a care agency, a cleaning contractor, or a delivery app. Sectoral bargaining is tied to an industry definition and to organizations that often do not exist or cannot speak for precarious workers.
[C1.3] The more fragmented the sector, the harder it is to identify who bargains, who is bound, and what happens to workers misclassified as independent contractors. That is precisely where the need is greatest.
[C1.4] Sectoral bargaining also multiplies complexity where compliance capacity is weakest. A multi-tier wage scale plus scheduling, travel, training, and benefit rules may help organized incumbents but overwhelms small employers and invites evasion through misclassification, off-the-books hours, and re-labeling work. Enforcement still requires inspectors, complaints, and penalties. If the state must build that machinery, it can instead attach targeted rules to a rising statutory floor—such as travel pay for home-care workers or predictable-scheduling requirements—without making every standard depend on fragile negotiations.
[C1.5] Repeated hikes have a further democratic advantage: they are visible, simple to communicate, and hard for insiders to capture. Sectoral deals can trade away the interests of newcomers, informal workers, or small firms in exchange for stability among established players. The better path is to raise and update the minimum wage, then layer enforcement and sector-specific protections where needed. That delivers higher standards without surrendering the floor to bargaining structures that fragmented service industries cannot reliably sustain.

[P2.1] CON’s central claim is that a minimum wage is portable while sectoral bargaining is not. But portability of one number is not portability of decent standards. A home-care worker can carry the same hourly floor from agency to agency and still lose unpaid travel time at every one. The proposition asks which regime sets minimum standards, not which single number travels best.
[P2.2] The claim that sectoral bargaining cannot identify who bargains [C1.3] proves too much. Fragmentation is exactly why statutory extension, sectoral boards and lead-firm liability matter: they can bind employers who never join an association and cover workers whose union is weak. Misclassified contractors can be brought into a sectoral definition, with platform and franchisor responsibility, far more directly than an individual minimum-wage complaint can reach them. CON assumes the state can define “home care” to attach travel pay, but somehow cannot define it to set a wage scale and travel rules together.
[P2.3] On complexity, CON compares an elegant statute with a messy negotiation. But their fallback—raise the floor and “layer” scheduling, travel and enforcement rules—requires the same sectoral machinery, the same inspectors, and the same definitional choices. They are describing sectoral standard-setting while pretending it is a minimum-wage hike. And if compliance capacity is weak, a higher floor without joint liability is the easier thing to evade: pay cash, cut hours, relabel the worker.
[P2.4] Finally, visibility is not adequacy. Minimum wages are also politically frozen, exempted and eroded; repeated hikes are a permanent political fight that leaves standards outside pay untouched. Sectoral agreements can be extended, monitored by joint funds, and enforced up chains. CON defends a floor.
[P2.5] PRO defends the floor plus the rules that determine whether service workers are actually paid for the job they do.
