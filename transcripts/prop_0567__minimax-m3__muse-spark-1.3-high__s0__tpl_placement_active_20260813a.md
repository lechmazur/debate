[P1.1] When a teenager is shot by police, the public wants to see what happened. When an executive is acquitted of fraud, voters want to judge whether justice was done. Yet ordinary trials — the cases that shape most citizens' understanding of law — remain invisible to all but a few. The default of courtroom secrecy is the last protected monopoly on public information in a democracy, and cameras break that monopoly.
[P1.2] The principle that justice must be seen to be done is foundational, not decorative. Cameras serve that principle directly. Florida has permitted cameras in trial courts since the 1970s, and the predicted collapse has not come: no wave of witness intimidation, no jury contamination crisis, no measurable impairment of fair trials. Federal courts have run pilot programs reaching similar conclusions.
[P1.3] When transparency is tested, the sky does not fall. That fact alone shifts the burden to opponents of cameras to justify continued secrecy.
[P1.4] The opponent will be tempted to build their case on speculative future harms. But the harms of opacity are concrete and ongoing. Wrongful convictions are harder to expose without a complete record. Prosecutorial overreach and judicial bias go unexamined.
[P1.5] Press sketches and reporter summaries are routinely inaccurate — and once distorted, those distortions can never be fully undone. Direct recording gives the public the option to judge for itself, instead of taking the courts' word on trust.
[P1.6] This motion says "most" trial proceedings, not all. Juveniles, sexual assault complainants, confidential informants, and undercover officers can be shielded by clear, narrow exceptions written into the rule itself. PRO offers a default of transparency with carved-out protections. CON offers a default of secrecy imposed on the public's own courts — the harder position in a democracy, and the one that should lose.
