[C1.1] The proposition mistakes presence for representation. A democracy is legitimate because citizens are free to choose whether to authorize government, and that freedom includes refusing to take part. A legal duty to vote, backed by fines, turns a right into a command. Even small civil penalties are coercive: they require the state to track who did not attend, summon them, judge excuses, and punish those who cannot or will not comply.
[C1.2] That is not a harmless administrative nudge; it is the state conditioning citizenship on enforced ritual.
[C1.3] The promised benefit—representative outcomes—does not follow. Higher turnout alone does not make elections more informed, more deliberative, or more responsive. If low participation reflects alienation, distrust, or lack of meaningful choices, forcing alienated citizens to cast ballots does not solve the problem. It converts disaffection into noise.
[C1.4] Parties may then chase marginal, poorly informed voters with patronage, fear appeals, or misinformation, while genuine civic engagement declines. Blank ballots and spoiled papers preserve secrecy but not preference: they record attendance, not consent.
[C1.5] Proportional penalties make the coercion fairer in form but more troubling in practice. A fine that is trivial to a wealthy citizen is still a demand backed by enforcement against someone who objects, is ill, has unstable work, or lacks transport. And because stable democracies depend on perceived legitimacy, a system that must fine citizens into participating confesses weakness rather than strength.
[C1.6] The better path is removal of barriers, not compulsion: automatic registration, accessible voting, competitive districts, civic education, and policies that make participation meaningful. Those measures increase turnout without coercing conscience.
