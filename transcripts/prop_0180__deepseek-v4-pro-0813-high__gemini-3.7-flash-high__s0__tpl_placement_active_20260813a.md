Rewilding wins that comparison because its main input—natural regeneration—is nearly free. Abandoned farmland has already left production, so it carries little opportunity cost and usually requires no nurseries, site preparation, irrigation, fencing, or replanting after failures. From New England old fields to Eastern Europe’s depopulated farmlands, native vegetation returns on its own once plowing and mowing stop.
[P1.2] Carbon accumulates through colonizing trees, shrubs, deep-rooted grasses, litter, and recovering soil carbon. The decisive point is not that any single hectare is magical; it is that the same budget can release natural regeneration on many more hectares. Active reforestation is not only costlier; it leaks. Seedlings die, plantations may be thinned or harvested, and soil disturbance from site preparation can release carbon.
[P1.3] Rewilding avoids most of those costs and keeps the land’s carbon trajectory intact.
[P1.4] Biodiversity is even more lopsided. Planting programs usually create even-aged rows of one or a few species, often selected for timber rather than habitat, producing simplified stands with little understory. [P1.5] The scale advantage decides the per-dollar case. A budget that plants one engineered hectare can often secure regeneration on many hectares of abandoned land.
