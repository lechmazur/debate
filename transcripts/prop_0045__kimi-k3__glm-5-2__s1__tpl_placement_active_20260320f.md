[P1.1] Every hiring decision is a bet about the future: will this person do the job well? A test used to place that bet is justified only if it improves the odds. MBTI and DISC do not, and that is the whole case.
[P1.2] Start with the science. MBTI sorts continuous personality traits into sixteen rigid types, discarding most of the information it collects. Its test-retest reliability is poor—retake it weeks later and you may well land in a different type—so it cannot measure people consistently, let alone predict their performance. Industrial-organizational psychology has spent decades ranking selection methods by how well they forecast job success; structured interviews, work samples, and cognitive ability tests sit near the top.
[P1.3] Typological instruments like MBTI and DISC barely register. A properly validated Big Five measure carries some signal; a four-letter type carries almost none. Tellingly, even the company that publishes the MBTI says it should not be used for selection. When the vendor disclaims the use, employers should listen.
[P1.4] The costs are real, because invalid predictions are not harmless. A weak candidate with a "leader" profile gets hired over a strong one whose letters look wrong. In team-building, the type becomes a cage: "I'm that introvert type, I don't present," or "he's a high D, what do you expect." Junk labels excuse bad behavior and flatten real people.
[P1.5] And since employment law requires selection tools to be demonstrably job-related, an unvalidated test is legal exposure, not just superstition.
[P1.6] CON's only path is to show these tests genuinely predict performance or team outcomes. That evidence is not there. What CON will show instead is that the tests are popular, fun, and spark conversation—but so does a horoscope, and the mechanism is the same: vague, flattering categories that fit everyone. Popularity is not validity.
[P1.7] Employers should stop buying it.

[C1.1] The proposition bundles two different use cases—hiring and team-building—under a single standard, then applies that standard selectively. That move fails on both fronts.
[C1.2] For team-building, predictive validity is the wrong criterion entirely. A personality framework that gives colleagues a shared vocabulary for communication styles doesn't need to forecast job performance to be useful. When a team member says "I tend to process before I speak," and DISC gives them a shorthand to say it with, the value is the conversation that follows—not the label's psychometric precision. Demanding that a framework for self-reflection prove it predicts quarterly output before it clears HR is like requiring a thesaurus to pass a statistics exam before anyone opens it.
[C1.3] For hiring, no competent employer uses these instruments alone. They sit alongside structured interviews, work samples, references, and trial periods. The real question is not whether MBTI independently predicts performance but whether it adds incremental signal when combined with other tools. Employers routinely use instruments with modest individual validity because they contribute different information than resumes and interviews alone.
[C1.4] The proposition assumes that "stop using" is costless. Remove a structured personality framework and you do not eliminate personality assessment from hiring; you push it underground into the hiring manager's private gut theory about what a good fit looks like. That is less transparent, less auditable, and less legally defensible than a standardized instrument anyone can inspect and improve. A flawed but visible test you can evaluate beats an invisible bias you cannot even name.
[C1.5] So the actual choice is not MBTI versus science. It is between imperfect, visible tools and unexamined instinct. Employers face that trade-off daily, and the motion pretends the second option does not exist.
