[P1.1] When a single influencer post can turn a quiet shoreline or neighborhood lane into a parking lot and a trash heap overnight, high-tourism destinations have the right and the duty to limit that promotion. Fragile spots have ecological, infrastructural, and social carrying capacities that viral traffic routinely exceeds. Geotagging and stylized promotion do not merely inform; they manufacture concentrated demand faster than local management can respond.
[P1.2] The pattern is familiar. A hidden beach, narrow trail, or residential courtyard appears in a polished reel with precise coordinates. Within days, tour vans arrive and the features that made the place special begin to degrade. Vegetation is trampled, waste systems overflow, and wildlife retreats.
[P1.3] Residents lose quiet streets, face rising housing costs from short-term rentals, and cannot move through their own towns without performing for strangers. These costs are predictable when attention is dumped onto places never built for mass visitation.
[P1.4] Destinations already manage demand with permits, timed entry, and seasonal closures. Limiting influencer-style geotagging of fragile spots applies the same logic to the channel now driving the sharpest spikes. The measure need not ban personal photos or general destination marketing. It targets promotional pinpointing when harm is evident: tools that obscure exact locations, permit requirements for commercial filming, and clear local guidance against amplifying vulnerable sites.
[P1.5] This is not hostility to tourism or speech. Tourism stays welcome when it fits capacity. Speech stays free when it is not functioning as advertising that shifts cleanup, congestion, and displacement onto residents who never consented. Sustainability means matching promotion to what a place can bear.
[P1.6] Where viral traffic damages both the site and resident life, continued geotagging is extraction, not discovery. Destinations should limit it.
