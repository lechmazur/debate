[P1.2] The case for a universal primary care guarantee rests on three pillars. First, economics. Primary care is the highest-value, lowest-cost layer of medicine: vaccinations, blood pressure management, diabetes screening, prenatal checkups. These interventions are inexpensive individually but prevent the strokes, amputations, and neonatal crises that devour health budgets.
[P1.3] Emergency departments already function as a de facto safety net in systems without guaranteed access — the most expensive possible venue for the cheapest possible care. Guaranteeing the floor is fiscal prudence, not largesse.
[P1.4] Second, market failure. Primary care generates massive positive externalities — herd immunity, infectious disease surveillance, a healthier workforce — that private markets systematically underprice. Insurance markets, left alone, avoid exactly the people who need care most. No serious economic framework suggests a pure market delivers efficient primary care; this is textbook territory for public guarantee.
[P1.5] Third, notice how modest this proposition is. It does not nationalize medicine. It explicitly preserves private markets for elective and supplementary services — cosmetic surgery, premium rooms, faster access to specialists. Countries from Denmark to Costa Rica to Australia run versions of exactly this hybrid: a guaranteed public foundation with private options layered above.
[P1.6] These are not dystopias of rationing; they routinely outperform less universal systems on life expectancy per dollar spent.
[P1.7] So the burden falls squarely on the other side: to argue that some citizens should be able to be priced out of a checkup, a vaccine, a blood pressure pill — in the name of what, exactly? Efficiency points our way. Evidence points our way. Guarantee the floor.
