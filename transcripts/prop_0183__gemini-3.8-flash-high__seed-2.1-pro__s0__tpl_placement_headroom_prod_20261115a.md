[P1.1] Humanity is living through an unprecedented era of cultural amnesia. While a clay tablet lasts millennia and a printed book survives centuries, digital heritage is uniquely fragile. Websites disappear overnight, server architectures collapse, independent journalism vanishes behind defunct URLs, and proprietary software becomes unusable as soon as corporate licensing servers shut down. To save this record, nonprofit digital libraries like the Internet Archive perform the indispensable, non-commercial task of archiving our shared cultural history.
[P1.2] Yet traditional copyright enforcement treats the technical reality of digital preservation as an act of infringement. Digital preservation cannot occur passively; it requires crawling, copying, format-shifting, and emulating code so that historical materials remain readable. In a physical library, buying a book exhausts the publisher's distribution right, allowing preservation and lending. In the digital realm, rights-holders use restrictive licensing, technological locks, and the threat of catastrophic statutory damages to criminalize the very acts necessary to keep culture accessible.
[P1.3] Market forces will never preserve humanity's long-tail heritage. Commercial publishers and software developers focus exclusively on immediate monetization. When a video game, an early blog platform, an orphaned academic text, or a regional newspaper ceases to be commercially viable, corporations do not archive it; they abandon it. If nonprofit institutions must wait ninety-five years for copyrights to expire before they can legally preserve and display these artifacts, the underlying bits will have degraded into unrecoverable digital dust.
[P1.4] Copyright was established to promote the progress of science and the useful arts, not to enforce the destruction of historical records. Exempting qualified nonprofit digital libraries from traditional copyright enforcement does not destroy commercial markets; it prevents private monopolies from holding civilization's collective memory hostage. We must protect the digital library before our modern history is erased forever.

[C1.1] This proposal rests on a false binary: that we must either grant nonprofit digital libraries blanket exemption from traditional copyright enforcement, or watch humanity’s digital heritage crumble to dust. That is a choice no one is forcing us to make. Existing copyright already includes targeted, bounded safeguards for legitimate archival work: fair use carveouts for format-shifting at-risk material, negotiated licenses for preserved content, and narrow exceptions for orphan works whose holders cannot be located. Those guardrails exist for a reason: to balance preservation against the core purpose of copyright, which is to let creators earn a living making the culture we will one day want to preserve.
[C1.2] A blanket exemption sweeps far past narrow preservation of defunct, abandoned content. It would legalize exactly the kind of overreach the Internet Archive already demonstrated when it launched its unregulated National Emergency Library, stripping all lending limits from millions of in-print, commercially viable books to offer unlimited free access at the height of the pandemic, when working authors and small independent presses had already lost most of their income to lockdowns. That is not preservation—it is uncompensated mass distribution that directly undercuts the market for new creative work.
[C1.3] The proposition offers no guardrails to define who qualifies as a legitimate nonprofit archive, no limits on when archived content can be displayed versus stored for future recovery, and no compensation structure for living creators. It would turn copyright from a system that incentivizes new art and research into a hollow protection easily dodged by any group willing to label itself a digital library, while doing nothing that targeted, negotiated archival policy cannot already achieve without robbing creators of the revenue they need to keep producing the culture worth preserving for future generations.
