[C1.1] The motion is not an anti-deception rule. It is a compulsory no-surcharge guarantee: when an adult travels with a “young child,” adjacency must be supplied, and neither seat fees nor premium fares can ration it. That makes a scarce seating pattern a protected entitlement, with costs pushed somewhere else.
[C1.2] Calling the fee a “ransom” [P1.3] assumes the operator always creates the split. Often it does not. A holiday train is full; earlier passengers selected paired seats; the family booked a late, cheap fare; a connection was missed; equipment changed. If no ordinary adjacent pair remains, an algorithm cannot manufacture one.
[C1.3] The operator must move another passenger, open a paid or premium seat for free, block saleable inventory, or override someone else’s priority. “Nobody loses a seat” is evasive: passengers buy locations, not abstract capacity.
[C1.4] So the real question is who pays. A solo traveler who chose an aisle weeks ago, a passenger needing a particular row, another parent, or a couple already together can lose the seat they reasonably expected. The rule also invites strategic booking: use a child on the reservation to claim protected pairs, or demand that a higher-cabin pair be delivered at economy price. Regulators would need age thresholds, proof of accompaniment, multiple-caregiver rules, peak exceptions, and compensation for displaced passengers—a new allocation system, not a small tweak to software.
[C1.5] If safety is the trump, the wording is far too vague: does it cover lap infants, seven-year-olds, twelve-year-olds, and children one row away?
[C1.6] A no-deception principle does not establish this motion. Operators should say what a fare includes and should not charge to fix their own assignment error, but that is not a reason to abolish every price difference for adjacency. Prices, early selection, and voluntary swaps ration scarce pairs before departure; a no-surcharge mandate shifts the bill to random travelers and makes availability worse. Child welfare deserves targeted accommodation, not a rule that socializes every family-planning uncertainty.
