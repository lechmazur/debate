[C1.1] The proposition asks us to accept slower drug approvals as the price of proportionate demographic representation in trials. That price is paid in lives—immediate, measurable, and certain—while the benefit is speculative, partial, and achievable through other means.
[C1.2] Consider what "materially slows" actually means. For a lethal disease with no existing treatment, every month of delay is a cohort of patients who die waiting. The proposition treats this as acceptable because it assumes the alternative—faster approval on narrower evidence—produces drugs that fail in underrepresented groups.
[C1.3] But that conflates two very different outcomes: having no drug at all, and having a drug whose dosing or adverse-event profile is imperfect for some populations. The first is catastrophic. The second is manageable through post-market surveillance, label refinement, and pharmacovigilance systems that already exist and can be strengthened without gating approval.
[C1.4] More importantly, proportionate representation as a rigid requirement does not deliver what PRO promises. Forcibly stratifying enrollment by age, sex, and ethnicity assumes those categories capture the biological variance that matters. They do not. Drug response varies by genotype, comorbidity, organ function, and environment—factors that cut across demographic lines.
[C1.5] A trial that is proportionally representative by surface categories can still miss the pharmacogenomic signal that actually predicts harm. The mandate gives the appearance of rigor without guaranteeing the substance.
[C1.6] The structural result is predictable: trials stall waiting for recruitment quotas, sponsors deprioritize drugs for smaller or harder-to-recruit populations, and the patients with the least political voice—those waiting for any treatment at all—bear the cost. A drug approved late is not a safer drug. It is a drug that arrived after the people who needed it were already dead.
