[P1.1] International shipping moves most of world trade while running almost entirely on heavy fossil fuels, yet no shipper, flag state, or port acting alone can force a clean transition. That is exactly why a binding IMO timetable for low- or zero-carbon fuels is necessary, even if freight costs rise.
[P1.2] First, voluntary action creates a race to evasion. Ships can reflag, refuel where rules are weakest, and outcompete cleaner rivals for decades because vessels last so long. An owner choosing expensive green methanol or ammonia today is punished by charterers who can simply hire a cheaper, dirtier ship tomorrow. Only a global, enforceable fuel standard removes that penalty for doing the right thing and stops carbon leakage from becoming the business model.
[P1.3] Second, certainty is what builds the alternative. Fuel producers, engine makers, and ports will not invest at scale in green corridors, bunkering, storage, and retrofits on the basis of aspirations. A dated requirement — progressively tightening carbon intensity until zero-carbon fuels dominate — tells capital where to go and when. That predictability will drive innovation and economies of scale, shortening the period of higher costs rather than locking in endless subsidies or pilot projects.
[P1.4] Third, the cost objection is weaker than it sounds. Shipping is a small fraction of the final price of most goods, from electronics to clothing, so even a meaningful rise in freight spreads thinly across consumers. Compare that to the costs already imposed by climate disruption on ports, supply chains, coastal states, and food imports. Paying somewhat more for freight is not an indulgence; it is paying the true price instead of dumping it on people who never chose the fuel.
[P1.5] Without a binding timetable, shipping will remain the sector everyone exempts and no one cleans.

[C1.1] A binding IMO timetable forcing universal low- and zero-carbon marine fuel use, regardless of cost, is a poorly targeted mandate that will fail to deliver promised emission cuts, inflict its harshest harms on the world’s most vulnerable people, and let the worst polluters evade accountability.

[C1.3] Second, claims that freight costs are a trivial share of final prices only hold for high-margin goods like smartphones and branded clothing. Even modest price hikes push staple food and medical supplies out of reach for import-dependent low-income countries and small island states that have no alternative to shipping, while the extra revenue flows straight to fuel producers rather than to communities harmed by maritime emissions.
[C1.4] Third, a rigid, early timetable locks in premature, unproven fuel pathways before green methanol, ammonia, and port electrification are available at global scale.
