[P1.1] When every approved antidepressant, every course of ECT, every protocol has failed, what does a patient with severe, treatment-resistant depression owe the state? Under the current narrow clinical-trial model, the answer is often: keep suffering, or take your chances underground. The proposition asks governments to permit carefully regulated psychedelic-assisted therapy outside those narrow trials for precisely these patients, and the case begins with a simple ethical fact: when a population has exhausted conventional care, the burden of proof shifts from access to exclusion.
[P1.2] Clinical trials are, by design, exclusionary. They screen out the most complex cases—multiple comorbidities, prior hospitalizations, unstable housing—precisely the patients most likely to benefit from a fundamentally different modality. A veteran with PTSD and a patient with chronic, refractory depression may both be excluded from the same protocol for different eligibility criteria, even though both are the intended beneficiaries. Proof of concept in a controlled trial is not proof that the therapy works for the sickest people in the general population.
[P1.3] To keep access confined to trials forever is to mistake a research instrument for a treatment delivery system.
[P1.4] The opposition will invoke safety, but the motion already incorporates the strongest safeguards: carefully regulated, trained clinicians, controlled settings, medical screening, and monitoring. This is not decriminalization; it is medicalization. Regulation lowers risk by replacing unsupervised use with clinical oversight, standardized dosing, and integration support.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, the status quo carries its own risks—suicide, chronic disability, and clandestine use in unsafe environments—that no regulator is counting.
[P1.6] Other high-risk treatments—transplants, deep brain stimulation, opioids for palliative care—are permitted outside trials under strict protocols. Psychedelic-assisted therapy deserves the same posture once a plausible evidentiary basis exists. For severe, treatment-resistant conditions, the choice is not between risk and safety; it is between managed access and continued neglect. Permit it.

[C1.1] The claim that the burden shifts “from access to exclusion” is exactly backwards. When every ordinary option has failed, patients are more vulnerable, not less, to an intervention with uncertain benefit and harm. Desperation justifies urgent research, fairer trial access, and protocol-driven data collection; it does not justify lowering the state’s threshold for permitting a new therapy. Refractory depression, PTSD, polypharmacy, mania history, and suicidality make the need for generalizable evidence stronger, not weaker.
[C1.2] PRO says trials exclude complex cases, then calls exclusion a reason for release. That inference collapses. If comorbidities, unstable housing, or prior hospitalizations were screened out, the evidence is weakest precisely where this motion would prescribe. A veteran with PTSD, a brain injury, and unstable housing is not a screened trial participant with extra paperwork.
[C1.3] Narrow trials do not show efficacy for the sickest patients; they show we do not yet know. Fix the trials: broader eligibility, pragmatic protocols, registries, long follow-up, independent review. Do not turn uncertainty into routine clinical permission and hope regulation produces knowledge afterward.
[C1.4] “Carefully regulated” also overpromises. This is not a tablet with stable dosing; it is a drug-induced altered state, hours of vulnerability, and a therapeutic relationship where suggestion, memory, and authority matter. Screening can miss bipolar or dissociative vulnerability. Monitoring cannot reliably prevent post-session destabilization, emotional dependency, memory distortion, or delayed psychotic or manic reactions.
[C1.5] A regulator can certify room, dose, and credentials; it cannot standardize a suggestible patient’s encounter with a powerful therapist, or guarantee that integration protects rather than persuades.
[C1.6] The high-risk comparisons fail: opioids and surgery have defined targets, familiar titration, and measurable endpoints; here, blinding is weak, therapist effects are large, and durability is unsettled. And “managed access versus continued neglect” is a false choice. Wider research networks and research-linked special-access pathways can help desperate patients without blessing prescribing outside research, inviting wellness-clinic drift, or making later caution look like cruelty. Keep the evidentiary fence.

That is a non sequitur. Missing evidence for complex PTSD, brain injury, unstable housing, and suicidality does not show therapy works there; it shows the state would be prescribing outside the validated population.
[C2.2] Urgency supports rolling enrollment, pragmatic eligibility, public funding, and fast research-linked access. It does not support making an unvalidated intervention a treatment system for people least able to absorb another destabilizing exposure.
[C2.3] “Mandatory data collection” [P2.2] is the key weakness. Collection after permission is not evidence before permission. In ordinary care there is no randomization, no stable comparator, weak blinding, strong expectancy, therapist attention, and heterogeneity. If someone improves, the clinic cannot tell whether the drug, hours of support, spontaneous fluctuation, or extra care did the work.
[C2.4] If someone deteriorates later, adverse-event forms cannot reliably separate delayed destabilization from the illness. So [P2.5]'s promise that regulators can collect evidence and suspend programs is too late: the very features that make this therapy hard to study are magnified once it is delivered as care.
[C2.5] A dangerous status quo raises the stakes; it does not relax the proof standard. Nor is this a demand for certainty; it is a demand for justified confidence in the population the motion names. ECT, clozapine, DBS, and palliative opioids have established targets, familiar dosing or procedure, monitored toxicities, and known decision rules [P2.4]. Psychedelic-assisted therapy asks the state to authorize a prolonged altered state, powerful therapist effects, suggestion, and unsettled durability.
[C2.6] Legal comparability is not evidentiary comparability.
[C2.7] The choice is not managed access versus neglect. It is evidence-producing access under protocol versus a public guarantee that is premature, hard to reverse, and prone to wellness drift. Desperate patients deserve the fastest honest route to knowledge, not uncertainty relabeled as care.
