[P1.1] Deterrence works by changing an adversary's cost-benefit calculus. Sustained military aid to Ukraine does that directly; an immediate negotiated settlement does the opposite—it confirms that aggression pays.
[P1.2] The core issue is what each option teaches Russia. A settlement pressed now freezes the territorial gains Russia has made, legitimizes force as a bargaining tool, and gives Moscow time to rebuild before the next expansion. We have seen this pattern before: the Minsk agreements did not stop Russian aggression—they paused it while Russia prepared a larger invasion. A settlement rushed under pressure repeats that exact cycle.
[P1.3] Sustained aid, by contrast, imposes continuous costs. Every month Ukraine holds the line with Western weapons, Russia bleeds manpower, equipment, and economic capacity. That is what deterrence actually looks like—not a document signed under duress, but a demonstrated reality that advancing further means unacceptable losses.
[P1.4] The argument for immediate negotiation assumes Russia bargains in good faith and sees a signed agreement as binding. There is no evidence for that assumption. Russia violated the Budapest Memorandum, violated Minsk, and framed its 2022 invasion as achieving goals that previous settlements were supposed to resolve. Negotiating with an aggressor who has not been defeated on the battlefield produces terms shaped by the current balance of force—not by justice or stability.
[P1.5] Critically, deterrence extends beyond Ukraine. If Russia consolidates gains and regroups, the Baltic states, Moldova, and other neighbors face a recalibrated threat. Sustained aid signals to Moscow that the West will not abandon frontline states and that every additional meter of aggression will be met with escalating cost.
[P1.6] A settlement ends a battle but starts a countdown. Sustained aid ends the illusion that Russia can wait the West out. The only durable deterrent is making aggression too expensive to repeat—and only sustained military support achieves that.
