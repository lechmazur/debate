[P1.2] First, these products collapse the natural action-outcome lag that acts as a universal brake on impulsive gambling. Ultra-fast products erase this gap entirely: in-play betting lets users wager on the next play, corner, or minute of a live sports match while they watch it unfold, and instant “crash” casino games resolve in under 10 seconds.
[P1.3] There is no time for second thoughts; a user can place 20 successive micro-bets during a single 90-minute football match, chasing small losses each time, before they process how much they’ve spent.
[P1.4] Second, these products are embedded in high-arousal contexts that amplify impulsivity. When your team is moments away from a game-winning goal, the platform’s push notification to bet on the next scoring opportunity bypasses rational decision-making entirely.
[P1.5] Regulatory bodies in the UK and Australia have already documented this distinct harm: a disproportionate share of acute, large single-session gambling losses come from ultra-fast products, even though these products make up a small fraction of overall gambling spend. Opponents may claim all gambling is harmful, but that argument ignores the motion’s core premise: harm profiles differ, so regulation must too. We don’t regulate bicycles and semi-trucks the same way just because both are vehicles, and we shouldn’t apply identical rules to a weekly lottery ticket and a game designed to drain your bank account in 10 minutes flat.

[C1.6] Finally, the bicycle-and-truck analogy refutes its own side. We don't ban fast trucks; we set speed limits for everyone and license risky drivers more tightly. Universal rules, targeted enforcement on risky behavior — that is precisely the model this motion asks governments to abandon.
