[P1.1] The EU’s current system is not neutral; it assigns the cost of protection by geography. A person arriving in Greece, Italy, Spain or Malta becomes that state’s problem, even though the legal promise being tested is European. Asylum is a common obligation. When one state’s border becomes the EU’s asylum waiting room, the system has already failed.
[P1.2] Mandatory burden-sharing quotas fix a collective-action failure. Frontline states cannot absorb arrival spikes alone, and expecting them to manage most arrivals does not produce control. It produces overcrowded camps, long delays, pushbacks and political backlash that undermines both Schengen and asylum rights. Quotas do not create new arrivals.
[P1.3] They distribute responsibility for people already in the EU according to capacity—population, GDP, unemployment and past intake—so no state is left to fail alone.
[P1.4] That distribution is also more effective, not merely fairer. Predictable relocation lets receiving municipalities plan housing, language support and integration in advance. It reduces the incentive for abusive border practices because frontline states are no longer forced to choose between absorbing every claim or violating rights. It also weakens secondary movement: if fair allocation is enforced, an asylum seeker has less reason to cross several countries to find a functioning process.
[P1.5] Quotas are not charity from north to south. They are cost-sharing for a legal regime the whole EU claims to uphold. Unless protection obligations are shared, the EU will keep lurching between crises in which the most exposed states pay the highest price for a common promise. The real choice is not between quotas and national control; it is between managed shared responsibility and recurring collapse at the external border.
