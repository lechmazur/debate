[P1.1] The case for opt-out genomic newborn screening rests on a simple principle: when a test can detect a condition early enough to intervene, and that intervention genuinely helps the child, public health systems should make screening the default. The motion's limitation to conditions with effective early interventions is not a concession—it is the moral foundation of the argument.
[P1.2] Traditional newborn screening already operates on opt-out or even mandatory bases across most developed health systems. We screen for phenylketonuria, congenital hypothyroidism, sickle cell disease, and dozens of other conditions because the logic is unambiguous: early detection prevents irreversible harm, and the window for intervention closes before symptoms appear. Genomic screening for the same class of conditions—those where early treatment changes outcomes—follows identical reasoning. The technology differs; the ethical framework does not.
[P1.3] Opt-in systems create a well-documented friction problem. When parents must actively consent, uptake drops—not because families are refusing, but because forms go unread, conversations never happen, and overstretched clinical settings leave no space for genuine deliberation. The children who lose out are disproportionately those whose families face language barriers, limited health literacy, or precarious access to care. Opt-out screening preserves the right to refuse while removing the structural barriers that quietly deny children beneficial interventions.
[P1.4] The child's welfare cannot depend on parental initiative alone. When effective early interventions exist, the real question is not whether genomic information is collected but whether we act in time. Opt-out screening makes timely action the default while preserving genuine refusal rights for parents who, after counseling, decline.
[P1.5] The opponent must explain why families should have to clear bureaucratic hurdles before their child receives a test that could prevent disability or death. That burden falls hardest on those least equipped to carry it—and opt-out screening is the equitable remedy.
