[C1.1] A scarce curb needs rules, not a bidding contest. The choice is not dynamic prices or the disorder PRO describes. Cities can keep short loading and passenger pickups mostly free while reserving loading bays, limiting dwell times, and enforcing bans on double-parking. [C1.2] PRO’s central assumption is that commercial operators can readily change when and where they stop. But routing software cannot make a restaurant receive supplies while its doors are locked, or make a passenger’s medical appointment move outside rush hour. Deliveries and pickups often occur at particular places because that is where people need them. When the trip cannot change, the charge changes the bill, not the traffic.
[C1.3] And “commercial operators pay” does not establish who ultimately bears that bill. Platforms and delivery firms can seek to pass costs to customers, merchants, or drivers. A large fleet can spread charges across many deliveries; a small merchant cannot necessarily consolidate its way out of needing one urgent shipment. Dynamic charges make essential access less predictable and ration it by willingness to pay, which is not the same as public value.
[C1.4] PRO also bundles pricing together with the benefits of legal loading zones and enforcement. Those are separate policies. Marking a bay creates lawful loading space; a dwell limit creates turnover; enforcement keeps a bike lane clear. A payment system does none of those things by itself.
[C1.5] If enforcement is weak, charging lawful stops can leave illegal stopping attractive. If enforcement is strong, cities already possess the tool needed to make free, time-limited access function.
[C1.6] Neither a congestion charge nor a curb fee solves these constraints merely by being dynamic. The claimed gains depend on substantial behavior change; the burdens arise whenever a necessary trip is charged. Keep ordinary access mostly free, regulate scarce space directly, and do not mistake collecting money for clearing traffic.
