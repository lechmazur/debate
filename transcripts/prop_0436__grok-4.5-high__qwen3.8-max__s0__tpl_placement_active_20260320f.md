[P1.1] Food-delivery and reservation apps have become the default way millions choose where to eat. When those apps bury cheaper independent restaurants under layers of sponsored listings, they degrade the product users came for: reliable discovery of nearby options that fit their budget.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. A user opens an app hungry, intending to sort by distance or price. Instead of the neighborhood taquerias or the family Thai place two blocks away, top slots fill with chains and well-capitalized spots that paid for promotion. Independents without marketing budgets sink.
[P1.3] Users scroll past them or never see them, then pay more or settle for worse value. That is not neutral platforming; it is paid steering that makes cheaper nearby options harder to find by design.
[P1.4] This harms three groups at once. Consumers lose transparent choice and often money. Independent restaurants lose the visibility that proximity and ratings once delivered. Local food scenes thin out as cash flows to whoever can afford the bid, not whoever cooks best or charges least.
[P1.5] Platforms may call this ordinary advertising, yet ordinary advertising does not justify distortion so severe that ordinary search becomes unreliable.
[P1.6] The proposition does not ban ads. It draws a clear line: privilege must not reach the point where users struggle to locate cheaper independents nearby. Defaults can emphasize distance, price, and organic relevance. Sponsored badges can remain visible without drowning organic results.
[P1.7] Disclosure can be plain rather than buried. When apps cross that line, they stop serving discovery and start manufacturing demand for the highest bidder. That practice should end.
[P1.8] Users deserve apps that surface what they actually want. Independents deserve a fighting chance on merit. The market works better when the cheapest good option two streets over is easy to find, not an afterthought beneath the sponsored carousel.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a business model for a harm. Food apps are not public utilities required to rank restaurants by distance and price. They are competitive marketplaces that fund delivery logistics, reviews, maps, and customer support through advertising and commissions. Sponsored placement is how they keep the app free or cheap for users and how restaurants of all sizes buy a chance to be seen.
[C1.2] The motion’s trigger—“so heavily that users struggle”—is too vague to govern. Struggle compared with what? A first-time user, a determined bargain hunter, someone using filters? Every major app already offers price filters, distance filters, ratings, cuisine categories, and search.
[C1.3] If a user wants the cheapest nearby independent, the path is usually a tap or two, not an impossible scavenger hunt. Occasional clutter is not coercion. Users can compare apps, use maps, check storefronts, or simply walk outside.
[C1.4] Worse, the proposition would likely hurt independents. Visibility is scarce. A small restaurant with great food but no foot traffic can use sponsorship to appear above a chain on slow nights. Reservation apps in particular let independent chefs fill empty tables with targeted offers.
[C1.5] If sponsorship is throttled because a regulator fears users might “struggle,” platforms will monetize elsewhere: higher commissions, delivery fees, or subscription paywalls. Those costs are passed to restaurants and diners, and the smallest operators feel them most.
[C1.6] The real standard should be consumer access to meaningful choice, not mandated neutrality. As long as sponsored results are identifiable and organic listings remain reachable, advertising improves discovery by funding better search, photos, reviews, and delivery reliability. The proposition would replace that with an unworkable line and chill the investment that makes local options easier to find. Apps should remain free to sell prominence, provided they do not deceive users; that balance serves diners and independents better than a blunt rule against sponsorship.
