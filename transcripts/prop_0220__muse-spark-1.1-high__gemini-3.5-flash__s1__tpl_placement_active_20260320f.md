[P1.1] Retirement insecurity is not a problem of insufficient reward for saving. It is a problem of not saving at all.
[P1.2] Automatic enrollment solves the right problem; bigger tax deductions solves the wrong one.
[P1.3] For decades voluntary opt-in 401k-style systems have plateaued at participation rates around half to two-thirds of eligible workers, and far lower for low-income, young, and part-time employees. Switch the default to opt-out and participation jumps to over 90 percent and stays there. Not because people are forced - they can leave - but because inertia, present bias, and paperwork finally work for them instead of against them. That means money starts compounding in their 20s and 30s instead of their 40s, which is the single biggest driver of long-run security.
[P1.4] Increasing tax deductions does the opposite. A deduction is worth your marginal tax rate. For a household earning enough to worry about eviction if the car breaks down, that value is near zero. For a high earner, it's large, but that high earner is already saving.
[P1.5] The literature on tax-preferred accounts consistently finds most of the response is asset shifting — moving money from a taxable account to a tax-advantaged one — not new household wealth creation. You lose public revenue without creating new savers.
[P1.6] Long-run household financial security is measured by how many households arrive at old age with something, not by how much more something the top quintile holds. Auto-enrollment democratizes access: it builds emergency-adjacent balances through auto-escalation, reduces reliance on high-cost debt in old age, and disproportionately helps those who would otherwise have zero. Tax deductions concentrate benefits at the top where security already exists.
[P1.7] One policy expands the base, the other fattens the peak. Only the first increases security for households in general.

[C1.1] Long-run household financial security is not just about having a tiny, stagnant retirement account; it is about holistic financial resilience and maximizing capital accumulation. Auto-enrollment fails on two fronts: the anchoring trap and the liquidity crisis.
[C1.2] First, auto-enrollment anchors savers at dangerously low default rates, typically around three percent. Behavioral economics demonstrates that many employees treat these low defaults as an official recommendation of adequacy. Instead of proactively calculating their actual savings needs, they remain stuck at the low baseline. Consequently, auto-enrollment actually reduces long-run savings for middle-class workers who would have contributed far more under an active-choice, voluntary system incentivized by robust tax deductions.
[C1.3] Second, auto-enrollment compromises short-term liquidity, which destroys long-term security. Forcing low-income households into illiquid retirement accounts leaves them vulnerable to immediate financial shocks. When faced with an emergency, like a medical bill or car repair, these households are forced to either pay a ten percent early-withdrawal penalty to access their own money, or resort to high-interest credit card debt. Shuffling cash into restricted accounts at the expense of an emergency fund does not build security; it creates a debt trap.
[C1.4] In contrast, increasing tax deductions directly rewards active, intentional saving. By boosting the tax advantages of voluntary contributions, we supercharge the compounding power of every dollar saved. This incentive appeals directly to middle-class families, encouraging them to actively plan and save at rates far higher than a passive default. It preserves essential liquidity, fosters financial literacy, and builds substantial, self-directed wealth.
[C1.5] Auto-enrollment merely creates millions of small, dormant accounts that are frequently cashed out and penalized when workers change jobs. True financial security requires active engagement and robust capital growth, which only enhanced tax incentives can drive.
