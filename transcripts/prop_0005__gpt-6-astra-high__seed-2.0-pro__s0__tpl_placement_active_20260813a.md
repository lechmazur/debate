[P1.1] Prescription drugs should be chosen because they fit a patient’s medical needs, not because a manufacturer purchased the most persuasive campaign. Prohibiting pharmaceutical companies from advertising prescription medicines directly to consumers across mass media protects that distinction. The central question is not whether patients deserve information. It is whether paid product promotion improves the decisions that information should serve.
[P1.2] Advertising selects its message by commercial return, not clinical importance. A campaign can accurately describe a drug’s benefits while leaving patients without the comparison that matters: whether an older medicine, a cheaper alternative, a non-drug intervention, or no treatment would be better for them. Listing side effects does not repair that imbalance. A truthful sales pitch is still not a treatment assessment.
[P1.3] The prescription requirement does not eliminate this problem. It places the pressure inside the consultation. Imagine a patient requesting an advertised medicine when an inexpensive established treatment would be equally appropriate. The clinician can refuse, but must spend scarce appointment time correcting a preference deliberately manufactured outside the clinic.
[P1.4] If the clinician accommodates the request, the commercial distortion can become a recurring prescription cost. Neither outcome requires doctors to be careless or patients to be foolish.
[P1.5] The strongest defense of advertising is that it prompts people to seek help. But that benefit does not establish a need for branded promotion. Symptom education, independent health information, and outreach encouraging appropriate consultation can raise awareness without steering people toward a sponsor’s product. [P1.6] The prohibition should cover every mass-media format because the conflict does not disappear when a television commercial becomes a sponsored online video. Disclosure regulates what a promotion says; it does not change why particular treatments receive promotion. Preserve access to medicines and information, but remove the purchased pressure to turn an audience into customers.
