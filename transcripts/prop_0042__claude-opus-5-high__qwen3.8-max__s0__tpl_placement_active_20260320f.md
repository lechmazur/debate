[P1.1] Right now the price of a pound of beef is a lie. It reflects the feed, the labor, and the transport — but not the methane, not the nitrogen runoff in the drinking water downstream, not the antibiotic resistance bred in crowded barns, not the cardiovascular and colorectal disease burden that lands on public health systems decades later. Someone pays those costs. The question is only whether it's the person choosing to consume the product, or a stranger with contaminated well water and a child with asthma.
[P1.2] So the central clash is not "tax versus no tax." It is who pays. Today, the poor already pay — twice. They live nearer the feedlots and slaughterhouses.
[P1.3] They have the least insulation from the heat, floods, and crop failures that livestock emissions accelerate. And they get the worst diet-related disease outcomes with the least access to treatment. An untaxed meat sector is not a subsidy to the poor; it is a transfer from the vulnerable to the intensive producer, laundered through an artificially cheap shelf price.
[P1.4] The mechanism is ordinary and well understood. We tax tobacco and fuel not out of puritanism but because unpriced harm produces overconsumption. Meat is the largest remaining category of everyday consumption where the damage is enormous, diffuse, and completely absent from the price signal. Correct the signal and three things happen: consumption shifts at the margin toward chicken, legumes, and eggs; producers face real pressure to cut methane, improve manure management, and reduce herd intensity; and government collects revenue precisely proportional to the harm done.
[P1.5] That last point is what defeats the regressivity objection before it arrives. A meat externality tax generates money. That money can be returned — as direct rebates, expanded food assistance, subsidized fruit and vegetables, school meals. A household can face a higher beef price and still end the year with more purchasing power and better health.
[P1.6] The proposition accepts higher meat prices. It does not accept poorer households, and it doesn't have to.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to endorse a policy even if it makes food less affordable for lower-income households. That phrase is not a side effect; it is the concession. When the state chooses to raise the price of a basic source of calories and protein, it must show the burden will not fall on the people least able to bear it. This motion does not.
[C1.2] A full-externality meat tax is not a precise instrument. It would require governments to price methane, runoff, antibiotics, land-use change, and disease risk for every production system, then update those estimates as technology changes. That is not a clean Pigouvian tax; it is an invitation to lobbying, arbitrary administration, and litigation. A beef tax based on national averages punishes extensive and pasture-based producers alongside industrial feedlots, while imported meat may escape the tax entirely unless borders are policed with complex adjustments.
[C1.3] Production can move abroad, leaving lower prices elsewhere and the same global emissions.
[C1.4] The regressivity problem is not cured by promising rebates. The motion taxes production, but the price rise lands on consumers. Low-income households spend a larger share of income on food, have less ability to switch quickly, and often live in places where affordable substitutes are not equally accessible. If beef becomes expensive, the realistic replacement is not always lentils and vegetables; it may be cheaper ultra-processed food with its own health burdens.
[C1.5] Rebates also depend on future budgets and political continuity. A household facing higher grocery bills today cannot pay with a hypothetical transfer tomorrow.
[C1.6] PRO identifies real harms, but this motion chooses the bluntest possible tool. If poor communities bear pollution and disease costs, the remedy should not be another bill at the grocery store. Targeted regulation, subsidies for healthier and lower-impact foods, procurement standards, and income supports can reduce harm without forcing poor households to finance the transition through higher dinner prices.
