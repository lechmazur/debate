[P1.1] Indefinite immigration detention means locking someone in a cell with no trial, no sentence, and no end date—not because a court found them dangerous, but because their paperwork is unresolved. No liberal democracy would tolerate this for its own citizens. The motion asks us to end that anomaly and replace it with what actually works: presumptive release, individualized supervision, and structured case management.
[P1.2] Notice what the motion does not do. It does not abolish detention. Governments retain short-term detention for identity checks and removal logistics, and courts can still order continued custody where a specific, evidenced flight or safety risk exists. What it prohibits is detention without limit—warehousing people for months or years when there is often no realistic prospect of removal at all, because their home country won't issue documents or is unsafe to return to.
[P1.3] Detaining someone indefinitely while removal is impossible is not enforcement; it is punishment without conviction.
[P1.4] The affirmative case rests on three pillars. First, principle: liberty is the default in every other area of law. Even criminal defendants get bail hearings and time limits. Immigration status—a civil matter—cannot justify weaker protections than we give accused criminals.
[P1.5] Second, effectiveness: case-management alternatives consistently produce very high appearance and compliance rates across countries that have tried them, because most migrants have every incentive to see their case resolved. Reporting requirements, caseworkers, and legal support keep people in the system far more reliably than fear-driven detention that pushes people underground once released without support.
[P1.6] Third, cost and harm: detention is extraordinarily expensive per person per day—many times the cost of community supervision—and it produces documented psychological damage, especially to children and trauma survivors, that taxpayers ultimately absorb.
[P1.7] So the comparison is stark: a regime that is unlimited, punitive, and wasteful, versus one that is bounded, supervised, and effective. Any government serious about both rule of law and functional enforcement should choose the latter.

The proposition promises a trade that isn't on the table.
[C1.7] Indefinite detention without review is a real abuse and should be confined. The motion doesn't confine it—it abolishes the tool governments need for the cases that justify it, and substitutes a presumption the evidence cannot support.
