[P1.1] A pension promise is a ratio: years contributing versus years collecting. When these systems were designed, retirement was a short coda to a long working life. Today it is routinely a fifth or more of adulthood. Nothing in the financing changed to match that; the extra years were simply handed to retirees and billed to workers.
[P1.2] Indexing restores the ratio. Raising payroll taxes does not — it keeps the ratio broken and asks a shrinking cohort to pay more for it. That is the central clash: who pays for longevity, the people who receive it, or the smaller generation behind them?
[P1.3] Note the asymmetry in the levers. A higher retirement age converts a beneficiary into a contributor: it adds revenue and subtracts spending with one move. A payroll tax hike only adds revenue, and it does so by raising the price of hiring. Employers respond with fewer formal jobs, more contracting, more informality — which shrinks the very base you were taxing.
[P1.4] Heavy payroll burdens already push contributions into gray-market work. You cannot tax your way out of a demographic problem by discouraging the demography you need.
[P1.5] Denmark and the Netherlands have already legislated longevity links; the mechanism is not utopian, it is administrative. And an automatic rule depoliticizes what tax hikes never can: each increase arrives predictably, decades ahead, so people can plan, save, and adjust — rather than gambling on the next election.
[P1.6] The obvious objection is the warehouse worker who cannot last until 70. That is an argument for indexing the age while protecting the person: disability pathways, actuarially fair early exit, hardship exemptions for long careers. It is not an argument for making a 25-year-old on a modest wage fund an ever-longer retirement for everyone else.

Today’s smaller cohort produces vastly more output per worker than past generations did. Even that twenty-five-year-old [P1.6] is better off paying slightly higher contributions during prime working years than facing a broken body and a locked pension door at sixty-five.
