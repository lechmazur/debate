[P1.1] Fast-fashion marketplaces turn speed and low prices into a model that shifts hidden risks onto consumers and workers. Consumers may receive garments of uncertain material or quality; workers may face dangerous conditions behind layers of subcontractors. If some ultra-cheap cross-border shopping slows, that is decisive. When a model stays profitable only by making safety unverifiable and labor invisible, its low price is not an efficiency; it is an externalized cost.
[P1.2] Two duties directly address this. Product-safety rules must follow the product: platforms should require risk-appropriate testing, traceable manufacturer and importer information, and effective recall and complaint systems, especially for children’s garments and other high-risk goods. Because marketplaces control listings, rankings, payments, and data, they can identify repeat offenders, remove unsafe items, and preserve evidence for regulators. Risk-based duties make greater volume and platform influence carry greater responsibility, rather than imposing an arbitrary tax on commerce.
[P1.3] Second, labor transparency should require production-site and key-subcontractor disclosure, audit findings, corrective actions, confidential worker grievance channels, and anti-retaliation safeguards. Perfect public disclosure is unnecessary: traceable production and credible due diligence expose hidden hazards, focus enforcement, and let buyers reward safer factories. They also prevent platforms from profiting from an “unseen supply chain.”
[P1.4] Why regulate platforms, not only brands? They present themselves as trusted gateways, process transactions, and earn on volume. A seller cannot call the platform an innocent postbox while the platform controls what is displayed, recommended, and paid. If platforms capture the trust benefit, law should not let them evade corresponding accountability.
[P1.5] The slowdown is targeted: more paperwork, fewer unverifiable listings, and greater visibility before an unsafe product or abusive factory reaches consumers. Rules may raise prices or reduce assortment, but they convert hidden risk into visible cost. They also preserve genuine competition: safe products and decent work should not finance a race to the bottom. The burden falls hardest on opaque ultra-cheap sellers—precisely where consumer protection is weakest.
