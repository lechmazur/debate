[P1.1] Public money is not a private donation; it is a public trust. When a government funds religious schools, it is not doing religion a favor—it is paying for an educational public good. The only responsible rule is the one this proposition states: fund such schools only when they meet strong civic, curricular, and non-discrimination standards comparable to public schools. That condition is the price of admission, not a penalty on faith.
[P1.2] Civic and curricular standards protect the child, not the state. A student in a tax-funded school must leave able to read, reason, compete in the modern economy, and understand the rights and duties of citizenship. That means a real curriculum—science, math, history, literature—and civic education that teaches pluralism rather than contempt for outsiders. A publicly funded school that erases the Holocaust, denies evolution, or tells children that adherents of other faiths or no faith are less worthy is not offering a religious alternative; it is using public funds to produce ignorant and divided citizens.
[P1.3] Non-discrimination standards protect the community’s money from being used against it. Public schools cannot expel a child for being gay, fire a teacher for leaving the faith, or refuse enrollment to families of another religion. A religious school taking state funds should be held to the same baseline. Otherwise taxpayers become involuntary subscribers to practices many find unconscionable, and government subsidizes a parallel system of exclusion.
[P1.4] The real contrast is stark. Unconditional funding lets public money entrench segregation and inequality; a blanket ban discards schools that often serve poor neighborhoods and already teach solid secular material. The proposition avoids both errors: religious communities keep their ethos and worship, while the publicly funded educational core plays by common rules. Any opponent must show either that these common rules are impossible for a serious religious school to accept, or that even a school meeting them must still be denied public funds.
[P1.5] Neither survives.

[C1.1] The proposition looks like a reasonable compromise, but “comparable to public schools” is not a floor—it is a template. Public schools are not one neutral model; their curricula, civic instruction, hiring rules, and sex-education policies vary by district and state. “Comparable” therefore hands officials open-ended discretion to decide which religious school is sufficiently like the majority’s school. That is not a safeguard; it is a licensing regime for conscience.
[C1.2] Consider a serious religious school that teaches reading, math, science, history, and constitutional government well, but hires co-religionists and teaches sexual ethics according to its faith. Under a genuinely comparable non-discrimination and curricular standard, it can be told that its hiring policy or doctrine makes it not comparable. To qualify, it must become a public school with optional prayer. The proposition does not separate “core academics” from mission; it says strong civic, curricular, and non-discrimination standards comparable to public schools.
[C1.3] Those standards reach hiring, admissions, student speech, and contested values. The condition is not a price of admission; it is a demand to surrender the thing that makes the school religious.
[C1.4] The false choice is unconditional funding or this proposition. Governments can attach narrow, neutral, outcome-based conditions: safety, financial accountability, basic literacy and numeracy, and teaching the constitutional rights of others. Those protect children and taxpayers without making funding contingent on sameness. PRO’s horror examples—Holocaust denial, contempt for other faiths—are answered by basic academic and civic standards, not by a demand for public-school comparability.
[C1.5] A school can teach evolution, constitutional rights, and financial literacy while retaining religious worship and a faith-based anthropology. The proposition goes further and makes the school’s distinctiveness itself suspect.
[C1.6] The real question is whether “comparable” is a neutral safeguard or a majoritarian filter. It is a filter—and one that favors well-resourced schools able to document and litigate their way to approval. Reject the proposition.
