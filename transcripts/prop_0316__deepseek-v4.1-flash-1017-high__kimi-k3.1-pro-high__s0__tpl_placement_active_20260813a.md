[P1.1] Romantic opportunity is not a trivial interface feature. When a dating app decides whose profile is placed in front of whom, it is allocating attention, social contact, and sometimes safety. If an AI system ranks profiles or shapes exposure, users are not browsing a neutral directory; they are being sorted by a machine whose criteria they cannot see. The proposition asks for something modest: a right to know that this is happening and, at a meaningful level, how.
[P1.2] Not source code. Not a veto. Notice.
[P1.3] That matters because invisibility converts a design choice into a personal verdict. Two users with similar profiles can have wildly different reach because one is predicted to generate more engagement. Without notice, the low-reach user interprets silence as rejection and may buy upgrades to fix a problem created by ranking. Disclosure lets people make informed choices, avoid wasting money on a rigged game, and challenge errors that suppress them.
[P1.4] Secrecy also makes discrimination nearly impossible to detect. If an app shapes who gets shown using inferred traits or proxies for race, gender, age, or attractiveness, the people affected cannot even know they are in a sorted category. Notice and a meaningful description of inputs give users, researchers, and regulators a starting point to ask whether exposure is fair. Without it, bias is invisible by design.
[P1.5] The strongest objection is that ranking models are proprietary and complex. But the right is to know, not to audit every weight. We already accept that consequential automated decisions need plain-language disclosure. Dating apps can explain that AI ranks profiles and describe the kinds of signals used without handing over trade secrets.
[P1.6] A company may keep its model secret; it may not keep the fact that it is reshaping your social world secret from you. The right to know is the minimum condition for consent, dignity, and accountability.
