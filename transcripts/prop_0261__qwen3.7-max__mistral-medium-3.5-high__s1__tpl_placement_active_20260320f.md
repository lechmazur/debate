[P1.1] The law must adapt to protect property rights in the digital age. A book is a book, whether printed on paper or rendered in pixels. We must guarantee that purchased digital assets pass to next of kin because the current system relies on a legal fiction that unjustly strips consumers of their property.
[P1.2] When a consumer clicks "buy" on an e-book, a digital movie, or a video game, they reasonably believe they are purchasing an asset. However, tech platforms use boilerplate Terms of Service to reclassify these transactions as non-transferable licenses. This is a corporate strategy to prevent secondary markets, avoid royalty payouts, and maximize platform lock-in. Inheritance law should not be held hostage by adhesion contracts designed to artificially restrict consumer rights.
[P1.3] Guaranteeing digital inheritance pierces this corporate veil and restores property parity.
[P1.4] Consider the physical equivalent. When someone dies, their physical bookshelf, vinyl collection, and DVD library pass seamlessly to their heirs. [P1.5] If the law allows a physical book to be inherited, it must allow its digital counterpart to be inherited.
[P1.6] Furthermore, digital libraries represent significant financial and sentimental value. A lifetime of purchasing digital media can amount to thousands of dollars and decades of careful curation. Under the status quo, when a user dies, this wealth evaporates. The platform keeps the money while revoking access, resulting in unjust enrichment.
[P1.7] Guaranteeing transferability to next of kin ensures digital wealth is treated with the same respect as physical wealth. It aligns the law with consumer expectations, prevents corporate overreach, and protects a family's right to inherit the legacy their loved one paid for.

When you “buy” an e-book or digital movie, you acquire a non-exclusive, non-transferable license to access a copyrighted work. Inheritance law cannot override copyright without destroying the incentives that produce digital content.
[C1.2] Forcing platforms to transfer licenses would breach their contracts with rights holders, triggering mass lawsuits and the withdrawal of content from digital storefronts.
[C1.3] The privacy implications are even more alarming. Digital accounts are vast repositories of intimate data: search histories, location tracks, private messages, even biometric information and health data from connected devices. Mandating inheritance forces platforms to hand over these accounts to heirs, regardless of the deceased’s wishes or the sensitivity of the data. There is no practical way to separate the licensed content from the personal data without breaking the service.
[C1.4] Technical workarounds are impossible: DRM, device authentication, and account-based entitlements are fundamentally incompatible with transfer.
[C1.5] Economically, the proposition backfires spectacularly. Digital goods are priced low because the license is limited to the original user. If assets become inheritable, creators will demand higher royalties to account for perpetual access, raising prices for every consumer. Worse, many may abandon digital sales entirely, fearing the legal and technical chaos.
[C1.6] The current system delivers affordability and convenience; the proposed law delivers higher costs, fewer choices, broken services, and a flood of lawsuits from rights holders.
[C1.7] Finally, the physical book analogy fails. A physical book is a static, standalone object with no ongoing relationship to its creator. Digital content is a dynamic service—constantly updated, patched, and delivered through an ecosystem that assumes a single, original account holder. The law must respect this fundamental difference.
