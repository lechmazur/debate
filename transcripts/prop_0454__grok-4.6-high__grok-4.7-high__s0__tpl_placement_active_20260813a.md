[P1.1] When a plant closes, paychecks stop the same week. Families cut groceries and rent, nearby shops lose sales, and more workers are let go. Recessions are that cascade. Automatic cash transfers that expand as unemployment rises interrupt it at the source, putting money in the hands of people who just lost work before the next round of layoffs.
[P1.2] Discretionary stimulus cannot match that speed. Legislatures argue, bills stall, and funds arrive after firms have failed and workers have left the labor force. An automatic rule needs no new vote. When unemployment crosses a threshold, cash goes out.
[P1.3] When jobs recover, the extra transfers shrink. That two-way design makes it a stabilizer, not a permanent expansion.
[P1.4] Those recipients spend nearly everything they get. Lost wages are rent, food, and debt service, not savings. That high propensity to consume props up demand where it is collapsing, shielding other jobs and the tax base. A deeper slump scars workers, closes businesses, and shrinks revenues for years.
[P1.5] Extra public debt during the downturn is the insurance premium against that damage. Refusing the outlay does not keep the books clean; it often leaves a smaller economy and a worse debt-to-GDP path.
[P1.6] In a typical downturn, private demand is missing and interest rates fall. Markets absorb more government paper more cheaply. Cyclical debt that protects GDP is fiscally saner than a rigid budget that lets output crater. Waiting, then scrambling with late packages, produces both more unemployment and more lasting fiscal harm.
[P1.7] This rule does not authorize endless borrowing in expansions; it pays when unemployment rises and stops when it falls.
[P1.8] Governments should lock the rule in now. The alternative is delay, under-insurance, and a worse path for jobs and the public finances.

[C1.1] Automatic cash transfers that swell with unemployment trade a short-term spending bump for longer joblessness and heavier debt. That is not stabilization; it is a ratchet.
[C1.2] People who just lost work need income, but unconditional cash replaces the wage without requiring search, training, or acceptance of available jobs. When benefits track the unemployment rate itself, the policy subsidizes the duration of joblessness. Generous replacement of lost wages has often lengthened spells out of work rather than hastened return. A cascade interrupted on paper can become a plateau in the labor market.
[C1.3] The two-way design does not protect the books. Downturns often leave unemployment elevated for years through mismatch and lost skills. Political pressure then freezes the higher transfers or raises the trigger, so the “temporary” debt never fully reverses. Interest compounds while private investment is crowded out.
[C1.4] Once debt ratios are already high, markets do not reliably absorb extra paper more cheaply; risk premia rise and future taxes are priced in today, damping the demand the transfers were meant to support.
[C1.5] Shock type matters. If unemployment rises from collapsed demand, some transfer may help. If it rises because supply is constrained—energy shocks, pandemics, sectoral collapse—extra cash bids up scarce goods, feeds inflation, and erodes real wages for those still working. An automatic rule cannot tell the difference.
[C1.6] Discretion, slow as it is, can be sized and conditioned to the cause. Existing unemployment insurance already expands with job loss, but with time limits and search rules this proposal would bypass.
[C1.7] Extra public debt is not a free insurance premium. It is a claim on future output. When transfers dull reemployment and invite permanence, that claim grows faster than GDP. Governments should not lock in a rule that makes the next recession more expensive and the recovery weaker.

[P2.3] [C1.2] treats the trigger as an individual work disincentive. A rate-based stabilizer is a macro switch. [P2.4] The rate trigger fills the demand hole those rules cannot.

[P2.6] [C1.4] and [C1.7] only bite if the transfers fail to protect GDP. Crowding-out and risk premia are weakest when private spending and investment are already collapsing. A rigid budget that lets output crater still raises the debt ratio, only with more scarring.

Discretion that can be "sized and conditioned to the cause" arrives after shops have closed. [P2.8] The rule does not pretend every shock is identical; it refuses to wait for a bill once unemployment is rising.
[P2.9] Their alternative is slower aid, a deeper cascade, and debt without the output it was supposed to buy.
