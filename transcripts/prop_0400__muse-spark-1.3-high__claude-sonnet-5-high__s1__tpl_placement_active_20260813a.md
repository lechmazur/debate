[P1.1] When the government cuts disability benefits, denies asylum, or recommends a prison term, it is exercising coercive power that can destroy a life. That power is legitimate only if a human being takes responsibility for it. A right to a human decision-maker draws that line.
[P1.2] Automated systems are powerful pattern-matchers, not judges. They flatten lives into proxies: gaps in work history become fraud risk, a mis-scanned document becomes an immigration red flag, a zip code policed more heavily becomes higher criminal risk. Errors that would be isolated mistakes by a caseworker become mass denials when coded once and run on millions. Several benefits agencies have already had to reinstate payments after algorithms wrongly flagged ordinary claimants.
[P1.3] Without a human with authority to decide differently, there is no brake.
[P1.4] Only a human can do what high-stakes justice requires: hear an excuse, weigh remorse, recognize an exceptional circumstance the training data never saw. A machine can estimate probability; it cannot grant mercy, interpret ambiguous law in light of purpose, or say, this rule produces an absurd result here. Due process has always meant a chance to be heard by someone who can actually listen and change the outcome.
[P1.5] This right does not ban automation. Governments could still use software to sort files, draft decisions, catch errors, and speed routine cases. It simply requires that for decisions with serious consequences, a person makes the final call, gives reasons, and can be appealed to and held accountable. That is narrow, workable, and already how courts and senior officials function.
[P1.6] Efficiency is real, but it is the wrong master. A faster system that deports the wrong person or leaves a sick person without income for months has not saved money; it has multiplied harm while hiding who chose it. Consistency matters, but uniformly applying a flawed model to everyone is not fairness.
