[P1.1] A noncompete is not merely a private promise; it is a demand that government help one employer limit where a person may earn a living after leaving. That coercive power should be exceptional. The right rule is a strong restriction covering nearly all workers, with only narrow, role-specific allowances—not broad exemptions for whole industries, occupations, or vaguely defined “knowledge workers.”
[P1.2] The central harm is suppressed mobility. A nurse unable to join another hospital, a salesperson barred from serving the same market, or an engineer threatened for moving to a rival has less leverage to demand better pay, hours, or treatment. Even clauses unlikely to survive court review can chill movement because workers often lack the money, time, and legal confidence to test them. Employers, meanwhile, face less pressure to retain staff through good conditions.
[P1.3] Across an economy, that means weaker wage competition, poorer matching between workers and jobs, and fewer new firms able to recruit experienced talent.
[P1.4] Broad employer exceptions would swallow the rule. Almost every employer can describe ordinary experience as “confidential,” training as an “investment,” or customer familiarity as a “relationship.” If those labels create categorical exemptions, the employers with the strongest lawyers will convert a general protection into paperwork. [P1.5] Legitimate business interests do not require controlling a former employee’s entire livelihood. Trade-secret law addresses stolen secrets. Nondisclosure rules protect genuinely confidential information. Non-solicitation provisions can protect specific customer relationships.
[P1.6] Employers can also retain workers through higher pay, deferred compensation, or agreements tied to the sale of a business. These tools target misconduct or compensate commitment; noncompetes instead prohibit lawful competition before any wrongdoing occurs.
[P1.7] The decisive comparison is simple: employers may lose a convenient retention device, but workers regain bargaining power and the economy regains movement, competition, and entrepreneurship. Government should protect specific secrets, not grant broad ownership over human beings’ future work.
