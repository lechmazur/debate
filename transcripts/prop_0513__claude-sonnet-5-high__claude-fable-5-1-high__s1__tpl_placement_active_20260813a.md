[P1.1] Every merger review asks one question twice: does this deal let firms raise prices, or does it let firms cut what they pay? Antitrust has spent seventy years perfecting tools for the first question and almost none for the second. That asymmetry is the case for today's motion, and it is the case I intend to win.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. A merger of two hospital systems in a mid-sized region doesn't just risk higher prices for patients—it also lets the combined entity become the only real employer of nurses, radiology techs, and anesthesiologists in that labor market. Workers can't "shop around" any more than patients can, but unlike patients, workers face a market with high switching costs baked in: licensing that's often state-specific, relocation costs, non-compete clauses, and thin local demand for their specific skill. A merger that leaves consumer prices untouched can still let the new firm suppress wages 5-10% below competitive levels, simply because there's nowhere else to sell your labor.
[P1.3] Truckers, meatpacking workers, and teachers in single-employer towns face exactly this dynamic.
[P1.4] The current framework misses this systematically, not accidentally. Standard HHI-based merger review defines markets on the output side—hospital beds, retail footprint, product substitutability—and treats the labor side as a cost input to be minimized, which agencies then reward rather than scrutinize. A merger that concentrates buying power over labor while leaving output prices flat sails through review today. That's not a neutral gap; it's a policy that treats one form of market power as illegal and the mirror-image form as invisible.
[P1.5] The asymmetry is also indefensible on welfare grounds. A dollar taken from a worker's paycheck through suppressed wages is exactly as real a welfare loss as a dollar taken from a consumer's wallet through inflated prices. Workers are disproportionately the same people who are consumers—so a framework that protects them only in one role while ignoring harm in the other is arbitrary, not principled.

[C1.1] The motion doesn't ask whether workers matter. It asks whether merger review should weigh a speculative, hard-to-define labor effect exactly as heavily as the price effect it was built to measure. It shouldn't, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the two harms are not symmetric in detectability. A product market has a definable boundary: hospital beds in a county, cement within trucking distance. A labor market has no such edge. A radiology tech can become a sales rep; a meatpacker can drive a forklift at a warehouse; a cashier can work for any employer in town.
[C1.3] Employers compete for workers across industries, so labor markets are usually far less concentrated than the product markets the same firms sell into. Demanding "equal rigor" in a domain where the boundaries dissolve doesn't produce rigor—it produces litigated guesswork dressed up as HHI tables.
[C1.4] Second, parity creates a standard that cannot decide cases. Picture a grocery merger that credibly lowers prices three percent for half a million shoppers while narrowing employer options for two thousand stockers. Today the answer is clear. Under parity, the agency must weigh diffuse gains against concentrated losses with no exchange rate—and every merger that generates efficiencies by consolidating headcount becomes presumptively suspect.
[C1.5] That chills exactly the productivity-raising deals that lift wages over time.
[C1.6] Third, monopsony has better remedies than blocking mergers. Non-competes can be banned outright. No-poach and wage-fixing agreements are already illegal collusion. Labor law, minimum wages, and unions exist precisely to counter employer bargaining power—consumers facing a monopolist have no comparable institution.
[C1.7] Merger review is a once-only, blunt instrument; using it to fix wage suppression is like using zoning to fix rent.
[C1.8] The current asymmetry isn't neglect. It reflects that price effects are frequent, measurable, and universal, while merger-driven wage suppression is rare, contested, and better addressed elsewhere. Elevating the second to equal footing would make merger review slower, less predictable, and worse at the one thing it demonstrably does well.

And if a merger really does lower prices for half a million shoppers while gutting wages for two thousand stockers, that's not an unanswerable dilemma—it's a case where the harm should at least be counted before agencies wave it through, not one where invisibility is preferable to difficulty.
