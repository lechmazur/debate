[P1.1] Agricultural land is not merely another financial asset. It is the productive base of a food system, a community’s fixed asset, and the means by which new farmers can enter. The risk arises when many tracts are controlled by institutions whose incentives are set by distant capital markets rather than by the land or the people dependent on it.
[P1.2] That separation creates incentives. Institutional buyers can use leverage and bundle parcels, outbid farm families, raise the capital needed to enter farming, and acquire land for future development or financial gain. Some lease land to capable farmers, but concentrated ownership still weakens tenants’ bargaining power and makes access for new operators depend on a few powerful owners. Even if individual holdings are well run, their combined effect is to concentrate control.
[P1.3] Rural communities bear costs absent from investors’ returns. Farmland conversion can hollow out local businesses; soil and water depletion externalize damage; and a sudden sale or change of use can destabilize production. A system may show respectable yields while becoming less diverse, less locally rooted, and more exposed to one owner’s decision to exit or redevelop.
[P1.4] A well-designed ownership cap would not ban agricultural investment. Institutions could still finance farms through leases, debt, or equity, and productive tenants could remain. The cap would limit capture of farmland, preserve land for independent farmers, keep land access more competitive, and reduce dependence on absentee portfolio managers. Transparent thresholds and enforcement against concealment would make it substantive, not cosmetic.
[P1.5] The likely objection is that this sacrifices efficiency for an abstract ideal. But efficiency measured only by current output ignores resilience, entry, and costs imposed on communities. If the food system collapses when distant capital withdraws, its apparent efficiency was fragility. A cap trades a limited share of unrestricted financial ownership for diversified ownership, fairer land access, and durable rural communities.
[P1.6] That is prudent protection, not an attack on investment.

[P2.1] The opposition’s main claim is a false choice: either regulate conduct or cap ownership. Resilience depends on behavior and structure. Rules against conversion, extraction, and unfair leases tell owners what not to do; they do not stop lawful institutions from accumulating enough farmland to outbid farm families and shape tenants’ access. The motion need not make a cap replace stewardship rules.
[P2.2] Consider a fund that owns a large block of prime farmland and obeys every environmental and leasing rule. It may never pollute or convert a field, yet its presence raises the price families face and gives a few managers extraordinary influence over land availability. That is the structural harm the opposition ignores. Ownership is not a moral category; institutional concentration is the relevant variable, and a cap directly limits it.
[P2.3] The lease-market answer also fails. Divested parcels need not go to incumbent large farmers; governments can pair the cap with transparent sales, community purchase rights, or purchase priorities for new operators. More fundamentally, institutions that genuinely supply land to entrants can do so through long leases, debt, or equity without owning the soil. A cap removes title, not capital or access.
[P2.4] The supposed patient-capital buffer is also doubtful: if many funds hold large blocks, they may react to the same financial pressures by retreating or shifting land use together, creating the synchronized shock diversification is meant to prevent. Patient financing can remain available without patient ownership. Nor is avoidance a unique defect: enforcement against beneficial ownership, options, and control agreements is required for any land rule, conduct-based or cap-based.
[P2.5] Finally, the opposition attacks the cap as though it were the sole safeguard. Conduct regulation and ownership caps answer different questions: one disciplines decisions; the other prevents excessive control. Governments need both, but only the cap addresses the proposition’s central risk. The opposition’s preferred tools may complement a cap; they do not make it unnecessary.

A pension fund absorbing a bad year is a buffer; a cap removes it while doing nothing about a leveraged family buyer. Resilience comes from making control transparent and conduct accountable, not from an arbitrary ratio that can be evaded by an option and circumvented by a lease. That is the decisive comparison: a cap changes who is visible, not who decides.
