[P1.1] Routine does not mean a pig organ in every hospital or the end of human donation. It means an approved, reimbursed, audited option at specialist centers, used regularly for patients unlikely to get a human organ. On that standard, fifteen years is credible: organ shortage is a supply problem, and genetically modified pigs turn organ supply into a controlled manufacturing platform.
[P1.2] Human donors depend on death, geography, matching, and luck. A pig herd can be bred to a fixed genotype, screened for pathogens, scheduled for retrieval, and edited in layers: removing sugar antigens behind hyperacute rejection, adding human complement and clotting regulators, controlling growth. That does not erase chronic rejection or infection; it converts biological mismatch into iterative engineering. Recent experimental and compassionate-use implants of pig kidneys and hearts show short-term function.
[P1.3] The next fifteen years are about durability, scale, and protocols, not proving possibility.
[P1.4] The cautious case says risk: porcine viruses, zoonoses, coagulopathy, immunosuppression, welfare, cost. These are reasons for tight regulation, not freezing the field. The status quo has its own ledger: dialysis years, deterioration on waiting lists, repeated admissions, deaths before a match. A controlled pathway can require pathogen-screened herds, animal and recipient testing, registries, informed consent, lifelong surveillance, and stopping rules.
[P1.5] That is safer than scattered one-offs producing publicity but weak comparative data.
[P1.6] Accelerated means staged conditional licensing, not deregulation. Start with kidneys for highly sensitized or otherwise stranded patients, where function is measurable and dialysis can rescue failure; expand as one-year and longer outcomes accumulate. Trials, herd qualification, surgical training, reimbursement, and registries all take years. Waiting for perfect evidence now makes routine use within fifteen years implausible.
[P1.7] The choice is not perfect safety versus pig organs, but a scarce donor lottery versus a scalable, auditable platform whose risks fall with disciplined use. Accelerated pathways buy speed and control; caution without a pathway preserves the shortage and postpones the evidence needed to end it.

Gene edits remove sugar antigens and add human regulators, but they cannot rewrite decades of immunology. [C1.5] Tight regulation is not bureaucratic caution; it is the only barrier between experiment and catastrophe.
[C1.6] Moreover, the choice is not pig organs or nothing. [C1.7] Keep the research going, but do not mistake a few surgeries for a platform. Accelerate nothing until durability, zoonosis control, and scale are proven, not merely possible.
