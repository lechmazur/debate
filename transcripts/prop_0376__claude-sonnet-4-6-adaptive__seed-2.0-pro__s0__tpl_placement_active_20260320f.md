[P1.1] The proposition is blunt by design: cap rates, accept the trade-off, defend the outcome. That bluntness is a feature, not a flaw, because it forces a real moral and empirical confrontation that the usual debate about consumer credit carefully avoids.
[P1.2] Start with the harm being addressed. Credit cards carrying 30, 35, or even 40 percent annual interest aren't pricing risk—they're extracting wealth. A borrower who carries a modest balance at those rates can pay more in interest over a few years than the original principal. That isn't financing a purchase; it's a structured transfer of money from people with thin margins to institutions with thick ones.
[P1.3] The law tolerates this because access is treated as a sufficient defense: someone lent to this person, therefore nothing was done wrong.
[P1.4] The motion rejects that reasoning, and it is right to do so. Access to a harmful product is not a benefit. If a cap causes lenders to stop offering 36-percent cards to the riskiest borrowers, what those borrowers lose is the opportunity to pay ruinous rates. That is not obviously a loss worth mourning.
[P1.5] Many will find other options—community banks, credit unions, employer advance programs, secured cards at lower limits—precisely because a rate cap redirects capital toward more sustainable lending models rather than high-margin extraction.
[P1.6] The CON case will rest heavily on the argument that cutting credit access harms the very people we want to protect. But that argument assumes that high-rate credit reliably improves their lives. The evidence on debt spirals suggests otherwise. People don't primarily get trapped in 30-percent card debt because of emergencies they could plan around; they get trapped because the interest compounds faster than they can repay.
[P1.7] The real question isn't "access or no access." It's "which floor do we insist the market respect?" Every functioning credit market has floors—fraud rules, disclosure requirements, bankruptcy protections. A rate cap is simply another floor.
[P1.8] The debate is about where to draw it.
