[P1.1] Central districts are the one place on earth where driving a private car imposes the largest cost on everyone else per mile traveled. That is the entire case, and it is why driver opposition, however strong, cannot be the deciding factor.
[P1.2] Start with the mechanism. Congestion is a classic tragedy of the commons: each driver entering a packed downtown grid barely feels the marginal delay they cause, but they impose it on hundreds of other drivers, bus riders stuck behind them, delivery trucks, ambulances, and pedestrians breathing exhaust. Nobody pays for this cost at the point of decision, so nobody weighs it. A price is simply the mechanism that makes the true cost visible before someone decides to drive downtown at 8:45am instead of taking the train, shifting an errand to noon, or carpooling.
[P1.3] Look at London and Stockholm. Both saw double-digit drops in central traffic within the first year of pricing, faster average speeds, and, critically, revenue that got reinvested into buses and transit that let lower-income commuters opt out of paying at all. That is the model: the fee doesn't just deter driving, it funds the alternative. Singapore has run a version of this for decades precisely because unpriced roads in a dense core degrade into gridlock that hurts everyone, including the drivers who oppose the toll.
[P1.4] Now the key point on "even when drivers strongly oppose it." Opposition is exactly what you'd predict from anyone facing a new, visible cost for a previously free good — homeowners opposed property tax reassessments, factories opposed pollution fines, and restaurants opposed indoor smoking bans. In every case the opposed group was the direct payer, not the neutral judge of net social benefit. Intensity of driver opposition measures how much drivers dislike paying, not whether the policy is good policy.
[P1.5] If we let the most vocal, most affected minority veto policies that produce diffuse benefits for cyclists, bus riders, asthma sufferers, and the drivers' own future commute, we guarantee cities never fix collective-action problems.

[C1.2] Their protest tells you they are captive, not selfish. Pricing captivity does not create efficiency; it extracts money for the crime of having to work downtown.
[C1.3] That is why congestion pricing in the real city is regressive. A downtown lawyer pays the toll from an expense account and enjoys an emptier road. A cleaner or delivery driver pays the same toll as a far larger share of wages, or accepts a two-bus, hour-longer day. The promise that revenue will someday fund brilliant transit is jam tomorrow.
[C1.4] The bill arrives today, for years, while in most cities there is no Stockholm-grade subway waiting as an alternative. You get to pay for the bus system you were promised after you already paid the toll.
[C1.5] Copying London or Singapore without copying their transit first gets the pain without the payoff. It also wounds the center itself. Small shops, clinics, and restaurants live on regional customers who drive because they must. Push them to suburban strips with free parking and you do not abolish driving; you relocate it, hollowing the downtown while traffic and exhaust pile up in the poorer ring neighborhoods just outside the cordon.
[C1.6] Durable reform needs consent. Build the frequent buses, bus lanes, off-peak delivery rules, and parking reform that expand choice, then ask drivers to pay. Ramming a toll through over loud objections buys evasion, backlash, and repeal, and poisons trust for the next climate policy. A city should not be faster for those who can pay and unworkable for those who keep it running.

[P2.1] CON's "captivity" framing collapses under its own logic. If commuters genuinely cannot shift time, mode, or route, then the toll does not change their behavior at all — it simply funds transit for everyone else while they keep driving. The regressivity complaint and the captivity complaint cannot both be true for the same trip. In reality, most peak-hour central traffic is discretionary — the second car in a two-car household, the errand that could move to 10am, the trip that could end at a park-and-ride.
[P2.2] Pricing targets exactly that margin, which is why London and Stockholm saw such large volume drops without downtown grinding to a halt for essential workers.
[P2.3] On regressivity: the status quo CON defends is already regressive. Free roads mean the plumber and the lawyer both sit in the same gridlock, but the bus rider — statistically poorer — pays the full cost in stolen time while the lawyer's time is worth more per hour and he's still driving. A price plus targeted exemptions for shift workers, disability plates, or low-income residents, funded by the toll itself, is strictly more progressive than "everyone waits in traffic equally, but the wait costs the poor more."
[P2.4] On "jam tomorrow": London's revenue funded bus service improvements within the same years the charge operated, not after some hypothetical subway. The toll and the alternative arrive together because the toll pays for it. Demanding transit "first" guarantees paralysis — no city funds decade-long transit expansion out of general taxes while congestion is free and growing.
[P2.5] On hollowing downtown: the empirical record from London and Stockholm is emptier roads and steady or rising foot traffic, not abandoned storefronts — because the customers who actually spend money downtown were never the ones idling in gridlock; they're the ones now able to get there faster by bus or bike.
[P2.6] And CON's "consent" standard is just driver veto power by another name — the same veto that kept smoking indoors and pollution unpriced for decades.
