[C1.2] Start with what the motion actually removes. PRO's own statistic concedes the problem: even a 90%-plus appearance rate under ideal, well-resourced case management still means roughly one in ten people do not show up.
[C1.3] Across a system processing hundreds of thousands of cases, that is not a rounding error — it is tens of thousands of people who vanish from the process, and they will not be a random sample. The people most likely to abscond are precisely the people with the strongest incentive to do so: those with the weakest claims to remain, or with records that make return most likely. A blanket presumptive-release rule systematically self-selects the highest-risk cases into disappearance.
[C1.4] The actual defect PRO describes is real but different from what they propose to fix it. Detention becoming "indefinite" — years without review — is a failure of judicial oversight and time limits, not proof that detention itself must be banned. A time-limited, individually-reviewed detention system, with automatic judicial check-ins and release defaults absent a specific, articulated risk finding, solves the exact harm PRO describes without stripping states of any tool for the hard cases.
[C1.5] Finally, case management has no teeth without a credible backstop. If the worst consequence of ignoring a check-in is nothing, compliance depends entirely on goodwill — precisely the goodwill absent in the cases where it matters most. Removing detention as an option doesn't perfect the system; it disarms it.
