[P1.1] A price comparison is only useful if the prices being compared are the prices people will pay. Right now, for families, they aren't.
[P1.2] Picture two parents and two children searching for flights. One option shows a fare that looks about forty dollars cheaper per person. They pick it. Three screens later, they learn that checked bags cost extra each way, and that sitting next to their six-year-old requires paid seat selection on every leg.
[P1.3] The "cheap" flight is now the expensive one, but they've already entered names, birthdates, and a card number. Most people don't go back. That is not an accident; it is the design. Drip pricing works precisely because it moves the real price past the point of comparison.
[P1.4] That is the case in one sentence: the comparison stage is where competition happens, so that is where the truth has to be.
[P1.5] Three reasons this should be a requirement, not a courtesy.
[P1.6] First, for families these costs are not optional extras. A solo traveler can fly with a backpack and take a middle seat. A family with a toddler cannot realistically travel without luggage or leave a small child seated among strangers. When a cost is practically unavoidable, hiding it from the headline price is simply misstating the price.
[P1.7] Second, voluntary disclosure fails for a structural reason. Any airline that shows the honest total looks more expensive in ranked search results than a rival that hides fees. Honesty gets punished, so nobody can afford to go first. Only a common rule fixes that, and it rewards carriers that are actually cheaper rather than those best at concealment.
[P1.8] Third, the burden is small. Sellers already know their own fee schedules; they charge them minutes later. Asking for traveler count, ages, and bags up front, then displaying a total, is a filtering feature some sites already offer. The base fare can still be shown and unbundled pricing can still exist.
[P1.9] Nothing is banned except the surprise.
