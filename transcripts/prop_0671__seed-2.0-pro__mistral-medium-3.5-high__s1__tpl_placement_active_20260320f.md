[C1.1] PRO frames device restriction as prudent timing, but the policy risks entrenching the digital divide under the guise of caution. We don’t withhold picture books from toddlers because they can’t read novels—we scaffold learning with age-appropriate tools.
[C1.2] This isn’t about technology versus tradition—it’s about ensuring all students have equal opportunity to develop essential modern skills from the earliest grades.
[C1.3] The developmental arguments are overstated. Fine motor skills aren’t exclusive to paper; quality educational apps can build precision and coordination equally well. The distraction concern ignores that modern platforms can be completely locked down—no notifications, no off-task access. If devices work safely in middle school, the same safeguards can work in first grade.
[C1.4] Meanwhile, paper has its own distractions and offers no engagement advantage for digital-native learners. The issue isn’t the device but how it’s used, and proper implementation can mitigate any potential downsides.
[C1.5] Most critically, this policy treats digital literacy as secondary, but digital competence is now foundational. Screen reading, digital creation, and interface navigation are as essential as traditional literacy. For students with disabilities, devices are often essential for accessible learning. Paper-only early education doesn’t prepare children for a future where digital skills are non-negotiable.
[C1.6] The risk isn’t falling behind in penmanship—it’s falling behind in the skills that drive 21st-century opportunity. Unlike handwriting, digital fluency only grows more critical. A policy that delays this preparation doesn’t protect children; it leaves them unprepared for the world they’ll inhabit. In an increasingly digital society, the cost of delayed digital literacy is far greater than any perceived benefits of a paper-only approach in early grades.
