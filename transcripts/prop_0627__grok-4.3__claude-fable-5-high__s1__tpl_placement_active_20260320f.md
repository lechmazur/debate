[P1.1] A constitutional right to a minimally clean environment anchors every other guarantee of life and health. Polluted air and water directly impair bodily integrity and shorten lifespans; without an enforceable baseline, those impairments cannot be challenged until they produce measurable victims. Courts already impose minimum standards on prisons, schools, and housing precisely because legislative majorities may tolerate substandard conditions for some groups. The same logic applies when governments permit contamination that sickens entire neighborhoods.
[P1.2] Political processes systematically undervalue diffuse future harms. Industrial permits, land-use approvals, and lax enforcement generate concentrated benefits for well-organized interests while dispersing costs across populations with weaker immediate voice. An enforceable right supplies the missing corrective by allowing affected citizens to obtain orders that restore the minimum floor rather than waiting for electoral realignment or regulatory reversal.
[P1.3] The “minimally” qualifier limits judicial intervention to clear violations, not optimization of environmental policy. Courts can identify thresholds below which respiratory disease clusters or aquifer contamination become intolerable without dictating fuel standards or zoning maps. Comparable minimum-rights doctrines in other domains have not produced paralysis; they have instead forced governments to justify continued inaction.
[P1.4] Absent such a right, the most severe environmental injuries remain remediless until they escalate into outright disasters. Communities already experiencing chronic exposure have no lever to halt ongoing degradation short of proving a traditional constitutional violation that rarely fits the facts of gradual pollution. Recognition closes that gap.

[C1.1] A right is only as good as its definition, and "minimally clean and healthy" has none. Every other judicially enforced minimum—a habeas petition, a search warrant, even prison conditions—involves a discrete state action against an identifiable person. Environmental quality is the opposite: it is the aggregate output of millions of decisions about energy, transport, agriculture, and housing, mediated by science that is probabilistic and contested. Ask three toxicologists where "minimally healthy" air sits and you get three answers, each entangled with cost, geography, and competing risks.
[C1.2] A right that cannot be specified ex ante does not constrain government; it transfers the power to specify it to whichever judge draws the case.
[C1.3] That transfer is the real stake. Environmental policy is the textbook polycentric problem: tightening one emission standard raises electricity prices, which pushes households toward wood-burning, which worsens particulate exposure elsewhere. Legislatures and agencies are built to weigh these tradeoffs with technical staff, public comment, and revisability. A constitutional ruling is none of those things—it is rigid, generalist, and nearly impossible to correct when the science moves.
[C1.4] Constitutionalizing the floor freezes today's judicial guess into tomorrow's binding error.
[C1.5] Nor does the right deliver for the communities PRO invokes. Dozens of constitutions worldwide already contain environmental rights, and the pattern is telling: they are either dead letters or they spawn sweeping injunctions courts cannot administer—ordering river cleanups that require budgets, land acquisition, and decades of engineering no judge can supervise. Courts that issue unenforceable mandates spend down their legitimacy, the very asset they need for the rights they can enforce.
[C1.6] Finally, the remedy already exists. Clean air and water statutes, citizen-suit provisions, nuisance law, and administrative challenge give affected residents concrete levers tied to enforceable standards. The honest fight is over funding and enforcement of those tools—a political fight constitutionalization doesn't win, it merely disguises.
[C1.7] The proposition asks courts to promise what only legislatures can deliver. That bargain corrodes both.

[P2.1] The claim that no judicially manageable standard exists for a minimum environmental floor collapses once we compare it to the standards courts already apply to conditions of confinement, school adequacy, and public health mandates. Those doctrines identify unacceptable thresholds—disease vectors, structural collapse, denial of basic medical care—without requiring judges to run institutions. A minimally clean environment is no different: measurable indicators such as particulate levels that correlate with documented respiratory harm, or aquifer contamination exceeding established health benchmarks, supply concrete lines courts can enforce.
[P2.2] The polycentric objection misstates what the right does. It does not compel courts to optimize electricity prices against wood-smoke exposure or to redesign transport systems. It merely prohibits governments from authorizing degradation below the floor that demonstrably injures health. Legislatures remain free to choose among every lawful compliance method above that line, exactly as they do when courts order states to remedy unconstitutional prison crowding without dictating bed allocations.
[P2.3] Existing statutory remedies have already proven inadequate for the communities the right is meant to protect. Citizen suits and administrative challenges depend on agency rules that can be narrowed, delayed, or captured. When those rules fall below health-protective levels or enforcement budgets disappear, affected residents have no remaining lever short of proving a traditional constitutional violation that the gradual, cumulative nature of pollution rarely satisfies. The proposed right closes precisely that remedial gap without displacing the statutes; it supplies a backstop when political processes fail to maintain even the minimum.
[P2.4] Constitutionalizing the floor does not freeze policy. It prevents governments from sliding beneath an objective health baseline while leaving all discretionary choices above that baseline untouched.
