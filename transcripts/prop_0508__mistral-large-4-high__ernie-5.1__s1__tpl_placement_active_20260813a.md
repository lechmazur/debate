[P1.1] The motion asks us to choose where trade blocs should spend their scarce negotiating capital. Tariffs are the wrong currency for the modern economy. While tariffs tax goods at the border, the fastest-growing trade flows are services—software, cloud computing, telemedicine, architectural design—and these cross borders digitally, invisible to customs duties yet choked by regulatory friction that no tariff schedule can fix. Negotiating tariff cuts consumes years of political capital, triggers backlash from protected industries, and delivers marginal gains at a time when most duties are already low.
[P1.2] Mutual-recognition agreements attack that friction directly and cheaply. When a bloc recognizes a partner's professional licenses or data-security certifications, it eliminates redundant re-licensing, duplicate compliance audits, and forced local incorporation. An engineer licensed in Lisbon can bid on infrastructure projects in Warsaw without waiting eighteen months for credential validation. A telehealth platform certified in Toronto can serve patients in Dublin the next day, not after years of regulatory navigation.
[P1.3] These gains require no customs infrastructure, no duty schedules, and no years of tariff negotiation—just a political commitment to trust verified standards. This is how competitive blocs actually grow in the digital age.
[P1.4] By contrast, new tariff cuts face structural exhaustion. Most tariffs between advanced economies are already low, often in single digits. The marginal gain from shaving another percentage point off industrial goods pales next to the productivity unlocked by letting a nurse, a software architect, or a financial analyst work across a border without bureaucratic delay. We are no longer trading widgets; we are trading expertise and data flows that tariffs cannot tax but mutual recognition can liberate.
[P1.5] The choice is between marginal arithmetic on yesterday's goods and transformative access to today's talent. Prioritizing tariff cuts is retrofitting the twentieth century while ignoring where comparative advantage actually lives.

[C1.1] The PRO paints mutual recognition as frictionless magic—an engineer in Lisbon instantly bidding in Warsaw. But this fantasy ignores what actually happens when you open regulatory borders. Mutual recognition means accepting another bloc's standards as equivalent, which creates a race to the bottom: why maintain rigorous data-privacy rules or professional-safety standards if lowering them wins you market access? The EU's long struggle to get even basic data-protection adequacy decisions from other blocs shows this is not a quick political commitment; it is years of granular, intractable negotiation over standards that reflect deeply different values.
[C1.2] Tariff cuts, by contrast, deliver gains that are broad, measurable, and democratically distributed. When a bloc cuts tariffs on imported food, clothing, or industrial inputs, every consumer and every downstream manufacturer benefits—not just a credentialed professional class. A farmer in Kenya exporting avocados to Europe gains nothing from a software architect in Nairobi getting faster credential recognition in Brussels, but gains enormously from a reduced tariff on perishables. The PRO case implicitly privileges the interests of urban, educated professionals while dismissing the material livelihoods of the working class that tariff reduction directly supports.
[C1.3] Moreover, the PRO claims tariff cuts face "structural exhaustion," but this is only true within narrow advanced-economy corridors. Most of the world's trade blocs include developing members where tariffs remain high—sometimes twenty, thirty percent or more—and those cuts are transformative, not marginal. Prioritizing mutual recognition over those cuts means telling a Vietnamese textile worker that her bloc should spend its negotiating capital on cloud-computing certifications instead of on the tariffs that actually determine whether her factory stays open.
[C1.4] The proposition asks us to choose. I say the choice is between narrow, risky, standards-laden deals for a professional elite and tangible, economy-wide relief for everyone else.
