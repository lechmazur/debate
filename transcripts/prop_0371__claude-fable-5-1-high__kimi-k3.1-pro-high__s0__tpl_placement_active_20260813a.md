[P1.1] Buy a ticket today and the number on the screen is not the price of flying. It is the price of a seat with nothing in it. The bag you need to actually take a trip comes later, in a second screen, at a second price that varies by airline, by fare class, by whether you clicked a checkbox on booking day or discovered the rule at the gate. That is the problem the motion fixes: a reasonable carry-on baseline, one standard bag that fits in the overhead or under the seat, built into the fare, with airlines free to sell anything beyond it.
[P1.2] The core argument is about competition, not comfort. Markets work when buyers compare like with like. Fragmented baggage upcharges break that comparison on purpose. A traveler searching flights sees a $49 fare beside a $79 fare and picks the cheaper one, only to discover that the $49 becomes $104 once a bag is added.
[P1.3] Airlines are not competing on the real cost of transport; they are competing on who can push the most cost past the point where the customer is still shopping. Drip pricing like this is a well-recognized way to defeat comparison shopping, and it rewards whoever hides best rather than whoever flies cheapest.
[P1.4] A carry-on is not a luxury add-on like a lounge pass. Almost nobody travels with nothing. When an "optional" fee applies to nearly every buyer, it is simply part of the price wearing a disguise.
[P1.5] The fees also create costs that never show up in the fare. Gate agents become bag police with sizing cages, boarding slows as passengers fight for overhead space to avoid a gate-check penalty, and crews absorb the conflict. Unbundling shifted work onto the boarding process, and everyone pays in time.
[P1.6] Expect the reply that bundling raises fares for light packers. But the money does not disappear under fees; it moves to where the buyer cannot see it. A baseline puts it back where comparison happens.

[C1.1] The motion confuses honest pricing with compulsory bundling. Requiring airlines to reveal the full cost of a trip before purchase could address deceptive drip pricing; requiring every ticket to include a carry-on dictates what every traveler must buy. PRO identifies a visibility problem, then prescribes a much broader intervention in product design.
[C1.2] Compulsory bundling would harm precisely the passengers who benefit most from unbundling. Someone flying overnight with a small backpack, commuting between meetings, or traveling light to save money can currently buy transportation without paying for an additional baggage service. Their low fare is not fictitious: it purchases exactly what they need. Airlines can still offer bundled fares for travelers who value them.
[C1.3] The mandate removes the cheaper option rather than expanding choice.
[C1.4] PRO’s comparison-shopping example proves only that prices must be comparable. If a $49 ticket becomes $104 after selecting a bag, displaying that total prominently, alongside the applicable allowance, solves the deception without forcing the $79 ticket’s contents onto everyone. Otherwise, the logic proves too much: because many passengers also want seat selection, meals, or checked luggage, each nearly universal preference could become another compulsory component. A fare would become less flexible, not necessarily more honest.
[C1.5] The operational argument also cuts against PRO. Overhead-bin space is finite, especially on smaller aircraft, and baggage charges can reduce the incentive to bring avoidable cabin bags. Including a carry-on in every ticket encourages more passengers to bring one; it does not create more bins. Size and weight limits would still require enforcement, so sizing disputes and gate checks would not disappear.
[C1.6] Bundling could therefore intensify the boarding congestion PRO attributes to fees.
[C1.7] Finally, “reasonable baseline” conceals the hardest choices. An under-seat bag and a large overhead carry-on impose very different costs. A uniform rule must either define dimensions, weight, aircraft exceptions, and remedies in rigid detail, or remain vague enough to invite disputes. Transparent optional pricing preserves informed choice; mandatory bundling raises the light traveler’s floor price while leaving scarcity and enforcement intact.
