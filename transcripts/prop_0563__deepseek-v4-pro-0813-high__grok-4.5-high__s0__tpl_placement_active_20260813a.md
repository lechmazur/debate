[P1.1] A standing long-term military commitment to Ukraine is not charity and it is not a substitute for other priorities. It is the cheapest credible deterrent the United States and its allies can buy against a larger war in Europe.
[P1.2] The central strategic fact is that Moscow’s best weapon is time. Russia’s current approach rests on outlasting Western patience, exploiting election cycles, parliamentary delays, and alliance fatigue. Every pause in aid gives Russian forces time to fortify, reconstitute, and launch offensives while Ukraine must conserve ammunition and cannot plan beyond a few months. A standing commitment reverses that calculus.
[P1.3] It tells Moscow that time is not on its side, raising the long-run cost of occupation and making a negotiated stabilization more plausible. Short, reactive aid packages do not create that pressure; they widen the very windows Russia wants.
[P1.4] The commitment also carries the deterrent load for other theaters. If American and allied support is visibly conditional, NATO’s eastern members will doubt the security guarantee that keeps them from ramping up unilateral militarization or hedging toward accommodation. Adversaries elsewhere notice when the leading alliance in the world cannot sustain support for a democracy fighting an invasion. A durable commitment reassures allies, stabilizes European defense planning, and tells revisionist powers that aggression will be met with sustained costs, not a one-year spike of concern.
[P1.5] The resource tradeoff is real but badly framed. Military aid is unusually efficient: it uses existing allied stockpiles, expands allied defense production, and degrades one of the principal military threats to the United States and Europe without sending American or NATO forces to fight. If the West underfunds this now, the likely alternative is far more expensive later—emergency deployments, larger defense budgets, refugee crises, energy shocks, and a much higher risk of direct confrontation. A standing commitment lets governments plan and share the burden instead of lurching from crisis to crisis.
[P1.6] The choice is not aid versus domestic needs; it is a bounded preventive investment versus an unbounded reactive cost. Make the commitment standing.
