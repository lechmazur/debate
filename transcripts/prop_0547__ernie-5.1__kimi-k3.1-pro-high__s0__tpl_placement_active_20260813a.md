[P1.1] The proposition asks us to compare two worlds: one where a teen opens an app and sees what friends posted in order, and one where an algorithm decides what keeps their eyes glued. The difference is not trivial—it is the difference between passive exposure and engineered amplification.
[P1.2] Engagement-optimized feeds are built to maximize time on platform. They do this by learning which content triggers the strongest reactions—outrage, envy, fear, compulsive curiosity—and feeding more of it. For adolescents, whose brains are still developing sensitivity to social evaluation and peer comparison, this is not a neutral delivery mechanism. It is a funnel into content specifically calibrated to provoke the emotional volatility that characterizes anxiety and depression.
[P1.3] Consider the mechanism plainly. A chronological feed shows you what people chose to post. An engagement feed shows you what the algorithm predicted would make you stay—often content that is more extreme, more emotionally charged, or more socially comparative than anything your real network produced. A teen scrolling chronologically might see a friend's vacation photo and move on.
[P1.4] The same teen on an optimized feed gets served a cascade of curated perfection, then body-image content, then provocation, each video auto-playing into the next because the system learned that pattern holds attention.
[P1.5] This is not speculation about screen time in general. The claim is specifically about the algorithmic curation layer. Chronological feeds lack that amplification engine. Non-personalized feeds treat every user the same and lack the feedback loop that tailors content to individual vulnerabilities.
[P1.6] The algorithmic version uniquely creates a closed system where a teen's existing insecurities become the very signal the system exploits.
[P1.7] The developmental evidence is clear: adolescents are disproportionately affected by social comparison and negative feedback cycles. An engagement-optimized feed industrializes those cycles. That is the central harm, and it is the case the opposition must defeat.

[C1.2] First, the objective does not imply the content. Engagement can reward watch time, likes, shares, or return visits; none logically requires distress. [C1.3] The actual claim requires evidence about deployed feeds and clinical outcomes, not a story about what an especially reckless system might serve.
[C1.4] Second, the comparison is not “engineered harm versus benign reality.” Chronological feeds still contain curated photos, public popularity metrics, bullying, alarming news, and friends’ endless highlights. [C1.5] Autoplay may be risky, but it is not personalization.
[C1.6] Third, PRO’s developmental argument proves susceptibility, not causation. Evidence linking overall social-media use with distress does not randomize adolescents to feed types and measure later anxiety or depression. Reverse causation matters: an already lonely or anxious adolescent may scroll longer, search different content, and generate different recommendations. Comparing those users with lighter users mistakes the vulnerability for the feed’s effect.
[C1.7] CON does not need to show algorithms are harmless. Some designs and some vulnerable users plausibly produce harm. But the motion asserts a general comparative increase in two serious outcomes. Without feed-specific causal evidence, and with mechanisms pointing in both directions, that assertion fails.
