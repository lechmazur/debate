[P1.1] Frontier AI is becoming a compute-gated science. The decisive question is not whether large firms will have the biggest clusters—they will—but whether independent academic research can exist at the frontier at all. If access depends on ad hoc corporate credits and goodwill, firms become the gatekeepers of what can be studied, replicated, and criticized.
[P1.2] Compute is now infrastructure, like telescopes, accelerators, or genomic databases. No serious country leaves those entirely to firms, because the research they enable produces public goods no single company can fully capture. AI is the same, only more general: auditing model behavior, testing safety claims, building public-interest models, studying economic effects, and training the next generation of researchers all require credible access to serious compute. A lab that can only rent small slices cannot reproduce a frontier result or independently verify a safety claim.
[P1.3] It can comment on corporate science; it cannot check it.
[P1.4] Coordination across countries adds what fragmented academic access cannot: scale and rules. Pooled procurement can buy frontier-class capacity no university can buy alone; shared allocation can match researchers to the right hardware; common security and publication conditions prevent a race to the bottom and make results comparable. Coordination also reduces duplication—why should dozens of countries build small, underused clusters instead of interoperable public capacity?
[P1.5] The alternative is not a neutral market. It is private gatekeeping over the inputs of a transformative technology, with research agendas shaped by commercial priorities and public scrutiny dependent on the firms being scrutinized. Countries coordinate public compute because the bottleneck is too important to leave to a handful of firms. That is the path to independent research, real accountability, and broad talent development.

[C1.1] The core problem with the proposition is that it replaces one gatekeeper with a worse one. A coordinated public compute system does not produce neutral access; it creates a politically managed chokepoint. Academic researchers would trade corporate goodwill for intergovernmental committees, procurement rules, national security reviews, and allocation boards. That is dependence, not independence.
[C1.2] The deeper error is treating frontier AI as if it were a telescope: a fixed instrument you can build once and share. Frontier capacity is not just GPUs. It is tightly coupled hardware generations, networking, data pipelines, operations staff, and continuous reinvestment. Public multi-country procurement is too slow for this cycle.
[C1.3] A cluster specified today can be last-generation by installation, while large firms are upgrading because they operate commercial services that pay for refresh. The result is not a public frontier; it is a costly lagging facility that few top researchers use for serious work.
[C1.4] International coordination compounds the problem. Countries disagree on export controls, privacy, and dual-use boundaries. Pooling compute means pooled governance, and pooled governance means the most restrictive veto dominates. Researchers would face nationality screens, usage audits, and de facto censorship.
[C1.5] A major shared infrastructure would also be a concentrated target for espionage and abuse, raising security risks far beyond corporate clouds.
[C1.6] The real bottleneck for academics is not ownership; it is money. Large firms already sell compute on ordinary market terms. Governments can solve access by funding compute credits or contractual access on commercial clouds, where researchers retain the option of switching providers. That buys real access without creating a state-run compute bureaucracy.
[C1.7] The motion is a false choice between public ownership and private gatekeeping; the better reconciliation is public funding, competitive private provision, and enforceable access conditions.

[P2.3] AI is harder, not impossible. And even capacity slightly behind the absolute frontier can support independent auditing, replication at scale, and talent development—precisely what commercial credits do not guarantee when firms prioritize paying customers.
[P2.4] The governance objection [C1.4] is real but self-defeating. Corporate access already comes with unilateral terms, nationality restrictions, and export controls. Coordination makes those rules visible and negotiable; fragmentation leaves each researcher facing the most restrictive firm or state alone. Pooled governance is a design problem, not a reason to hand agenda control to the firms being studied.
[P2.5] Nor is security concentration unique to public systems: commercial clouds are concentrated too. Coordinated capacity can set common security baselines and distribute sensitive workloads; ad hoc credits scatter data across providers with no shared standards.
[P2.6] The decisive comparison is credible independence. CON’s model makes every safety audit, every replication, and every public-interest model a favor granted by a commercial gatekeeper. That is dependence with extra steps.
