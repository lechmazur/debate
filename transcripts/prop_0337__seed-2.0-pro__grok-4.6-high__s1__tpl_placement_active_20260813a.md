[P1.1] Right now, every major social platform defaults every new user into the most addictive possible feed. That choice is not neutral. It is a deliberate decision to prioritise ad revenue over the well-being of people whose brains are still growing.
[P1.2] This policy does not ban anything. It does not lock anyone out of content. Any user under 18 who actively wants the full addictive algorithm can still go into settings and turn it on. It simply stops rigging the default against a group that cannot fairly resist the psychological pressure platforms have perfected.
[P1.3] We know the prefrontal cortex that governs impulse control does not mature until early adulthood. Platforms have entire teams dedicated to exploiting that exact developmental gap. Nobody signs up for an account hoping to lose three hours a night to mindless scroll. Almost no teens even know the feed behaviour can be changed.
[P1.4] Yes, engagement will fall. That is not an objection, that is the entire point. The alternative is accepting that it is acceptable for trillion dollar companies to design products that intentionally create compulsive behaviour in children.
[P1.5] We do not let cigarette companies default pack designs to candy flavours even if it reduces tobacco sales. We do not let casinos place slot machines in high school cafeterias even if it would drive more gambling revenue. This is the exact same principle. When the user cannot meaningfully consent to the addictive design, you do not get to default them into harm for profit.
[P1.6] The tradeoff is not close: a modest fall in platform ad revenue will always be worth preventing millions of young people from forming life-disrupting compulsive habits before they are old enough to choose that risk.

[C1.2] A “less addictive” feed is not a known safer design. It is a slogan. Strip ranking and personalization and you do not automatically get healthier use. You often get a noisier, less relevant, or more random stream: whatever was posted last, not friends, not interests, not the clip that actually helps with homework or finds a team.
[C1.3] The same systems that can over-recommend also connect isolated kids to communities, tutors, language peers, and local information. Collapsing all of that into “addiction” is the first error.
[C1.4] The default is also clumsy where it matters. Thirteen is not seventeen. A teen with present parents already has device limits and shared settings. A teen without them will not be rescued by a duller home screen.
[C1.5] They will open another app, lie about age, or leave for smaller platforms with weaker rules. Engagement does not disappear. It moves somewhere worse.
[C1.6] The cigarette and casino analogies fail because those products have no legitimate role in a child’s day. A social feed does: cancelled practice, group projects, a diaspora community, a first job listing. We do not force every under-18 driver into first gear because acceleration can be abused. We teach, we set household rules, and we refuse to call a worse engine a safety feature.
[C1.7] If compulsive use is the worry, the honest tools are timers, family pairing, night cutoffs, and controls parents can actually operate. A forced, undefined demotion of relevance is not protection. It is a performance of care that trades connection and agency for an engagement drop companies will immediately route around.
