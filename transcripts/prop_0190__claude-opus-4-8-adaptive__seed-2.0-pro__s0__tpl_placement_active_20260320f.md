[P1.1] Insurance, at its core, is a promise: we pool our risks so that the unlucky among us are not crushed alone. For essential coverage—the health care that keeps you alive, the home insurance you cannot get a mortgage without—that promise is the whole point. My claim is narrow and defensible: where coverage is essential, the law should cap how far premiums can be split by statistical risk, because perfect actuarial sorting destroys the solidarity that makes the pool worth having.
[P1.2] Consider what "actuarially fair" pricing actually means at the limit. The more precisely insurers predict your risk, the more they charge exactly the people who most need protection. The diabetic, the person with a genetic predisposition, the family living in a flood-prone neighborhood they could never afford to leave—these are the people whose premiums rise toward the cost of the very catastrophe they're trying to insure against. At that point insurance stops being insurance.
[P1.3] It becomes a bill for being unlucky. You cannot insure a house that has already burned; actuarial fairness, taken to its conclusion, refuses to insure the people already standing in the fire.
[P1.4] And most of this risk is not chosen. Your genes, your inherited conditions, where you were born, the disabilities you live with—you did not earn these. Charging someone more for an accident of birth is not fairness in any moral sense; it is fairness only to the spreadsheet. Social solidarity says the healthy help carry the sick, the lucky help carry the unlucky, because today's healthy person is tomorrow's patient.
[P1.5] This is why nearly every functioning society already limits actuarial pricing for essentials—community-rated health systems, bans on genetic discrimination, mandated coverage of pre-existing conditions. These laws exist precisely because unrestrained risk-rating prices the vulnerable out of survival.
[P1.6] My burden is not to abolish risk-pricing everywhere. It is to show that for essential coverage, legal limits are justified because solidarity must outrank the actuary's table. Defend universal access, and I win.
