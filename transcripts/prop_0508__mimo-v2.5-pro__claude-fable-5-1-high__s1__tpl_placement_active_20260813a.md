[P1.1] Tariffs are twentieth-century tools. Twenty-first-century trade runs on services, data, and human capital—and that is precisely where mutual-recognition deals deliver outsized gains.
[P1.2] Consider: in major economies, services account for over 60% of GDP and the bulk of employment. Yet cross-border services trade is hamstrung not by tariffs, but by regulatory misalignment. A German architect cannot practice in South Korea; an Indian IT firm must re-certify software in each ASEAN state. These barriers are invisible but costly.
[P1.3] Mutual recognition of professional credentials and digital standards directly dismantles them, enabling labor mobility, scaling digital businesses, and fostering innovation.
[P1.4] Meanwhile, tariff cuts face diminishing returns. Average tariffs in blocs like the EU or USMCA already hover in low single digits. Further cuts yield minimal welfare gains compared to the massive transaction costs of negotiating them. In contrast, an MRA for digital services—say, recognizing each other's data protection frameworks—can unleash billions in e-commerce and cloud computing overnight.
[P1.5] Prioritizing MRAs also aligns with the strategic interests of trade blocs. It builds deeper integration by harmonizing standards, which boosts competitiveness and resilience. It addresses modern supply chains where services and digital inputs are intertwined with goods. And it sends a signal: we are serious about trade in the economy of the future, not just the past.
[P1.6] The economic literature consistently shows that non-tariff barriers, especially in services, impose costs multiple times higher than tariffs. By prioritizing MRAs, trade blocs can achieve greater trade creation with less political friction, as they often require only regulatory cooperation rather than legislative overhaul.
[P1.7] Thus, trade blocs should lead with mutual recognition for digital services and professional credentials. That is where the high-value opportunities lie. Tariff cuts can follow, but they should not distract from the main prize.

[C1.1] Mutual recognition sounds like a shortcut. In practice it is the longest road in trade policy, and telling blocs to walk it first means telling them to deliver less, later, to fewer people.
[C1.2] Start with the record. The European Union has spent roughly three decades trying to make professional qualifications portable inside a single market with a shared court, a shared parliament, and enormous political will. Architects and engineers still hit national hurdles; the services directive was gutted before adoption. Australia and New Zealand, two nearly identical legal cultures, needed years of negotiation to recognize each other's licenses.
[C1.3] Attempts at EU–US mutual recognition largely stalled. If deep, trusting partners struggle, expecting looser blocs to lead with this is fantasy. Tariff cuts, by contrast, are a number in a schedule: agreed, published, self-executing at the border the next morning.
[C1.4] Second, tariffs are not a solved problem. Pointing at EU or USMCA averages ignores where trade blocs are actually being built. Intra-African tariffs under AfCFTA remain substantial; Mercosur, South Asia, and much of ASEAN's external trade still face double-digit rates on food, textiles, and vehicles. And tariff peaks survive even in rich blocs precisely on the goods poor households buy.
[C1.5] Meanwhile the past few years have seen tariffs rise sharply worldwide. Calling them "twentieth-century tools" while they are being weaponized in real time is not modern; it is disarming.
[C1.6] Third, ask who benefits. Tariff cuts lower the price of rice, medicine, and clothing and open markets for farmers and factory workers. Credential recognition helps architects, accountants, and IT consultants, people who are already the most mobile in any economy. Prioritizing the second over the first is a distributional choice, and a bad one.
[C1.7] Finally, the claim that mutual recognition carries "less political friction" gets it backwards. Licensing boards, bar associations, and data regulators are among the fiercest protectionist lobbies anywhere, and they wrap their interests in consumer safety and privacy. Blocs have finite negotiating capacity. Spend it on what can actually close.
