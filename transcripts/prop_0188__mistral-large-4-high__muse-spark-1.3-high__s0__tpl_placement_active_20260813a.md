[P1.1] Public money demands public proof. When taxpayers fund a clinical trial or a social-science study, they are owed not merely a polished final paper, but a verifiable record of what was promised before the first data point arrived. Yet the current incentive structure rewards exactly the opposite: researchers can torture datasets until they confess, bury null results in file drawers, and publish only those flashy positive findings that survive peer review’s outcome bias.
[P1.2] This is not a marginal problem of a few bad actors. It is a systemic failure built into the architecture of academic publishing. Voluntary preregistration has been available for years, yet adoption remains patchy because the system actively punishes transparency. If you reveal your hypotheses in advance, you surrender the flexibility to pivot, to HARK—hypothesize after the results are known—or to fish through dozens of subgroup analyses until something statistically significant emerges.
[P1.3] Rational researchers stay silent, and the literature fills with false positives. In clinical research, this is not merely embarrassing; it is dangerous. Drugs that looked promising in unregistered, outcome-selected studies later collapse under replication, wasting billions in public funds and exposing patients to interventions that were never truly effective.
[P1.4] Mandatory preregistration for publicly funded work breaks this trap. It locks in hypotheses, methodologies, and analysis plans before data collection begins, rendering p-hacking and selective reporting visible rather than invisible. But preregistration alone is insufficient if journals continue to reject tedious null findings. That is why journals must commit to publishing registered studies regardless of outcome—evaluating the rigor of the protocol rather than the glamour of the result.
[P1.5] This registered-reports model has already demonstrated that it can work, shifting incentives from "find something publishable" to "ask something important and answer it honestly."
[P1.6] The alternative is a research ecosystem that manufactures certainty where none exists, funded by the public and betraying its trust. We cannot afford to subsidize the illusion of knowledge any longer. Mandatory preregistration and outcome-independent publication are not bureaucratic hurdles; they are the minimum conditions for science that the public can actually trust.

[C1.1] Taxpayer accountability does not mean bureaucratic control over discovery. Requiring every publicly funded researcher to file hypotheses, methods, and analysis plans before seeing data replaces scientific judgment with contract compliance. It creates the illusion of rigor while strangling the process that produces it.
[C1.2] Real breakthroughs rarely honor their preregistered script. A clinician testing a depression drug notices sleep improves even when mood does not; an ethnographer enters a neighborhood to study job networks and finds housing evictions drive everything; a developmental lab finds toddlers will not sit through the planned task. Under a mandate, such pivots look like cheating rather than learning. Science needs disciplined flexibility, not locked-in bets that punish researchers for following evidence where it leads.
[C1.3] Compelling journals to publish regardless of outcome compounds the damage. Peer review is already exhausted. Flooding it with every null, underpowered, or poorly executed preregistered study does not cure publication bias; it buries signal in noise and forces the public to fund both the research and the mandatory dissemination of dead ends. Worse, weak nulls will be misread by doctors and policymakers as proof that nothing works.
[C1.4] Readers do not need all results; they need reliable synthesis, replication, and honest discussion of limits — none of which requires automatic publication.
[C1.5] The burden falls hardest where help is claimed most. Qualitative research, field experiments in unstable settings, pilot work, and rapid crisis response cannot meaningfully pre-specify analyses because the terrain is unknown. A mandate privileges large, well-staffed labs that can hire compliance officers while penalizing graduate students, community researchers, and scholars working with scarce resources.
[C1.6] Encourage transparency, data sharing, and voluntary registration. Do not make funding conditional on prediction and journals hostage to procedure. That turns public science slower, narrower, and less creative — the opposite of trustworthy.

[P2.2] The claim that outcome-independent publication "floods peer review with dead ends" inverts the actual crisis. We do not drown in published null results; we drown in false positives—promising drugs and interventions that collapse under replication because unregistered studies selected for significance. Registered reports already prove journals can evaluate protocol rigor regardless of outcome without collapsing under the volume, filtering noise while preserving essential signal.
[P2.3] Finally, the equity argument collapses under scrutiny. Today's system advantages exactly the large, well-staffed labs the opponent claims to defend, because they possess the statistical firepower to p-hack, fish through subgroups, and bury failures until something publishable emerges. Qualitative and field researchers already preregister sampling strategies and analytic frameworks, adapting the tool to their methods rather than abandoning transparency.
[P2.4] The choice is not between flexibility and rigidity, but between a publicly funded literature that admits what it actually knows—and one that manufactures certainty.
