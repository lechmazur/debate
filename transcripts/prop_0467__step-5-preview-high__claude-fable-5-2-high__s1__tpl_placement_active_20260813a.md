[P1.1] A cyberattack that plunges a city into darkness, shuts down a hospital's life support, or contaminates a municipal water supply is not merely a crime; it is an act of violence. The medium may be code, but the casualties, terror, and physical destruction are indistinguishable from a conventional bombing. International law must therefore recognize that when a state-directed cyber operation targets civilian critical infrastructure and produces—or is designed to produce—severe physical harm, it crosses the threshold of an armed attack.
[P1.2] The current legal ambiguity serves aggressors, not peace. Because existing frameworks treat cyber operations as something categorically distinct from kinetic warfare, states can inflict catastrophic damage while hiding behind semantic technicalities. A hospital disabled by ransomware and a hospital disabled by a missile strike both leave patients dead. The law should not pretend otherwise.
[P1.3] Classifying severe cyberattacks on civilian infrastructure as armed attacks removes this safe harbor and creates an unmistakable red line that rational actors think twice before crossing.
[P1.4] Critics will warn that this invites escalation. But the proposition demands only proportional military response options—not automatic war. Proportionality is already the cornerstone of just warfare. It ensures that any response is calibrated, lawful, and measured.
[P1.5] What the current system lacks is not restraint, but credibility. Without a credible military option, victims face a false choice between suffering silent sabotage or engaging in unbounded cyber retaliation that itself risks uncontrolled escalation.
[P1.6] The stakes are not abstract. Critical infrastructure is the nervous system of modern civilization. When it is attacked, civilians die. Treating such attacks as armed attacks under international law is not an expansion of conflict; it is the recognition that the nature of war has changed, and the law must change with it to protect the innocent.
