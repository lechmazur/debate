[P1.1] Transmission is not a luxury add-on; it is the delivery mechanism for cheap wind and solar. When new high-voltage lines stay stuck in permitting, the binding constraint is not panels or turbines, but the ability to site and build wires. A single county board, town meeting, or landowner coalition can block or reroute an interstate line, producing delay measured in years, cost escalation, curtailment of clean power, and continued reliance on gas and coal. That is the real cost of local veto: not inconvenience for a developer, but higher bills and dirtier air for millions who never appear at the local hearing.
[P1.2] Opponents will defend local veto as democracy and protection. But veto is not voice. A process can require early notice, environmental review, routing alternatives, and compensation while still preventing a narrow hold-out from defeating a project of regional or national importance. The tradeoff is real, and it should be accepted: reduce the veto, not the consultation.
[P1.3] If the choice is between a small group capturing the decision and a state or regional authority with a shot clock to approve a vetted route, the public interest lies in the latter. Democracy at the level of the whole grid outweighs a procedural veto at the fence line.
[P1.4] Consider a multi-state line carrying wind power that must cross several counties before it can deliver electricity to population centers. With broad regional support, each county still becomes a veto gate; litigating or rerouting around one hold-out can add years and hundreds of millions in costs. Faster buildout requires shifting final siting authority upward—state or federal backstop, clear deadlines, and eminent domain with fair compensation—so review is thorough but not endless. The losers are not locals; they are delay and fossil fuels.
[P1.5] Prioritizing transmission buildout over local veto is how we turn clean-energy targets into actual electrons, and that is the metric that should decide this debate.
