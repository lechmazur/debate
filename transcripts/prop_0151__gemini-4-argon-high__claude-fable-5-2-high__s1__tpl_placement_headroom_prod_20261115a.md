[P1.1] Today's online harassment dogpiles are not spontaneous bursts of public criticism; they are industrial-scale pile-ons engineered by platform architecture. When a mid-sized streamer stumbles over their words or a fan artist posts an unpopular opinion, outrage entrepreneurs extract a ten-second clip or slap a mocking quote-post on top, stripping all context to farm engagement. Recommendation algorithms detect the spike in angry replies and rocket that bait to millions of strangers. Within hours, an ordinary creator faces thousands of threats, doxxing attempts, and coordinated abuse across every account they own.
[P1.2] Treating this ecosystem as healthy counter-speech confuses a debate with an ambush. Reactive moderation—banning individual trolls days after a target has already been driven offline—is useless against velocity-driven harm. The damage of a dogpile comes from its sudden, overwhelming volume, which paralyzes the target before human moderators ever review a report. Moreover, the constant threat of becoming the internet's daily punching bag severely chills creative expression.
[P1.3] Fans stop sharing art, smaller creators quit, and fandom spaces hollow out until only cynical drama farmers remain.
[P1.4] Crucially, platforms do not need to censor opinions to solve this; they must simply introduce friction into their own outrage engines. Slowing a dogpile means targeting viral velocity rather than viewpoint. Platforms can empower users to retroactively disable quote-posts, duets, or clips when a post attracts a swarm. They can automatically pause algorithmic recommendations on threads experiencing sudden, anomalous spikes in hostile engagement, and prompt users to view the original context before sharing a clipped snippet.
[P1.5] These circuit-breakers leave legitimate criticism intact while starving outrage merchants of the algorithmic momentum required to assemble a mob. Because major platforms designed the features that monetize and accelerate mass harassment, they bear the responsibility to build the brakes.
