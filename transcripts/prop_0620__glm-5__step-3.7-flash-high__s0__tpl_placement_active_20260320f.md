[P1.1] The proposition is justified by three converging realities: the structural impossibility of meaningful consent under current conditions, the distinctive harms of large-scale behavioral targeting, and the proper allocation of burden in data relationships.
[P1.2] First, consent has become a legal fiction. When a user encounters a website, they face infrastructure designed to extract maximum data while minimizing friction. Terms sprawl across dozens of pages. Dark patterns nudge users toward sharing.
[P1.3] The alternative—declining service—often means excluding oneself from essential platforms for communication, commerce, and civic participation. This is not genuine choice but engineered surrender. The individual cannot meaningfully negotiate with entities that have invested billions in persuasion infrastructure. A presumption of unlawlessness flips this dynamic: the data collector must earn permission through clear, specific requests, rather than the user having to discover and contest hidden appropriations.
[P1.4] Second, large-scale data harvesting for advertising differs qualitatively from ordinary commercial data use. Aggregating browsing history, location data, social connections, and purchase behavior creates profiles of predictive power that no individual could anticipate or evaluate. Advertisers purchase access to "impulse buyers," "financially vulnerable," and "life event triggers"—categories that enable exploitation of psychological weaknesses at scale. The Cambridge Analytica scandal demonstrated how such targeting can manipulate democratic processes themselves.
[P1.5] Ordinary transactional data collection does not carry these systemic risks.
[P1.6] Third, presumptive unlawfulness with opt-in consent correctly places the burden on the party seeking to profit. Data harvesters have the resources, expertise, and incentive to present clear choices; individuals lack the time and technical knowledge to audit every data practice they encounter. The law routinely imposes heightened consent requirements where power asymmetries prevent genuine negotiation—in medical treatment, financial advice, and consumer contracts. Personal data harvesting, which touches equally fundamental interests, deserves the same treatment.
