[P1.1] Prohibition has made cannabis one of organized crime’s most reliable monopolies: consumers pay an illicit premium, suppliers gain outsized margins, and rival groups fight over routes and distribution. The answer is not to leave that profit untouched. A uniform national legalization and licensing regime would convert cannabis from a protected illegal commodity into a transparently taxed, legally supplied market. The first-order objective should be displacement: organized-crime groups lose the revenue and territorial control that depend on serving otherwise legal consumers.
[P1.2] Regulation is the mechanism that makes legalization weaken crime rather than privatize it. National standards for licensing, product testing, age verification, taxation, and enforcement can channel revenue through accountable businesses while preventing powerful criminal organizations from obtaining merely legal shells. Taxes and tracked invoices would give governments leverage over the market, and compliance requirements would make criminal enterprises increasingly difficult to disguise. Governments could then concentrate scarce policing resources on violent smuggling, trafficking, and exploitation, not routine possession.
[P1.3] This strategy also changes the enforcement incentive. Under prohibition, police must pursue enormous numbers of low-level offenses, and prosecutors often gain little from dismantling major organizations. A regulated market allows agencies to build cases against financiers, corrupt officials, violent gangs, and trafficking networks rather than repeatedly arresting sellers. Uruguay’s experience illustrates the practical choice: by regulating cannabis through a state-controlled legal market while preserving restrictions on commercial private production, lawmakers sought to draw users away from illicit supply and give the state a role in the market.
[P1.4] Although its model can be improved, the underlying principle is sound: public regulation can capture value that prohibition surrenders to criminals.
[P1.5] A national framework is essential because patchwork rules encourage organized crime to exploit regulatory gaps. Cannabis should become lawful, but cannabis trafficking, violence, and exploitation should remain intensely enforceable offenses. This is a strategy for redirecting state power away from abundant low-level violations and toward the criminal organizations that profit from prohibition.

[C1.4] Worse still, cartels routinely co-opt and penetrate legal commodity markets.
