[P1.1] Picture a widow in her seventies. She sees a video of a famous financier — his face, his voice, perfectly cloned — urging her to invest her savings. She loses everything. Her family reports the ad.
[P1.2] The impersonated celebrity's lawyers report it. The platform confirms it's synthetic fraud. And the same ad, from the same account network, runs again next week. Under current law, the platform owes her nothing.
[P1.3] That is the narrow injustice this motion corrects.
[P1.4] Notice the motion's precision. This is not strict liability for everything users post. It is not liability for missing a scam. Liability attaches only when a platform *ignores repeated, verified reports*.
[P1.5] That is classic negligence: knowledge plus capacity plus refusal to act. We hold every other industry to this standard. A landlord who is told repeatedly about a broken lock and does nothing is liable when a tenant is attacked. A store that knows about a spill and leaves it pays for the fall.
[P1.6] Platforms are the only actors we exempt from the oldest principle in tort law — and they are the only actors who can actually stop the harm.
[P1.7] That last point is decisive. The fraudster is anonymous, offshore, unreachable. The victim can do nothing. [P1.8] When the party best positioned to prevent harm also monetizes it, immunity guarantees the harm continues. Liability simply prices it correctly.
[P1.9] Expect warnings about floodgates and over-removal. But the "repeated verified reports" trigger is itself the filter: platforms already run notice-and-takedown for copyright at massive scale, and the sky did not fall. If a company can protect Disney's intellectual property within hours, it can protect a retiree's life savings.

[C1.1] The proposition turns platforms into insurers of third-party speech. It sounds narrow—repeated, verified reports—but in practice it requires a private company to decide, under threat of damages, whether a video is unauthorized, synthetic, and fraudulent. Those are legal and factual judgments. A celebrity complaint may be genuine, or it may be a licensed promotion, parody, or dispute over consent.
[C1.2] If the platform hesitates, it faces ruinous suits; if it overreacts, it silences lawful speech and commerce. That remove-first incentive is not a bug of liability; it is the predictable result.
[C1.3] The least-cost avoider argument fails. The platform can take down an ad, but it does not control the fraudster, the victim’s trust, or the payment system that actually moves the money. Banks, card networks, wallet providers, and law enforcement have far more direct tools to stop and reverse fraud. Making the ad platform pay for losses turns it into a deep-pocket guarantor for criminal acts that can continue off-platform, through messages, websites, or lookalike accounts.
[C1.4] That is not negligence; it is vicarious liability dressed in a notice trigger.
[C1.5] “Repeated verified reports” is no safe filter. Verified by whom? Victims may sincerely misidentify scams; rivals and public figures can weaponize reports; synthetic media can be authentic-looking yet authorized. Copyright takedown is not a model because ownership is usually documentable, while fraud requires intent, causation, and loss.
[C1.6] And fraud networks adapt instantly: ban one account and ten appear. If platforms can be liable for “the same network” despite not seeing it, the trigger collapses into liability for failing to predict evasion.
[C1.7] The state can punish fraudsters, require payment-rail clawbacks, mandate transparency, and condition safe harbors on reasonable response. But civil damages for ignoring reports will produce over-removal, entrench large platforms, and leave victims suing the wrong defendant. The right answer is targeted fraud enforcement, not making platforms insurers of every impersonation claim.
