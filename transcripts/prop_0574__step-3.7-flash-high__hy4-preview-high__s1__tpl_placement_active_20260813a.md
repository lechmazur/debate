Requiring explicit, prior permission from an artist’s estate before generating new AI works in their style or likeness is a minimal, necessary guardrail that protects artistic integrity, honors creators’ wishes, and stops the market from being flooded with low-effort, exploitative fakes that devalue genuine posthumous work.

Their vocal timbre, stylistic choices, and artistic persona are not public domain simply because they have died. When a studio uses AI to generate a new performance in that style without permission, it profits from a resource that belongs, in part, to the artist’s heirs. Consider the 2022 unlicensed AI “Tupac” track that mocked the rapper’s activism and was condemned by his estate: this was not a tribute, but a commercial product that distorted his legacy for clicks, and only came down because the estate had the resources to fight it.
[P1.3] Most estates lack those resources, leaving families powerless as studios churn out fake, disrespectful content in their loved one’s name.
[P1.4] This guardrail does not stifle creativity—it only stops unaccountable commercial exploitation. Authorized posthumous AI projects, like the finished Beatles track “Now and Then” approved by the surviving members and relevant estates, prove this technology can be used respectfully when legacy holders give consent. The rule we propose does not ban AI tributes; it simply requires that those responsible for an artist’s legacy have a say in how their identity is used. Without this requirement, studios have no incentive to prioritize respect over profit, and cheap, low-quality AI fakes will crowd out genuine, carefully curated posthumous work.
