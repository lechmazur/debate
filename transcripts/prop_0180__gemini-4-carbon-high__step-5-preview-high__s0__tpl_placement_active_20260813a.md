[P1.1] Active reforestation treats nature like an assembly line: grow saplings in nurseries, haul them out, and pay crews to plant neat rows. In Europe and North America, that industrial model fails the metric that actually governs conservation budgets: ecological return per dollar.
[P1.2] Start with the denominator. In high-wage Western economies, nursery propagation, machinery, plastic tree tubes, herbicide, and manual labor make active planting extraordinarily expensive per hectare, even before summer droughts kill off swathes of fragile transplants. Meanwhile, abandoned farmland across rural Iberia, Eastern Europe, and the eastern United States typically sits within reach of remnant woods and retains viable soils. Wind, jays, and mammals disperse native seeds for free.
[P1.3] Because rewilding channels money into low-cost, landscape-scale catalysts—removing barriers, plugging agricultural drains, or restoring natural grazers—the same budget restores many times more acreage than planting crews ever could.
[P1.4] Active reforestation also sabotages its own carbon math. Mechanical site preparation—plowing, ripping, or mounding—oxidizes existing soil carbon before saplings absorb an ounce. From there, active programs hit an inescapable bind: most rely on dense, even-aged stands of fast-growing conifers that become tinderboxes for wildfire and bark beetles, while the minority that custom-plant diverse native mixes drive per-hectare costs even higher. Rewilding escapes that trap.
[P1.5] It leaves soil carbon undisturbed from day one and builds genetically diverse mosaics—rewetted soils, deep-rooted scrub, and mixed native woodland—that lock durable carbon both above and below ground.
[P1.6] For biodiversity, the contest is not even close. Rows of same-age nursery trees produce dark, uniform canopies with starved understories. Rewilding lets abandoned fields evolve through dynamic stages of wildflower meadow, thorny thicket, deadwood, and uneven forest—the exact structural complexity where pollinators, fungi, amphibians, and songbirds thrive.
[P1.7] Paying top-tier wages to engineer fragile plantations squanders scarce conservation funds. Letting natural processes do the heavy lifting buys far more resilient carbon and richer biodiversity per dollar.
