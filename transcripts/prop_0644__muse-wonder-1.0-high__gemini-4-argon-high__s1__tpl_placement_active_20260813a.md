[P1.1] Emergency powers are a loan, not a transfer. When a legislature lets an executive rule by decree, restrict movement and assembly, close businesses, spend without normal appropriations, or bypass procurement and privacy safeguards, it temporarily trades liberty and deliberation for speed. If that trade has no automatic expiry, inertia does the governing: the exceptional becomes the baseline because no one has to stand up and defend keeping it.
[P1.2] That burden shift is the point. Under a sunset, the executive must come back, present evidence that the emergency still exists, explain what each broad power has been used for, and win a vote. Opponents do not have to assemble a majority, overcome a veto, or persuade a deferential court to claw power back. Hearings create a public record — what worked, what was abused, what is no longer needed — so renewal can be narrowed, not just rubber-stamped.
[P1.3] History shows why inertia is dangerous. Authorizations passed after 9/11 were invoked for years in countries and conflicts far from the original attack. Pandemic declarations in many democracies were renewed by executives themselves long after hospitals stabilized, and powers written for disease control drifted toward protests, borders, and ordinary crime. None of this requires a dictator; it only requires busy legislators, a cautious opposition, and an executive who finds convenient tools too useful to surrender.
[P1.4] A sunset does not tie hands in a real crisis. The initial grant can be immediate and broad; the first renewal can come in weeks, later ones at longer intervals, and a legislature that believes the evidence can renew in a day. What it cannot do is let broad coercion continue by silence. If powers are truly necessary, they can survive daylight and a vote.
[P1.5] If they cannot, they should expire.

[C1.1] Automatic sunsets do not just require accountability; they manufacture recurring governance cliffs in the middle of crises. The case for sunsets assumes that if emergency powers remain necessary, a legislature simply renews them "in a day" [P1.4]. In real, polarized democracies, legislatures fail to act not because a majority wants powers to end, but because of procedural bottlenecks, filibusters, fractured coalitions, or hostage-taking. By shifting the default to automatic termination [P1.2], a small faction no longer needs a majority to halt an emergency response—it only needs to run out the clock.
[C1.2] Just as routine budget deadlines become vehicles for partisan extortion, mandatory emergency renewals let splinter groups hold disaster relief, epidemic containment, or wartime mobilization hostage for unrelated concessions.
[C1.3] That calendar cliff also cripples operational effectiveness. Managing a crisis requires credible continuity. Financial authorities halting a banking panic, health agencies signing supply contracts, and commanders coordinating with allies cannot function on rolling thirty-day probation. When everyone knows executive authority might evaporate next week over a parliamentary dispute, markets freeze, suppliers hesitate, and adversaries gain a roadmap.
[C1.4] A hostile state or insurgent group need only wait out the calendar and escalate pressure right before a renewal vote. Meanwhile, dragging crisis managers into recurring hearings [P1.2] drains bandwidth precisely when execution matters most.
[C1.5] Worse, mandatory renewal votes often backfire against liberty. Consider the post-9/11 example [P1.3]: key surveillance powers actually had automatic sunsets, yet frightened lawmakers repeatedly renewed them anyway to avoid blame for the next attack. The result was worse than inertia: repeated legislative re-endorsement gave the executive political cover and made courts far more deferential, treating fresh votes as proof that sweeping coercion remained necessary.
[C1.6] Democracies do not need automatic self-destruction to prevent abuse. The superior safeguard is empowering legislatures to affirmatively revoke or narrow emergency powers by simple majority at any time, backed by judicial review. That stops executive drift without letting legislative gridlock paralyze the state at midnight.

[C2.6] Sunsets invite paralysis when action is needed and rubber-stamped deference when it is not.
