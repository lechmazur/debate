[P1.1] Adults have a fundamental right to know their own origins. Once a person reaches adulthood, the state has no legitimate authority to permanently seal their identity from them. Access to one's own adoption records, including identifying information about biological parents, is essential to personal autonomy, medical safety, and equal personhood.
[P1.2] Every non-adopted person takes for granted the ability to know their medical history, ethnic background, and familial patterns of disease. Denying adoptees this knowledge creates a permanent second-class status. Genetic information can reveal risks of hereditary cancers, heart conditions, or mental health vulnerabilities—knowledge that can literally save lives or guide critical decisions about family planning. Withholding it is not neutral; it is active harm.
[P1.3] Identity itself is not a luxury. People need to form coherent life narratives. Learning that a birth certificate is a legal fiction, then being barred forever from the actual details, is a state-enforced lie that can breed lifelong distress. Open records in many places have shown that most adult adoptees seek knowledge first, not disruption; contact remains their choice alone.
[P1.4] Birth parents' privacy deserves weight, but it cannot be absolute against an adult's own body and history. At relinquishment the state may seal records temporarily to protect the infant and parental choice. Extending that seal into the adoptee's adulthood converts a temporary safeguard into an indefinite property interest in another person's identity. Parents who place a child accept that the child will grow into an independent adult with rights of their own.
[P1.5] The legal right claimed here is access to records, not forced relationships. It restores what was taken and what every other citizen already possesses. Refusing it prioritizes outdated secrecy over the living adult whose story it is. That bargain is indefensible.
[P1.6] Adults must control their own history.
