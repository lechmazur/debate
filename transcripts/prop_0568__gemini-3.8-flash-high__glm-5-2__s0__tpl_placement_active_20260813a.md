[C1.3] Forcing a supermarket to take paper currency does not open a bank account, remove overdraft fees, or provide identity documents. It simply preserves their exclusion at the checkout lane while letting policymakers claim they acted.
[C1.4] That preservation comes at a cost. Cash handling is not free, and the expense is not trivial: armored transport, safe storage, employee training, shrinkage, and robbery risk. For major retailers, these costs are absorbed into prices paid by every customer, including the low-income shoppers the mandate purports to serve. A mandate that raises grocery prices to protect cash users effectively taxes the poor to subsidize a payment format that itself reflects financial exclusion.
[C1.5] The mandate is also far broader than advertised. "Essential goods" covers everything from baby formula to over-the-counter medicine to batteries and cleaning supplies. "Major retailers" sweeps in pharmacy chains, big-box stores, and grocers operating on thin margins in food deserts. When compliance costs rise in low-revenue locations, the rational response is closure or relocation, not absorption.
[C1.6] A mandate can accelerate the very food-access problem it claims to solve.
[C1.7] Finally, the resilience argument proves too much. If digital infrastructure fails broadly, the solution is emergency preparedness, government relief distribution, and backup power, not a permanent retail mandate justified by rare outages. Markets already discipline cashless retailers: customers leave, cities respond, and many reverse course voluntarily.
[C1.8] Exclusion is real, but a cash mandate is a costly bandage on a deeper wound. Governments should fix banking access directly rather than offloading the problem onto retail checkout counters.
