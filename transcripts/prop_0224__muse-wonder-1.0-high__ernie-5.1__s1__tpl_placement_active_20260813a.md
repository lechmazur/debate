[P1.1] Keeping an existing nuclear plant alive is the fastest emissions cut a government can buy. The reactor, grid connection, cooling, permits and workforce already exist. A life-extension decision can preserve roughly a gigawatt of around-the-clock, zero-carbon power for another 20 years with refurbishment and regulatory work measured in a few years, not the decade needed to plan, permit, connect and back up an equivalent portfolio of new renewables. Close that plant instead and the grid does not replace it with sunshine at midnight; it burns more gas, as grids have repeatedly done after premature nuclear shutdowns.
[P1.2] That firmness is why nuclear wins per public dollar, not just per generated megawatt-hour. A nuclear unit typically runs above 90 percent of the time. The same annual output from solar may require three to four times the nameplate capacity, plus wind for other hours, plus batteries, plus transmission to move power from where it is produced to where it is used. Each layer attracts its own subsidy, permitting fight and supply chain.
[P1.3] By the time the public has paid for overbuilding, storage, balancing and new lines to deliver comparable reliability, the supposedly cheap option has bought the same ton of avoided carbon several times over.
[P1.4] New build strengthens the same arithmetic when programs are treated as fleets rather than one-off monuments. Repeated designs in South Korea and the United Arab Emirates show that standardization, stable regulation and an experienced supply chain can deliver plants predictably. Those reactors then operate for 60 years or more, spreading their upfront public support over enormous lifetime output. Wind, solar and batteries, by contrast, need replacement and reinvestment within much shorter lives, while their marginal emissions value falls as midday solar saturates and clean power is increasingly curtailed.
[P1.5] From 2026 to 2036, speed favors what can stay online now and run constantly, not what must be rebuilt as a whole system before it reliably replaces fossil fuel.

[C1.5] PRO's argument that firm baseload power makes nuclear more valuable per dollar ignores that batteries and transmission are becoming cheap enough to complement variable renewables without multiplying costs the way PRO suggests. [C1.6] The proposition requires nuclear to win on both life-extension and new-build together. New-build is irrelevant within this decade; life-extension cannot compete on per-dollar emissions speed.
