[P1.1] A worker cannot choose whether wages count as taxable income. A wealthy household often has much more control: assets can appreciate without being sold, and borrowing against them can finance spending without realizing a taxable gain. A system built almost entirely around income and consumption therefore risks taxing ordinary livelihoods more reliably than extraordinary economic power.
[P1.2] An annual tax on large net worth closes that gap. Wealth is not merely yesterday’s income. It provides security, access to credit, control over businesses, and the ability to withstand losses that would devastate other households. Those advantages exist even in years when reported income is small.
[P1.3] The relevant principle is straightforward: substantial capacity to contribute should not disappear from the tax system simply because it takes the form of ownership rather than a paycheck.
[P1.4] The appropriate policy is a modest annual levy above a very high household threshold, supplementing—not abolishing—income and consumption taxes. Ordinary savings stay below that threshold. The base should cover assets broadly, subtract genuine liabilities, and avoid special exemptions that reward shifting wealth between legal categories. Revenue can support public services or reduce pressure on taxes that fall on work and everyday purchases.
[P1.5] Valuation and liquidity are design problems, not automatic vetoes. Marketable assets have observable prices. Private businesses require consistent appraisal rules and review. [P1.7] Nor is the alternative costless. Forgoing this tax means raising revenue elsewhere, cutting services, or accepting greater debt. Each can burden investment and economic opportunity too. The comparison is not between a wealth tax and a world without distortions.
