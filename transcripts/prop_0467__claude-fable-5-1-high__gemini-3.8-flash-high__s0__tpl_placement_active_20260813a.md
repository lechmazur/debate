[C1.3] Consider the reality of the cyber domain. First, unlike a missile with a clear ballistic trajectory [P1.1], cyber intrusions are defined by plausible deniability, third-party routing, and forensic ambiguity. Attackers routinely route code through compromised servers in uninvolved nations or plant false-flag signatures. If an attack on a power grid or fuel network immediately triggers the legal right to a military response, states will be authorized to launch kinetic strikes on the basis of probabilistic code analysis.

Treating extortion or service outages as the legal equivalent of an invasion gives governments a blank check to justify military strikes over server downtime.
[C1.6] Existing international law already provides robust tools: proportional cyber countermeasures, asset freezes, diplomatic isolation, and law enforcement. Article 51 of the UN Charter deliberately set an exceptionally high bar for kinetic self-defense to prevent disputes from escalating into catastrophic wars. Abandoning that standard will not deter adversaries hiding behind proxies; it will only ensure that the next major power outage leads straight to a missile exchange.
