[P1.1] The question is not whether statistical risk differs; it plainly does. It is whether insurers should be legally free to turn those differences into higher prices for protection people cannot reasonably go without. For essential coverage, they should not.
[P1.2] First, actuarial fairness is fairness to a transaction, not necessarily fairness to a person. A genetic predisposition, disability, or chronic illness may justify a higher predicted payout without justifying a higher premium. Charging more makes those already more vulnerable to illness bear an additional financial burden because of that vulnerability. Statistical accuracy describes risk; it does not settle who ought to carry its cost.
[P1.3] Second, essential insurance has a social purpose beyond matching each customer with an expected loss. It provides a common guarantee that illness or accident will not also mean exclusion from basic security. Unlimited risk segmentation reverses that purpose. Lower-risk customers can be drawn into cheaper pools, while higher-risk customers face rising premiums or restricted access.
[P1.4] The market then becomes increasingly efficient at sorting people, but increasingly poor at protecting them. Solidarity is not a sentimental decoration on insurance; it is the reciprocal assurance that any of us may one day need the pool we help sustain.
[P1.5] Third, individualized pricing expands the power to classify. Insurers need not use an openly objectionable trait if data and proxies can approximate it. People may then be penalized through correlations they cannot control, understand, or effectively challenge. For essential protection, the law should not make access depend on surrendering ever more personal information merely to prove that one deserves an affordable premium.
[P1.6] These limits need not mean fiscal indifference. A defined basic package can use community-rated premiums, broad participation, public subsidies where necessary, and risk adjustment among insurers. Optional extras and nonessential insurance can remain open to wider risk pricing. The legal line therefore protects equal access without pretending risks or costs disappear.

[C1.1] Solidarity funded by hiding the bill is not solidarity; it is an unlegislated tax on the wrong people. That is the heart of this motion's flaw.
[C1.2] First, banning risk pricing does not make costs disappear; it reassigns them. Heavy users of essential coverage are, on average, older and wealthier; light users are younger and poorer. A flat premium forces the 26-year-old renting a room on modest wages to subsidize the 62-year-old homeowner's higher expected claims. PRO calls this solidarity [P1.4].
[C1.3] In practice it is redistribution by accident of birth — invisible, unexamined, and often regressive. If the fortunate should pay for the unfortunate, the honest instrument is progressive taxation funding subsidies, not a price rule whose burden lands on whoever is low-risk, whatever their means.
[C1.4] Second, law cannot repeal arithmetic. If premiums ignore risk, low-risk buyers walk away; the pool worsens; premiums climb; more leave. PRO's answer — "broad participation" [P1.6] — is compulsion: mandates and penalties aimed at exactly the marginal young buyers least able to pay. Solidarity enforced by penalty notice is a strange fellowship.
[C1.5] Third, prohibition does not eliminate classification; it drives it underground. An insurer barred from pricing risk openly will select it covertly — through marketing, network design, and benefit structures that attract the healthy and deter the sick. PRO fears opaque proxies [P1.5]; their own remedy multiplies them, trading transparent prices for cherry-picking that regulators cannot see and customers cannot challenge.
[C1.6] Finally, risk pricing is instructive, not merely accurate. Where risk is partly controllable — smoking, driving, home maintenance — premiums make prevention pay. Flatten them and you tax prudence to subsidize recklessness.
[C1.7] The motion promises fellowship and delivers a hidden regressive tax, unstable pools, covert selection, and dulled incentives. Solidarity worth having is funded openly, not smuggled into premiums.
