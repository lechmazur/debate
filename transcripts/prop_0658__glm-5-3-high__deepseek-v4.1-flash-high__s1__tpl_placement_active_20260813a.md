[P1.1] A presumption of illegality is not a ban. It is a burden allocation: the state must justify watching everyone before it does so, rather than citizens having to prove afterwards why they shouldn't have been watched. That burden allocation is exactly what biometric mass surveillance demands, and here's why.
[P1.2] First, the harm is structural, not incidental. Traditional surveillance follows suspicion: a person, a warrant, a limit. Biometric mass surveillance inverts this—it captures everyone's face, gait, or iris by default, building a permanent record of lawful movement. A young woman visiting a clinic, a journalist meeting a source, a citizen attending a protest: none of them broke any law, yet each generates searchable location history.
[P1.3] The harm occurs at the moment of collection, before any misuse. You cannot "un-collect" a dragnet, and you cannot meaningfully consent to it. This is why presumption matters: after the infrastructure exists, after databases fill and dashboards ship, reversal becomes politically and practically near-impossible. The presumption is society's one clean chance to say "show cause first."
[P1.4] Second, the record on error and abuse is not speculative. Facial recognition systems have long performed worse on women and people with darker skin, meaning misidentification—and the street stops, detentions, and suspicion that follow—lands disproportionately on already over-policed communities. And function creep is the norm, not the exception: tools sold for counterterrorism end up tracking shoplifters, then protesters, then ex-partners. Every administrative state drifts toward using what it has built.
[P1.5] Third, the emergency carve-out answers the only serious objection. If a city faces a bombing, an abducted child, an active manhunt—fine: a narrow, time-limited, judicially reviewable authorization can be obtained in hours. What defenders of constant surveillance actually want is to skip that step permanently.
[P1.6] But "we might need it someday" has never been a sufficient reason to abolish the presumption of innocence for everyone, always.
[P1.7] Absent emergency, dragnet surveillance of innocent people in public should be unlawful until proven necessary. That's the proposition. It protects liberty without handcuffing genuine emergencies—and the burden belongs exactly where it places it.

[C1.1] PRO calls this a burden allocation. It is a ban with an asterisk. Once the only lawful path is a narrowly defined emergency, routine, regulated, non-emergency use becomes illegal. That is not a presumption; it is a prohibition dressed in procedural language.
[C1.2] Public spaces are already spaces of lawful observation. Cameras, license-plate readers, officers on foot—none require individualized suspicion for every glance. Biometrics do not create observation; they make a known face matchable at scale. The real questions are purpose, retention, access, and oversight.
[C1.3] Those can be answered by statute: use only for listed serious crimes, delete non-matches immediately, require warrants for persistent tracking, ban use at protests, clinics, and places of worship, audit every query, and punish misuse. That regime targets the abuse PRO describes while preserving legitimate use.
[C1.4] The emergency-only rule leaves huge public-safety gaps. A serial rapist who strikes monthly, a trafficking network, a fugitive wanted for murder, a child abduction that does not fit a formal emergency—these are not one-off crises, but they are exactly when identifying a face in a crowd matters. Under this proposition, police must wait for a catastrophe or let the offender keep moving. And if agencies cannot test and govern systems in ordinary conditions, the first deployment happens in a crisis, with panic, poor protocols, and no accountability.
[C1.5] PRO says the harm occurs at collection. In public, collection is already happening; the marginal harm is permanent searchable identity. That is a design choice, not a law of nature. Match-and-discard systems, strict deletion, and independent audits can prevent the dragnet database.
[C1.6] The proposition instead treats every biometric deployment as guilty until an emergency proves it innocent—sacrificing ordinary safety without stopping mission creep.
[C1.7] The choice is not mass surveillance or emergency-only paralysis. It is regulated, rights-protecting use versus a categorical prohibition dressed as a presumption. Vote CON.
