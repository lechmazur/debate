[P1.1] The primary obligation of any legal system is to secure the basic safety of its citizens. When individuals accused of crimes pose a high, scientifically validated risk of committing serious violent offenses while awaiting trial, the state must have the authority to detain them. Allowing broader use of validated, risk-based pretrial detention tools is not just a policy preference; it is a moral imperative to prevent irreversible harm.
[P1.2] Opponents of this measure often focus on the potential increase in pretrial detention rates. However, this concern conflates indiscriminate, wealth-based detention—like cash bail, which detains the poor regardless of risk—with precise, risk-based detention. By focusing strictly on validated predictions of serious violent reoffending, we isolate the truly dangerous. When a validated algorithmic tool, built on empirical data and stripped of subjective human biases, indicates a high probability of violence, ignoring that warning is an abdication of public duty.
[P1.3] The societal cost of a single preventable violent crime—be it homicide, sexual assault, or physical violence—is catastrophic. Victims suffer irreversible trauma or loss of life, and communities lose their security. These harms are permanent. In contrast, pretrial detention is a temporary, legally safeguarded measure subject to strict due process and speedy trial requirements.
[P1.4] We must weigh the temporary deprivation of liberty for a high-risk individual against the permanent deprivation of life or safety for an innocent victim. When validated tools tell us, with high statistical confidence, that a person is highly likely to commit a violent crime if released, the choice is clear. Protecting the public from violent victimization must take precedence. Broadening the use of these tools ensures that safety, not wealth or judicial guesswork, determines who remains in custody.

[C1.1] Risk assessment tools cannot deliver the precision needed to justify wider pretrial detention. Even the best-validated instruments produce false positives, labeling people as high-risk who would never commit violence. Expanding their use therefore detains individuals who pose no actual threat, simply because an algorithm places them in a statistical group. That trade-off converts a prediction error into weeks or months of lost liberty for defendants who have not been convicted.
[C1.2] The claimed distinction between risk-based and wealth-based detention collapses in practice. Validation studies rely on historical arrest and conviction data that already embed earlier disparities in policing and charging. When those same patterns feed the tool, the result is not neutral risk assessment but automated replication of past biases. Higher detention rates then concentrate on the same neighborhoods already over-policed, without evidence that the added incarceration prevents the violent acts it targets.
[C1.3] Temporary detention still inflicts permanent costs. Job loss, housing instability, and family separation occur immediately upon jailing and compound over time. A person held for sixty days on a risk score may lose employment that would have kept them stable and law-abiding. These collateral harms are real and measurable; they are not offset by the abstract promise that some future crimes somewhere might have been averted.
[C1.4] The legal system already possesses narrower tools, such as targeted supervision, electronic monitoring, and swift revocation for new offenses. Broadening detention authority is not required to address genuine threats; it is instead the easiest administrative shortcut. Once courts accept statistical forecasts as sufficient grounds for confinement, the threshold for detention steadily lowers, moving the system from proof of dangerousness to actuarial convenience.
[C1.5] The core objection is not that safety is unimportant, but that the proposed expansion sacrifices verifiable individual rights for speculative aggregate gains that the imperfect tools cannot reliably deliver.
