[P1.1] Kinetic anti-satellite tests are among the few military activities that damage the attacker as surely as the target. When a missile shatters a satellite, it doesn't remove a threat—it manufactures thousands of metal fragments orbiting at nearly eight kilometers per second, each one a bullet that never stops firing. China's 2007 destruction of the Fengyun-1C weather satellite generated more trackable debris than any prior event in orbit, and Russia's 2021 test scattered fragments into the same altitude band the International Space Station must transit.
[P1.2] The station has had to maneuver repeatedly to avoid debris clouds. These are not hypothetical harms; they are documented consequences of exactly the activity the proposition would prohibit.
[P1.3] The deeper danger is kinetic. At orbital velocities, collisions between debris fragments and functioning satellites spawn more debris. This cascade can persist for generations, progressively closing off low Earth orbit. The cost falls on everyone: the state that conducted the test, its adversaries, and the commercial constellations that now carry global communications, navigation, and climate monitoring.
[P1.4] No nation benefits from a debris field that imperils its own reconnaissance satellites and crewed spacecraft.
[P1.5] This is a textbook collective action problem. Each spacefaring state has unilateral reason to test its ASAT capability, yet universal restraint would leave every state better off. Only a treaty changes the payoffs, converting a destructive race into a shared norm.
[P1.6] Critically, the proposition is narrow. It bans tests, not the existence or deployment of ASAT weapons. Deterrence is preserved; states retain their arsenals and their retaliatory options. What ends is the one activity with enormous spillover costs and near-zero unique military benefit—verification is straightforward, since launches and intercepts are visible to existing tracking networks.
[P1.7] Adopt the ban. The alternative is treating shared orbits as a firing range whose debris outlives every satellite now in use.

Testing validates guidance, sensors, command links, and operator performance; it also reveals failures before a crisis. Prohibiting all tests encourages states either to field weapons whose behavior they do not understand or to relocate development into ambiguous programs.
[C1.3] Uncertain capabilities are not a stable foundation for deterrence: they invite miscalculation, worst-case planning, and premature use.
[C1.4] Nor is verification as simple as seeing a rocket. Tracking networks may detect launches and collisions, but a legal regime must determine purpose, responsibility, and whether an event falls within “kinetic ASAT testing” rather than missile defense, rendezvous operations, or another dual-use activity. Detection is not attribution, and attribution is not adjudication. Transparent states accept the constraint; states willing to exploit ambiguity retain options.
[C1.5] The treaty therefore risks selecting for compliance among those least likely to create the danger.
[C1.6] The debris concern is real, which is precisely why the rule should track debris. A deliberately low-altitude test with rapidly decaying fragments is not equivalent to shattering a satellite at a long-lived operational altitude. Altitude and debris-lifetime limits, advance notification, consultation, and debris-generating-test moratoria address the externality without banning low-risk validation. The growing voluntary commitments against debris-generating direct-ascent tests provide a more realistic base for that approach.
[C1.7] PRO’s case works only by treating every test as Fengyun-1C and every weapon as trustworthy without testing. Neither premise survives. Reject a universal test ban that weakens reliable deterrence, rewards ambiguity, and still does not secure orbit.

[P4.3] A2: Low altitude is not harmless. No test is necessary for deterrence: arsenals and declared retaliatory capability already compel worst-case planning.
[P4.4] A debris-limit alternative is arbitrary and manipulable—states can engineer conditions to skirt "rapid decay" while preserving the infrastructure and operators for deadlier tests. It transforms a clear prohibition into endless technical litigation.
