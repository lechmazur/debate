[P1.1] Every default is a choice someone else makes for you. On most food-delivery apps, that choice is made by the party that profits most when you pay more. Open the app and delivery is preselected, the "priority" tier is often highlighted, a tip suggestion is filled in, and pickup sits behind a toggle many users never notice. The question in this debate is simple: when the app sets the starting point, should it lean toward what the customer probably wants, or toward what maximizes the platform's take?
[P1.2] My first argument is that defaults decide outcomes. Behavioral economics has shown the same thing again and again: people stick with preset options, whether in retirement enrollment, organ donation, or software settings. That means defaults are never neutral. When the app preselects delivery, many people end up paying delivery, service, and priority fees they never actively chose, even when the restaurant is a few minutes away.
[P1.3] Shifting defaults toward pickup or the cheaper tier doesn't remove any option. It just stops treating inertia as consent.
[P1.4] My second argument is that the current steering exploits a misalignment of incentives. Fees are often revealed late, split across several line items, and inflated relative to the menu price. A customer who ordered from a restaurant two blocks from home could easily pay much more than the food itself costs. Surfacing and defaulting to lower-cost paths when they fit, such as short distances, repeat pickup users, or standard over priority timing, corrects a system engineered around the customer's inattention.
[P1.5] My third argument is that the benefits spread beyond the wallet. Pickup orders typically carry lower commissions for restaurants, which operate on thin margins. Fewer unnecessary short-haul trips means less congestion and fewer cars idling outside restaurants.
[P1.6] The CON side will say convenience matters and couriers need orders. Both points are fair, but neither survives the motion. Anyone who wants delivery still gets it with one tap. And a business model that depends on customers not noticing a cheaper option is not protecting workers.
