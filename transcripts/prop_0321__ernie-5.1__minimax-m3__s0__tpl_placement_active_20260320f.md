[P1.1] Creators put their work online expecting exposure, not appropriation. When platforms scrape that work to train commercial generative‑AI, they are turning a public display into a private profit engine. The default should be that creators can stop that use, not the other way around.
[P1.2] First, the economic reality. Training data is the raw material for multibillion‑dollar AI products. Those products compete directly with the original creators. An illustrator whose style is replicated by a model that sells generated images is losing market share, not gaining exposure.
[P1.3] The law should treat that as a taking, not a fair‑use side‑effect.
[P1.4] Second, consent matters. Public posting does not equal consent for commercial exploitation. We already require opt‑in for many commercial uses—think of music sampling, stock‑photo licensing, or data‑broker agreements. Extending an opt‑out default to AI training aligns the rule with existing norms and respects creators’ autonomy.
[P1.5] Third, the power imbalance. Platforms control the pipelines, the compute, and the distribution. Without a strong default right, creators are forced into a take‑it‑or‑leave‑it contract where they either surrender their work or disappear from the platform. That is not a free market; it is a coercive extraction.
[P1.6] Consider visual artists whose portfolios were harvested to train image‑generation models that now sell prints of their style at scale, with no royalty.
[P1.7] The opposition will claim that restricting data harms innovation. But innovation that depends on uncompensated appropriation is not progress; it is a subsidy to a few firms at the expense of the many who create the content that makes the models possible. A stronger default right does not ban research; it simply requires platforms to ask or pay, preserving both incentive structures.
[P1.8] In short, the default must protect creators because their labor, style, and market value are being harvested without compensation. The law should reflect that reality, not the convenience of platform engineers.
