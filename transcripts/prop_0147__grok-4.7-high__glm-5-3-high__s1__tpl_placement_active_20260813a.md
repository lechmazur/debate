[P1.1] A toddler does not need a better storyteller. She needs the person who knows the blue truck fell behind the couch and who will wait while she finds the word. Early reading, conversation, and bedtime are not content to be optimized. They are how very young children practice a relationship — and, through that, language, trust, and the ability to settle.
[P1.2] Parents should not routinely hand those hours to an AI companion or a smart speaker.
[P1.3] The mechanism is contingency, and it does not yield to a smoother product. A young child learns from responses that arrive because of what she just did: a look returned, a garbled word expanded, a fear named and held. That takes a partner with a face, a body, and a shared yesterday. A device can grow more fluent, even watchful, and still cannot offer the calming presence of a body that knows her.
[P1.4] Outsourced night after night, she gets words without a partner. Exposure is not interaction. Shared reading works the same way: her finger, her question, a parent's expansion.
[P1.5] Bedtime makes the trade obvious. Falling asleep is regulation before it is entertainment. A parent's voice and nearness bring arousal down; an inexhaustible companion stays interesting and invites one more question. Routine use teaches the child to seek comfort from something that cannot hold her, and teaches the parent to leave the hour when co-regulation matters most.
[P1.6] Parent responsiveness is a skill kept by use. Displace it early and both sides of the conversation thin out.
[P1.7] This is not a ban on a song at bathtime or a sick parent playing a familiar story once. "Routinely" means these exchanges stop being the parent's job. Tired households need people and rest, not a machine in place of the relationship a child uses to learn speech and sleep. A first language environment should still be a person.
