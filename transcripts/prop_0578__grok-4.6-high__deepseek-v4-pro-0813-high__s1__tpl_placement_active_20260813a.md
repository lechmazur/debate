[C1.2] A wrongful removal is permanent, frequently unappealable in practice, and potentially fatal. The promised gains are speculative; the added risk is not.
[C1.3] The first problem is that expanded staffing does not guarantee better judging. A rapid hiring surge under political pressure to clear dockets tends to produce metrics built around completions: cases closed, hearings held, orders issued. New judges, often with little immigration or asylum background, are more likely to use that new capacity to resolve cases quickly rather than provide more time per case. Capacity alone does not create caution; the institution’s incentives run toward velocity.
[C1.4] Remote hearings multiply the danger. Video and phone hearings degrade credibility assessment. Judges miss posture, hesitation, and the way a trauma survivor avoids a question. Interpretation over remote audio is more prone to omission, especially for indigenous-language speakers.
[C1.5] Respondents may struggle to identify their interpreter, confer privately with counsel, or understand what is happening when the connection drops. These failures do not appear as unfairness in a caseload statistic; they appear as a “not credible” finding and an order of removal.
[C1.6] Compliance gains are overclaimed as well. A near-term date only improves compliance if notice reaches the right person, is understood, and can be accessed. Remote notice failures and technology barriers can raise in absentia orders instead of lowering them. Fairness is not a scheduling output; it is the ability to be understood, represented, and believed.
[C1.7] The comparison that should govern the rest of this debate is stark: delay causes slow-motion harm, but this proposal creates a faster route to final, irreversible error.
