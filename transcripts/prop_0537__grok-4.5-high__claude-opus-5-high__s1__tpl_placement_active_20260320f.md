[P1.1] Governments that entrust critical public-interest AI—pandemic modeling, emergency logistics, defense analysis, public-health diagnostics—to private clouds and foreign data centers choose dependence over duty. That choice is reckless.
[P1.2] Sovereign compute means the state can guarantee access when it matters. Private providers can throttle, deprioritize, or sever service for commercial, legal, or geopolitical reasons. A foreign capital can pressure a hyperscaler, impose export controls, or interrupt infrastructure in a crisis. Supply chains and cloud access have already been weaponized as leverage.
[P1.3] Critical AI workloads cannot sit on someone else’s terms.
[P1.4] Public missions diverge from private incentives. Commercial clouds optimize utilization and margin. They will not idle spare capacity for rare disaster simulations, nor rank a rural hospital network above a high-paying commercial model. Sovereign capacity lets governments reserve and steer compute toward resilience, equity, and long-horizon science markets undervalue.
[P1.5] Dependence also erodes accountability. When systems that administer benefits, climate planning, or security run on opaque external stacks, democratic oversight shrinks to a vendor petition. Sovereign infrastructure keeps the keys and the logs inside the polity citizens can sanction.
[P1.6] This is not a bid to nationalize every GPU. It is a narrow claim: for the thin, vital layer of public-interest uses where continuity, priority, and control are non-negotiable, governments must build and hold capacity rather than rent it indefinitely. Ordinary workloads may stay efficient on commercial clouds. The loads that define state capacity in an AI age require ownership as insurance.
[P1.7] Heavy dependence is not pragmatism. It is a slow transfer of strategic agency to boardrooms and other capitals. States that refuse the transfer retain the ability to act when contracts and foreign goodwill fail.
