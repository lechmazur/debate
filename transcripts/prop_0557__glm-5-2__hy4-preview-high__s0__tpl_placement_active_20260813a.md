[P1.1] Public universities serve the broadest cross-section of students in American higher education. Reinstating standardized testing restores the one tool best suited to that task.
[P1.2] A 3.8 GPA means different things in different schools. One offers seventeen AP courses; another offers none. One grades harshly; another inflates. Without a common benchmark, admissions officers cannot compare applicants on equal footing.
[P1.3] Standardized tests, whatever their imperfections, provide that common metric.
[P1.4] The deeper case is equity. When universities drop test requirements, they don't eliminate competition—they replace one measure with others that favor the already-advantaged. Essays reward coaching. Extracurriculars reward money and free time.
[P1.5] Letters of recommendation reward familiarity with elite norms. [P1.6] Instead, they tilted it toward students whose advantages are less visible—parental education, test-prep disguised as "academic enrichment," and consultant-polished applications.
[P1.7] Grade inflation compounds the problem. Since the pandemic, transcripts increasingly cluster at the top, compressing the signal. When most applicants arrive with high GPAs, admissions officers lose the ability to distinguish genuine readiness from institutional generosity.
