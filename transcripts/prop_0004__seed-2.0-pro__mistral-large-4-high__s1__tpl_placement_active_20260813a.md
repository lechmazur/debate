[C2.2] If you hire twenty-five percent more workers to cover the lost hours, your total wage bill rises by twenty-five percent, even if each individual produces the same output in fewer hours. The productivity gain stays with the worker; the cost lands on the employer.
[C2.3] Even if one national logistics firm survived the transition, a single employer is not an economy. And logistics is not healthcare. A warehouse can compress schedules—running trucks longer on four days—but a hospital cannot compress a cardiac arrest into Monday through Thursday. [C2.4] Yes, hospitals rotate days off today, but those are part-time contracts, weekend premiums, or overtime. Either path raises unit labour costs.
[C2.5] My case does not rest on hypothetical disasters. A legislated twenty-five percent rise in unit labour costs across advanced economies is arithmetic, not speculation. When labour becomes more expensive per unit of output in globally competitive markets, firms respond by shedding workers, freezing hiring, or relocating capital. These are mechanical consequences, not doomsaying.
[C2.6] The proposition confuses individual productivity with economic reality. A worker may produce the same output in thirty-two hours, but if the firm must pay twenty-five percent more in total wages to cover the same five-day output, competitiveness erodes and jobs follow. You cannot legislate away the price of labour.
