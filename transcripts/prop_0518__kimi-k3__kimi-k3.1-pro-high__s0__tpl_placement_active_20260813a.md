[P1.1] Noncompete clauses began as protection for genuine secrets. They ended as a condition of employment for sandwich makers, hairstylists, and camp counselors. Tens of millions of workers — credible estimates put it around one in five in the United States — cannot take a better job across the street without risking a lawsuit they cannot afford. That is not intellectual property protection.
[P1.2] It is wage suppression with a legal department.
[P1.3] Three reasons to restrict them for nearly all workers.
[P1.4] First, the legitimate interests are already guarded by sharper tools. Trade secret law, nondisclosure agreements, non-solicitation clauses, and training repayment agreements cover everything an employer honestly claims to fear losing. When a firm instead reaches for the bluntest instrument — a ban on working for any competitor — it is not protecting secrets; it is destroying the worker's only real leverage, the outside offer. Every broad exception invites firms to launder wage suppression through "legitimate business interest" language.
[P1.5] Second, broad exceptions don't merely weaken the rule; they invert it. Vague standards like "reasonableness" get adjudicated after the fact, in court, by the party with lawyers. A low-wage worker served with a cease-and-desist letter does not litigate — she complies. Bright-line restriction is the only version that protects people who cannot afford to be right.
[P1.6] "Nearly all workers" already leaves room for genuinely narrow cases; broad employer exceptions are the loophole that swallows the rule.
[P1.7] Third, mobility is the engine. Regions that refuse to enforce noncompetes — California most famously — built dense labor markets where knowledge spills between firms, startups spin out of incumbents, and wages rise because employers must compete for talent. An employer who wants to keep a worker already has a lawful, effective tool: pay more. When firms prefer the injunction to the raise, that tells you what noncompetes are actually for.
[P1.8] The proposition asks employers to compete in the labor market rather than contract out of it. That is what a free labor market actually requires.

Noncompetes imposed on low-wage workers are abusive and should be unenforceable. But converting that justified outrage into a rule covering nearly every worker sacrifices legitimate protection without solving the underlying problem: unequal bargaining and litigation power.
[C1.2] PRO’s claim that sharper tools already protect every legitimate interest is simply wrong. Trade-secret litigation usually begins after the employee has left, when the crucial evidence may sit inside a competitor’s systems. An NDA prohibits disclosure, but proving disclosure is difficult when sensitive knowledge shapes a rival’s design, pricing strategy, or product timetable. Non-solicitation clauses protect customers, not confidential technology.
[C1.3] A short, narrowly defined noncompete can sometimes prevent the harm rather than compensate for it afterward. Prevention matters especially to smaller firms that cannot survive years of litigation against a better-funded rival.
[C1.4] Nor does “nearly all workers” create the bright line PRO promises. The risk does not track a worker’s job title or wage alone; it tracks access, function, and the actual restraint. Once nearly everyone is categorically covered, litigation merely shifts to whether someone fits an exception, whether a firm counts as a competitor, and whether particular information qualifies for protection. Employers with lawyers will still navigate that uncertainty better than workers.
[C1.5] The better safeguard is therefore targeted, not categorical: prohibit noncompetes for low-wage and low-access workers; require advance notice and meaningful consideration; strictly limit duration, geography, and competitive scope; and place the burden on employers to justify each restraint. Garden leave or paid notice can further reduce the burden on workers. These rules directly attack coercion without declaring that confidential business interests are almost always fictitious.
[C1.6] California proves that an innovative economy can flourish without routine noncompetes; it does not prove that noncompetes never protect legitimate investment. Its universities, capital markets, and technology cluster cannot be treated as one variable’s controlled experiment.

[P4.3] A2: Notice what CON just conceded by saying "fully paid." A fully paid restraint is garden leave: the firm buys the sensitive interval, the worker keeps her salary, and the cost sits where the claim of value sits. That is precisely the priced prevention I defended [P2.5], and nothing in the motion forbids a firm from purchasing it. What the proposition forbids is the unpaid version — protection financed out of the worker's wages because the firm would rather not pay.
[P4.4] And where disclosure genuinely cannot be proved, the answer cannot be to ban lawful work on suspicion. We do not strip an engineer of her livelihood for a use of her own mind that no one can demonstrate. An unprovable harm is a reason to invest in retention, not a license to presume theft.
[P4.5] Both questions share one spine, and it is the spine of this debate. CON asks: who must secure the worker's freedom in advance, and who absorbs a loss the firm cannot prove? Their answers: the worker must be right before she moves, and the firm is compensated free of charge. Mine: the worker moves by default, and the firm pays for whatever protection it claims to need.
[P4.6] Every live issue — chilling effects, small firms, inevitable use — reduces to that allocation of cost and proof. Put both on the party with resources, and you have the proposition.
