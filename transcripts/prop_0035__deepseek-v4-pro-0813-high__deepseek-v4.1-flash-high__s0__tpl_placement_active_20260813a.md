[P1.1] Clean indoor air belongs in the same category as clean water, safe food, and fire exits: a baseline protection for any building the public must enter. Buildings are not private bubbles. Shared air transmits respiratory disease, wildfire smoke, mold, and pollutants; when one under-ventilated classroom or office becomes a transmission site, the harm spills over to families and hospitals. Because occupants—schoolchildren, workers, visitors—cannot meaningfully inspect or fix a building’s ventilation, individual choice fails.
[P1.2] Markets also under-supply ventilation. Owners pay the cost of filters, upgrades, and energy, while the benefits—better attendance, sharper cognition, fewer infections—go to students, employees, and public health systems. That split incentive is why decades of voluntary guidance have failed. Schools still run with windows sealed and CO2 rising enough to measurably dull concentration; offices recycle stale air through sick-building complaints; and public buildings become high-risk settings during flu, COVID, and smoke events.
[P1.3] Enforceable standards reset that incentive. They create a common floor, so no owner gains a competitive advantage by skimping on air safety. Modern demand-controlled ventilation and better filtration can meet such standards without wasting energy. The price is small compared with lost learning, missed workdays, asthma flare-ups, and avoidable outbreaks.
[P1.4] In any case, we do not treat sanitation or fire codes as optional because compliance costs money.
[P1.5] This is not a radical expansion of the state. It extends the same reasoning we already use for plumbing, food handling, and carbon monoxide alarms to the air people breathe for eight hours a day. Governments should set enforceable indoor air quality and ventilation standards because clean indoor air is a public necessity, not a private amenity. A child in a sealed classroom cannot choose better ventilation; the government can.

[C1.1] PRO's analogy to clean water and fire exits is doing all the work, and it breaks. We know how to make water potable and exits wide. Indoor air is a mixture: CO2, PM2.5, viruses, mold, VOCs, outdoor smoke. The right response in a wildfire zone is filtration; in a crowded clinic, ventilation and masks; in a moldy basement, moisture repair.
[C1.2] One enforceable numeric standard across schools, offices, and public buildings cannot capture that. It either sets a low bar that misses real risks or a high bar many buildings cannot meet.
[C1.3] That leads to the central tradeoff PRO ignores. Enforceable standards cost money: monitoring, filters, ductwork, HVAC capacity, energy. Older schools and small businesses—precisely those least able to pay—would face the largest retrofits. If government mandates them without full funding, districts cut teachers, maintenance, or programs; small employers cut staff or close.
[C1.4] If it funds them, the money comes from somewhere else, often other public health priorities. A mandate does not create resources.
[C1.5] Worse, standards create false assurance and compliance theater. A monitor reading acceptable at 10 a.m. says little about a packed afternoon or a smoke event. Owners learn to satisfy the metric, not the health goal. Enforcement then becomes inspection burden and litigation, with arbitrary waivers for favored or struggling buildings.
[C1.6] And the science often cannot supply a bright line. The same CO2 level can mean crowding in one room and sensor error in another. Different pollutants require different controls. Regulators will either pick arbitrary numbers or write complex rules that only large institutions can navigate—again favoring the wealthy.
[C1.7] Ventilation matters. But the motion's instrument is a blunt, expensive, premature one-size-fits-all rule. Better to target resources to sick buildings, require transparency, and set outbreak-specific measures, rather than pretend a single enforceable number can make every public building safe.

[P2.1] The complexity objection is not an argument against enforceable standards; it is an argument for well-designed ones. CON says indoor air is too varied to regulate [C1.1]. But we do not refuse to regulate drinking water because it can contain lead, bacteria, nitrates, and PFAS. We set contaminant-specific limits, treatment duties, and monitoring requirements.
[P2.2] The motion says “standards,” not “one identical number for every room.” A school could face a minimum outdoor-air ventilation rate, filtration requirements for smoke and pathogen conditions, and CO2 monitoring. A clinic could face higher air-change rates. That is complexity handled within enforceable rules, not paralysis.
[P2.3] The cost argument cuts against CON. Under-regulation is not free. Old schools are exactly where sealed windows and mold impose asthma, absenteeism, and outbreaks on children who cannot leave. Calling retrofits expensive only tells us who currently bears the cost: the occupants.
[P2.4] A phased enforceable floor, with public funding where needed, is no more radical than phased removal of lead pipes or adding fire alarms. We do not say small landlords cannot afford exits, so skip exits. We set the baseline and address affordability through timing or aid. The proposition concerns whether government should set the floor, not whether every retrofit must be instant.
[P2.5] False assurance also argues against weak, unenforced guidance, not against enforceability. A single reading can be gamed; that is why standards include continuous monitoring, smoke-event triggers, and inspection. CON’s preferred transparency and outbreak-specific measures have been the status quo and failed: owners can post poor readings and do nothing. Enforcement changes the consequence.
[P2.6] So the clash is not perfect precision versus freedom. It is whether a measurable, enforceable baseline should replace discretion that has left children and workers breathing known hazards. Complexity and cost are reasons to set standards carefully, not to keep the floor absent.
