[P1.1] Large employers should be legally required to negotiate with elected worker councils on workplace AI because these systems remake jobs, authority, and risk more profoundly than almost any prior technology. When a firm rolls out keystroke monitors, automated scheduling, or generative tools that rewrite tasks, frontline workers absorb the first costs: intensified pace, opaque evaluations, lost discretion, and displacement pressure. Leaving those choices to management alone entrenches a power imbalance that voluntary consultation cannot correct.
[P1.2] Elected councils supply democratic legitimacy and ground-level expertise. Workers know the workflow frictions, safety gaps, and edge cases that vendors and executives routinely overlook. Real negotiation—bargaining over rollout terms, training rights, data limits, human overrides, and transition support—turns that knowledge into enforceable agreements. Notice periods or suggestion boxes leave the final call with the same parties who may chase short-term metrics over durable productivity and fairness.
[P1.3] Legal compulsion is required for large employers precisely because they possess the resources and market power to ignore softer norms. Competitive pressure rewards the cheapest surveillance tools and fastest automation that externalize social costs. A statutory duty does not ban AI or freeze innovation; it forces justification of how deployment protects dignity, shares gains, and manages harm. Co-determination traditions already show that technology consultations raise acceptance and cut later conflict without halting progress.
[P1.4] Without the mandate, AI defaults to pure managerial control—an instrument of extraction. With it, deployment becomes a negotiated social process. That match between AI’s scale and a democratic check is the only workable settlement.
