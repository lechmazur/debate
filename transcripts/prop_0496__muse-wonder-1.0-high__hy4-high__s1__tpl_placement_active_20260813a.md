[P1.1] Combat is not a service to be outsourced. It is the gravest decision a democracy makes — who kills, who dies, and in whose name. When that decision is handed to private military contractors, the public loses control over it, and that is why combat roles must be banned.
[P1.2] First, contractors are a deliberate loophole around democratic limits. Legislatures cap troop deployments, require authorizations, and face voters when casualties mount. Contractors sit outside those checks. A government can double its effective fighting force without asking for another vote, exceed a troop ceiling announced to the public, and sustain an unpopular war because contractor deaths are not tallied on the evening news in the same way as soldiers' deaths and their contracts rarely face the same scrutiny.
[P1.3] The war continues, but the political price of continuing it disappears.
[P1.4] Second, the chain of accountability that makes force democratic snaps once profit enters. A soldier answers up a public chain of command, under military law, subject to courts-martial, inspectors, and legislative inquiry. A contractor answers to a firm that can claim commercial confidentiality, move personnel across borders, and fight disclosure. When contractors killed civilians in Baghdad's Nisour Square in 2007, years of jurisdictional confusion followed over who could investigate, charge, and punish — confusion that would have been unthinkable for uniformed troops.
[P1.5] That is not an accident; it is what happens when lethal force is governed by contract rather than by command.
[P1.6] This creates moral hazard for leaders. If escalation is politically cheap and legally murky, leaders will choose it more often and answer for it less. Voters cannot reward or punish what they cannot see, count, or stop.
[P1.7] Supporting troops with food or transport is not the issue. Pulling triggers, guarding territory with lethal force, and conducting offensive operations are sovereign acts. Keep them solely with forces the people, through law, can authorize, limit, and hold to account.

[C1.1] The slogan that “combat is not a service to be outsourced” [P1.1] confuses the sovereign decision with the personnel carrying it out. Democratic accountability attaches to authorizing force, defining the mandate, setting rules, and punishing abuse. It does not evaporate because a rifle-holder receives a company paycheck. If a legislature can fund, limit, audit, and terminate a mission, it can make that control reach contractors.
[C1.2] Nor is a private chain a black hole. A contract is a legal instrument: it can require personnel rolls, casualty reporting, command integration, clear criminal jurisdiction, and legislative notice. A firm can be fined, barred from future work, or removed; its employees can be prosecuted. Profit gives the state a lever.
[C1.3] The Nisour Square confusion in [P1.4] shows bad jurisdictional drafting, not a reason to prohibit every contracted defensive role.
[C1.4] The loophole argument [P1.2] proves too much. Executives can obscure force through classified units, proxies, or misleading labels; democracies answer with authorization, reporting, and oversight, not blanket bans. Troop ceilings can cover all armed personnel under public contract, contractor deaths can be counted in the same tables, and contracts can be disclosed with redactions. The remedy for hidden force is compulsory visibility.
[C1.5] A ban needs a bright line that does not exist. Is an embassy guard returning fire in a siege in combat? A convoy escort defending supplies? A trainer firing back in an ambush?
[C1.6] Either it sweeps in armed security or governments relabel it into euphemisms. Worse, prohibition shifts force toward intelligence assets, proxies, or host-nation armed groups, where voters have fewer documents and fewer named ministers.
[C1.7] Regulated public contracts leave fingerprints: a payer, a file, a rulebook, an accountable minister. Prohibition leaves the demand for force intact and drives it behind the curtain. Democratic control comes from enforceable authorization and scrutiny, not from mistaking a uniform for accountability.

Governments do not hire combat contractors despite the visibility gap; they hire them because of it. [P2.2] If a contract must mimic enlistment — same reporting, jurisdiction, command and public tally — private combat offers no democratic advantage, only an extra layer that can be loosened when inconvenient.
[P2.3] That layer matters in practice. Fining or debarring a firm is corporate punishment after the fact, not command before the trigger is pulled. A commander can relieve a soldier instantly, confine him, and court-martial him under one public system. A contracting officer cannot command a corporate employee the same way; that employee can resign, leave the country, or be moved by his employer while investigators argue over jurisdiction.
[P2.4] Nisour Square was not one clerical error. Its diffusion was useful precisely because no single public authority fully owned the killing: State Department contract, private employer, Iraqi victims, American prosecution years later.
[P2.5] The definitional objection also fails. Law routinely distinguishes combat from support, offensive from defensive, armed from unarmed. A ban can cover offensive operations and armed guarding or escort in an active conflict where lethal engagement is foreseeable. Borderline trainers do not erase the core case: hired personnel holding ground and shooting.
[P2.6] The proxy warning proves too little. Regular troops remain available — and that is the point. If a mission cannot survive a vote, a cap, and a public casualty count, leaders should not purchase a quieter substitute. Illegal evasion through proxies is a reason for enforcement, not for legalizing evasion by contract.
[P2.7] A uniformed chain beginning and ending with the voter leaves a stronger trace than corporate fingerprints.
