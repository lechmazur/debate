[P1.1] Obesity is not a knowledge deficit, it is an environment surplus. We did not get a population-wide rise in weight because millions of people simultaneously lost willpower. We got it because the food environment changed: ultra-processed, hyper-palatable calories became the cheapest, most available, most heavily promoted option everywhere, from gas station checkouts to school hallways and hospital vending machines.
[P1.2] You cannot out-educate a $10 billion marketing machine. Individual behavior campaigns — eat less, move more, read the label — assume people make food choices in a vacuum, weighing health information rationally. But choice is shaped by price, placement, and persuasion. When a child sees cartoon tigers selling sugar before breakfast, when a dollar buys more calories in chips than in carrots, when delivery apps push combos at midnight, information is not the binding constraint.
[P1.3] That's why decades of poster campaigns, calorie counts, and personal responsibility messaging have coincided with rising obesity. They ask individuals to swim upstream without touching the current.
[P1.4] Changing the current works. When Chile put black warning labels on junk food and banned its marketing to kids, purchases of sugary drinks fell sharply. When London banned junk food ads on the Tube, households bought substantially fewer unhealthy calories. Taxes on sugary drinks in cities from Berkeley to Mexico have cut consumption.
[P1.5] None of these require heroic individual discipline; they make the healthy choice the easy, default choice.
[P1.6] This is also the equity case. Behavior campaigns help the already advantaged — those with time, money, grocery stores nearby. Food environment reform helps most where the environment is worst, in neighborhoods flooded with fast food and targeted most aggressively by soda marketing.
[P1.7] Public policy should focus where leverage is greatest. That is not in another app telling people what they already know, but in stopping an environment that profits from them ignoring it.
