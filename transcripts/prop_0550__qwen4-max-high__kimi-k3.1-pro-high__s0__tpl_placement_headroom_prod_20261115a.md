[P1.1] The decisive word in this motion is "expanding." No one disputes that college graduates out-earn high school graduates on average. But averages describe the people already enrolled. Expansion buys the next student — the one who was on the fence, underprepared, or working thirty hours a week — and that student's outcome is not the average.
[P1.2] Push tuition subsidies outward and you enroll more people whose odds of finishing are weakest. A large share of American starters — roughly two in five by common counts — never complete a bachelor's in six years. For them the subsidy purchases credits, debt, and the worst cell in the earnings table: some college, no degree. Cheap tuition doesn't buy persistence, advising, or a diploma.
[P1.3] An apprenticeship is built differently. It's a job with a written wage ladder. The learner is paid from day one, raises are tied to demonstrated competence, and the credential is recognized across the industry, not just by one campus. The employer funds the training because it needs the worker — demand is expressed in payroll, not in hope.
[P1.4] Follow both to age thirty. The apprentice started earning at eighteen, has a decade of experience, seniority, licensure, and no loan balance. Journeyman electricians, industrial maintenance technicians, and utility line workers out-earn most young adults holding only a high school diploma. Swiss and German apprentices become master craftsmen, supervisors, engineers — with the option to study later, financed by their own savings.
[P1.5] Subsidies also inflate credentials: more degrees, same jobs, higher requirements. Apprenticeships can't inflate that way, because a firm only opens a slot it intends to fill.
[P1.6] A subsidy buys a lottery ticket on completion. An apprenticeship buys a wage floor and a ladder. At the margin, the ladder raises more lifetimes of earnings.

[C1.2] First, scale changes the apprenticeship. Existing programs select promising candidates for occupations with proven demand. Expansion cannot assume those wages, completion rates, or job guarantees survive. Paid slots require supervision, equipment, and work; tuition subsidies use colleges that can enroll broadly across fields.
[C1.3] When demand turns cyclical or the next applicants are less prepared, employer-linked places are rationed, shortened, or tied to narrower tasks. A wage during training is not evidence of a larger lifetime return.
[C1.4] Second, PRO stops the film at age thirty, precisely when early earnings favor the apprentice. A degree’s value includes later entry into management, professional licensure, further education, and movement between employers and industries. [C1.5] Decades of compounding and mobility cannot be waved away as “hope.”
[C1.6] Third, college noncompletion is a reason to target subsidies intelligently, not proof that additional enrollment has low returns. [C1.7] PRO’s credential-inflation claim is equally selective: apprenticeships can proliferate credentials without creating durable demand. Employers express demand for today’s vacancies, not necessarily workers’ earnings decades later.
