[P1.1] Antitrust bans the creation of market power, not just higher price tags. A merger that lets the combined firm dictate prices to customers is illegal. A merger that lets it dictate wages to workers is the same economic act, viewed from the other side of the bargaining table. Buying labor is buying an input; monopsony power is monopoly power in reverse, and the statute says substantial lessening of competition in any line of commerce.
[P1.2] Current practice has an irrational blind spot. Agencies routinely model whether a merger of two supermarkets in the same town raises grocery prices by 2%. They rarely ask whether that same merger allows the only two large employers for cashiers and stockers in that commuting zone to suppress wages by 10%. When two hospital systems merge in a rural county, patient prices may stay flat due to regulation, but nurses lose their only alternative employer.
[P1.3] The competitive harm is not hypothetical; it is where the workers live.
[P1.4] The harm matters as much and is less self-correcting. Workers cannot arbitrage like consumers can switch stores. Relocation costs, occupational licensing, search frictions, and information gaps make labor markets highly concentrated and sticky. Wage suppression reduces output just as monopoly pricing does -- fewer nurses hired, fewer shifts covered, lower quality -- and it distributes income upward from people who depend on wages to firms with market power.
[P1.5] Labor law does not fix market structure; it presupposes it.
[P1.6] Treating monopsony as seriously means analytical parity, not automatic blocking. Define relevant labor markets by occupation and commuting zone, measure concentration, examine evidence of outside options and frictions, and assess merger-specific efficiencies. The agencies already have the data and the tools. Equal seriousness simply ends the unjustified discount for harm that hits people as earners rather than shoppers.

[C1.1] Merger review is a blunt instrument wielded under extreme uncertainty. Treating labor-market monopsony as seriously as consumer-price effects would not merely expand antitrust; it would transform a constrained tool into an incoherent one, raising error costs that dwarf any theoretical benefit.
[C1.2] The first problem is administrability. Defining a relevant product market is hard enough; defining a relevant labor market is often arbitrary. A cashier and a stocker may share an employer, but do they share a market? Does a software engineer in Austin compete with one in Seattle when remote work is common, or only when it is mandatory?
[C1.3] Wages are not like shelf prices. They are multidimensional—health benefits, equity, schedule flexibility, remote days, training opportunities—and vary by individual human capital in ways no agency can model ex ante. If antitrust agencies must now adjudicate these fluid, opaque markets under the same evidentiary burden as a 5% price increase in retail gasoline, the result is paralysis. Deals with clear consumer benefits will be delayed, deterred, or killed because of speculative wage effects that the agency cannot verify and the court cannot review.
[C1.4] Second, the remedy mismatch is severe. Merger review is binary: permit, block, or impose structural conditions. If a merger lowers consumer prices but might suppress nurse wages in a rural county, antitrust offers no scalpel. Blocking the merger sacrifices the consumer gain to address a labor harm better suited to labor law—collective bargaining, wage boards, or targeted employment regulation.
[C1.5] Consumer-price analysis has a coherent anchor: the welfare of buyers in the market where the merger occurs. Labor-market analysis would force agencies to become central planners of regional employment, a task for which they lack data, expertise, and democratic legitimacy.
[C1.6] Equal seriousness sounds symmetrical, but symmetry here means treating a clear, administrable standard as fungible with a speculative, multidimensional one. That is not enforcement; it is overreach.

[C2.1] The PRO side insists labor markets are merely product markets viewed from the other side of the bargaining table. That symmetry is elegant, rhetorically convenient, and fundamentally false.
[C2.2] [P2.2] argues market definition is equally messy in both domains, but the messiness is not comparable. Product substitution leaves a transparent trail: prices, quantities, and cross-elasticities drawn from actual transactions. Labor substitution does not. When a worker rejects one employer for another, the signal is confounded by shift length, vesting schedules, commute time, child-care proximity, and remote flexibility.
[C2.3] SOC codes and commuting zones [P2.3] map geography and job titles; they do not reveal which of these margins is actually competitive. PRO’s rural hospital scenario is administrable only because it is cherry-picked. Introduce remote work, hybrid roles, or skill portability and the relevant market dissolves into speculation that no court can reliably review.
[C2.4] [P2.4] likens wage bundles to smartphone features, but the comparison fails. A phone has a list price. Wages do not. They are individually negotiated, vary with human capital within the same job code, and include non-pecuniary terms no agency can model ex ante.
[C2.5] Payroll data [P2.5] is ex post noise; separating a merger-specific wage effect from local housing costs, licensing barriers, or individual productivity differences is not analytical parity. It is alchemy dressed as econometrics.
[C2.6] [P2.6] weaponizes the phrase "any line of commerce" to mandate expansion. Taken seriously, that reading collapses merger review into industrial planning, because every merger affects some upstream input market somewhere. Consumer-price effects provide a coherent limiting principle; without it, agencies become central planners by default, lacking either the expertise or democratic legitimacy for the role.
[C2.7] As for remedies, [P2.7] pretends labor law is helpless. It is not. Collective bargaining, wage boards, and sectoral standards target labor harm directly without sacrificing consumer efficiencies. PRO would trade a scalpel for a sledgehammer and call it justice.
