[P1.1] The question is not whether Chinese electric vehicles are cheap. They are. The question is what Europe becomes if it lets cheapness decide its industrial future. The motion says "primarily," and that word matters: it asks which lens governs policy when the two goals collide.
[P1.2] Mine is simple. Decarbonization that dismantles Europe's industrial base and hands its energy transition to a strategic rival is neither durable nor green in any meaningful sense.
[P1.3] First, Europe has run this experiment already. In the late 2000s, Europe led the world in solar manufacturing. Then Chinese producers, backed by cheap state credit, subsidized land and provincial support, flooded the market below sustainable prices. European manufacturers collapsed within a few years.
[P1.4] Panels got cheaper, yes, but Europe now depends on China for the overwhelming majority of wafers, cells and modules. It gained no lasting climate advantage from losing that industry. It only lost the industry. The same mechanism now threatens cars, batteries, heat pumps and wind turbines.
[P1.5] The EU's own anti-subsidy investigation concluded that Chinese EV makers benefit from unfair state support. That finding is the premise of the tariffs, not a protectionist excuse.
[P1.6] Second, the auto sector is not just another industry. It is the backbone of manufacturing employment in Germany, Central Europe, Italy and Spain, with millions of jobs across the supply chain. Lose it, and you do not simply lose factories. You lose the engineering ecosystem that would build Europe's next clean technologies.
[P1.7] Third, dependence is leverage. China has already restricted exports of gallium, germanium, graphite and rare-earth technologies when it suited Beijing. Connected cars and grid inverters run on software that can be updated remotely. An energy transition running on a rival's hardware is a vulnerability wired into every street and substation.
[P1.8] Finally, the threat lens protects decarbonization itself. Climate policy survives only with public consent. If voters come to see the green transition as the thing that closed their plant, populists will kill it. Treating Chinese imports as an industrial threat keeps the transition politically alive.

[C1.1] The motion turns on "primarily," and that is where it fails. Europe can use trade defenses without making threat the governing lens. A threat lens makes tariffs, friction and delay the default; an opportunity lens makes speed the default and defense the exception. With binding 2030 and 2035 targets, speed is the decisive criterion.
[C1.2] Affordability is the bottleneck of decarbonization. European EV sales stalled the moment subsidies were cut, because domestic models are expensive; Chinese imports supply the mass-market segment Europe's carmakers declined to build. Every year of delayed electrification locks in fifteen more years of combustion emissions. The climate cost of the threat lens is certain and immediate; the industrial harms are slower and manageable.
[C1.3] The solar precedent just invoked is my best evidence. Cheap Chinese cells made solar the cheapest electricity in history, and Europe's deployment records came after manufacturing moved east. Europe lost panel factories and gained its decarbonization workhorse, with more jobs installing and maintaining panels than it ever had producing them. The lesson: compete where Europe can win — deployment, grids, software, next-generation technology — rather than defend commodity manufacturing at the price of the transition.
[C1.4] Dependence also runs the other way. An EV runs on domestic electricity; a petrol car runs on imported oil. Europe learned from Russian gas what fuel dependence means, and electrification is the exit. A BYD cannot be choked off like a pipeline.
[C1.5] Software and data risks are real, but they are regulatory problems; Brussels sets cybersecurity and data rules on foreign technology constantly without treating it as hostile.
[C1.6] Finally, the threat lens manufactures the backlash it fears. Tariffs raise prices on the exact goods voters must embrace for the transition to stay popular, and retaliation lands on German car exports and French brandy. Europe's own carmakers are choosing partnership — joint ventures, Chinese battery plants on European soil. Treating these imports primarily as a threat fights the last war and loses this one.
