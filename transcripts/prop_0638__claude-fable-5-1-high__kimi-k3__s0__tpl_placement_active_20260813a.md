[P1.1] Fundamental science is the one activity governments fund whose value lies almost entirely in what nobody predicted. That single fact decides this debate. A short-cycle project grant demands that a scientist state, in advance, what she will discover, on what timeline, with what deliverables. That is a coherent way to buy a bridge.
[P1.2] It is an incoherent way to buy knowledge you do not yet have.
[P1.3] Look at what the competitive system actually produces. Researchers in many fields now spend a large share of their working time writing proposals, most of which fail. Review panels, rationally, reward the safe and legible: the incremental extension of last year's result, the project whose success is already half-demonstrated in preliminary data. Three- to five-year cycles force labs to chase publishable milestones rather than hard questions.
[P1.4] The result is a system optimized to look accountable while quietly filtering out the very work it exists to find.
[P1.5] Consider the counterexamples. Katalin Karikó's mRNA work struggled for years to win grants before it underpinned pandemic vaccines. CRISPR emerged from unglamorous curiosity about how bacteria fight viruses. Bell Labs and the old Max Planck model funded people and institutions, not projects, and produced transistors and Nobel Prizes.
[P1.6] The Howard Hughes investigator model, which gives long, flexible support to scientists rather than proposals, is widely credited with producing more breakthrough work and, tellingly, more failures. Those failures are not a bug. They are the price of the tail.
[P1.7] That is the concession I make openly: block grants will waste money on labs that go nowhere, and it will be harder to point at any given dollar and name its output. But accountability per dollar is a proxy, and when you optimize a proxy you destroy the thing it stood for. The real metric is knowledge produced per decade, and by that metric the paperwork-heavy system loses.
[P1.8] Block grants can still carry institutional review, periodic evaluation, and redistribution among departments. What they remove is the demand for prophecy. Fund the explorers, not the itinerary.
