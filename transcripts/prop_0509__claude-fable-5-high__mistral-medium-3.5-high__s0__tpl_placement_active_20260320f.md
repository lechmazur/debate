[P1.1] The question is not whether 2 percent matters. It is whether the fastest path back is worth deliberately throwing millions of people out of work. It is not, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the costs are wildly asymmetric. Inflation running at 3 or 4 percent instead of 2 is an annoyance: prices drift, contracts adjust, and wage growth typically follows. A recession, by contrast, destroys things that never come back. Workers laid off in downturns suffer scarring—lower lifetime earnings, lost skills, broken career ladders.
[P1.3] Businesses fail, investment collapses, and the labor force participation of marginal workers erodes for years. Hysteresis means an engineered recession doesn't just impose temporary pain; it permanently shrinks the economy's capacity. Trading a permanent loss for a slightly faster return to an arbitrary number is bad arithmetic.
[P1.4] Second, patience often works. The post-pandemic episode is the clearest case in modern memory: inflation peaked far above target, and the doomsayers insisted only mass unemployment could cure it. Instead, supply chains healed, energy shocks faded, and inflation fell most of the way back with unemployment near historic lows. Had central banks deliberately engineered a recession in 2022, they would have inflicted enormous suffering to "fix" a problem that was already largely self-correcting.
[P1.5] Monetary policy operates with long lags; slamming the brakes to hit a target quickly almost guarantees overshooting into needless contraction.
[P1.6] Third, credibility is about direction, not speed. Expectations stay anchored when the public believes the central bank is committed to bringing inflation down over a reasonable horizon. Nothing in the historical record shows that taking three years instead of one—while clearly tightening—unmoors expectations. What does damage credibility is a central bank that crashes the economy and must immediately reverse course.
[P1.7] The choice is between tolerable, temporary, broadly shared costs and severe, permanent, concentrated ones borne by the most vulnerable. Patience is not weakness; it is competence.
