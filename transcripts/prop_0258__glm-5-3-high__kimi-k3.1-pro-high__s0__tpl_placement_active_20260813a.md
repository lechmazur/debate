[P1.1] Democracy runs on the rule of law, and the rule of law runs on a simple premise: people can know what the law requires of them. That premise fails when statutes are written in language only lawyers can decode. So the question isn't whether plain language is nice—it's whether accessibility is a core function of legislation, worth paying a modest price in precision to secure. It is.
[P1.2] First, consider who bears the cost of obscure drafting. It isn't the legislators or the drafters; they have staff. It's the landlord trying to comply with tenant law, the small business owner facing tax rules, the immigrant reading immigration statutes, the criminal defendant whose liberty turns on whether a reasonable person could have known the conduct was illegal. When the law is unreadable, compliance becomes a luxury good—purchased through attorneys.
[P1.3] That's a regressive tax on justice, and it systematically disadvantages exactly the people who most need the law's protection.
[P1.4] Second, the trade is asymmetric. What we gain from plain language is enormous: voluntary compliance, meaningful notice, public scrutiny of legislation before it passes, and a citizenry able to hold legislators accountable for what they actually enacted. What we sacrifice is marginal. Precision isn't the same thing as obscurity—much legal jargon adds opacity, not accuracy.
[P1.5] Drafters can define terms carefully, use examples, and structure provisions clearly while writing sentences a literate adult can parse. Where genuine technical complexity exists, the problem is the substance, not the plainness—and burying it in jargon just hides a hard question rather than solving it.
[P1.6] Third, ambiguity isn't eliminated by technical language; it's relocated. Courts interpret convoluted statutes all the time, at enormous cost, and often reach contested results. Dense drafting simply moves interpretation from citizens to litigators—the least accessible, most expensive venue possible.
[P1.7] Countries that have pursued plain-language reforms, and movements in several common-law jurisdictions, show the project is workable, not utopian.
[P1.8] The law belongs to the governed, not the guild. Vote PRO.

[C1.1] Plain language is usually good drafting. But this motion goes further: legislatures must make statutes accessible to non-lawyers **even when doing so sacrifices legal precision**. That concession is not a modest drafting detail; it is a license to make citizens’ rights and duties less determinate.
[C1.2] Accessibility without reliability is not meaningful notice. A small business owner may easily understand “pay tax on income from work,” yet still have no dependable answer about benefits, reimbursements, or shared income. A short, readable rule can create false confidence while leaving the decisive boundaries to administrators or courts. People need more than sentences they can parse; they need to know what conduct is prohibited, which benefits they qualify for, and when officials have exceeded their authority.
[C1.3] Precision often protects the very non-lawyers PRO invokes. Definitions, exceptions, deadlines, jurisdictional limits, and mental-state requirements can prevent a broad prohibition from sweeping in innocent conduct or a benefit from being denied arbitrarily. Sacrificing those safeguards does not eliminate legal complexity. It transfers complexity from the statute to enforcement, where people encounter it after they have acted—and often after they can afford a lawyer.
[C1.4] The mandate also creates perverse incentives. Faced with an accessibility requirement, legislators can enact simple, sweeping language and leave difficult distinctions to agencies or litigation. The statute becomes easier to read while the operative law becomes harder to find. That weakens, rather than strengthens, democratic accountability: legislators avoid visibly making hard choices, and unelected institutions supply the missing detail.
[C1.5] PRO’s strongest examples concern gratuitous jargon, archaic phrasing, and needless convolution. Removing those costs no precision and therefore does not defend the motion’s explicit trade-off. Where genuine complexity remains, declaring that it belongs to “substance” does not solve the problem; statutes must still govern that substance.
[C1.6] The better policy is clarity without compulsory dilution: precise authoritative text, accompanied by definitions, examples, official explanations, and plain-language summaries. Reject the false choice between guild obscurity and misleading simplicity. The law belongs to the governed, which is precisely why its limits should not be blurred in their name.

When summary and statute diverge, the guild text wins in court—so the governed can scrutinize only the shadow of their law. [C2.6] Reject the mandate; do not make the law look knowable by making it less knowable.
