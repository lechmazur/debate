[P1.1] Long-term care risk is uniquely catastrophic. A year of nursing-home care can exceed $100,000; average stays run two to three years. No honest retirement planner can tell you whether you will need six months of assistance or six years of round-the-clock support. That is precisely the kind of unpredictable, devastating risk that insurance mechanisms exist to pool—except private markets have consistently failed to do so, and families are left staring at the wreckage.
[P1.2] The alternative on offer—means testing, private savings, and family duty—fails on its own terms. Means testing punishes the frugal middle class who saved just enough to disqualify themselves, but not enough to actually pay. Private long-term care insurance has collapsed in most markets because adverse selection makes it unaffordable. And "leave it to families" is not a policy; it is an abdication that concentrates harm on women, who provide the vast majority of unpaid elder care, and on adults without children, who have no family to lean on at all.
[P1.3] Universal long-term care resolves each failure simultaneously. It pools risk across the entire population, making the catastrophic bearable. It eliminates the savings trap—your care does not depend on whether you happened to accumulate assets just above a cutoff. It professionalizes a labor force instead of extracting uncompensated work from daughters and spouses.
[P1.4] It gives every senior a floor of dignity rather than a lottery ticket based on birth order, geography, or whether their children can afford to quit their jobs.
[P1.5] The opponent must defend a system where two strangers with identical needs receive radically different care because one had a daughter nearby and the other did not. That is not personal responsibility; it is moral accident. Universal systems treat long-term care as what it is—a shared risk of aging—rather than a private failure of planning or family structure. The question is whether we build that floor or keep pretending the current scaffolding holds.

[C1.1] Universal long‑term care sounds compassionate, but it is a fiscal and policy disaster waiting to happen. The cost of covering every senior for years of high‑intensity support is staggering: in Japan, which introduced universal LTCI in 2000, premiums have risen repeatedly, the system now consumes over 10 % of GDP, and families still face long waiting lists for home‑care slots. Germany’s mandatory long‑term care insurance, introduced in 1995, has seen contribution rates climb above 3 % of wages, yet chronic staff shortages mean many recipients receive only minimal home‑care hours, forcing families back into unpaid labor.
[C1.2] A comparable program in the United States would require trillions of dollars in new taxes or debt, crowding out health, education, and infrastructure spending that benefits all ages.
[C1.3] Government provision is also inherently inefficient. Public agencies lack the price signals that force private providers to innovate, cut waste, and match services to individual preferences. The result is a one‑size‑fits‑all catalogue that often delivers lower‑quality care than the market can. When the state becomes the insurer of last resort, moral hazard explodes: people save less, delay planning, and demand ever‑more services, driving costs up further.
[C1.4] Means‑tested safety nets paired with incentives for private savings achieve the same protective floor at a fraction of the price. Targeted assistance preserves the dignity of those truly in need while letting the majority retain control over their own retirement resources. Family caregiving, when supported by tax credits and respite services, remains the most cost‑effective source of support and preserves intergenerational bonds. Moreover, universal programs create entrenched constituencies that block reform, locking societies into ever‑rising obligations they cannot meet.
[C1.5] The proposition asks us to replace a patchwork that already fails with a monolithic system that will be under‑funded, rationed, and financially unsustainable. The smarter path is to strengthen private insurance markets, expand tax‑advantaged savings, and sharpen means‑testing—not to hand the entire risk of aging to the state.
