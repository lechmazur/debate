[P1.1] A medical choice is not a referendum on someone else's virtue. It is a decision about a particular body that will bear the scar, the pain, the risk, the dependency, or the relief. That fact gives bodily autonomy a direct moral weight that dignity appeals can only borrow. Dignity can matter, but it enters as a consideration; autonomy is the title to decide.
[P1.2] Autonomy in medicine is not mere desire. It is informed, competent, voluntary choice: the patient understands options and consequences, and decides. Its authority comes from the fact that no one else must live in that body. The patient, not the observer, bears the consequences—their faith, their tolerance, their life plan.
[P1.3] A patient with kidney failure may refuse dialysis because the life it buys feels intolerable; a Jehovah's Witness may refuse blood; a disabled patient may reject a treatment framed as reducing "indignity." Others may call these choices tragic, even wrong. But the moral question is who decides what happens inside a person's body. The answer must be the person.
[P1.4] Dignity is often a mirror held up by outsiders. Sometimes it means "do not humiliate this patient," which autonomy already honors. But when dignity is used as a trump, it usually means "your choice offends my vision of a life worth living." That is the move autonomy blocks.
[P1.5] When they conflict, someone is deciding for the patient. "Dignity" has been invoked to force feeding tubes, deny contraception, refuse pain relief, and pressure disabled people toward death or survival. Its content is contested, its authority external, and its costs fall on the patient.
[P1.6] So the rule should be clear: in competent adults' personal medical choices, autonomy wins when it conflicts with dignity. Dignity may inform, persuade, or comfort. It may not commandeer the body. Moral weight follows the person who must live—or die—with the decision.
[P1.7] That is not hard-heartedness; it treats patients as agents, not wards.

[C1.3] The conflict PRO needs is the one it avoids: the competent request that medicine participate in degradation.

[C1.7] The person is not exhausted by the decision. Dignity names what remains. Autonomy cannot outrank the status that makes its claim possible.

[P2.6] CON says the person is not exhausted by the decision. True—but decisions are not made by metaphysics. Someone must be authorized, and the only candidate who bears the consequences is the patient. Dignity may shape how care is offered; it may not commandeer the body.
