[P1.1] Right now, gig workers cannot actually leave their platform. If you drive three years building a 4.9 rating on one ridehail app, you start at zero when you switch. That is not a free market. That is vendor lock-in, enforced not on customers, but on the people doing the work.
[P1.2] This policy fixes that. Standardized portable ratings and work history does three clear, unarguable good things. First, it ends the single largest barrier to worker mobility. Right now a top-rated delivery rider will take a 30-40% income hit for six weeks when switching platforms, while they rebuild trust with customers.
[P1.3] That is not an acceptable cost for choosing a better employer. It traps people on platforms that cut pay, remove safety protections, or mismanage shifts, because leaving means throwing away thousands of hours of earned work reputation. Second, it forces platforms to compete for workers instead of just trapping them. Right now platforms have zero incentive to treat established workers well.
[P1.4] They know you cannot take your most valuable work asset with you. With portable ratings, platforms will have to compete on pay, support, and working conditions, not just on locking good workers in. Third, this harms no one. Ratings and verification history are work product built by the worker, not the intellectual property of the platform.
[P1.5] Customers do not lose out: a verified record of on-time deliveries is just as valid on one platform as another. Standardized transfer does not force platforms to honor bad ratings, only accept verified, objective work history. This is not heavy regulation. This is enforcing the basics of a fair labour market.
[P1.6] Workers own the reputation they build. Anything else is a rigged game.

[C1.1] A 4.9-star rating is not a universal metric like temperature; it is the product of a specific platform’s rules, fraud filters, and customer expectations. Forcing platforms to build standardized transfer tools treats reputation as raw data that means the same thing everywhere. In practice, that erodes consumer safety, undermines platform competition, and traps workers under a permanent cross-platform record.
[C1.2] First, ratings are neither objective nor interchangeable. The claim that a verified record is "just as valid on one platform as another" [P1.5] is plainly false. A five-star history dropping fast food on a porch tells a home-repair, caregiving, or ride-hail platform nothing about whether a worker is safe inside a stranger’s home or behind the wheel with passengers. Even between direct competitors, platforms differentiate themselves by how rigorously they vet workers, detect fake transactions, and police rating inflation.
[C1.3] Standardized portability turns the weakest platform’s security into the entire market’s vulnerability. Bad actors can farm "verified" trips and five-star scores on a lax, low-security app, then port that manufactured credibility onto high-trust platforms where customers rely on ratings for physical safety.
[C1.4] Second, this mandate hurts the very workers it claims to liberate. Multi-apping is already routine, and competing platforms regularly offer sign-up bonuses and guaranteed earnings to recruit switchers—directly refuting the idea that platforms cannot compete for labor today [P1.3]. What workers actually need when leaving an abusive or poorly managed platform is a clean slate. Once standardized transfer tools exist, receiving platforms will inevitably expect applicants to import their past records to qualify for preferred work.
[C1.5] Refusing to port your history immediately signals that you are hiding a bad score. An unfair customer complaint or biased rating system on one app will now follow a worker across the entire industry.
[C1.6] Finally, because platforms bear the legal and reputational risk when service fails, no responsible company can blindly trust a competitor's vetting standards—turning this mandate into either ignored data or a serious safety hazard.
