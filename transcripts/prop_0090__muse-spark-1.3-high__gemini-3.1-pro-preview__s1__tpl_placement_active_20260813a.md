[P1.1] Driving is an eyes-up, hands-on task, and core controls should work that way. A physical button for heat, defrost, or hazards can be found by touch and pressed once without looking. A touchscreen forces the opposite: look down, find the right screen, swipe, tap through menus, confirm you hit the right virtual target while the car bounces. At highway speed that glance is hundreds of feet blind.
[P1.2] No software animation justifies that added risk.
[P1.3] That difference matters most when seconds matter. Hit black ice and fogging glass, you need defrost now, not after the infotainment boots. See a crash ahead, you need hazards instantly, not buried two taps deep. Screens freeze, lag in cold, wash out in sun, ignore gloved fingers.
[P1.4] A knob or toggle has no boot time, no menu tree, no glare problem. It works when electronics are stressed, which is exactly when you need it.
[P1.5] Touchscreen maximalism was sold as innovation, but it is mainly cost-cutting dressed as futurism. One large panel is cheaper than dozens of tested switches, and every function can be moved with an update. That movability is the problem. Muscle memory dies when climate controls shift after an overnight download.
[P1.6] Rental drivers, older drivers, anyone with shaky hands or poor vision pays the price for a clean dashboard aesthetic they never asked for.
[P1.7] Keep touchscreens for navigation, music, settings. But keep a hard, consistent layer for driving itself: temperature, fan, front and rear defrost, hazards, lights, wipers, horn, volume. Those are not apps. They are safety equipment, and safety equipment must be findable blind, operable instantly, and identical every time.
[P1.8] Buttons do that. Screens do not.

[P2.2] The claim that voice keeps eyes entirely on the road confuses hands-on-wheel with mind-on-road. In a skid or sudden fogging you do not want to recite commands and wait for confirmation. You want one press, felt through gloves, that works with the radio blasting and the network down.
[P2.3] Docked icons do not solve the core flaw: you still must look to hit flat glass with no edges while bouncing at highway speed. A low dial can be found blind; a screen never can. [P2.4] A hazard triangle that moves or enlarges under hard braking means the target shifts when panic narrows vision most. A merely imperfect knob stays learnable; a perfect layout that shifts is never learnable.
[P2.5] We are not asking for a cockpit of clutter, but eight or ten fixed controls for heat, defrost, hazards, lights, wipers. That is not regression, it is redundancy: when the central computer lags, freezes, or reboots — and every driver has watched it do so — safety equipment still works. Software should manage comfort when it can. Hard controls must command safety when it cannot.

Modern designs map high-urgency overrides directly to steering wheel stalks or haptic thumb controls. The driver never removes their hands.
[C2.3] PRO defends the legacy dashboard dial, which inherently forces a driver to reach away from the steering column—an action demonstrably less safe than a steering-wheel toggle.
[C2.4] Second, PRO claims screens are "flat glass with no edges" [P2.3], ignoring haptic feedback. Modern automotive displays provide localized vibration and resistance, mimicking a physical press without the mechanical liability. Furthermore, PRO assumes physical buttons never fail [P2.5]. [C2.5] Core vehicle functions routed through screens do not run on the infotainment browser; they operate on isolated, automotive-grade real-time operating systems that do not "freeze" just because a music app buffers.
[C2.6] Third, PRO misunderstands dynamic interfaces [P2.4]. A virtual hazard button that enlarges during hard braking does not "shift" its location—it increases its hit-box. This makes it far easier to strike in a panic than a static, half-inch plastic triangle. Muscle memory is not compromised by making a target bigger when peripheral vision narrows.
[C2.7] Ultimately, PRO concedes they want "eight or ten fixed controls" [P2.5]. But forcing designers to reserve a permanent, rigid block of dashboard real estate for systems that are overwhelmingly automated prevents that space from displaying active, situational safety warnings when emergencies actually happen. A static dashboard is a dumb dashboard. By tethering vehicle design to analog switches, PRO sacrifices the exact intelligent adaptability that keeps modern drivers safe.
