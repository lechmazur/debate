[P1.2] First, inflation expectations, once unanchored, compound violently. Workers demand higher wages; firms raise prices to cover those wages; the spiral feeds itself. Breaking that spiral later requires precisely the punishing interest-rate hikes that created deep recessions in the early 1980s. [P1.3] Short-term pain is real but contained; long-term destabilization is far worse.

[P1.5] Saving the median worker from a hidden inflation tax matters more than insulating a minority from temporary job displacement, especially when robust unemployment insurance and retraining programs exist.
[P1.6] Third, the Phillips curve tradeoff is short-lived. Any employment gains from running hot evaporate once expectations adjust, leaving only higher inflation and no lasting job growth. The 1970s demonstrated this exact pattern: tolerating inflation above target produced stagflation, not prosperity. Central banks learned that lesson the hard way, and their credibility since then has anchored expectations and supported growth.
[P1.7] Abandoning that discipline now squanders credibility built over four decades.
[P1.8] Finally, the proposition specifies "near target" and "modest"—it demands neither zero inflation nor aggressive austerity. [C1.1] The proposition sounds reasonable because it smuggles in a false promise: that central banks can precision-engineer a "modest" rise in unemployment. History says otherwise. When the Federal Reserve tightened aggressively in the early 1980s, unemployment didn't rise modestly—it roughly doubled, peaked near 11 percent, and stayed elevated for years. The disinflation worked, but the human cost was staggering and concentrated among the most vulnerable workers.
[C1.2] "Modest" is a word doing a lot of concealing.
[C1.3] The deeper problem is that the Phillips curve is not a dial. Central banks cannot select an unemployment rate the way you select a temperature. Monetary policy operates with long, variable lags and through financial channels that amplify unpredictably. A central bank that promises to raise unemployment "just a little" to hit its inflation target is making a promise it cannot keep—and the overshoot risk runs in both directions.
[C1.4] Unemployment also scars. Research on hysteresis shows that workers displaced during downturns suffer lasting earnings losses, and labor force participation can decline permanently. The proposition treats unemployment as temporary friction. For the workers actually displaced, it is a multi-year blow to earning capacity, health, and family stability—costs no unemployment insurance system fully repairs.
[C1.5] Over the next three years specifically, much inflation pressure may be supply-driven—energy shocks, geopolitical disruption, demographic shifts. Demand-side rate hikes address demand-side inflation. Using them to fight supply-side price increases means crushing employment without reliably restoring price stability. That is the worst of both worlds.
[C1.6] Central bank credibility comes from sound judgment under uncertainty, not mechanical obedience to a target. A bank that engineers recession to hit a number it cannot precisely control confuses discipline with rigidity—and rigidity is not competence.
