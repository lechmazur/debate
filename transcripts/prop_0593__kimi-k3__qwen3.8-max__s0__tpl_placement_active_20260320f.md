[P1.1] Every year, millions of workers sew shirts, mine cobalt, and harvest cocoa inside supply chains that no regulator, investor, or shopper can actually see. Rana Plaza killed more than a thousand garment workers in a building that had been audited. That is the world voluntary due diligence built: thirty years of glossy reports, and accountability that evaporates the moment a factory sits two subcontractors deep.
[P1.2] The motion fixes each failure with a matching design feature. Mandatory, because voluntary disclosure is self-selecting — the cleanest firms publish, the worst stay silent, and silence becomes the winning strategy. Standardized, because without common metrics a report is a brochure: you cannot compare what you cannot measure, and greenwashing thrives in incomparability. Audited, because self-graded homework is not diligence; independent verification with liability attached is what turns paper into behavior.
[P1.3] Remove any leg of that tripod and the regime collapses back into CSR theater.
[P1.4] The economics are on our side. Disclosure corrects a textbook information failure: consumers and investors say they value ethical sourcing but cannot price it, so responsible firms are punished for costs that abusers externalize. Mandatory reporting levels that playing field — which is why major firms themselves have backed laws like France's vigilance duty and Germany's supply-chain act. The burden is sensibly scoped to large multinationals, the only actors with both leverage over suppliers and the back offices to comply; for a firm with billions in revenue, an audited report is a rounding error.
[P1.5] Expect the other side to say audits fail and paperwork doesn't save lives. Their alternative is the status quo that gave us Rana Plaza: trust, volunteered. Transparency is not the whole of enforcement — it is the precondition for every other kind. You cannot punish what you cannot see, boycott what you cannot trace, or sue over what you cannot prove.
[P1.6] This motion makes the invisible visible. That is why it wins.

[C1.1] The proposition mistakes paperwork for protection. It assumes that a standardized, audited report makes supply chains visible and therefore safer. But the mechanism it creates rewards certification, not remediation. A multinational can hire consultants, tick boxes, and pay an auditor for a favorable opinion while abuse migrates to lower tiers, where the report’s scope, language, and checklist cannot reach.
[C1.2] PRO’s own example convicts the remedy. Rana Plaza was not invisible because nobody had written a report; it was deadly because audit-style compliance can miss structural danger, subcontracted work, and worker fear. Adding more standardized audits does not cure that failure; it risks entrenching it. Auditors are usually paid by the firms they audit, visit announced facilities, review documents, and sample a tiny slice of a vast chain.
[C1.3] They are good at finding what is easy to document, bad at uncovering coerced overtime, wage theft, confiscated passports, or harassment that workers cannot safely report.
[C1.4] Standardization makes this worse by flattening context. Human-rights risks differ between a Congolese cobalt mine, a Southeast Asian seafood fleet, and a Central American banana plantation. A common template pushes firms toward generic indicators and away from the messy, site-specific leverage that actually prevents harm. The result is comparability without truth: investors can rank reports, but workers get no new remedy.
[C1.5] The most dangerous incentive is exit. If a firm must publish audited diligence and fears liability for what it discloses, the rational response is not always to fix a bad supplier. Often it is to cut it, move to a cleaner-looking jurisdiction, or push risk into unlisted subcontractors. That “de-risking” can destroy jobs and push abuse deeper underground.
[C1.6] The test is whether workers are protected, not whether firms produce impressive documents. This motion gives the appearance of accountability while leaving the hardest abuses least visible.

Human-rights abuse often lives in fear, cash payments, falsified time sheets, and subcontractors outside the audit plan. A firm can show policies, training logs, and grievance channels while the actual remedy is empty.
[C2.3] PRO says disclosure shows whether firms tried leverage [P2.6], but a report can narrate failed leverage without proving any real effort. It turns diligence into storytelling, judged on formatting rather than conditions on the ground. Standardized metrics make this easier because firms optimize what is countable: number of supplier visits, codes signed, hotlines installed. The outlier PRO celebrates may be the firm honest enough to report problems, while the “clean” report becomes a market asset.
[C2.4] On exit [P2.5], visibility after the fact is not protection. If a multinational drops a risky supplier to keep its report tidy, workers may lose income before any NGO notices a year-end change. Annual disclosure is backward-looking; abuse can migrate within months. And if liability is real, the safest legal strategy is not deeper engagement but narrower scope: fewer disclosed suppliers, more jurisdictional arbitrage, more informal sourcing.
[C2.5] Finally, PRO says we offer no rival mechanism. Their burden is not to force us to choose the status quo; it is to prove this mandate improves workers’ lives. A report that is readable but unreliable, delayed, and easily gamed does not meet that burden.
