[P1.1] A publicly subsidized team is not a private club that happens to play downtown. It is a public–private partnership. When a city funds a stadium, taxpayers become investors accepting deferred returns—jobs and identity—on the understanding the team stays. Relocation strands that investment: the city keeps the debt, fans keep the memories, and the owner keeps the franchise value public money helped build.
[P1.2] That is not a market exchange; it is a bait-and-switch.
[P1.3] Start with the imbalance. Leagues grant territorial monopolies. A team is not a shoe store a competitor can replace down the block. Fans, nearby businesses, and bondholders make relationship-specific investments with no resale value.
[P1.4] Once a team takes major subsidies, it captures the upside of that monopoly while holding a unilateral exit option. The credible threat of leaving lets owners extract more public money and then depart. St. Louis helped fund a stadium for the Rams and kept the debt after the team moved west; San Diego subsidized the Chargers and lost them to Los Angeles. Those outcomes are not accidents; they follow from making exit cheap for the owner and devastating for everyone else.
[P1.5] A binding approval process corrects that asymmetry. It does not chain a franchise to a city, nor let government seize a team. It says: before a subsidized team transfers the publicly funded asset—the fan base, the taxpayer-backed arena, the bonds—the community whose money helped build that value has a binding voice. The team may still relocate if the community consents or the public is made whole.
[P1.6] What it may not do is take the subsidy, monetize the local monopoly, and leave taxpayers to absorb the loss.
[P1.7] The stock reply is that contracts and markets suffice. They do not. Stadium deals are struck under relocation threats that make real exit clauses impossible; lawsuits rarely return a team. The remedy is not micromanagement; it is to condition public money on reciprocal accountability.
