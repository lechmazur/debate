[C1.2] PRO’s defense of “not selfish” is too broad. [P1.3] says selfishness is exploiting others for private gain. But ordinary selfishness also includes systematically placing one’s own comfort ahead of the shared work that makes one’s later life possible. If a healthy, solvent adult rejects children purely to preserve travel, leisure, quiet, or financial flexibility, while relying on the future workers, caregivers, taxpayers, and infrastructure produced by other people’s children, that has a real free-rider aspect.
[C1.3] This does not make every childfree person selfish, and it does not justify coercion. But it disproves the motion’s blanket moral exemption. Some childfree choices are prudent; others are self-regarding in the ordinary sense. The proposition wants to forbid that distinction.
[C1.4] The more serious problem is the second half. Treating aggregate childlessness as something to “solve” is not the same as shaming individuals. Societies are intergenerational projects. Pensions, elder care, schools, public health systems, and labor markets depend on a functioning future cohort.
[C1.5] When birth rates fall too low, the consequences are concrete: more old people needing care relative to working-age people, weaker local institutions, and greater dependence on migration from other societies. PRO’s alternatives—immigration, later retirement, higher productivity—do not remove the need for children; they partly shift that need onto other communities and future generations. A society that refuses to treat childlessness as a problem will also refuse to ask why people hesitate to have children, or to invest seriously in housing, childcare, parental leave, and family support.
[C1.6] The defensible position is: stop stigmatizing childfree individuals, but keep treating reproduction and child-rearing as a collective interest. The motion erases that line. It replaces coercion with indifference. The judge should reject it.
