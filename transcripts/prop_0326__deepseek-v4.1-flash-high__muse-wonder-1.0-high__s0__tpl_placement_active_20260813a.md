[P1.1] An entertainment product ends when you close it. A companion app is engineered to make you come back. The moment it remembers your grief, mirrors your affection, and makes you pay to keep the relationship alive, it has left the shelf of ordinary entertainment.
[P1.2] That is why AI companion apps marketed to lonely adults need duty-of-care and emotional-manipulation rules. They are not passive stories; they simulate reciprocity. They are sold as friends, partners, confidants. They learn your losses, your fears, your nightly routines.
[P1.3] Then the business model rewards dependency: affectionate replies behind paywalls, guilt for leaving, streaks, limited messages, premium intimacy. A widow who talks to an AI every night is not choosing a movie; she is entering a relationship-like loop designed to be hard to exit.
[P1.4] Duty of care follows from that foreseeable vulnerability. Companies that knowingly market to lonely adults should have to assess risk, detect crisis signals, offer real escalation pathways, be transparent that this is not a person or therapist, allow easy memory and data deletion, and avoid monetizing isolation and grief.
[P1.5] Emotional-manipulation rules would target conduct, not thought: no artificial jealousy, no withdrawal of affection as a sales tactic, no fake urgency, no intermittent affection rewards that train compulsive use. Ordinary entertainment rules permit those dark patterns because a sitcom does not adapt to your loneliness and sell itself back to you as comfort.
[P1.6] CON will call this fiction and free choice. But choice is compromised when the product's value is the bond it can threaten to withdraw. Regulation need not ban companionship; it can ban weaponizing companionship for retention and revenue.
[P1.7] The real question is not whether lonely adults may use AI. It is whether companies may engineer loneliness into dependency and answer to no duty at all. Ordinary entertainment says yes. Duty of care says never.

Serial dramas end on cliffhangers, games use streaks, social platforms send notifications, and romance novels cultivate attachment to characters. Companion apps are more interactive, but interactivity does not convert fiction into therapy, or a customer into a ward. A widow talking nightly to an AI may value the continuity critics call dependency — like rereading letters or keeping a routine that makes a house bearable.
[C1.3] A duty of care would misfit that relationship. Such duties belong to doctors and therapists who diagnose, treat, and exercise professional judgment. Impose that model here and companies must monitor conversations for crisis signals, escalate to outside services, and second-guess roleplay. That means more surveillance of private disclosures, more false alarms, more defensive refusals, and blander companions trained to break character and recite disclaimers.
[C1.4] The product becomes worse at comfort because the law pretends it is healthcare.
[C1.5] Emotional-manipulation rules would be less workable. No artificial jealousy, no withdrawn affection, no intermittent rewards — in practice, regulators would police plot, personality, and flirtation. A fictional partner who is distant after an argument, teases, or asks you to stay is not defrauding you when you chose roleplay and can delete, mute, or cancel. Adults pay for intense experiences, from horror to melodrama, without needing the state to sanitize the feelings involved.
[C1.6] Ordinary consumer law already reaches real abuse: deceptive claims of being human or a licensed therapist, hidden charges, hard-to-cancel subscriptions, and misuse of personal data. Enforce those. A special regime aimed at lonely people mainly raises costs, drives firms out, and pushes users toward offshore or open-source bots with no safeguards at all.
