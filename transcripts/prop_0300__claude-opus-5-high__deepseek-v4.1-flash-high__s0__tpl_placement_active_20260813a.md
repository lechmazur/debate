[C1.3] When a government says “we did this, we are sorry,” it moves from contested history to acknowledged responsibility. In many systems, that admission can strengthen claims, toll limits, or be cited in international forums. Overriding the warning is not moral leadership; it is imposing unbudgeted liability on taxpayers without a statute.
[C1.4] Picture a state facing decades-old claims. A formal apology may unlock litigation, but there is no appropriation, no eligibility rules, no cap. The result is not justice; it is a queue of people with moral claims and no reliable remedy, while the government later says courts or parliament must decide. That is a bait-and-switch.
[C1.5] The better path is a designed reparations scheme—acknowledgment, categories, limits, funding—then apology as part of settlement, not before it.
[C1.6] The claim that apology moves matters from courtroom to negotiating table only works if the state already controls the settlement. Canada’s apology came with a negotiated framework; Australia’s did not create a blank check because legal architecture limited it. Those examples prove the need for structure, not for ignoring warnings. Japan’s equivocation shows denial is bad, but it does not show apology should override legal advice; it shows truth and money must be settled together.
[C1.7] States should acknowledge history. They should not be more willing to issue formal apologies precisely when advisers warn those apologies will increase compensation claims. That is not ethics versus law; it is symbolism versus democratic responsibility. The state can say “this happened” without saying “pay whatever a court later decides.” When the warning sounds, legislate the remedy—do not ignore the lawyer.
