[P1.1] Automatic stabilizers that expand cash transfers when unemployment rises are the most reliable tool for blunting recessions, and the temporary rise in public debt is a necessary and productive cost. When unemployment climbs, household income and spending fall at once. Firms lose sales, lay off still more workers, and the contraction spirals. Automatic cash transfers reverse that cascade at its source: they put money into the hands of people who will spend it immediately.
[P1.2] That spending keeps shops open, supply chains moving, and secondary jobs intact. The recession becomes shorter and less deep.
[P1.3] Discretionary packages cannot match this. They require political agreement, come too late, and are often too small or poorly targeted. Rules that expand transfers as unemployment crosses set thresholds act at speed and without negotiation. Households and businesses can plan around that certainty, which further dampens the downturn.
[P1.4] Debt will rise while the transfers are operating. That is exactly what sound countercyclical policy demands. In a slack economy the multiplier on cash transfers is high: each dollar preserves more output than it costs. Preserving growth lowers the eventual debt burden relative to GDP far more effectively than attempts to hold the line on borrowing.
[P1.5] Economies that refuse support suffer permanent scars—lost skills, shuttered firms, higher structural unemployment—that raise future fiscal costs and leave debt ratios worse, not better.
[P1.6] The alternative is false thrift. A government that lets demand collapse to protect today’s balance sheet simply trades a temporary and manageable debt increase for a larger, longer, more expensive crisis. Automatic expansion of cash transfers is insurance with a high payoff: it stabilizes output, protects families, and leaves the public finances healthier once recovery takes hold.

[C1.1] Adopting automatic cash transfers triggered by rising unemployment institutionalizes a dangerous asymmetry: spending accelerates when a threshold is crossed, but nothing automatically ensures repayment. Treating swelling public debt as a trivial cost ignores that borrowing to fund consumption leaves no assets to service the liability. Governments should reject this fiscal ratchet.
[C1.2] First, automaticity strips away the deliberation that separates necessary stimulus from reckless borrowing. Once a rule locks in larger transfers at a given jobless rate, the political cost of overriding it becomes prohibitive even when debt loads, currency risk, or maturity walls warn against expansion. Speed is not wisdom. Removing elected scrutiny from the decision to deepen deficits locks in obligations that bias policy toward perpetual deficits.
[C1.3] Second, cash transfers are a poorly targeted blunt instrument. Official unemployment data lag, exclude gig and informal workers, and flatten regional crises. Blanket transfers therefore reach households with retained savings while missing the truly liquidity-constrained, eroding the very multiplier its defenders promise. Stabilizing the productive side—credit to viable firms, wage subsidies that preserve job matches—outperforms spraying borrowed money at consumption that vanishes without a trace.
[C1.4] Third, the claim that this debt is temporary and self-liquidating relies on rosy growth arithmetic. When downturns coincide with energy transitions, demographic decline, or supply constraints, borrowed consumption cannot generate enough output to outgrow the liability. Debt becomes structural, crowding out investment and raising interest burdens long after recovery.
[C1.5] This is a false choice between automatic transfers and heartless austerity. Governments can maintain countercyclical tools without binding themselves to an irreversible debt escalator. Because automatic cash transfers invite chronic deficits, poor targeting, and stimulus errors during supply shocks, governments should refuse to adopt them.

The rule expands whenever unemployment ticks up, yet it cannot distinguish a demand shortfall from a supply squeeze, measure whether the jobless rate signals mild distress or deep crisis, or tell whether the recipient is a liquidity-constrained worker or a household with untouched savings. It borrows first and calibrates never.
[C5.2] The proposition offers a false choice between fast automatic support and slow paralysis. The automatic cash algorithm, by contrast, sprays borrowed consumption across the economy regardless of need, leaving no asset behind to service the debt. When the trigger misfires during supply shocks or pays savers during mild downturns, the liability becomes structural long before any promised sunset.
[C5.3] Stabilization is not about the velocity of spending alone, but about the wisdom of outlays. A government that mortgages its fiscal space through an indiscriminate debt escalator does not blunt recessions; it fuels them with misallocated borrowing. Because automatic cash transfers surrender the very judgment required to distinguish a healthy buffer from a reckless gamble, CON wins.
