[P1.1] Weekend late-night transit extensions beat earlier closing times because they attack the real flashpoint: the journey home, not the clock. Earlier last orders assume the danger is how late people are out. The harms we care about — fights outside venues, rage in taxi queues, impaired drivers — spike during mass egress, when thousands of impaired, impatient people are forced onto the street at the same time and must scramble for scarce rides. Closing early compresses that exit; late transit spreads it out and gives it a safe valve.
[P1.2] Earlier closing is time rationing; late transit is mobility. Drunk driving is not merely a drinking problem — it is a ride problem waiting to happen.
[P1.3] Picture a bar district at 3 a.m. With no train or night bus, everyone exits at once. They compete for limited taxis and surge-priced rideshares. Crowds build, tempers flare, and some decide the drive is worth the risk because waiting an hour in the cold feels worse.
[P1.4] With a frequent night bus, late Underground, or U-Bahn running to 5 a.m., people leave in streams. A lit platform with staff replaces a dark corner and a scavenger hunt. The impaired person has a predictable, affordable ride, so the car stays parked; the street stays calmer because the crowd is not released through a single bottleneck.
[P1.5] Earlier closings also displace risk rather than remove it. When venues shut sooner, drinking migrates to pre-loaded house parties and unregulated after-hours flats — no trained staff, no capacity limits, no transport plan — and people drive home from scattered locations. Transit policy does not require less drinking; it changes the ecology of dispersal. Earlier closing is damming the river; late transit widens the channel.
[P1.6] If the goal is less disorder and fewer drunk drivers, the lever that gets people home safely is the stronger one.
