[P1.1] When a child cannot join a class, a patient cannot reach a doctor, and a worker cannot apply for a job because the connection at home is too slow or does not exist, that is not an inconvenience. It is exclusion from modern citizenship. That is why internet access must be a legally enforceable universal service, not a luxury product left to postcode and income.
[P1.2] Essential services have migrated online by design, often by government design. Schoolwork, benefits claims, tax, banking, GP bookings, and most hiring now assume a stable broadband connection capable of video, uploads, and multiple users. Telling disconnected households to use a library for an hour is like telling families without water to queue at a well: it ignores that participation requires reliable access at home, every day.
[P1.3] Markets alone will not close this gap, for the same reason they did not electrify every village or connect every telephone. Laying fibre to sparse, low-income, or difficult areas costs more and returns less, so providers rationally cherry-pick profitable neighbourhoods and leave others with obsolete speeds. A universal service obligation corrects that structural failure: as a condition of operating, providers and the state must deliver a defined minimum everywhere, funded fairly across the system.
[P1.4] Legally enforceable matters. Voluntary pledges and subsidised schemes can be cut, narrowed, or left unenforced; a right creates a floor and a remedy. If a household falls below the standard, there is someone answerable, a deadline, and a consequence. That predictability also helps schools, clinics, and employers build services knowing citizens can actually reach them.
[P1.5] This does not mean unlimited free luxury for all. It means, like water and electricity, a guaranteed baseline of safe, reliable quality — updated as technology and social needs change — that no one is priced or placed out of. A society that makes life online-only cannot make connectivity optional.

[C1.1] Water and electricity are physical, standardized utilities: a litre is a litre, a kilowatt-hour is a kilowatt-hour. High-quality internet is nothing like that. The pipe is only one layer of a stack that includes devices, digital literacy, content, and software. A legally enforceable right to a 'high-quality' connection cannot deliver the child’s homework or the patient’s teleconsultation if the household lacks a laptop, skills, or relevant content.
[C1.2] Nor can a connection mandate compel the state to guarantee the platforms, schools, or clinics that make the link useful. The motion confuses infrastructure with outcomes.
[C1.3] Worse, 'high-quality' is a moving target. The broadband that met the standard a decade ago is now an obstacle to participation, and today’s baseline will be obsolete tomorrow. Legally fixing a floor forces the state either to freeze technology at an artificially low, soon-to-be-inadequate level, or to mandate endless upgrades that spiral into fiscal black holes. Water does not change its chemical composition every business cycle; internet service does.
[C1.4] A society that redefines the floor every election invites instability; one that refuses locks in mediocrity.
[C1.5] Enforceability compounds the rigidity. Once connectivity becomes a legally enforceable universal service, households acquire an entitlement that courts, regulators, and treasuries must defend. Providers will game minimum standards, lobbying to set thresholds just above their cheapest existing assets. Litigation will substitute for policy, and adaptation will slow because every upgrade carries legal liability.
[C1.6] Contrast this with successful connectivity expansions—competitive fiber builds, targeted vouchers, public access points—which adjust quickly without judicial entanglement.
[C1.7] The stakes are not trivial, but the mechanism is. Countries can prioritize near-universal connectivity without pretending it is a static utility akin to water. They can subsidize, build, and regulate aggressively while leaving room for technological change. A right answers every variation in need with the same blunt remedy: sue.
[C1.8] That is not how a dynamic network should be governed, and that is why this motion should fail.

[P2.2] Denying the prerequisite because it is not sufficient would have blocked every utility we now take for granted.
[P2.3] The moving-target argument is a false choice between freezing technology and fiscal chaos. Water and electricity standards have never been static: sanitation rules, voltage, reliability, and safety requirements all evolved as society changed. A universal service law likewise need not engrave today's megabits in stone. It can set the legal duty — adequate access for ordinary participation — and require an independent regulator to update the technical floor periodically.
[P2.4] That preserves adaptability while preventing governments from letting the floor rot.
[P2.5] Enforceability is presented as rigidity, gaming, and lawsuits. In reality, gaming flourishes without an enforceable floor, when providers can promise coverage, lobby for weak voluntary targets, and still skip unprofitable streets. A legal duty reverses the burden: regulators can impose deadlines, require reporting, and order remedies, with courts as a backstop rather than the first tool. That is how utilities are policed, not by suing over every outage.
[P2.6] Finally, the alternatives concede the goal while removing the guarantee. Competitive fibre builds go where returns are highest; vouchers depend on annual budgets and take-up; public access points offer rationed time, not home access for homework, telehealth, or shift work. If, as [C1.7] claims, countries can 'subsidize, build, and regulate aggressively,' the only thing a right adds is that failure is no longer acceptable for the hardest-to-reach. Opposing enforceability therefore does not protect flexibility.

The right PRO defends therefore either guarantees only the pipe—useless for the exclusion PRO decries—or promises outcomes the state cannot enforce. That is a category error, not a prerequisite.
[C2.3] A right to a bare connection cannot compel the platforms, schools, and clinics that PRO admits are essential.
[C2.4] The moving-target problem is not solved by delegating it to a regulator. Voltage and sanitation standards evolve around physical safety; internet quality evolves around social expectations. A law requiring "adequate access for ordinary participation" asks a court to decide whether ten megabits suffice for a video class, a benefit claim, and shift work. That is a rationing judgment, not a technical one.
[C2.5] It will be made in litigation, not by engineers. Calling this adaptability ignores that the regulator's floor becomes a legal minimum the moment it is written.
[C2.6] Enforceability worsens gaming. Providers will lobby to set the legally binding floor just above their cheapest existing assets, then hide behind it. Administrative targets can be raised without a breach of statutory duty; a right makes every improvement a lawsuit. And the alternatives are not concessions—they are better mechanisms.
[C2.7] Subsidies, competitive builds, and public access can adapt when technology shifts. A right cannot. It freezes the network at whatever level the best-organized lobbyist secured in year one, leaving the hardest-to-reach with a remedy but not a connection worth using.

If the pipe is so insufficient that guaranteeing it is pointless, then their vouchers and builds — which also deliver mainly a pipe — are pointless too. [P4.5] The same logic answers the flexibility claim. A regulator either can update a broadband floor intelligently or cannot; adding a legal consequence does not make engineers incompetent, it makes ignoring their floor costly. Flexibility without consequence is simply permission to stop where profit stops. A guaranteed floor that moves beats an aspiration that can be abandoned.

[P5.1] The choice is not between a connection and a remedy. It is between a connection some households may claim as citizens, and one they receive only while a contract, budget, or profitable build happens to reach them.
[P5.2] That distinction decides this debate. Once schools, benefits, healthcare and work assume home access, disconnection is not a market gap — it is exclusion created by how society now operates. The opponent's tenders and targets can deliver many lines, but they leave the final, hardest household negotiable: too costly this year, outside this tender, below this year's priority. A universal service removes that negotiation.
[P5.3] It says the floor moves with technology, policed by a regulator with deadlines and consequences, but the obligation to reach everyone does not expire.
[P5.4] Guaranteeing the pipe does not pretend to guarantee every device or skill. It guarantees the one thing without which devices, teaching and platforms are useless, and which profit alone will predictably withhold from exactly those most excluded.
[P5.5] Water and electricity became rights not because pipes solved every problem, but because modern life made them prerequisites no one could be left without. Connectivity has crossed that line. Leave it optional, and citizenship remains optional too.
