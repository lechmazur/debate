[P1.1] Large events land on real places and real people. Neighbors absorb the road closures, the noise, and the policing. Members and longtime supporters supply the loyalty that keeps a team or artist viable when demand is ordinary. A system that opens first to a dynamic general-sale market treats both groups as an afterthought.
[P1.2] Organizers should reserve a meaningful share for local residents or members before that scramble begins.
[P1.3] Dynamic sale is built to extract the highest price in a short, chaotic window. It rewards the fastest software, the best queue position, and whoever can pay most at that instant. It does not reward the resident who will live with the event’s costs, or the member who showed up when tickets were easy. Those people are not less entitled because a national surge can outbid them for a few minutes.
[P1.4] A verified local or member allocation is a modest correction for a market that otherwise allocates by speed and cash rather than by stake.
[P1.5] This is not a ban on markets. The reserved share can be limited, sold at a published price, and released if it goes unused. The rest of the inventory can still move through dynamic general sale. What changes is that hosts and loyalists are not reduced to leftovers after bots and surge buyers have taken their cut.
[P1.6] Events that fill their seats while locking out the neighborhood that hosted them, and the fans who built the demand, trade short-run extraction for resentment and a thinner long-run audience.
[P1.7] The claim that the highest bidder always values the seat most confuses ability to pay in a frenzy with who ought to be in the building. Organizers already segment inventory through holds, sponsor blocks, and fan-club windows. Extending that practice to a meaningful local or member share is consistent with how these sales already work, and it is the fairer default when the event depends on a place and a community.
