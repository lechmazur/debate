[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a rights business for a software business. Sports broadcasting is not selling streams; it is selling licenses. Leagues auction broadcast rights at fixed, enormous prices—billions for major leagues, hundreds of millions for single competitions. The marginal cost of serving one more viewer is near zero, but the marginal cost of acquiring the content is not.
[C1.2] Fragmenting the subscriber base into single-team passes does not reduce the rights bill; it shatters the collective revenue required to pay it. There is no "forcing"—there is simply the arithmetic of paying for what you licensed.
[C1.3] The cross-subsidy the proposition dismisses as exploitation is actually the funding mechanism for the entire ecosystem. Women's sports, Paralympic coverage, and niche competitions survive because bundle subscribers collectively fund them. A standalone pass for a small-market team cannot cover the production costs of broadcasting that team's games. Without the broad bundle base, those competitions do not get cheaper passes; they get no coverage at all, and the diversity of sport collapses into whatever a few global brands can afford.
[C1.4] The premise that fans want one team is also empirically weak. The typical supporter follows multiple competitions—the title race, continental tournaments, international duty. A fan forced to purchase separate passes for each competition pays more than a single bundle. The "cheaper" pass is cheaper only for a hypothetical consumer who watches exactly one team; for the actual fan, fragmentation is a price increase disguised as choice.
[C1.5] Finally, team-specific pricing invites a winner-take-all market. Broadcasters will price marquee clubs at premiums to offset lost bundle revenue, while small-market teams—unable to command high standalone prices—watch their production budgets collapse. The bundle is not a prison; it is the mechanism that keeps the entire sport funded and visible.

Nothing about a team pass cancels a league's rights bill; it changes who is charged for what. And rights holders selling to broadcasters want the widest possible paying audience, which is exactly what narrow passes deliver by converting non-buyers into payers.
[P2.2] "There is no forcing" is the strangest sentence in the reply. When the only route to watching one club is to buy every sport the distributor happens to hold, that is precisely the structure the proposition objects to. Note the revealing asymmetry: if bundles are genuinely the preferred product, offering cheaper passes would cost broadcasters nothing—the passes would simply be ignored. The industry's resistance to the option suggests the bundle is doing work that consumer preference alone would not sustain.
[P2.3] The niche-sports argument proves too much. Cross-subsidy is not abolished by offering passes; bundles survive as an upsell for multi-sport households and can still carry smaller competitions. But if keeping women's or Paralympic coverage alive requires hiding the price of everything behind a single tier, that is a funding problem to be solved transparently—through tiered pricing or explicit earmarking—not by taxing the single-club fan who will never watch it. Concede the funding challenge; do not concede that compulsory breadth is the only answer.
[P2.4] On multi-competition fans: buying two competition passes can still come in under a broad bundle, and the fan who genuinely wants five competitions can buy the bundle. Choice does not make anyone overpay. It only removes the subsidy that intermediaries collect from fans with narrow tastes.
[P2.5] Finally, the winner-take-all worry cuts against bundles, not for them. Rights auctions already pool money toward marquee clubs. Team-level passes make each club's real audience visible, which is the factual basis for arguing that smaller clubs deserve a share rather than hoping the aggregate pool leaks down to them.
