[P1.1] When a multinational retailer can decide a warehouse picker's every movement through an app yet treat the worker as a supplier's employee, employment law is not protecting workers; it is laundering responsibility. The same is true when a delivery group's algorithm sets routes, pay, and discipline but classifies riders as independent businesses. Large companies increasingly use platforms and subcontracting chains not merely to find flexible labour, but to keep control while moving social costs onto individuals and public budgets. This motion asks the EU to close that gap by making the company that benefits from fragmented work pay a higher share of social contributions when it relies on those chains.
[P1.2] The dividing line is simple: social contributions should follow economic control. Direct employment bundles direction and contribution; platform and subcontracted work unbundles them. Flexibility is legitimate, but it should not become a discount coupon for pensions, sick pay, unemployment insurance, and workplace injury cover. Requiring a higher contribution share from large companies that use such chains is a corrective charge, not a ban on outsourcing.
[P1.3] It lets flexibility survive while removing its hidden public subsidy.
[P1.4] Consider construction, where a main contractor often dictates schedules and standards down a ladder of subcontractors. Each layer is thin, mobile, and close to insolvency; social risks end up unfunded or quietly absorbed by the state. A higher contribution at the top does not destroy contracting; it makes the lead firm price the true cost of the flexibility it captures. The same logic applies to digital platforms that depend on nominally self-employed labour at massive scale.
[P1.5] CON will call this a cost shock, but for large firms these contributions are a small fraction of revenue, and much of the apparent saving today is simply shifted risk. The choice is not between flexible markets and rigid rules; it is between a social market where control carries responsibility, and a grey market where scale buys exemption.

[C1.1] The motion does not tax control. It taxes a form of organisation. Any large firm that uses a labour platform or a subcontracting chain pays a higher share of social contributions, whether the workers are properly employed, insured, and paid, or not. That is not a corrective charge.
[C1.2] It is a surcharge on contracting itself.
[C1.3] The claim in [P1.2] is that contributions should follow economic control. The motion does not do that. A retailer that buys specialist security from a firm which employs its guards, pays full contributions, and runs its own rosters is charged the same as a firm that dictates every picker movement through an app. Some construction chains are thin and abusive.
[C1.4] Many are licensed trades doing work the main contractor cannot do. Punishing both does not price the true cost of flexibility. It prices the existence of a specialist.
[C1.5] Nor does the bill stay with the multinational. Lead firms rewrite contracts. The extra charge is pushed down the ladder: thinner margins for the subcontractor, fewer formal hours for the worker, or the job shifted to a firm just under the size threshold, or outside the Union. The reassurance in [P1.5], that these contributions are a small fraction of revenue, confuses a retailer's sales with a contractor's margin.
[C1.6] In labour-intensive work they are a large share of the wage bill.
[C1.7] If the wrong is sham self-employment, the remedy is status — presume employment where control is real, and enforce it. If the wrong is an insolvent layer leaving contributions unpaid, the remedy is joint liability for what was already owed. A higher rate that applies even when every contribution has been paid is not compensation for a gap. It double-charges honest chains and teaches abusive ones to fragment, relabel, or leave the formal system.
[C1.8] Responsibility already has sharper tools. This surcharge builds cliffs, avoidance, and a reason to keep the riskiest work informal.
