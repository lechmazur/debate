[P1.1] Critical minerals are not ordinary commodities. Lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, and rare earths sit inside precision munitions, naval systems, fighter avionics, transformers, and the batteries that will decide industrial power for decades. Buying the mine, the concentrator, or the processing plant is not a hotel sale. It is a transfer of high ground.
[P1.2] Firms closely tied to rival states do not buy that ground as private capital. They buy it as instruments of strategy: offtake that can be redirected, processing that can be slowed, prices that can be weaponized, and know-how that can migrate home. The relevant tie is control—state banks, golden shares, party structures, export-license dependence, executives who answer to a security apparatus—not a passport stamp. In a thin market with decade-long lead times, even one extra mine or refinery under rival command can set the terms of a crisis.
[P1.3] National-security review should therefore presumptively block. The opposite default, open unless a reviewer proves imminent danger, fails this market. Harm is delayed: a polite joint venture today becomes a supply veto when ships are already at sea. Information is stacked: host governments see a bid; the rival state sees command relationships, hidden offtake, and political finance.
[P1.4] Reversal is worse than prevention. Once title, long-term contracts, and processing expertise have moved, “unwinding” is a slogan, not a remedy.
[P1.5] A presumption is a burden of proof, not a permanent embargo. The buyer must show that rival-state leverage cannot follow the asset through ownership, debt, personnel, or offtake. Absent that showing, deny. Anything weaker invites capture: delayed reviews, political pressure for “investment,” and a completed deal too costly to unwind.
[P1.6] Allied capital can still fund mines; rival command should not. Critical-mineral security is decided at the closing table. If the buyer is an arm of a rival, that closing should not happen.

[C1.1] The question is not whether rival control of critical minerals is dangerous. It is whether a presumption of denial makes you more secure or less. It makes you less, for three reasons.
[C1.2] First, the presumption starves the very supply it claims to protect. Critical-mineral projects are capital-hungry, slow, and routinely uneconomic without a partner who already runs refineries and guarantees offtake. Today the firms with that expertise and appetite are disproportionately tied to the rival. A presumption does not swap them for allied capital; allied capital has been declining these projects for a decade.
[C1.3] It swaps them for nothing. Picture a graphite deposit in an allied country with one financier and one buyer, both with rival-state links. Block it, and the deposit stays in the ground while the rival's existing refineries keep every point of market share. The rival's grip on processing is the actual vulnerability, and blocking inbound investment does not loosen it by a single ton.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's central premise—that ownership equals command—is wrong for assets that cannot leave. A mine in your territory operates under your export licenses, your permits, your labor law, and your emergency powers. If a crisis comes, the host government, not the shareholder register, decides where the ore goes. Sovereignty is the remedy PRO pretends does not exist.
[C1.5] Unwinding a hotel is hard; seizing a stranded mine is what states do routinely. Leverage runs toward the host, not the buyer.
[C1.6] Third, targeted review already exists and works better. Case-by-case scrutiny can impose mitigation: mandatory domestic offtake, divestiture of board seats, ring-fenced technology, trusted-operator requirements. That captures the investment and neutralizes the threat. A presumption throws away the scalpel for a wall—and "closely tied" is elastic enough to sweep in Indonesian, Gulf, and Kazakh capital with ordinary rival-state relationships, teaching producer countries that Western markets are closed and rival ones are open.
[C1.7] Security is measured in secure tons available in a crisis. A presumption of denial produces fewer of them.
