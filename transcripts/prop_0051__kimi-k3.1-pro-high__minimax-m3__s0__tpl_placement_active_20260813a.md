[P1.1] A five-dollar dress or cut-price phone charger is not a triumph of efficiency if its price depends on unsafe production, untraceable sellers, or workers whose conditions nobody will disclose. Fast-fashion marketplaces should not be allowed to convert those hidden costs into a consumer discount.
[P1.2] Stronger obligations need not ban cross-border shopping or demand that every parcel be slowly inspected. They should require marketplaces to verify seller identities, obtain credible safety documentation, apply stricter checks to higher-risk goods, preserve traceability for recalls, and remove repeat offenders. On labor, they should require meaningful supply-chain disclosure: where goods are made, which subcontractors are involved, what risks have been identified, and what happens when violations are found. Transparency is not a promise of perfection; it is an end to profitable ignorance.
[P1.3] Marketplaces are the right place for these duties because they are not passive noticeboards. They recruit sellers, design search and recommendation systems, process payments, coordinate logistics, and take revenue from scale. A shopper cannot inspect a factory or identify which overseas entity supplied a garment. The platform can demand information before granting access to millions of customers.
[P1.4] If its business model depends on onboarding sellers faster than it can perform basic checks, that is a reason to slow onboarding, not to abandon responsibility.
[P1.5] The likely objection is affordability: poorer consumers benefit from ultra-cheap goods, while compliance raises prices and protects established retailers. But a discount produced by missing safeguards is not freely bestowed on consumers; part of the bill is deferred to the child injured by an unsafe product, the worker denied recourse, and the compliant business undercut for obeying rules. Proportionate, platform-level duties also spread fixed compliance costs across enormous sales volumes rather than placing the whole burden on each buyer.
[P1.6] Some delay and some price increase are therefore not regrettable defects in the motion. They are the explicit trade-off it makes: slightly less instant, anonymous abundance in exchange for products that can be traced and labor practices that can be challenged. [C1.1] The motion’s central mistake is to make marketplaces responsible for harms they do not create and often cannot control. Unsafe products are designed and manufactured elsewhere; abusive labour occurs in factories and farms; regulators possess the testing and enforcement powers platforms lack. If platforms become universal gatekeepers, they will issue attestations, not guarantee safe factories or fair wages. “Verified” will describe a supplier form, not a working factory.
[C1.2] Stronger obligations also have a predictable distributional cost. Seller verification, product testing, traceability, and disclosure through subcontracting chains impose costs per seller and often per product line. Ultra-cheap cross-border marketplaces are where small overseas sellers can reach consumers without a domestic retail footprint. Those costs can be passed on, reduce selection, or drive sellers to less visible services.
[C1.3] Large brands absorb paperwork better; a compliance burden can thus subsidise incumbents and weaken the shopping benefit the motion claims to protect.
[C1.4] Labour transparency is especially vulnerable to ritual. Published supplier lists can conceal exploitation through subcontractors and nominal trading firms; they can make a polished disclosure without giving workers a wage, representation, remedy, or safer job. Meaningful progress requires enforceable standards, inspection, and grievance mechanisms, principally against producers and importers whose conduct is at issue. Adding disclosure requirements to platforms may improve paperwork while leaving the workplace unchanged.
[C1.5] Product-safety duties show the same mismatch. General requirements can suit identifiable hazards, but requiring every cross-border marketplace to prove compliance before goods move risks treating administrative delay as safety. It may favour established brands and domestic sellers, while unsafe sellers move to informal channels. “Even if” shopping slows is not a justification: the test should be whether a targeted duty reduces expected harm at reasonable cost, not whether every additional check deserves immunity from scrutiny.
[C1.6] Risk-based enforcement should target manufacturers, importers, sellers, and customs authorities, with platforms helping recalls when feasible. Blanket marketplace liability displaces responsibility rather than strengthening it.

[P2.1] The opposition judges platform duties by a standard no regulator could meet: unless platforms guarantee safe factories and fair wages, they should merely assist recalls “when feasible.” That is a false choice. Customs, producer enforcement and platform duties are complements. A manufacturer may be beyond a shopper’s practical reach; the marketplace controls whether its seller receives storefront access, payment processing, promotion and logistics. Responsibility should follow that control.
[P2.2] Nor is this “blanket marketplace liability” or a form for every parcel. [C1.6] attacks a straw proposal. Stronger obligations can be proportionate: verify sellers, demand credible documentation, intensify checks for greater hazards, preserve recall records and remove repeat offenders. A form alone is not safety; anonymous sellers and frictionless relisting are invitations to evade it.
[P2.3] The comparison is not paperwork versus perfection, but platforms that detect patterns and cut off access versus platforms that profit first and assist after harm.
[P2.4] The labour objection repeats the error. Disclosure can be gamed, so it must cover subcontractors, identified risks and responses to violations, with consequences for false disclosure. Workers certainly need inspection, grievance mechanisms and remedy; concealment makes all three harder. Transparency is not a substitute for enforcement.
[P2.5] It lets workers, buyers and regulators locate responsibility and challenge denial. A duty is not worthless because it does not single-handedly raise wages.
[P2.6] Leakage and cost do not overturn the motion. Sellers moving elsewhere lose the scale, visibility and integrated services that made marketplace evasion lucrative; imperfect enforcement is no reason to preserve its easiest route. Affordability cannot mean lower prices financed by unknown product risks and invisible labour abuse. Platforms can spread verification and traceability systems across high volumes and calibrate duties to risk, limiting burdens on small sellers.
[P2.7] Compliant incumbents gaining an advantage over evasion is not an argument for protecting evasion. The opposition waits for injury, then requests help if feasible. We use existing platform power before sale. Some friction is the price of preventing harm rather than merely documenting it.

[C2.3] “Responsibility should follow control” [P2.1] conflates exclusion power with production knowledge. [C2.4] If unsafe goods reappear under new identities, one seller’s exclusion changes little. Without reliable inspection, documentary compliance creates false assurance, potentially worse than visible anonymity.
[C2.5] The labor half is fragile. Fragmented supply chains often cannot be reconstructed from listing data. A supplier list may name a compliant first-tier factory while exploitation occurs below it. Sanctions for false disclosure punish the intermediary; they do not improve a worker’s wage or remedy.
[C2.6] Independent inspection and enforcement must do that. Transparency helps locate responsibility when the chain is observable. Otherwise stronger rules yield audit theater or impossible subcontractor mapping.
[C2.7] PRO understates incidence [P2.6]. Testing, documents, audits and chain mapping impose seller-specific marginal costs, hitting small sellers hardest. Displaced sellers can move to rival platforms, social commerce or direct shipping, taking products and traceability with them. “Proportionate” and “risk-based” do not solve this: risk is what sellers have incentives to conceal, and regulators must test whether platform checks work. PRO shows why platforms are useful intermediaries, not why they are competent factories.
[C2.8] Narrow identity checks, complaint handling and recall support are sensible; the proposed demands for credible product documentation and deep labor-chain transparency are not. Slower shopping cannot substitute for evidence of reduced harm.

[P5.1] A platform need not be a factory inspector to be responsible for the door it opens. That is the decisive issue. Marketplaces choose which sellers receive storefronts, payments, promotion, and logistics; they cannot profit from that access while treating basic safety evidence and supply-chain visibility as someone else’s business.
[P5.2] CON’s alternative preserves precisely the gap the motion closes: producers may be abroad, subcontractors concealed, and repeat sellers able to reappear, while the platform’s own contribution remains largely reactive. Inspection and worker remedy are essential, but they need information, records, and identifiable sellers. Transparency enables accountability; it does not pretend to replace it.
[P5.3] Nor does possible document fraud defeat pre-sale duties. Fraud justifies penalties, targeted checks, and removal—not a system that collects a name and waits for complaints. CON demands proof that every platform duty outperforms direct enforcement, yet offers no proof that anonymity and after-the-fact cooperation protect anyone better. The status quo is not cost-free; its costs are borne by injured consumers, invisible workers, and compliant sellers.
[P5.4] Some compliant small sellers may face friction, and some shopping may become slower or dearer. But access to millions of customers is not an entitlement to untraceability. Slower shopping is a cost; untraceable harm is a business model. The motion rightly accepts the former and refuses to subsidize the latter.
