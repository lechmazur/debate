[P1.1] A like count is not information about a post; it is a verdict on a person, displayed in public, updated in real time. This motion asks for one small change: platforms should let users switch those public verdicts off, offered by default, instead of engineering every feed around visible popularity. That switch is cheap, reversible, and preserves every function that matters, while dismantling an architecture built to make people, especially teenagers, feel chronically measured.
[P1.2] First, mental health. The documented harm of social media is not communication itself but quantified social comparison. A teenager who posts a photo and watches it stall at twelve likes while her friend's hits three hundred learns nothing about photography; she learns a ranking of her own worth. Instagram hid like counts for millions of users in a years-long experiment precisely because the scoreboard creates pressure — and the mechanism is obvious: when approval is a public number, people post less freely, delete "underperforming" uploads, and treat each post as an exam.
[P1.3] Second, public discourse. Visible metrics trigger herding. Experiments on social news sites have found that an early visible upvote attracts more upvotes regardless of quality — the displayed number starts thinking for the reader. Rich-get-richer dynamics reward outrage and conformity over substance.
[P1.4] Hide the count, and readers must weigh the argument itself.
[P1.5] Third, the remedy is modest. Nothing is banned. Creators who want counts keep them; private analytics visible only to the poster preserve genuine feedback. The switch costs a settings toggle.
[P1.6] My opponent will praise likes as feedback and discovery tools, but this motion removes neither — it removes only the public scoreboard while keeping the underlying data for anyone who wants it.
[P1.7] So the clash is simple: my opponent must defend the popularity contest as a default design philosophy against a change that lets anyone opt out and harms no one who opts in. When the remedy is a choice and the status quo is coerced comparison, the default should serve the user, not the leaderboard.
