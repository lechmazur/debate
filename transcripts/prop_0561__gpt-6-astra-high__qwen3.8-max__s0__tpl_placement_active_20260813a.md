[C1.1] The proposition mistakes a safety-net problem for a platform-employment problem. The goal should be protecting people, not forcing every casual, app-mediated hour into an employer-style ledger. Mandating health, leave, and retirement contributions from platforms based on hours worked would make gig work less open, less flexible, and less available.
[C1.2] Gig platforms compete by lowering transaction costs and allowing people to work in small, irregular increments: a student delivering two evenings a week, a parent accepting rides between school runs, someone testing a market. The moment each hour carries mandatory benefit liabilities, platforms have a strong incentive to avoid small-hour workers. They can respond by deactivating low-earning accounts, limiting who can log on, steering work to fewer reliable workers, or imposing scheduling controls to maximize productivity per hour. That converts open participation into managed shifts and harms marginal workers first.
[C1.3] The hours metric is also poorly suited to multi-platform work. A driver might use three apps simultaneously, wait without a fare, or reject low-value jobs. If waiting time counts, platforms will reduce idle access and tighten acceptance rules; if it does not, the proration claim looks arbitrary. Administrators must audit millions of micro-hours across competing apps, creating compliance costs that favor large incumbents and push platforms toward full-time-style fleets—the opposite of what makes gig work useful.
[C1.4] This is not a case against benefits. It is a case against attaching them to platform hours as a payroll substitute. Health coverage, leave, and retirement are better supplied through universal or publicly portable systems funded broadly, or through per-transaction contributions that workers can direct to the provider they choose. Those models protect people without turning platforms into hour-counting employers.
[C1.5] The real choice is not protection versus indifference. It is whether governments build portable security for all workers, or impose a rigid employment template that prices out the people it claims to help.
