[P1.1] Conventional agriculture already enjoys enormous public support for fertilizers, pesticides, crop insurance, and commodity payments. Those subsidies never waited for decades of proof that soils would remain intact or aquifers unpoisoned. They were justified by short-term yield. Demanding conclusive long-term yield data only for regenerative practices is not prudence.
[P1.2] It is a double standard that protects the status quo. Farmers plant what the check rewards.
[P1.3] Cover crops, longer rotations, reduced tillage, compost, and managed grazing work through known soil physics and biology. Organic matter holds water. Living roots cut erosion. Diversity breaks pest cycles and reduces purchased nitrogen.
[P1.4] After a transition dip, many farms see yields stabilize, especially in drought, when conventional fields fail first. We do not need a forty-year global trial to know that paying farmers to mine soil is a worse public investment than paying them to rebuild it.
[P1.5] Yield is also the wrong single test. Subsidies exist because food systems create public goods and public harms. Water quality, flood control, soil carbon, and farm survival do not appear in one harvest number. The damages of the current model are already visible: coastal dead zones, thinning topsoil, and fertilizer-dependent operations that collapse in a dry year.
[P1.6] Those harms are not hypothetical pending a longer trial. Waiting for perfect yield proof means underwriting that damage for another generation.
[P1.7] Equal scale matters because transition is costly. Seed mixes, new equipment, and a possible early yield trough are rational barriers when neighbors still receive cheap nitrogen and conventional insurance. A conservation program the size of a brochure cannot compete with an input subsidy the size of a harvest. Public money already distorts the market.
[P1.8] Matching that support is how government stops locking farmers into extraction. Delay is not neutrality. It is another decade of subsidizing the hole we are already in.

[C1.3] Once neighbors are paid to qualify, "regenerative" will be defined by whoever can cash the check, not by soil physics.
[C1.4] Yield is not a fetish that ignores dead zones. It is why a food-system subsidy exists. A transition dip on one farm is a management problem. [C1.5] Drought resilience on some ranches does not license parity for every practice, crop, and climate before the long-run numbers are in.
[C1.6] The bundle is not one technology. Reduced tillage, longer rotations, compost, and managed grazing do not share a yield curve or a public return. Some pay for themselves. Some fail on heavy clay, short seasons, or high-rent grain ground.
[C1.7] Equal-scale support cannot tell them apart. It pays the label.
[C1.8] Visible damage from the current model is a reason to retire the worst payments and to fund trials, measurement, and targeted transitions. It is not a reason to duplicate the subsidy machinery at full size while the productivity question is still open. Premature parity is how a hypothesis becomes an entitlement.
