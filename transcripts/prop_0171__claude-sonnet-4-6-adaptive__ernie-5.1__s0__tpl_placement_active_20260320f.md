[P1.1] The central question is not whether challenge trials carry risk—they do—but whether that risk, carefully bounded, is outweighed by the cost of the alternative. The alternative is delay, and delay kills people.
[P1.2] Animal models routinely mislead us. Malaria parasites, respiratory viruses, many bacterial pathogens, and virtually all human-specific infections behave differently in mice, ferrets, or non-human primates than in people. When the biology diverges, a promising vaccine may clear animal studies and fail in humans, or a dangerous candidate may look safe in rodents until field trials expose the truth. The failures are not marginal—they are systematic.
[P1.3] Regulators who require animal proof before human challenge trials are not being cautious; they are demanding evidence that frequently cannot deliver what it promises.
[P1.4] Challenge trials break this bottleneck. With a controlled infectious dose, a small cohort of healthy, consenting volunteers, and close clinical monitoring, researchers can read a genuine efficacy signal in weeks rather than years. For fast-moving pathogens—pandemic influenza, emerging coronaviruses—that compression of time is the difference between a vaccine arriving during an outbreak and arriving after one. Dozens of participants in a challenge trial could spare thousands from infection if the timeline shortens meaningfully.
[P1.5] The ethical objections are serious, and the proposition says so openly. What the proposition also says is that seriousness is not the same as finality. We already accept that people can consent to live kidney donation, to early-phase oncology trials that offer no personal benefit, to military service. The question is whether a consenting, healthy adult who understands the risks may volunteer to be deliberately exposed to a pathogen under rigorous clinical safeguards.
[P1.6] Paternalism has its own body count—it just isn't tallied in the same place.
[P1.7] The framework already exists: independent ethics review, stringent volunteer selection, rescue therapy, and genuine informed consent. Broader permission means more trials within that framework, not outside it. That is the position worth defending.
