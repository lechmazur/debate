[C1.3] The motion asks us to abandon that settled approach for one technology alone.
[C1.4] And the opt-in alternative is worse than it sounds. Uniform, required use with real governance is often the fairer arrangement—it binds everyone to the same rules, and it gives the employer accountability for how the tool behaves.
[C1.5] The claimed harms are speculative extrapolations, and existing law already addresses the concrete versions. Data protection regimes govern collection and retention. Anti-retaliation and labor law govern misuse for union suppression. Health and safety law governs pace and burnout.
[C1.6] Where an always-on system crosses into surveillance or speed-up, remedies exist that don't require banning the coaching function entirely.
[C1.7] Meanwhile the benefits are real and unevenly distributed. A real-time writing coach helps non-native speakers get promoted. Prompts that enforce breaks help over-workers. Structured guidance helps new hires in high-turnover industries master jobs they'd otherwise wash out of.
[C1.8] Prohibition takes these gains away from the workers who need them most, to protect a hypothetical worker from a hypothetical abuse.
[C1.9] Where PRO sees compulsion, I see a tool whose legitimacy depends on how it's built and governed. That's a question for rules and enforcement—not for a categorical ban that outruns every harm on offer.
