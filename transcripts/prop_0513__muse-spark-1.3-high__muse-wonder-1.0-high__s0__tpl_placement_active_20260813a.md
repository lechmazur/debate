[P1.1] When two firms merge and then raise prices, we call it monopoly power and block it. When the same merger lets them cut wages, degrade schedules, and trap workers because there is nowhere else to go, we too often wave it through. That asymmetry has no economic justification.
[P1.2] Competition law protects competition, not shoppers alone. A labor market is a market. Employers buying labor can exercise buyer power exactly as sellers exercise seller power, with the same textbook result: pay below the competitive level, hire fewer people, and pocket the difference as deadweight loss. The victim wearing scrubs instead of holding a shopping cart does not make the harm less real.
[P1.3] Consider hospital mergers. In many towns two hospitals compete little for patients — prices are negotiated with insurers and partly regulated — but they are the only serious employers for nurses, technicians, and aides. Approved on consumer grounds, the merger then freezes wages, imposes non-competes, and forces longer shifts. The same pattern appears with meatpackers in a rural county, warehouses along one highway corridor, or retail chains in a small commuting zone.
[P1.4] Product markets look national; labor markets are local.
[P1.5] That locality is why a consumer-only test systematically misses harm. Workers cannot move like goods ship. Commutes, licensing, childcare, and housing anchor people in place. Concentration that would instantly trigger alarm on the selling side is treated as invisible on the buying side.
[P1.6] And enforcement is workable. Define markets by occupation and commuting area, examine hiring data, application flows, and wage effects — tools agencies already use. Treating monopsony seriously means giving that evidence equal weight, not a free pass for employers.
[P1.7] Lower wages achieved by strangling outside options are not efficiency; they are extraction. Equal markets deserve equal scrutiny.

[C1.1] Treat labor monopsony as seriously as consumer prices, and merger review stops being a test firms can pass and becomes two tests that can contradict each other.
[C1.2] Consumer effects are anchored in substitution: customers switch products, prices and output can be modeled, efficiencies checked against rivals. A labor 'market' is far slipperier. Workers trade wages against schedule, commute, safety, training, benefits, and promotion. Define the market narrowly — nurses in one commuting zone — and a merger looks like a stranglehold.
[C1.3] Include travel contracts, telehealth, clinics, retraining, and the next town, and it does not. Line-drawing, not competition, would decide cases.
[C1.4] Parity creates a trade-off the proposition cannot resolve. Many mergers lower prices by combining overlapping operations: one billing department instead of two, fuller shifts, shared specialists, a rural hospital kept open instead of closed. If a predicted localized wage dip carries the same blocking weight as lower prices, better care, or survival of the service itself, agencies sacrifice broad consumer gains for speculative labor forecasts. Workers lose twice — as employees in the model, and as patients and shoppers who pay more or lose access.
[C1.5] Wage harm is real, but merger control is the wrong instrument for most of it. Wage floors, collective bargaining, bans on abusive non-competes, licensing reform, and portable benefits target low pay and immobility directly, without killing a deal and sometimes the jobs attached to it. Antitrust already reaches naked wage-fixing and no-poach conspiracies, where harm is clear. Equal weight for ordinary mergers converts every small-town consolidation into a labor trial.
[C1.6] The result is delay, politicized bargaining over jobs, and a bias toward outsourcing and automation — structures that avoid review while offering workers less security. A disciplined consumer standard protects competition where causation is clearest and leaves labor policy to tools built to raise wages, not merely block transactions.

[C2.1] The product-market analogy proves too much. Consumers choosing between brands reveal, through purchases, how much a feature is worth in price. Workers choosing jobs reveal no equivalent: one nurse values nights, another training, a third will commute for a pension. Agencies can draw a commuting-zone box, but a box is not comparability.
[C2.2] Parity demands evidence strong enough to block a deal, not merely a plausible map.
[C2.3] Their nurse example assumes away the marginal constraint. Travel work being expensive and temporary does not make it irrelevant; it is expensive because the local employer must beat it. In product review, a costly outside option counts if enough buyers would switch at the margin to discipline price. Exclude travel, clinics, and neighboring employers because most nurses dislike them, and concentration is manufactured by definition.
[C2.4] The efficiency answer also begs the question. Savings that are only a wage cut are not synergy, but that is not the hard case. The hard case is real integration — one billing office, consolidated call, fuller specialist coverage — that lowers costs and prices while reducing demand for some local roles. Calling that extraction gives agencies no rule for when consumer gains lose to predicted wage losses, measured in different units for different people.
[C2.5] Nor does blocking unscramble anything. Merger review preserves the pre-deal world only on paper. Block a rural hospital combination and one facility may shrink or close, leaving fewer shifts, not a wage rival. Labor tools act after facts are known: they can raise floors, remove non-competes, or improve mobility without betting the service’s survival on a forecast.
[C2.6] Finally, no-poach prosecutions are not incoherent next to merger restraint. A pact to fix wages has no integrating benefit to weigh; a merger may. Product law draws exactly that line between naked price-fixing and a joint venture. Seriousness can mean investigating labor effects — the proposition demands equal blocking weight, and that is where measurement, trade-offs, and the counterfactual break down.
