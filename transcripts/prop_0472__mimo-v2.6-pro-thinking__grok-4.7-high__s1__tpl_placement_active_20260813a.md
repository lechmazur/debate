[P1.1] Low Earth orbit is a common-pool resource with no regulator, no traffic controller, and no binding rules for who cleans up. Mega-constellations are the first serious attempt to industrialize it — tens of thousands of satellites planned, launched in batches faster than any governance process has ever moved. The question is sequencing: govern first, or deploy first and negotiate with the wreckage later.
[P1.2] Consider the three harms in the motion. Collision risk: a single fragmentation event in a crowded shell can generate thousands of untracked objects, each turning every other satellite into a hypervelocity projectile. The nasty property of this risk is that it is partly irreversible — once the shell crosses a density threshold, no policy can undo it, because the debris cannot be swept economically. Astronomy: satellites reflect sunlight, streak across survey exposures, and saturate sensitive detectors.
[P1.3] Wide-field survey telescopes are precisely the instruments we need to find threatening asteroids and understand dark energy; degrading them to add a fourth broadband provider is a poor trade, and no firm currently pays for that loss. Debris governance: today's deorbit practices are voluntary conventions, adherence is inconsistent, and there is no binding requirement for remediation of existing junk. Proposing to launch another order of magnitude of hardware under rules that have not yet coped with a far smaller population is not boldness.
[P1.4] It is a gamble with a shared environment.
[P1.5] Note what a moratorium is: temporary, and conditional. The motion releases it when collision-risk, astronomy, and debris rules are "materially stronger." It does not prohibit constellations. It insists that rules precede lock-in rather than trail it.
[P1.6] Once millions of users depend on these services and thousands of satellites occupy preferred altitudes, the firms holding those slots gain veto power over the rules meant to bind them. Govern while the orbit is still relatively empty.
[P1.7] The burden falls on CON to explain why urgency in the market should outrun caution in a system that punishes haste for decades.
