[P1.1] A free curb does not eliminate the cost of scarce street space. It makes everyone else pay through blocked buses, double-parked delivery vans, circling drivers, and dangerous pickups. [P1.2] The practical model is straightforward: designated loading and pickup spaces, with published prices that rise when demand is high and fall when space is plentiful. Charges should reflect occupancy and congestion, not maximize revenue. Drivers should know the rate before committing, and payment should be integrated into fleet and platform systems wherever practical. Paying must never buy permission to block a bike lane or accessible crossing.
[P1.3] The central benefit is changing decisions before the street jams. A delivery operator can consolidate stops, move flexible deliveries outside the peak, or avoid leaving a vehicle at the curb unnecessarily. A ride-hail platform can direct pickups to a less crowded nearby block where safe and accessible. Not every trip can shift; enough flexible trips shifting can make room for those that cannot.
[P1.4] Keeping the price at zero gives operators no financial reason to account for the delay they impose on others.
[P1.5] Enforcement alone cannot solve that allocation problem. Even if every vehicle obeys the rules, more vehicles can seek legal stopping space than a busy block can accommodate. Time limits help, but do not distinguish an empty afternoon from the evening rush. Dynamic pricing does.
[P1.6] Fairness requires targeted accessibility protections and attention to small operators—not unlimited free use for commercial fleets. Revenue can support enforcement, safer loading areas, and transit, while reduced searching and delay can offset some operating costs. Cities can start with overloaded locations and adjust.
