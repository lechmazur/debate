Social media algorithms, caffeine, alcohol, and prescription nootropics all alter reward circuits and decision-making, yet we accept adult responsibility for their use rather than confining them to physicians. Adding a direct electrical channel does not create a new category of risk so much as it makes the existing one measurable and potentially tunable.
[C1.2] Restricting these interfaces to medical channels would hand a permanent monopoly to regulators and insurers over who may improve focus, regulate anxiety, or accelerate learning. Medical gatekeeping has already slowed access to proven neurotechnologies for non-critical cases; extending that model to consumer-grade devices would entrench the same delays while driving capable users toward unregulated gray markets or overseas developers. Voluntary safety standards, clear labeling, and liability rules can address calibration errors and hacking without requiring a prescription for every upgrade.
[C1.3] The manipulation concern is real but not decisive. Any device that delivers reward can be abused, yet we do not ban smartphones or slot machines on that basis. Instead we set age limits, require disclosures, and let informed adults weigh trade-offs. [C1.4] Autonomy supplies the decisive difference. A competent adult owns the electrical activity inside their skull. Requiring medical approval before they may adjust it substitutes institutional judgment for personal judgment and assumes regulators possess superior knowledge of that individual's goals and tolerances. History shows that consumer access to new interfaces—personal computers, GPS, smartphones—produced rapid safety improvements precisely because millions of non-patients experimented, reported problems, and forced iteration.
[C1.5] Medical-only distribution would remove that feedback loop and leave the technology both slower and more expensive.
[C1.6] The proposition therefore trades speculative catastrophic misuse for certain, widespread denial of benefit.
