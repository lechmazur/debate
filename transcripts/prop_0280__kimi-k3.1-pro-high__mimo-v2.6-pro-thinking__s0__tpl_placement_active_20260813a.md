Each item can be optional in theory yet compulsory in practice because skipping it invites accusations of being cheap or disrespectful. That pressure is especially indefensible at the beginning of a marriage, when money may be needed for rent, debt repayment, healthcare, children, or simply resilience against emergencies.

[C1.1] The proposition contains a bait and switch. It observes that weddings can be expensive and performative, then concludes that couples owe tradition "little obligation." Those are different claims, and the second does not follow from the first.
[C1.2] Start with the diagnosis. The costs PRO describes—staged venues, curated photography, destination weekends—are largely products of the modern wedding *industry*, not of tradition. A courthouse ceremony with family present, a church service with a potluck reception, a modest gathering where elders bless the couple: these are traditional weddings. What is expensive is a contemporary luxury package that borrows traditional vocabulary while replacing its substance.
[C1.3] If the target is commercial excess, say so; the proposition instead lets couples discard obligations of respect, inclusion, and ritual wholesale because some vendors overcharge.
[C1.4] Now the harder point: who the obligations are owed to. PRO treats wedding expectations as a debt imposed on couples by spectators. But weddings are among the few remaining rituals where a family publicly welcomes a new member, where two kin networks recognize each other, and where a community witnesses and holds people to their vows. Those goods are real, and they are produced precisely by following forms the couple did not invent.
[C1.5] The couple's preference alone cannot generate them, any more than a graduation means something when the graduate designs the credential. When couples abandon these expectations wholesale, they do not merely save money; they convert a communal act into private consumption.
[C1.6] PRO's own remedy is vulnerable to the complaint it raises. "Authentic," bespoke, non-traditional weddings are their own performance genre—Pinterest aesthetics, personalized vows performed for cameras, guests curated as an audience. Rejecting tradition does not end performance; it removes the script that once disciplined cost and spectacle. Traditions often *cap* extravagance by prescribing what is sufficient.
[C1.7] The buffet that "must" be lavish is failure of nerve, not of tradition.
[C1.8] The winning standard is whether obligations serve goods a couple cannot create alone—family reconciliation, communal recognition, generational continuity. They do. PRO would burn that inheritance to escape a caterer's invoice.
