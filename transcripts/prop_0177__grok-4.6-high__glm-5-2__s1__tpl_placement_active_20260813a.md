[C1.1] The proposition bundles two claims that should be pulled apart: that pig-to-human transplantation will become *routine* within fifteen years, and that we should *accelerate regulatory pathways now* to force that outcome. Both fail, and for connected reasons.
[C1.2] First, "routine" is doing enormous work in this motion. A handful of compassionate-use cases—desperately ill patients receiving experimental grafts under last-resort protocols—does not extrapolate to routine clinical practice. Routine means standardized manufacturing, durable long-term graft survival, manageable immunosuppression, predictable complication profiles, and broad clinical adoption. We are nowhere near those benchmarks.
[C1.3] Pig organs in human recipients have functioned for weeks, sometimes months, under extraordinary, non-reproducible conditions. The gap between "the organ was perfusing" and "this is a standard treatment offered at transplant centers nationwide" is not a gap of iteration. It is a gap of unknown unknowns—chronic rejection patterns we have not observed because no graft has lasted long enough to reveal them, physiological mismatches in organ size, growth, and hormonal regulation that emerge over years, and immunosuppressive regimens whose long-term toxicity in this specific context is entirely uncharacterized.
[C1.4] Second, the infection risk is categorically different from PRO's framing. Porcine endogenous retroviruses are integrated into the pig genome—you cannot simply "design them out" without consequences we do not fully understand. More importantly, the relevant risk is not to the individual recipient, who may rationally accept it, but to the population. A zoonotic pathogen adapted through a human host is a public health event, not a surgical complication.
[C1.5] That asymmetry—individual benefit weighed against population-scale tail risk—is exactly what deliberative regulatory pathways exist to manage.
[C1.6] Third, the "delay equals funerals" argument is emotionally coercive but analytically shallow. Every experimental medicine could be fast-tracked by that logic. The reason we do not is that premature deployment can generate harms that *exceed* the status quo: recipients who suffer prolonged, expensive, painful deaths from novel complications; public trust eroded by visible failures; resources diverted from scalable solutions like opt-out donation reform and bioartificial organ development.
