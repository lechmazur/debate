[P1.1] The motion is not a ban. It is a sequencing rule: scale robotaxis where the rules exist, and hold them at current footprints in dense cores until cities write the curb, labor, and safety frameworks that scaling demands. The burden falls on whoever wants to flood the most fragile street networks in the country with an unregulated technology before anyone has agreed on the rules of the road.
[P1.2] Three reasons that sequencing is right.
[P1.3] First, the asymmetry of error. If caps stay on six months too long, riders wait slightly longer and companies expand a bit slower — fully reversible. If caps come off too early, we get what we have already seen: vehicles stalling in intersections, blocking fire trucks and ambulances, dragging a pedestrian, double-parking across bike lanes because no robotaxi has a legal place to stop. San Francisco's fire department logged dozens of interference incidents during early expansion.
[P1.4] Those harms land on people who never consented to the experiment, and some are irreversible.
[P1.5] Second, leverage. Cities only get data-sharing, curb access fees, emergency-responder protocols, and labor transition plans while permission to scale is still on the table. We ran this experiment once with Uber and Lyft: let them scale first, regulate later, and cities spent a decade fighting for basic trip data while congestion and driver pay collapsed. Caps are the negotiating instrument that prevents a rerun.
[P1.6] Third, density is the hard case. Geofences already work — Phoenix and suburban corridors are fine proving grounds. Dense centers concentrate exactly what these systems handle worst: emergency vehicles, construction chaos, jaywalking crowds, contested curb space. Demanding rules before scale there is not technophobia; it's putting the test where failure is cheapest.
[P1.7] The choice is not robotaxis versus no robotaxis. It is whether cities write the rules before deployment makes rules impossible, or after the damage is locked in. Measured expansion under clear rules beats a land grab every time.
