[P1.1] Emotion-recognition systems in hiring and evaluation ask a machine to read a person's inner state from their face, voice, or body — and then to hold power over their livelihood with that reading. Countries should prohibit this, for three reasons: the science is weak, the harm is severe, and the employment setting destroys the safeguards that might otherwise make such tools tolerable.
[P1.2] First, the core inference is unreliable. Decades of research on facial expression suggest that people do not reliably broadcast stable internal emotions through fixed facial patterns; the same scowl can mean concentration, contempt, or a stiff neck. Voice "stress detection" fares no better. These systems don't measure emotion — they measure surface features and label them.
[P1.3] A tool that guesses is tolerable as a toy. It is not tolerable as a gatekeeper to employment.
[P1.4] Second, error is not randomly distributed. Cameras read faces unevenly across skin tones and lighting. Some people — those with facial differences, certain disabilities, autism, Parkinson's, or simply a flat affect — do not perform emotion the way training data assumes. Cultural norms around smiling, eye contact, and deference vary widely.
[P1.5] So the system systematically misreads exactly the people least able to absorb a bad score. In a labor market with unequal power, a low "enthusiasm" score is not feedback; it is a locked door with no explanation attached.
[P1.6] Third, employment is the worst possible venue for this technology. Consent is fictional when the alternative is not being hired. There is no meaningful redress: candidates are rarely told an algorithm scored their face, and even if told, they cannot contest a proprietary emotion label. And the tool chills expression itself — candidates learn to perform the right face, which punishes authenticity and rewards masking, a burden that falls hardest on neurodivergent workers.
[P1.7] The burden belongs on proponents. If emotion inference is speculative and its misuse in hiring is unfixable, prohibition is the proportionate response — not regulation of a guess, but an end to pretending it is a measurement.

[C1.2] Start with the strongest move in PRO's case: emotion-recognition systems are unreliable today. Grant it fully. The science is immature; error rates are real; disparate impact across demographics is documented. But notice what follows from that admission and what does not.
[C1.3] Unreliability is a reason to demand rigorous accuracy standards, mandatory bias audits, and informed disclosure to candidates — it is not a reason to prohibit. We do not ban drug testing because early immunoassays produced false positives; we required confirmation tests and regulatory oversight. The same playbook is available here. PRO skips straight to the most extreme remedy without explaining why the intermediate ones fail.
[C1.4] Now consider what employers actually do without these systems. Every hiring manager already makes emotion-adjacent judgments — "she seemed enthusiastic," "he lacked energy," "not a culture fit." These gut assessments are untethered to any standard, impossible to audit, and every bit as likely to penalize neurodivergent candidates or people from cultures with different expressiveness norms. An algorithmic tool, even an imperfect one, generates a score that can be examined, challenged, and improved over time.
[C1.5] A human hunch does not. Prohibiting the measurable version of a judgment while preserving the unmeasurable one is not protecting workers — it is protecting the status quo from accountability.
[C1.6] PRO's argument also suffers from overbreadth. The proposition covers all worker evaluation, not just initial hiring. In roles where emotional engagement is genuinely job-relevant — therapists, teachers, crisis counselors — structured feedback on affect could support professional development. [C1.7] AI capabilities have advanced rapidly across domains; emotion recognition may follow. A prohibition today forecloses that possibility permanently, replacing a malleable problem with an immovable law.

The sensor does not measure the enthusiasm I perform; it measures whether my face performs enthusiasm the way training data expects.
[P4.4] Masking here is not a skill gap that effort closes — it is a physiological and cultural tax that discriminates by body. And unlike a rehearsed answer, the performance is continuous and ambient: one flicker of flat affect mid-panel gets scored.
[P4.5] These two answers are why we still win the overall debate. CON keeps insisting a principled line does not exist and that regulation can handle the rest — but the line is real (inner state inferred from involuntary, identity-linked cues) and regulation cannot cross it.
