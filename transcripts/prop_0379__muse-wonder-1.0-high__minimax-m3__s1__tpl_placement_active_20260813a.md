[P1.1] A corporation cannot vote, cannot be drafted, cannot be jailed, yet under unlimited donations it can out-shout millions who can. That is the flaw this reform corrects: in a democracy, political power is supposed to track citizenship, not wealth.
[P1.2] Corporate money is not ordinary expression. A company spends from a treasury built by workers, customers, and shareholders who may despise the candidate funded. Managers do not donate to lose money; they donate as an investment, expecting access, softer regulation, contracts, or tax treatment in return. When an oil, pharmaceutical, or defense firm funds both sides of a race, it is not joining a debate.
[P1.3] It is hedging a risk and buying a seat at the table where rules are written.
[P1.4] Large individual donations create the same capture in personal form. A candidate who needs a handful of wealthy backers will spend evenings listening to them, not to nurses, drivers, or teachers who can give only a little. The agenda shifts toward donor obsessions — lower taxes on assets, looser planning rules, protection for incumbent industries — while supported but donor-indifferent policies stall. No explicit bribe is needed.
[P1.5] Dependence does the work.
[P1.6] Treating a donation as speech confuses the message with its amplifier. A low cap silences no one: every citizen can still speak, publish, organize, volunteer, vote, and give an equal, modest amount. Corporations can still lobby, publish research, and make a case. What they lose is the ability to convert economic dominance in the marketplace into political dominance in the legislature.
[P1.7] We already accept this logic elsewhere. Bribery is illegal even if a briber claims he is expressing approval, and vote-buying is illegal even if the buyer sincerely supports a candidate. Money transferred to gain political influence can be regulated precisely because its power comes from exchange, not argument.
[P1.8] Democracies that restrict corporate and large donations have not fallen silent. Their elections remain competitive and noisy. They are simply less for sale.
