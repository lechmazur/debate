[P1.1] The dominant dating‑app model—endless swipe feeds, algorithmic matching, and a market‑like interface—does not improve how most people find partners; it degrades it. First, the architecture creates choice overload. Presented with hundreds of profiles, users face the paradox of choice: more options predict lower satisfaction and higher indecision. Studies of online dating repeatedly show that heavy swipers report less commitment and more regret than those who meet a smaller, curated set.
[P1.2] Second, the model commodifies people. Profiles collapse to photos, a short bio, and a few metrics, encouraging evaluation on height, looks, income—traits that say nothing about compatibility. This “shopping” mindset treats potential partners as interchangeable items, eroding willingness to invest in the messy work of building a relationship.
[P1.3] Third, the algorithmic feedback loop amplifies bias. Apps surface profiles that generate quick swipes, not those that foster lasting connections, creating a self‑reinforcing cycle of short‑term matches, ghosting, and burnout. Consider the everyday experience: a user opens Tinder, swipes through 50 profiles in ten minutes, matches with three, exchanges a few messages, then hears nothing. This pattern repeats nightly, eroding confidence and fostering a sense that love is a numbers game.
[P1.4] Research on app usage shows the average user spends only a few seconds per profile—a timescale that precludes any meaningful assessment of compatibility. The result is a market where supply outpaces demand for genuine connection, leaving most participants feeling more isolated than before they downloaded the app. Empirically, surveys of dating‑app users reveal higher loneliness and lower long‑term relationship rates compared with pre‑app cohorts. The “most users” clause is crucial: while a minority of highly attractive or socially skilled individuals may benefit, the median user experiences more rejections, empty chats, and fatigue, translating into fewer stable relationships.
[P1.5] The opponent will point to increased access and convenience, but convenience without depth does not equal better relationship formation. The core claim is that unlimited choice, superficial metrics, and engagement‑driven algorithms systematically undermine the conditions needed for lasting partnerships, making the average user’s love life worse, not better.
