[P1.1] When Rana Plaza collapsed, more than a thousand garment workers died making clothes for brands that could not say which factories their orders had reached. That was not bad luck. Production had crossed borders while accountability stayed home, because no one was required to look, and no one was required to publish what they found.
[P1.2] Opacity is a competitive asset. A firm that traces its cobalt or its cotton to the source pays for the privilege; a rival that asks nothing sells cheaper and claims the same clean conscience. Voluntary disclosure cannot break that trap, because the firms with the worst supply chains have the strongest incentive to stay quiet, and the honest ones are punished for candour. Only a common floor removes the penalty for looking.
[P1.3] Standardisation and audit are what make the difference between information and advertising. Today's sustainability reports are bespoke brochures: each company chooses its own scope, its own metrics, its own success stories, and skips the tiers where forced labour actually hides. A shared template makes firms comparable; independent assurance makes the numbers falsifiable, and therefore costly to fake. We already accept this logic for financial accounts.
[P1.4] Audits miss frauds, yet nobody concludes that unaudited accounts would be better.
[P1.5] The motion is deliberately narrow: large multinationals, not corner shops. These are firms with existing compliance departments, legal teams and supplier databases, already filing under a dozen regimes. The marginal cost is real and modest; the marginal knowledge is enormous.
[P1.6] So the clash is not whether reports end abuse — they do not, alone. It is whether buyers, investors, regulators and workers get verified, comparable facts, or marketing they cannot test. Opposing this motion means defending the right of the world's most powerful companies to keep not knowing.

[C1.1] The Rana Plaza factories had been audited. So had the Ali Enterprises garment plant in Karachi, certified clean weeks before a fire killed hundreds. Social auditing did not fail at Rana Plaza because nobody required it; it failed because auditors with a day on site, paid by the buyer, checked fire extinguishers and ignored a building visibly cracking. The motion's answer is to make that exact ritual compulsory, standardised, and legally blessed.
[C1.2] That is the core problem. The proposition mandates a document, not an outcome. What actually fixed Bangladesh afterwards was the Accord: binding commitments, independent engineers inspecting structures, worker complaint channels with teeth. None of that is a report.
[C1.3] Governments have limited regulatory attention and political capital; spend it on due-diligence paperwork and the pressure for remedy, liability and enforceable agreements dissipates. Worse, a signed-off audited report becomes a shield. When the next disaster comes, the firm waves its compliant filing and says: we followed the standard.
[C1.4] Second, standardisation measures what can be counted. Templates reward suppliers mapped, audits completed, codes signed. They cannot capture whether a worker can refuse overtime or join a union. Firms optimise the metric; abuse migrates to the subcontractor nobody lists.
[C1.5] Third, disclosure mandates push firms to exit, not to improve. The US conflict-minerals rule produced a de facto boycott of artisanal mining in eastern Congo: companies could not prove cleanliness, so they stopped buying, and miners lost their only income. A large multinational facing a standardised report with its name attached will rationally drop the Bangladeshi or Ethiopian supplier and consolidate with a big, pre-certified plant. The poorest producers are cut out precisely because they are hard to document.
[C1.6] Opposing this motion is not defending ignorance. It is refusing to let an audited brochure stand in for accountability.
