[P1.6] This is not liability for every crash. It is a larger share of responsibility where the harm is foreseeable, the zone is clear, and the firm could have prevented the pattern at modest cost. [C1.1] The rider is the person who turns the handlebar. At the moment of a sidewalk collision, the person with hands on the steering, eyes on the path, and the ability to brake is the rider — not a fleet operator miles away watching a map. Legal responsibility that doesn't track control at the moment of harm isn't justice; it's just a search for the deepest pocket.
[C1.2] That matters because PRO's proposal punishes firms for a decision made by someone else. Every shared vehicle is sold with a rule against sidewalk riding and a rule against restricted zones. When a rider breaks those rules, that is a violation of the company's terms as much as the city's — the operator is the victim of the breach, not the author of it. Converting every rider's disobedience into corporate liability effectively makes companies the insurers of strangers' choices.
[C1.3] And PRO can't actually deliver the control it promises. Geofencing has real limits: GPS drift puts a scooter "on the sidewalk" when it is legally parked at the curb, and a vehicle traveling beside a restricted plaza is measured within meters of it. Power-cutting on that signal means stalling riders in traffic — a different injury, this one to the rider, caused directly by the "safety" feature. PRO waves at remote lockouts as if a map were a steering wheel.
[C1.4] The cost side is worse. Flattening liability onto operators forces them to price every ride for the worst-case rider, raise prices, pull vehicles out of lower-income neighborhoods, or exit markets — which pushes people back into cars. More cars means more pedestrian injuries, distributed across every street instead of concentrated where a rider made a bad choice.
[C1.5] Existing law already covers this. Negligence claims against riders, negligent-hire and product-defect claims against genuinely careless operators: the tools are there. "More legal responsibility" adds a vague extra layer that dilutes the rider's accountability while taxing a service that keeps people out of two-ton cars.
