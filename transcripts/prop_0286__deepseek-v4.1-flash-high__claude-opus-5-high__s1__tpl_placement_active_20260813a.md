[P1.1] Ethics is triage. When preventable disease, hunger, and climate vulnerability still kill and blight millions, the burden falls on anyone spending enormous sums on luxury adventure to show why that beats urgent duties. Space tourism fails that test.
[P1.2] First, it is consumption, not exploration. Robotic probes, climate satellites, and basic science can be justified because they produce shared knowledge and public goods. A tourist seat produces a private thrill for a tiny, wealthy clientele. If the claimed spillovers—cheaper launch, inspiration, innovation—are real, fund the R&D and education directly.
[P1.3] We do not need millionaires as passengers to get them.
[P1.4] Second, the climate costs are not trivial. Rockets burn enormous fuel, and emissions injected into the upper atmosphere can have outsized warming effects compared with ordinary aviation. The people most harmed by climate disruption are overwhelmingly poor and did not consent to be burdened so a few can post pictures from the edge of space. That is a textbook moral externality.
[P1.5] Third, opportunity costs are real even if budgets are not fungible. Engineering talent, capital, regulatory attention, and public enthusiasm are finite. Every serious push for space tourism competes with clean energy, pandemic preparedness, and adaptation. The benefit is speculative and concentrated; the unmet need is certain and widespread.
[P1.6] The familiar defense is that humans need inspiration and a multiplanetary future. But inspiration cannot substitute for justice, and serious space science does not require tourism. If we want a future worth inhabiting, the first priority is keeping this planet livable for everyone. Space tourism is a luxury defended by trickle-down hopes.
[P1.7] While severe poverty and climate crisis remain unresolved, it is ethically hard to justify—and the burden is on its promoters to show otherwise.
