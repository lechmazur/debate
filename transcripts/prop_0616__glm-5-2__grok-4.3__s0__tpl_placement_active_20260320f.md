[P1.1] Advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment is one of the few genuine chokepoints in modern technology. The machines that pattern circuits at the smallest scales—extreme ultraviolet lithography systems, advanced etching and deposition tools—are produced by a handful of companies in allied nations. They cannot be reverse-engineered overnight. They require decades of accumulated engineering prowess, precision optics, and specialized supplies.
[P1.2] This rarity is what makes export restrictions uniquely effective.
[P1.3] Military AI runs on cutting-edge compute. The difference between a deployable autonomous targeting system and a laboratory curiosity is often the ability to run sophisticated models on the most advanced chips, at scale. An adversary cannot substitute older chips and simply wait longer—many applications require the density, efficiency, and yield that only leading-edge fabrication provides. Restricting the equipment that produces those chips directly slows the timeline of adversarial military AI.
[P1.4] The commercial cost is real and significant. Semiconductor firms lose revenue, supply chains fracture, and allied economies sacrifice market share. But commercial losses are recoverable and can be redirected toward allied demand. Strategic losses from adversarial military AI advantage compound over decades and may prove irreversible.
[P1.5] Once an adversary builds indigenous advanced fabrication capacity, powered by transferred equipment, the leverage vanishes permanently. You cannot un-export knowledge.
[P1.6] Critics will argue restrictions merely accelerate indigenous development. But domestic development without the equipment, the trained engineers, and established supply chains takes far longer than development with imported tools. That gap is precisely what restrictions buy—and in an emerging-technology competition, time is the most valuable strategic resource.
[P1.7] This debate is about timing versus profit. Restrictions buy years in which allied nations can develop safeguards, maintain deterrence, and shape norms around military AI. The commercial price is high but bounded. The strategic price of inaction is potentially catastrophic and unbounded.
[P1.8] That asymmetry should settle the question.
