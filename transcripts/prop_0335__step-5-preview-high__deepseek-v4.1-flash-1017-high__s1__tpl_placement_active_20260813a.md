[P1.1] AI has not simply made online fraud cheaper; it has industrialized emotional betrayal. When a scammer can deploy a large language model to sustain months of daily intimacy, clone a voice to soothe a target in crisis, and generate a face that survives video calls, the harm is no longer just a fraudulent transaction—it is manufactured dependency. Consider the widow who chats nightly with an “oil rig engineer,” an AI construct tuned to her late husband’s writing style, until she wires her savings for a fake customs fee.
[P1.2] Treating this as ordinary online fraud misses the mechanism, the victim experience, and therefore the remedy.
[P1.3] Ordinary fraud regulation assumes a rational consumer deceived about a product. Here, the victim is not buying anything; they are investing in a relationship. Banks and platforms often deny reimbursement because the transfer was technically authorized. That logic is useless against a scheme whose entire purpose is to make the victim willingly authorize transfers to someone they believe they love.
[P1.4] Consumer-protection tools—unfair and deceptive practice standards, duty of care for platforms deploying romance-adjacent AI, and specialized victim recovery funds—are built for asymmetric harms that ordinary contract and criminal law ignore.
[P1.5] The scale matters too. Human con artists are capacity-constrained; AI personas can run thousands of relationships simultaneously, learning each victim’s vulnerabilities from their messages. This is not a lone bad actor but an infrastructure of manipulation, and infrastructure requires systemic regulation, not just case-by-case prosecution.
[P1.6] We already accept that some deceptions require special rules. AI-enabled romance fraud at industrial scale is a distinct category of harm: it attacks through trust rather than trickery, and it survives precisely because our ordinary fraud frameworks keep asking the wrong question—not “was this a lie?” but “did the victim press send?” Governments should stop filing these cases under generic online fraud and build a consumer-protection response that names the actual injury: intimacy weaponized for profit.

[C1.1] The fatal word in this motion is “distinct.” AI can make romance scams crueler and cheaper, but it does not create a new legal wrong. Strip away the chatbot, the cloned voice, and the generated face, and what remains is the oldest fraud fact pattern: a false identity, cultivated trust, and an induced transfer. A scammer with a human accomplice and a paper script commits the same injury as one with an LLM. The victim’s remedy should not turn on whether a machine wrote the love poem.
[C1.2] Creating a separate AI-romance category creates boundary games. Is a scam “AI-enabled” if the criminal uses a grammar checker, a deepfake for one call, or a human operator assisted by a model? Must the relationship be romantic, or does an AI “friendship” scam fall outside? Every threshold becomes a litigation gift to fraudsters and a reason for platforms and banks to deny responsibility.
[C1.3] Meanwhile, victims of equally devastating non-AI romance fraud get second-class treatment. Fragmentation also weakens enforcement: agencies lose clear metrics, ordinary fraud units assume a specialist team owns it, and specialist teams are small.
[C1.4] The real problems PRO identifies—authorized transfers, platform design, victim recovery—are not solved by renaming the category. They are solved by technology-neutral rules: unfair-practice standards for deceptive design, duties on platforms and payment providers, clear reimbursement rules for induced transfers, and training for police. Those can be strengthened under existing fraud and consumer-protection law without making AI the trigger.
[C1.5] AI is a force multiplier, not a new species of wrongdoing. Governments should prosecute and regulate the conduct—deception, exploitation of trust, and payment-system failure—not the presence of a model. The motion would make protection depend on the scammer’s toolchain. That is the wrong axis, and it will leave more victims outside the law.
