[P1.1] At sixteen, a citizen can work full-time, pay income and sales taxes, drive on public roads, leave compulsory schooling, and face adult criminal courts for serious offenses. Yet when legislatures decide how to spend their tax dollars, structure their schools, set youth minimum wages, or manage long-term sovereign debt, those same citizens are locked out of the polling booth. Reserving the franchise for eighteen-year-olds violates a foundational democratic standard: those who bear legal obligations and fund the state deserve a voice in how they are governed.
[P1.2] Beyond fairness, eighteen is the worst possible age to initiate someone into democracy. Voting is a habit built through repetition, and turning out in one’s first eligible election is a critical driver of lifelong participation. Yet eighteen is a moment of peak social disruption, when young people are leaving home, starting full-time jobs, or moving for university. Navigating voter registration during major life upheaval depresses turnout right at the starting line.
[P1.3] At sixteen, by contrast, most youth live in stable households and attend school. Lowering the voting age pairs classroom civics with real civic action, turning abstract lessons into a durable habit. That is why, in places like Austria and Scotland that extended the franchise to sixteen, sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds have turned out at notably higher rates than slightly older first-time voters.
[P1.4] Opponents inevitably argue that sixteen-year-olds lack sufficient maturity or political knowledge. But democracy does not impose a competence test on adults; we do not strip the ballot from older citizens who are uninformed, impulsive, or hyper-partisan. Furthermore, excluding sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds creates a severe policy distortion. In aging democracies, politicians naturally cater to older cohorts who vote in high numbers, systematically shortchanging education, climate resilience, and entry-level opportunities.
[P1.5] Lawmakers ignore sixteen-year-olds not because their lives are unaffected by government, but because they cannot hold politicians accountable. Lowering the voting age to sixteen corrects that democratic blind spot and builds stronger citizens for life.
