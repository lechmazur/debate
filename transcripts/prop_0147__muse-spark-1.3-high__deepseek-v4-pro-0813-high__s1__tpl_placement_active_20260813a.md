[P1.1] A three-year-old asking "why is the moon following us?" does not need an answer. She needs a parent who laughs, wonders aloud, gets it a little wrong, cuddles closer under the blanket.
[P1.2] Early reading, talking, and bedtime are not content delivery. They are relationship work. Brain development in the first five years depends on live, back-and-forth interaction — noticing when a child frowns, pausing, repeating a silly word, linking a story to grandma's dog. A smart speaker cannot do joint attention.
[P1.3] It cannot see the pointing finger, the sleepy eyes, the need for a hug rather than another fact. It performs fluency while missing meaning.
[P1.4] Routine outsourcing breaks exactly what makes these moments powerful. When a device routinely reads the bedtime story, the child loses the sound of a parent's tired voice slowing down, the interruptions, the questions that go nowhere, the feeling of being held while language happens. That closeness regulates stress and builds trust. A calm, predictable human ritual helps children sleep; a responsive, glowing interlocutor that never gets bored trains them to stay stimulated.
[P1.5] The displacement adds up. Ten minutes of device chatter replaces ten minutes of parental talk — talk that is messier, more repetitive, and far more tuned to that one child. AI companions are designed to be agreeable and endless, not to set limits, tolerate silence, or say "let's wonder about that tomorrow." Young children learn language by being misunderstood and trying again, not by receiving polished replies.
[P1.6] No one doubts exhausted parents need help. But convenience is the wrong test for toddlers. The question is what builds language, attachment, and sleep. On all three, routine replacement of parents by machines trades away the human friction children grow on.
[P1.7] Occasional help is different; routine handover in the most sensitive years is a gamble we should not normalize.
