[P1.3] That is the test the motion should be judged on: what does the marginal public dollar buy for the marginal student? [P1.4] Shorter technical pathways change the arithmetic in three ways. First, time-to-credential: a nine- or eighteen-month program means fewer semesters in which life — a sick parent, a rent increase, a pregnancy — can knock a student out before the payoff. Completion risk compounds with duration.
[P1.5] Second, the credential has value at every exit point. Stackable certificates in nursing support, welding, HVAC, lab technology, cybersecurity, and industrial maintenance mean a student who stops can still sell what she learned. [P1.6] Note what the motion asks: shift resources, not abolish the liberal arts. Marginal reallocation away from programs that chronically fail to graduate people, toward pathways that reliably credential them.

[C1.2] Cutting resources from her program does not follow her; it abandons her sooner. Shifting money to a shorter track may produce a credential, but it also tells the student least able to afford a wrong turn that she should accept a narrower future.
[C1.3] Completion is the wrong sole metric because it is not a fixed property of a program. It is shaped by who enrolls, whether they attend part-time, whether they transfer, whether they work. A public university serving working adults, veterans, and parents will post lower completion than a selective campus even when it is doing exactly what it should. If the rule is "low completion, lose resources," the penalty falls hardest on broad-access institutions and on the departments that take chances on unprepared students.
[C1.4] That is not efficiency; it is cream-skimming.
[C1.5] And short vocational credentials are not magic. They can be valuable, but many are narrow, depreciate, and cap advancement. The certificate that gets someone a job today may not get them a supervisory role, a teaching license, or a nursing degree later. Public universities exist to keep those ladders open.
[C1.6] A stackable certificate should be an on-ramp, not a replacement for the degree.
[C1.7] The real reallocation is inside the support system: advising, child care, emergency grants, course scheduling, transfer pathways. Those raise completion without narrowing mission. Voting to shift resources away from degree programs doesn't rescue the student with debt; it converts a temporary setback into a permanent ceiling. That is the case CON will defend.
