[P1.1] Antitrust law should judge coordination by its economic function, not by whether competitors met in a back room. A shared rent-setting algorithm can become the back room: landlords supply sensitive information, a common intermediary processes their positions, and recommendations steer supposedly rival properties toward aligned prices. When competitors knowingly route pricing through that system, antitrust scrutiny should track the coordination they have outsourced, not the absence of handshakes.
[P1.2] The mechanism is straightforward. In a concentrated rental market, one landlord hesitates to raise rent because rivals may undercut them or offer concessions. A shared tool trained on rivals’ current rents, occupancy, and pricing responses reduces that uncertainty. It can recommend similar increases, discourage discounts, and make deviations visible.
[P1.3] No landlord needs to telephone another; the intermediary supplies the assurance that competition would otherwise deny them. That is the classic danger of hub-and-spoke coordination, whether the hub is a trade association, a manager, or software.
[P1.4] Nonbinding recommendations and human override buttons do not answer this. Direct price coordination also need not produce perfect compliance to soften competition. Nor is parallel pricing alone enough for liability; the crucial evidence is the common algorithm, exchanges of nonpublic data, knowledge of competitors’ participation, and patterns showing adherence despite individual incentives to compete. Those facts justify discovery into contracts, data flows, design choices, and effects—not dismissal because a machine made the suggestion.
[P1.5] This standard does not condemn every pricing tool or presume guilt. Software using public, stale, or independently generated information can deliver efficiencies without pooling rivals’ competitive decision-making. But efficiency is a defense to examine, not an algorithmic immunity clause. Housing is not a market where “just following the app” is harmless: aligned recommendations can mean fewer concessions and higher rents across whole neighborhoods.
[P1.6] Efficiency remains relevant, but when landlords share the brain that sets rents, that is shared decision-making, not independent choice.

[C1.1] The proposition collapses a critical, longstanding antitrust distinction that protects both competition and consumers, treating every shared pricing input as equivalent to an explicit price-fixing agreement. Direct price coordination requires a mutual commitment to adhere to a common price, backed by accountability for rivals who undercut the group—something no generic algorithmic rent tool enforces. Landlords who use these tools retain full, unconstrained authority to set rents below the recommendation, offer move-in concessions, or slash prices to fill vacant units fast, with no coordinated retaliation from other users waiting to punish that choice.
[C1.2] Hub-and-spoke collusion, the doctrine PRO cites, already requires proof that competing “spokes” agreed to use the central hub to align prices, not merely that they used a common tool to inform independent choices. Most of these tools draw on public listing data, recent lease comps, and local occupancy trends—the same market intelligence large, vertically integrated rental operators already build for their own portfolios with expensive in-house analytics teams. Applying automatic, price-fixing-level scrutiny to mere use of these tools would not police collusion; it would strip small, independent owners of the ability to access basic market information, putting them at a permanent competitive disadvantage relative to the big corporate landlords that already dominate many metro rental markets.
[C1.3] Reduced pricing uncertainty is not collusive assurance. Competitive markets function best when participants can accurately gauge prevailing prices, rather than guessing blindly, leaving units vacant for months, or underpricing in ways that erode incentives to maintain and expand rental housing supply. Without the requirement to prove an actual agreement to restrain trade, the PRO standard would sweep in benign third-party data services, industry rent reports, even public rental listing platforms, chilling pro-consumer competition instead of targeting the rare, demonstrable cases where tools are designed to police price compliance among rivals.

[P5.2] “Scrutiny as if” coordinating directly does not mean conviction by subscription. It means courts ask the same functional questions: what nonpublic information entered the system, what common signals emerged, what users knew, and whether apparent independence was economically real. Public comparables and independent forecasts survive those questions; a shared pricing brain does not become benign because compliance is imperfect or an override button exists.
[P5.3] Small landlords deserve lawful analytics, not immunity from examination. Legal costs cannot make opacity a shield.
[P5.4] The choice is therefore not innovation versus automatic guilt. It is functional inquiry versus a software safe harbor. Where rivals outsource the decisions that competition requires them to make independently, the law should look through the code and scrutinize the coordination.
