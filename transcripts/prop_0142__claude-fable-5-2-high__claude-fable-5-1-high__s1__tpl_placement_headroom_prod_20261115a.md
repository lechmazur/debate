[P1.1] A fourteen-year-old scrolls past a skincare ad. The model's waist has been narrowed, her pores erased, her legs lengthened by software. Nothing on the screen says so. The girl doesn't compare herself to an image; she compares herself to what she believes is a person.
[P1.2] That belief is the product being sold, and it is false.
[P1.3] This motion asks for something modest: not a ban on retouching, but a ban on undisclosed heavy retouching in commercial content aimed at children. Edit all you like. Just say so.
[P1.4] First, it is deception in the plainest commercial sense. We already forbid advertisers from showing a toy doing things it cannot do, and we already require influencers to label paid sponsorships. A body that does not exist, used to sell a product that supposedly produced it, is the same lie in a different costume.
[P1.5] Second, the audience is uniquely exposed. Adolescents are in the peak years of social comparison and identity formation, and a substantial body of research links appearance-focused social media to body dissatisfaction. A thirteen-year-old often cannot tell where the person ends and the filter begins, and AI enhancement is making that line invisible even to trained eyes. When detection by the viewer becomes impossible, honesty by the seller becomes obligatory.
[P1.6] Third, the costs are trivially small. No speech is silenced, no image is removed, no artist is censored. The only thing lost is the advertiser's ability to profit from a child's mistaken belief that the image is real. If a business model depends on that mistake, that is an argument for the rule, not against it.
[P1.7] Labels won't cure insecurity, and needn't. They stop the lie and reward honest images. Who has the better claim: a child to know what's real, or a marketer to conceal it?

[C1.1] Nobody on this side defends lying to children. The question is whether a mandatory "this image was retouched" label actually protects a fourteen-year-old, or whether it is a feel-good sticker that makes her world more deceptive. It is the second.
[C1.2] Start with what labels do. France and Israel have required retouching disclosures on commercial images for years. The research that followed, as far as anyone can tell, found roughly no improvement in body satisfaction, and several experiments found the opposite: a disclaimer draws the eye to the exact body it warns about. A girl told "her waist was narrowed" does not shrug; she studies the waist.
[C1.3] The label converts a passing image into a lesson in what should have been fixed.
[C1.4] Second, the perverse inference. Once labels exist, their absence becomes a claim. The regulated skincare ad carries a tag; the unlabeled selfie from a classmate using a default smoothing filter carries none. The child learns that unlabeled means real, precisely when the vast majority of what she sees is unlabeled, unregulated, and filtered.
[C1.5] The motion polices the one percent of images with a legal department and certifies the other ninety-nine as authentic by implication.
[C1.6] Third, the line cannot be drawn. "Heavy" retouching versus light? Every phone camera applies skin smoothing before the photo exists. "Aimed at minors"?
[C1.7] Most influencer audiences are mixed and unknown even to the influencer. Regulators will either stamp everything, which teaches nothing, or chase edge cases selectively.
[C1.8] What children need is already available: existing false-advertising law when a product claims results it did not produce, platform-level filter tags that apply to peers too, and media literacy that treats every image as constructed. The motion substitutes a sticker for all of that and calls it honesty.
