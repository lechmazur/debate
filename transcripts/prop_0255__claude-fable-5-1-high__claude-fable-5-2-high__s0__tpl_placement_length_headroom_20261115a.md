[P1.1] When you delete a post, you are not performing a ritual. You are revoking consent. The question is whether a stranger may catch that revoked consent in a net, store it indefinitely, and sell it to whoever pays.
[P1.2] First, deletion is the only meaningful consent mechanism ordinary people have. Nobody reads privacy policies; everybody understands the trash icon. If brokers can archive deleted material, the delete button becomes theatre, and every confession, every location ping, every search typed and erased at 2 a.m. lives permanently in a warehouse the citizen cannot see or audit. A right to withdraw that cannot actually withdraw anything is no right.
[P1.3] Second, the harms are concrete and asymmetric. Picture a nurse who deletes a post about her depression before a job search, and a screening firm buys the archived version anyway. Picture location trails near a reproductive health clinic sold in bulk, as brokers have done. Picture a teenager's deleted photos resurfacing in a stalker's purchased dossier.
[P1.4] The broker earns cents per record; the citizen bears divorce, denial of credit, prosecution, violence. No price signal corrects that mismatch because the person harmed is never the customer.
[P1.5] Third, the counterarguments collapse under inspection. Fraud prevention and legal retention are first-party duties; the motion restrains the secondary market, not a bank keeping its own records. "The data was already collected" is an argument for the ban, not against it: collection without the ability to sell removes the incentive to hoard in the first place. And a market in what people have deliberately hidden is not innovation; it is the industrialisation of eavesdropping.
[P1.6] Prohibition does not break the internet. It restores the ordinary expectation that a thing you erased is erased, and that privacy is not merely whatever you failed to hide fast enough.

[C1.2] Start with "non-public." Your repayment history is non-public. So is the device fingerprint linked to an account takeover, the address trail that locates a parent dodging child support, the leaked-password archives that warn you when your credentials are circulating. Credit bureaus, fraud consortia and identity-verification services are data brokers, and non-public traces are their entire stock.
[C1.3] PRO says fraud prevention is a "first-party" duty [P1.5]. A bank's own records cannot tell it that this device defrauded three other banks yesterday. Fraud detection works only because someone in the middle pools signals across institutions. Ban that middle, and fraudsters win while thin-file borrowers get denied because lenders can verify nothing.
[C1.4] Now "deleted." Deletion is not only regret; it is also how scammers pull fake listings and harassers erase threats before the complaint lands. A rule that treats every erasure as sacred revoked consent hands the delete key to the people who most need to be remembered.
[C1.5] And the ban fails on its own terms. PRO exempts first parties, so the platform still holds the nurse's post forever. The delete button stays theatre; the motion merely ensures the largest platforms alone keep the archive.
[C1.6] The better path is obvious: regulate uses. Ban sales of health and location data, restrict screening firms, give citizens deletion rights against brokers, license and audit the trade. Punish the stalker's dossier, not the fraud check. Precision protects the nurse; prohibition protects the thief.
