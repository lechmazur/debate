[P1.1] Women freezing their eggs today face a high-stakes gamble marketed as a guaranteed investment. They pay ten to twenty thousand dollars, endure weeks of hormone injections, and stake their reproductive futures on advertising that promises "empowerment" and "peace of mind" while obscuring the brutal arithmetic of maternal age. This is not a dispute over word choice; it is a market failure where technical possibility is systematically substituted for statistical probability, leading women to delay childbearing based on misplaced confidence.
[P1.2] Current fertility marketing showcases forty-five-year-old success stories without clarifying those outcomes required retrieval at twenty-eight. Billboards feature confident professionals in their late thirties alongside slogans about "taking control," yet bury the critical caveat that live-birth rates per frozen egg drop precipitously after age thirty-five, often falling into single-digit percentages. The uncertainty is not merely omitted; it is architecturally hidden in dense footnotes while hope occupies the visual center. Women consequently make major life decisions—career pivots, relationship timelines, financial commitments—under the assumption that freezing guarantees future fertility rather than offering a probabilistic chance.
[P1.3] This demands regulatory intervention for the same reason we mandate warning labels on pharmaceuticals: when stakes are medical, financial, and chronologically irreversible, sellers cannot rely on fine print to disclose material risks. The information asymmetry is profound. Clinics possess granular data on cryopreservation outcomes by age at retrieval; patients confront aspirational lifestyle imagery designed to maximize consultation bookings. Voluntary disclosure fails because market incentives reward optimism; competitors who foreground statistical sobriety lose clients to rivals promising "future possibility."
[P1.4] Mandating that age-based success rates appear prominently in advertising—clearly visible before the consultation fee, not buried in paperwork—corrects this without banning hope. It ensures women understand the uncertainty curve before draining savings or postponing conception. Consumer protection requires that material risks match the prominence of material promises.
