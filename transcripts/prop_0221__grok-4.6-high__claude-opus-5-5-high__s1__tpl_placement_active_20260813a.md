[P1.1] High-dosage tutoring buys concentrated, personalized minutes on the skills that move reading and math. Class-size cuts mostly buy a slightly quieter room and a thinner slice of the same teacher’s attention. That is why the same dollar produces larger gains when spent on tutoring.
[P1.2] Shrinking classes by four or five students is not a tweak. It requires a large jump in teachers, benefits, rooms, and hiring. The extra adult still runs a whole class: transitions, behavior, whole-group lessons. Each child may gain a few extra seconds of feedback, not a redesigned learning experience.
[P1.3] Class-size effects have been modest except in the earliest grades and at very large, budget-breaking cuts.
[P1.4] Tutoring three or more times a week works on a different margin. A tutor with one, two, or three students can catch a decoding error or a place-value confusion and keep practicing until it sticks. Frequency compounds; a weekly session does not. Because sessions are short and targeted, you do not need a full-time certified teacher for every extra minute.
[P1.5] Trained tutors and paraprofessionals can deliver those minutes at a lower loaded cost.
[P1.6] A student two years behind in reading does not need the whole class shrunk by four seats. She needs someone to sit with her on phonics or fluency three times a week until the gap closes. Class-size dollars cannot make that bargain. They purchase a little more of everything, including time she already spends waiting through lessons she cannot access.
[P1.7] Gains therefore stack twice: more learning per hour of adult time, and cheaper hours of the adult time that matters. Tutoring can be aimed at unfinished reading and math without rebuilding the school’s entire staffing model. Scale favors using scarce certified teachers as designers and supervisors, not as the only people allowed to sit with a stuck child.
