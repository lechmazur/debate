[P1.1] Abuse in global supply chains survives on invisibility. A chocolate bar on a European shelf can depend on child labor thousands of miles away; a cheap T-shirt can depend on unsafe factories; a smartphone battery can depend on miners working in brutal conditions. Consumers cannot see it, investors cannot price it, and regulators cannot stop what they cannot track.
[P1.2] Voluntary corporate promises have not fixed this. For decades firms published glossy sustainability brochures while disasters kept happening, from factory collapses in Bangladesh to forced labor in fishing and agriculture. Without a legal requirement, disclosure stays selective, late, and unverifiable, and responsible companies that do investigate are undercut by rivals who look the other way.
[P1.3] Standardized, audited reporting breaks that pattern. Standardization means every large firm must answer the same questions — where are the risks, what was found, what was done — so claims can be compared instead of marketed. Auditing means an independent check, not self-grading. Just as mandatory financial audits did not end fraud but made it far harder to hide, mandatory human-rights audits would force problems into the open where pressure can work.
[P1.4] Focusing on large multinationals is both fair and effective. They have the buying power to demand safer factories and cleaner mines, and the resources to map suppliers. A mandate turns that leverage into duty and creates a level playing field: no firm gains advantage by staying ignorant.
[P1.5] Countries should require this because markets and morals both demand light. Investors need reliable risk data, shoppers need truth, victims need a lever that reaches beyond weak local enforcement. Sunlight does not solve everything, but without standardized truth, nothing else — lawsuits, boycotts, reforms — can function.

[C1.1] The motion sounds like sunlight; in practice it is a flashlight pointed at paperwork. Required standardized, audited due-diligence reports do not reach the abuses PRO describes. They create a compliance ritual that large firms are best equipped to game.
[C1.2] First, audits cannot reliably see what they claim. Supply chains are multi-tier, seasonal, subcontracted, and constantly changing. A short inspection of a listed factory says little about the informal workshop, labor broker, or mine two tiers down. And because the audited firm often selects and pays the auditor, the incentive is to produce a clean report, not a true one.
[C1.3] Factory audits did not prevent disasters in garment supply chains; they often coexisted with them.
[C1.4] Second, standardization invites box-ticking. Once every large firm must answer the same questions, the winning strategy is minimum compliance: polished policies, selected metrics, and audit-ready sites. Complex harm—debt bondage, retaliation, sexual harassment, wage theft—gets flattened into indicators. The report becomes a liability shield: “We disclosed the risk and had it audited.” That is not accountability; it is risk management for shareholders.
[C1.5] Third, the mandate misplaces costs and power. Large multinationals can absorb reporting and audit fees; smaller suppliers in poorer countries cannot, so they are squeezed, consolidated, or pushed into informal work that is even harder to see. If the rule creates legal exposure, firms may simply cut ties with high-risk suppliers, abandoning the workers it claims to protect.
[C1.6] Finally, reporting is not remedy. A victim of forced labor needs wages, freedom, and prosecution of the abuser, not another annual PDF. Targeted liability rules, criminal enforcement, import bans tied to proven abuse, and worker-led monitoring can impose consequences, while the motion mainly creates an industry of consultants and auditors. The question is not whether transparency sounds good.
[C1.7] It is whether this mandate reduces abuse more than it camouflages it. It does not.
