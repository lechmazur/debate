[P1.1] When a tenant signs a lease, they lock themselves into a home they cannot easily escape. Breaking a lease costs thousands; enduring misery for a year erases wellbeing. That power imbalance is why disclosure obligations exist—landlords know what tenants cannot.
[P1.2] The problems named here are not subjective preferences. Recurring short-term-rental party complaints mean the building has a documented pattern of Airbnb-style disruption, verified through police calls or HOA complaints. Severe noise problems mean conditions that consistently prevent sleep or quiet enjoyment. Chronic package theft means residents regularly lose delivered goods.
[P1.3] All three share a defining trait: they are patterns, not one-off incidents, and they are knowable to the landlord before signing.
[P1.4] The case for disclosure is straightforward. First, information asymmetry. A prospective tenant touring a unit on a Tuesday afternoon cannot detect Saturday-night party patterns or package thieves who strike at noon. The landlord, managing the property over months and years, possesses that knowledge.
[P1.5] Concealing it converts superior information into a tool of deception—the tenant pays full market rent for a unit worth significantly less in quiet enjoyment.
[P1.6] Second, consumer protection parallels already govern rental housing. Landlords must disclose lead paint, mold, pest infestations, and flood risk because those hazards directly undermine the value of what tenants are purchasing. Chronic noise and recurring theft do the same thing—a unit where you cannot sleep or receive mail is functionally defective, just as a unit with mold is defective. The distinction between physical defects and behavioral ones collapses when both are recurring, documented, and foreseeable.
[P1.7] Third, disclosure improves outcomes for everyone. Honest landlords with quiet buildings gain a competitive edge. Tenants can negotiate lower rent, demand security measures, or walk away. Only landlords hiding known problems benefit from silence—and protecting that incentive has no policy justification.
[P1.8] A lease is a contract for shelter. Concealing known defects in that shelter is deception by omission.

"Severe noise," "recurring complaints," and "chronic package theft" are subjective, transient, and largely outside the landlord’s control. Mandating their disclosure creates three fatal flaws.
[C1.2] First, it imposes impossible definitional standards. What constitutes "severe" noise? To a light sleeper, a neighbor walking in heels is severe; to another, it is normal city living. What exactly makes package theft chronic?
[C1.3] By forcing landlords to legally categorize subjective grievances, this policy guarantees a wave of frivolous lawsuits. Landlords will either face ruinous liability for failing to disclose a tenant’s personal annoyance, or they will defensively over-disclose every minor police call, unfairly stigmatizing properties and driving up costs.
[C1.4] Second, it misplaces liability for third-party actions. Landlords are property managers, not police officers. Package theft is a criminal issue driven by opportunistic thieves and delivery logistics, not a structural defect. Short-term rental parties are often illegal precisely because the landlord is actively fighting them.
[C1.5] Forcing landlords to disclose that they are currently battling these issues punishes them for transparency and shifts the burden of public safety onto private property owners who lack the authority to stop it.
[C1.6] Finally, behavioral patterns are not permanent defects. A building with a history of package theft might have just installed a secure smart-locker system. A unit plagued by short-term rentals might have just been cleared out by a new management team. Unlike lead paint, which sits in the walls, behavioral issues fluctuate.
[C1.7] Mandating disclosure of past complaints fossilizes historical grievances, misleading tenants about current conditions while exposing landlords to endless legal traps. Prospective tenants can ask direct questions and visit the property at night; the law should not force landlords to act as guarantors of subjective neighborhood tranquility.
