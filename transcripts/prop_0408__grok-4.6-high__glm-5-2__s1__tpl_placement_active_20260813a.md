[P1.1] A diploma already certifies that a student can write a coherent paragraph, calculate a percentage, and name the branches of government. It does not certify that they can read a W-2, price a car loan, or distinguish a fair credit offer from a trap. That gap is not an accident of scheduling. It is a choice to treat the most expensive adult skills as optional, and then to leave them to family luck and self-selection.
[P1.2] Family is not a curriculum. Some parents walk through interest, taxes, and insurance at the kitchen table. Many cannot. They work nights, repeat their own costly mistakes, or were never taught themselves.
[P1.3] Electives sort the same way. Students with a head start enroll; the ones most likely to sign the worst contracts do not. A graduation requirement is how you stop that sorting. It declares that this knowledge is not enrichment for the already prepared.
[P1.4] A serious course is not a week of slogans about piggy banks. It is working an amortization table, decoding a pay stub, comparing deductibles, and understanding why an employer match is money you forfeit if you ignore it. Those skills are the difference between keeping a first paycheck and watching it disappear into fees, interest, and penalties that compound for years.
[P1.5] We already mandate health, civics, and algebra because some forms of ignorance are too costly to leave private. Financial ignorance belongs in that category. The graduate who cannot budget or file taxes does not fail quietly. They fail into debt markets, collections, and public cost.
[P1.6] One required semester is cheaper than that bill.
[P1.7] Defending electives and family background means defending a system that delivers literacy to the already literate and leaves everyone else to learn from the first eviction notice.

[C1.1] The strongest case for requiring financial literacy assumes the course works: teach students about amortization and deductibles, and they will make better decisions. But classroom financial education has a stubbornly weak record of changing behavior. Students who take these courses score modestly higher on knowledge tests in the short term, but the gains decay within a year or two, and comparisons of actual financial behavior—borrowing, saving, default rates—between those who took courses and those who did not find effects that are small, inconsistent, or absent.
[C1.2] A graduation requirement that produces a temporary bump on a quiz is not the same as teaching someone to avoid a predatory loan at twenty-five.
[C1.3] The reason is structural. Most financial distress is not a knowledge gap. It is an income gap. The person choosing between a payday loan and a missed rent payment usually understands that payday loans are expensive.
[C1.4] They do not have a budgeting deficit; they have a money deficit. No semester of amortization tables changes what you can afford on a low wage. Treating financial hardship as an education problem lets policymakers claim they have addressed inequality while leaving wages, housing costs, and predatory lending untouched.
[C1.5] Meanwhile, a graduation requirement is not free. Adding a mandatory semester forces schools to cut something else—or to stuff financial literacy into an already strained schedule where it competes against the math and reading skills that underpin every financial decision anyway.
[C1.6] The health-class analogy also breaks down. Basic anatomy and civic structure are stable bodies of knowledge. Financial products change every few years. A course designed in 2025 will not prepare a student for the lending landscape of 2032.
[C1.7] Good financial judgment develops through repeated, real-stakes decisions—exactly what a classroom cannot provide.

[C2.1] The "no requirement is perfect" framing sidesteps the actual problem. Algebra requirements produce students who can do algebra. Financial-literacy requirements, by the evidence, produce students who briefly know more about finance and then behave no differently. The gap isn't between perfection and imperfection; it's between a course that works and a course that doesn't.
[C2.2] If gains decay within a year or two, a course taken at seventeen is cold by the time most students face a mortgage, a retirement election, or a major medical bill. The car loan and the lease that arrive immediately are exactly the decisions a teenager makes under parental supervision or with a cosigner—not the high-stakes solo decisions the course is meant to address.
[C2.3] The "two workers, same paycheck" example is telling. PRO has narrowed the benefit to marginal cases: workers who earn the same but make different choices on withholding or employer match. Those differences are real but modest—and they are differences a guidance-counselor session or an online calculator could address at a fraction of the cost of a mandatory semester.
[C2.4] The core problem remains. PRO's requirement is a universal mandate sold on the thinnest slice of benefit. The worker who understands payday loans are predatory but uses one anyway because rent is due does not need amortization tables. And the worker who doesn't understand them can learn more from a five-minute warning on the lender's own disclosure—already required by federal law—than from a forgotten elective.
[C2.5] "Sorted silence" versus an imperfect course is a false choice. The real alternative is targeted, just-in-time intervention that reaches people when the decision is in front of them. A disclosure at the loan counter outperforms a forgotten lecture. Mandating a semester that doesn't change behavior doesn't fix sorting—it just universalizes the ineffective.
