[P1.1] Windfalls from oil, gas, and minerals are not ordinary income. They are the one-time sale of a non-renewable asset that belongs as much to people not yet born as to today's citizens. The right test for managing them is not "how fast can we hand out cash" but "how do we convert depleting wealth into lasting prosperity." Sovereign wealth funds win that test on three grounds: time, stability, and state capacity.
[P1.2] First, intergenerational equity. A country pumping finite reserves today that pays out the full proceeds in cash is spending its children's inheritance on current consumption. A fund that saves a share of windfall revenue and invests it turns exhaustible capital into financial and public capital — infrastructure, education, health — that outlives the wellhead. Norway's Government Pension Fund Global shows the logic: a major oil producer banking its surplus rather than spending it, so resource wealth survives the end of extraction.
[P1.3] Second, macroeconomic stability. Commodity prices swing violently. Direct distributions push those swings into household budgets and aggregate demand: spending surges when prices boom and collapses when they fall, amplifying recession and fueling inflation and currency appreciation. A stabilization fund absorbs the boom and releases revenue in the downturn, insulating both citizens and the budget.
[P1.4] Cash cannot do this — once it is distributed, it cannot be recalled.
[P1.5] Third, state capacity. A fund with a published mandate, independent management, and transparent reporting creates a durable institution and an auditable pool of national wealth. Direct cash transfers create no asset, no investment base, and no capacity to finance development when commodity demand eventually declines.
[P1.6] The counterargument that funds invite elite capture cuts the other way: cash handouts are far easier to manipulate during elections, and the remedy for governance risk is better rules, not distributing the asset away.
