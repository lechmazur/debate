[P1.1] Hungry kids cannot learn. That is not a slogan. It is the operational fact that makes school meals part of the school itself, not a welfare add-on.
[P1.2] Targeting only poorer students fails on its own terms. Eligibility forms, income documentation, and lunch-line sorting keep children from eating even when they qualify. Families in unstable housing or with incomes bouncing around the cutoff do not complete paperwork on schedule. Children refuse the free meal because it marks them.
[P1.3] A targeted program that misses hungry students is not efficient. It is a sieve. When meals were offered to every child in emergency periods, children who had never applied showed up, including those hungry but not officially poor.
[P1.4] Universality treats lunch as what it already is: infrastructure for learning. We do not means-test the blackboard, the fire drill, or the school nurse. We provide them because a classroom only works if every child can participate. Breakfast and lunch belong in that category.
[P1.5] Concentration, attendance, and behavior improve when food is simply there. Those gains accrue to the whole class, not only to the child who ate.
[P1.6] Means-testing also wastes money on administration that could buy food. It creates cliffs: a family a few dollars over the line loses the meal while still being unable to pack a reliable lunch. Near-poor students—the ones targeted programs proudly exclude—are often the ones whose parents work irregular hours and skip breakfast.
[P1.7] Scarce budgets are real. They do not rescue targeting. A smaller program that fails to reach the hungry, humiliates those it reaches, and spends staff time chasing forms is a worse use of public money than a simple, universal tray. The question is not whether poorer children should eat.
[P1.8] It is whether we will keep using poverty paperwork as the gate to a meal that every student needs in order to do the job we already compel them to do.

[C1.7] Emergency expansions revealed children who were hungry and unenrolled.
