[P1.1] A 2 percent target is a convention, not a law of nature. It traces to a New Zealand policy choice around 1990, copied worldwide—never to evidence that 2 percent maximizes welfare. So the real question is narrower than my opponent wants: not whether to fight inflation, but whether it is worth throwing millions out of work to shave the last point or two faster.
[P1.2] The asymmetry is the case. A recession is not a pause button: it destroys firms that never reopen, pushes workers into long-term unemployment that scars earnings for decades, hollows out public finances, and through hysteresis permanently shrinks productive capacity. The Volcker disinflation bought price stability at double-digit unemployment and a double-dip recession. Those losses never came back.
[P1.3] Now weigh the alternative: inflation at 3 or 4 percent for a year or two longer. That cost is diffuse and largely reversible. Wages and contracts catch up; indexed benefits adjust; the distortion to relative prices is modest. No serious evidence shows that expected 3 percent inflation meaningfully damages long-run growth.
[P1.4] The gap between 2 and 3 percent is an aesthetic preference; the gap between a job and no job is a family's life.
[P1.5] CON will lean on credibility—that expectations unanchor if the target slips. We just ran that experiment. In 2021–23 inflation hit levels unseen in decades, yet long-term expectations in the US and euro area stayed near target in bond markets and surveys. Households distinguish a bank on a communicated glide path from one that has lost control.
[P1.6] Credibility rests on honesty about the path, not the speed of the crash.
[P1.7] Finally, the recession tool often cannot fix the inflation at hand. When price pressure comes from energy shocks and broken supply chains, crushing demand drills no oil wells and unblocks no ports; it just adds joblessness to the queue.
[P1.8] The standard is welfare. Moderate inflation erodes purchasing power that partly recovers; an engineered recession destroys livelihoods that never do.
