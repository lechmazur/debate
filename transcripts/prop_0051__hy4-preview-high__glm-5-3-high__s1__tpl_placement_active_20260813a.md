[P1.1] Ultra-cheap cross-border fashion is cheap only because the bill is sent elsewhere. A $5 dress shipped straight from a hidden factory is cheap because the marketplace stands between millions of buyers and thousands of suppliers, taking data and fees while disclaiming responsibility for what is made and whether it is safe. The case turns on one fact: marketplaces are the only actor that can see the whole chain, so they are the only realistic place to impose product-safety and labour-transparency duties.
[P1.2] Safety comes first. Small parcels routinely bypass the inspections given to containers. A domestic seller must meet flammability and chemical rules; an offshore seller on the same marketplace can mail untreated synthetic clothing straight to a child’s bedroom. If a costume is flammable, or finished with restricted dyes or metals, no warning arrives with it.
[P1.3] Obligations would force platforms to verify standards, keep records, and delist unsafe goods. Slower delivery is a fair price for preventing a burn or a poisoning.
[P1.4] Labour works the same way. The model depends on opaque subcontracting: a listing tells you nothing about the factory, hours, or pay. Shoppers cannot audit a chain they cannot see, and customs cannot police millions of tiny parcels. [P1.5] Transparency is not perfection; it is the first step toward enforcement and real choice.
[P1.6] The trade-off is real, but smaller than the defence of ultra-cheap shopping admits. Rules hit the ultra-cheap, non-compliant margin, not all cross-border shopping. Clothing stays affordable; it just stops being priced below the cost of lawful production. [C1.4] Second, safety law already applies. And intermediaries react predictably: they de-risk by delisting thousands of small suppliers wholesale.
[C1.5] The survivors are the large, capitalized factories. You don't empower workers by handing giants a regulatory moat.
[C1.6] Third, transparency mandates produce paperwork, not pay rises. The consumer gets a badge; the homeworker's wage doesn't move. Meanwhile the genuine gains of the model—supplier access for small producers in developing economies, competition that disciplines retail margins—get quietly traded away for the feeling of visibility.
[C1.7] The proposition concedes the trade-off is real. That concession is fatal, because the promised benefits are speculative and the costs are certain: higher prices, fewer sellers, concentrated power, and regressive harm. Slow, expensive, and unaccountable is not an improvement.
