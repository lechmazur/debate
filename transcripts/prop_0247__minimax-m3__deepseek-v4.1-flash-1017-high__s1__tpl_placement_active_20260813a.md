[P1.1] Sugar-sweetened beverages should carry a tax large enough to change purchasing decisions, because their private price ignores costs that the public often pays: obesity, type 2 diabetes, and dental treatment, with children especially likely to consume them. A modest levy that merely raises a few cents is unlikely to move behavior; a meaningful rate changes the price of the sugary option, encourages water or unsweetened alternatives, and gives manufacturers an incentive to reduce sugar.
[P1.2] The strongest objection is that the tax is regressive. But that objection confuses who initially pays a tax with who ultimately bears its cost. Lower-income households may indeed spend a larger share of their income on these drinks. They need not, however, bear that cost net of government revenue.
[P1.3] The money can finance free drinking water in schools and communities, healthier school meals, dental care, and targeted rebates for low-income consumers. If the revenue compensates affected households dollar for dollar, the policy can convert a regressive payment into a progressive transfer while still reducing consumption. Even if some compensation is imperfect, the benefits of less sugar-related illness accrue across society, not only to the drinker.
[P1.4] This framing also answers the concern that people will simply buy another unhealthy product. The relevant substitution is toward water, milk, or unsweetened drinks; if some calories are replaced elsewhere, that weakens but does not erase the health gain. A narrowly defined beverage tax is also more administratively straightforward than regulating an entire diet, and it creates immediate pressure where voluntary education has not.
[P1.5] The proper standard is therefore not whether every household pays the same share. It is whether the tax meaningfully reduces harmful consumption and whether government can use its revenue to protect those with less income. A meaningful tax advances both goals. Refusing it on regressive grounds would protect equal pricing while leaving the public to absorb the health costs of continued consumption.

[C1.1] The motion does not ask for a modest levy or a pilot. It asks for a rate high enough to meaningfully shift consumption, and it explicitly accepts that the burden will fall disproportionately on lower-income households. That is not an unfortunate detail; it is the mechanism. To change behavior at the till, the price increase must be large enough to matter.
[C1.2] For a household choosing between milk and soda, or between soda and nothing, a large increase is a real budget shock. The tax works by making everyday purchases harder for people with the least slack.
[C1.3] The familiar answer is revenue recycling: use the money for rebates, water, schools, dental care. But the proposition as written does not guarantee any of that. A tax is collected automatically; compensation depends on budgets, eligibility rules, paperwork, and political priorities. Rebates can be delayed, incomplete, or redirected.
[C1.4] The claim that the cost need not be borne net of government revenue is a conditional promise, not the policy being defended. If the case only works when every dollar is perfectly returned, it has not defended the tax as written—it has defended an ideal package that may never exist.
[C1.5] Even with a rebate, the household faces higher prices first and compensation later, and many lower-income households are poorly placed to bridge that gap. The tax can also push people toward other cheap calories, cross-border purchases, or home mixing; then the health gain is smaller than advertised while the burden is immediate. A tax high enough to “meaningfully shift” consumption is high enough to create these distortions.
[C1.6] If the aim is less sugar-related harm, targeted school standards, clean water access, limits on marketing to children, and direct support for low-income families can improve diets without treating the weekly shop as a revenue source. The proposition’s own test—meaningful reduction—does not require a regressive price shock. It requires a policy that helps people change, not one that makes change compulsory by making the unhealthy option unaffordable.

The proposition does not depend on perfect rebates: without them, revenue remains available to offset part of the health burden, while gains from reduced illness accrue beyond the purchaser. A legislature can earmark revenue for automatic per-person payments, so every resident receives the same cash amount while beverage prices remain higher. Lower-income households may pay a larger percentage of income, but compensation can offset much of that payment; disproportionate gross incidence need not determine the final distribution of costs.
[P2.2] Crucially, rebates do not reverse the price signal. Sugary drinks still cost more, but the lowest-income households no longer finance the correction alone.
[P2.3] The objection that higher prices come now and compensation later is not inherent. Automatic payments avoid application paperwork and a liquidity gap. Political failure is a generic objection to any program, not proof that government cannot enact the tax with revenue rules attached.
[P2.4] The opponent says meaningful reduction need not involve a price shock. That misunderstands the motion and its mechanism. This is not a prohibition or a claim that soda must become unaffordable. Consumers remain free to buy it; government stops treating sugar-related harms as costless.
[P2.5] School standards and marketing rules can complement the tax, but they do not change the price confronting shoppers or finance the care imposed by sugary consumption. They are complements, not decisive substitutes.
[P2.6] Finally, substitution toward another caloric drink, home mixing, or cross-border buying would reduce, not necessarily erase, the health gain. It does not erase meaningful displacement of sugary beverages when water and unsweetened alternatives become more attractive and producers face pressure to reformulate. The relevant comparison is not a flawless tax versus no response, but a policy that changes prices, raises revenue, and reduces harm against externalized costs. The opponent has shown that poor compensation would be inadequate, not that the proposition should be rejected.

It is a transparent price signal with measurable effects, revenue, and reformulation incentives against voluntary guidance and targeted programs that leave sugary drinks’ price unchanged. School standards and water access help, but leave the drink’s price untouched; they complement rather than replace the tax’s mechanism. Once the tax produces a sustained reduction, opponents cannot make every imperfection decisive.
