[P1.1] The housing crisis is not an abstraction. It is a teacher commuting two hours because she cannot afford to live near her school, a family of five in a one-bedroom apartment, a generation locked out of homeownership entirely. When historic-district protections stand between people and homes, cities must choose people. That is the whole case, and everything else is detail.
[P1.2] Notice what the motion actually asks. Not to abolish preservation, not to bulldoze landmarks indiscriminately—only to relax protections *when they block needed density*, accepting that some architecturally significant buildings will be lost. That is a modest, conditional trade, and it is one cities already make constantly in the other direction: we routinely sacrifice affordability, opportunity, and racial integration to preserve façades.
[P1.3] The scale of the problem is the key. In cities like San Francisco, Washington, and much of central London, historic and conservation districts blanket enormous swaths of precisely the land where demand is highest—walkable, transit-rich, job-adjacent neighborhoods. Preservation has metastasized from protecting genuine landmarks into a general-purpose veto. Entire districts of ordinary rowhouses get frozen at nineteenth-century densities, and every proposed apartment building faces years of review, redesign, and litigation.
[P1.4] The predictable result: scarcity, soaring rents, and displacement of the very working-class communities whose history preservationists claim to honor.
[P1.5] There is also a moral asymmetry worth naming. A building's aesthetic value accrues mostly to those who already live nearby—typically wealthier, older homeowners whose property values the freeze protects. The cost falls on renters, newcomers, and the young, who have no vote in the historic commission's hearing room. Preservation as currently practiced is regressive redistribution dressed in heritage language.
[P1.6] Yes, some beautiful buildings will fall. We can mourn that honestly while recognizing that cities are living things, not museums. Paris, Rome, and Boston were all built by demolishing what came before. A city that cannot house its people has failed at its first purpose, however lovely its cornices.
