[P1.1] Broad bans on generative AI fail a basic test of policy: schools cannot enforce a prohibition they cannot verify. On take-home assignments, AI-generated text and code are easy to produce and trivial to disguise. Automated detectors routinely misfire, flagging formulaic or non-native writers for misconduct while missing students who lightly edit machine outputs. Blanket bans therefore do not eliminate AI use; they merely drive it underground.
[P1.2] Teachers get trapped playing detective, honest students risk false accusations, and covert rule-breakers gain an unfair grade advantage over peers who follow the rules.
[P1.3] Treating AI use as permissible by default, conditioned on citation and process transparency, replaces futile policing with visible learning. Under this baseline, a student using AI on an essay or coding assignment cannot quietly paste a finished answer. They must cite the model, preserve version history or key prompts, and briefly explain how they verified claims, corrected errors, and shaped the final work. That requirement transforms assessment for the better.
[P1.4] Instead of grading prose fluency that software can now mimic for free, teachers evaluate human judgment: whether the student caught hallucinations, integrated specific course concepts, and improved upon raw machine output. Submitting unedited AI work becomes an obvious failure of contribution rather than a hidden shortcut.
[P1.5] Meanwhile, "permissible by default" sets a workable rule for everyday coursework while still allowing teachers to designate specific proctored, in-class assessments when measuring unassisted foundational skills.
[P1.6] Finally, regulated openness serves both equity and real-world readiness. Affluent students already receive unpenalized brainstorming, outlining, and editing support from private tutors and parents. Default permission gives every student access to an on-demand sounding board while bringing outside help into the open where educators can guide it. Beyond school, modern workplaces and universities expect graduates to direct, audit, and take responsibility for AI-assisted work.
[P1.7] Broad bans teach students either professional unpreparedness or covert dishonesty. Requiring citation and transparent process teaches them critical judgment and intellectual accountability.
