[P1.1] Japan and South Korea face the same missile in the same sky. North Korean launches overfly Japanese territory and fall near Korean waters; Chinese and Russian aircraft and ships probe both; cyberattacks hit both. [P1.2] Historical grievances are real: forced labor, comfort women, territorial disputes. But the motion does not ask either society to forget. [P1.3] Joint command structures need not mean one army under the other’s flag. That is sovereignty-preserving coordination, and it is exactly what five-minute warning times demand.
[P1.4] The alternative is not moral purity; it is dangerous improvisation. If Japan and South Korea each see only part of a North Korean launch, or if one delays data during a diplomatic spat, the US hub must patch the gap under pressure. That invites miscalculation, not reconciliation.
[P1.5] The confident objection is that trust must come before institutions. History shows the reverse: distrust is precisely why institutions matter. It also gives both governments domestic cover: they are not submitting to each other; they are following transparent rules.
[P1.6] Deepened security cooperation will not solve historical grievances. It will prevent those grievances from becoming a vulnerability that Pyongyang, Beijing, or Moscow can exploit. The choice is not history versus security. It is whether history gets a veto over survival.
