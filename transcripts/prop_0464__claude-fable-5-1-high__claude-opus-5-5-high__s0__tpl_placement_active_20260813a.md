[P1.1] The UN Security Council exists for one purpose: to act collectively against threats to peace. The veto guarantees it cannot do so exactly when the threat comes from, or is shielded by, one of five governments. That is not a flaw at the edges. It is a design that fails precisely in the cases that matter most.
[P1.2] Consider what this looks like in practice. Russia invades Ukraine and then vetoes the resolution condemning the invasion. For a decade, resolutions on Syrian civilians died at the hands of Damascus's patron. Washington has repeatedly shielded allies from even mild censure.
[P1.3] In each case, the accused sat as judge. A fire department that cannot respond when the arsonist is on the board is not a compromised fire department; it is no fire department at all.
[P1.4] The central objection will be the threat of withdrawal: abolish the veto and the great powers walk, leaving a hollow shell. Take that seriously, and it still loses. First, the threat is largely a bluff. The permanent members stay because the Council confers legitimacy they cannot manufacture alone.
[P1.5] Russia remained after the General Assembly overwhelmingly condemned it; the United States never left institutions that ruled against it. Powers exit clubs that constrain them only when the club offers nothing, and the Council offers a great deal. Second, if a member did walk, we would lose a chair that was already empty when it counted. The veto did not stop Iraq in 2003 or Ukraine in 2022.
[P1.6] It only stopped everyone else from responding.
[P1.7] Weigh the two worlds. Keeping the veto buys a Council whose membership is intact and whose record on great-power aggression is near-total paralysis. Abolishing it risks a Council that might lose a member but can finally speak and act on the gravest cases. Membership is not the goal; protecting people from aggression is.
[P1.8] An institution that can be held hostage by the parties it judges has already been captured. Yielding to withdrawal threats simply confirms who owns it.
