[P1.1] Ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks drive a preventable global public health crisis, causing millions of deaths annually from diabetes, heart disease, and obesity, and saddling taxpayers with trillions in avoidable healthcare costs. [P1.2] These harms fall disproportionately on low-income families who have far less access to affordable, unprocessed healthy alternatives, making reliance on vague "choice" campaigns not just ineffective but deeply unfair. Requiring prominent warning labels is a minimal, evidence-backed intervention that fixes a broken information market, reduces preventable disease, and makes the promise of consumer choice a reality instead of an industry-funded myth.

[C1.1] The PRO case for mandatory warning labels rests on a seductive but flawed assumption: that slapping a black stop-sign on a bag of chips solves a public health crisis. It doesn't. It creates new problems while failing to address the root causes of poor nutrition.
[C1.2] First, warning labels fundamentally mislead consumers by reducing complex nutritional science to binary "good" versus "bad" judgments. A frozen vegetable medley with added sodium for preservation would carry the same warning as a candy bar, training consumers to ignore the labels entirely—the exact phenomenon we've seen with excessive allergen warnings that have lost all persuasive power. When everything is flagged, nothing is.
[C1.3] Second, the Chile evidence PRO cites conceals critical tradeoffs. Researchers documented significant substitution effects: consumers switched from labeled products to unlabeled alternatives that were often equally caloric or nutritionally poor. A warning label on soda doesn't help if someone grabs an unlabeled fruit drink with comparable sugar content. This is policy theater, not public health.
[C1.4] Third, mandatory labeling regimes impose substantial compliance costs that fall hardest on smaller producers and regional food companies—the very entities often offering healthier alternatives to industrial conglomerate products. This entrenches market dominance by the largest food corporations best positioned to absorb regulatory burdens.
[C1.5] Most fundamentally, PRO frames consumer choice campaigns as "industry-funded myths," but this ignores the real successes of sustained public health education. Smoking rates plummeted over decades through education and cultural change, not warning labels alone. The PRO approach treats citizens as passive subjects requiring government intervention rather than as agents capable of learning and adapting. Consumer choice campaigns build long-term nutritional literacy; warning labels build label fatigue.
[C1.6] The question isn't whether to address nutrition-related disease, but how. PRO offers a quick-fix illusion that lets politicians claim action while avoiding the harder work of education, access, and cultural change.
