[P1.1] The core problem is simple: universities make a promise and bear none of the risk when that promise fails. They market a degree, set the tuition, and pocket the money up front. If the graduate can't earn enough to repay, the student eats the debt and the taxpayer eats the default. The one party with the most control over program quality and price—the school—walks away untouched.
[P1.2] That is a broken incentive structure, and the motion fixes it.
[P1.3] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.4] First, accountability follows control. Universities decide which programs to offer, how many students to enroll, what to charge, and what to promise in their brochures. When an actor controls those levers but faces zero consequences for bad outcomes, you get exactly what we see: programs that cost forty thousand dollars and reliably produce graduates earning poverty wages. "Skin in the game" is not punishment; it is aligning the decision-maker with the consequences of the decision.
[P1.5] Second, this targets the right cases. The motion is narrow and deliberate. Not one unlucky graduate—"repeatedly" and "poor earnings relative to debt." We are talking about programs that fail cohort after cohort, where the pattern is the institution's design, not a student's bad luck.
[P1.6] A school that consistently leaves people worse off should help cover the wreckage its own model produced.
[P1.7] Third, partial cost-sharing changes behavior before defaults ever happen. Once a college knows it pays a slice of failed loans, it will trim predatory programs, cut runaway tuition, invest in placement, and stop over-enrolling fields with no labor demand. The point isn't to collect penalties—it's to make those penalties unnecessary by improving programs. The students protected most are the future ones who never get steered into a debt trap.
[P1.8] The status quo socializes the losses and privatizes the tuition revenue. My opponent must defend a system where the only party powerless to fix the problem—the eighteen-year-old borrower—carries all the risk. I'd rather put the risk where the power already is.

[C1.2] Three reasons this fails.
[C1.3] First, the metric is unworkable. "Poor earnings relative to debt" sounds clean. It isn't. Whose earnings—first year or tenth?
[C1.4] Median or mean? Adjusted for region and cost of living? How many cohorts count as "repeatedly"—three, five? Who decides—the Department of Education, a courtroom, an accreditor?
[C1.5] Every honest answer produces a different rule, and every rule invites litigation before a single dollar moves. We would build a bureaucracy to measure misery, not a system to reduce it.
[C1.6] Second, the incentives cut the wrong way. Once a school pays when graduates struggle, it solves the problem by avoiding the people who struggle. First-generation students, low-income applicants, single parents, returning adults—precisely the borrowers who most need a path—become admissions risks the school is paid to refuse. A rural teaching program that produces modest earners but enormous social value gets penalized; a suburban pre-med track gets rewarded for being suburban.
[C1.7] The motion doesn't punish bad programs. It punishes good schools serving the students we claim to protect.
[C1.8] Third, the motion confuses authorship of the outcome. Universities design programs and set prices—true. But they don't control the labor market, the family's contribution, the borrower's choices, or the macroeconomy. A 2019 graduate earning less because 2020 was a pandemic isn't the school's design.
[C1.9] A theater major defaulting because Hollywood collapsed isn't either. The skin-in-the-game argument assumes the institution alone writes the outcome. It doesn't.
[C1.10] Better tools exist: income-share agreements, restored bankruptcy for student loans, better pre-enrollment counseling, tighter accreditation on completion outcomes. Each targets a real failure without making schools flee the students most exposed to it. Good intentions are not a substitute for good policy, and "the school should pay" is a slogan, not a solution.
