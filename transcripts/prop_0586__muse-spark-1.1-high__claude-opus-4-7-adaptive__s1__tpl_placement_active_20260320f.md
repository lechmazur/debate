[P1.1] A single national minimum wage is the bluntest tool in labor policy. A large jump - say from $7.25 to $17 federally - means a 30% labor cost shock in San Francisco but a 100%+ shock in Mississippi diners, rural nursing homes, and small retail. That uniformity is what economics predicts will destroy jobs. Sectoral boards do the same job surgically.
[P1.2] A wage board sets a floor by industry, occupation, and region after hearing workers, employers, and public members. Fast food in California can sustain $20 because of high prices, low turnover savings, and concentrated franchisors. Hotel housekeepers in Las Vegas face different margins than home care aides in Alabama. Boards can set higher where monopsony and profits are high, lower or slower where ability to pay is limited, and phase in with productivity offsets.
[P1.3] That tailoring directly contains employment loss while still lifting pay.
[P1.4] It also raises pay more effectively. A minimum only lifts the bottom rung. A sectoral standard can set wage scales, premium pay for difficult shifts, predictable scheduling penalties, and training ladders. That lifts everyone below median, not just the minimum-bound worker, and reduces churn that makes low-wage jobs costly.
[P1.5] Most importantly, it changes how firms compete. Under a flat hike, the easiest way to comply is cut hours. Under sectoral bargaining, when every fast-food brand must pay the same higher standard, you cannot undercut by paying less - you compete on productivity, service, and retention. Evidence from New York's fast-food wage board and California's Fast Food Council shows pay rising 20-30% without the employment cliff predicted for a blanket increase, because the standard applies evenly to all competitors.
[P1.6] Large across-the-board increases force you to choose between ignoring low-cost labor markets or crushing them. Wage boards let America raise low-wage pay because they ask the right question: what can this sector in this place actually afford, and what standards will keep workers instead of churning them?

[C1.4] **Boards import new distortions.** When a board sets wages only in fast food, employers reclassify jobs, shift to franchisee-of-record structures, automate kiosks, or push work to uncovered "fast casual." Boards also invite regulatory capture: incumbent chains help write rules that raise rivals' costs and choke entry, which harms workers through fewer job openings.
[C1.5] **And the legal reality:** US labor law (NLRA preemption, no Ghent system, no employer associations with bargaining authority) means "sectoral bargaining" here is really just a politically-appointed wage panel. That is a minimum wage with extra steps - and extra lobbying.
[C1.6] The right question is not "can a perfect board beat a terrible minimum wage?" It is which tool actually delivers. Minimum wages do; sectoral boards, in the American institutional context, do not.
