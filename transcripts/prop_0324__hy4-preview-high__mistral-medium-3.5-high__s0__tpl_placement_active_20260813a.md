[P1.1] Place identity is not a logo, a slogan, or a spike in hotel bookings. It is the durable, practiced sense that a location is someone's home and no one else's. If that is the goal, public institutions should default to creator-led local festivals and recurring community events, because identity is built by repetition and authorship, not by a single spectacle.
[P1.2] Start with time. Identity requires a calendar, not a countdown. A creator-led local festival or a monthly makers market can turn an empty lot into 'the market lot' or 'the festival green': after a decade, residents plan their Saturdays around it, children inherit it, and the space carries meaning that a one-off ribbon-cutting never will. A one-off prestige event produces a memory, sometimes a landmark, but memories without recurrence are nostalgia, not identity.
[P1.3] Even a leftover stadium or tower becomes 'ours' only if recurring local life adopts it; otherwise it is just infrastructure with a naming-rights clock. The street closed every summer for a neighborhood music night is 'our street.'
[P1.4] Second, authorship matters. Creator-led events let local chefs, musicians, makers, and organizers put their own idiosyncrasies on display. That specificity is what makes a place feel irreplaceable: the particular soup, the local language, the weird parade no consultant would approve. Mega-events are templated by sponsors and global consultants; they export a postcard image and ask residents to consume it.
[P1.5] Spectators do not become attached to brand activations; authors defend what they made.
[P1.6] Third, recurrence builds social infrastructure. Recurring events need volunteers, small vendors, neighborhood associations, and repeat partnerships. They knit civil society and keep money circulating locally. Mega-events often do the opposite: they securitize public space, displace informal users, and concentrate benefits in a short window before the crowd leaves.
[P1.7] A mega-event may raise a place's profile, but profile is not identity. Prefer means default, not never. On the metric that matters—durable, distinctive, resident-owned belonging—the recurring local event wins.

Identity isn’t just what locals feel; it’s how a place is perceived globally.
[C1.5] The durable belonging PRO prizes often flows from the pride of hosting the world, not from weekly markets many residents may never attend.
