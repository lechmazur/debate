[P1.1] A purchased digital ticket is property. When a fan buys a ticket, they acquire the right to attend—and that right carries the basic incidents of ownership, including the ability to transfer it. Restrictions on peer-to-peer transfer are restraints on alienation that primarily serve the business model of primary ticket sellers, not the interests of fans.
[P1.2] Consider the ordinary unpredictability of life. A fan purchases tickets months in advance. Then a family emergency arises, a work conflict emerges, or a friend unexpectedly becomes available. Under current restrictive regimes—where mobile tickets are locked to specific accounts and non-transferable—that ticket becomes worthless.
[P1.3] The fan cannot give it to a friend or resell it at cost. Instead, they are forced to absorb the loss or return to the primary seller's official resale platform, where prices often exceed face value. This is not consumer protection; it is captive-market economics that allows primary sellers to capture the entire secondary market.
[P1.4] The proposition includes a crucial qualifier: organizers may impose narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits. These can include face-value caps, identity verification, purchase quantity limits, and bot detection. Such measures directly target industrial-scale profiteering—the actual scalping that harms fans—while preserving the legitimate right to transfer tickets between individuals. The distinction is clear: stopping a scalper from hoarding fifty tickets at markup is different from preventing a fan from selling one ticket at cost when plans change.
[P1.5] Digital technology makes this balance achievable. QR codes, blockchain verification, and centralized databases allow organizers to track provenance and enforce transfer restrictions without banning peer-to-peer exchange. The infrastructure exists to distinguish legitimate fan-to-fan transfer from speculative abuse.
[P1.6] A legal right to transfer, subject to reasonable anti-scalping guardrails, restores fairness to a market currently rigged toward primary sellers. It respects fan autonomy while providing organizers with targeted tools to prevent genuine exploitation.

[C1.1] The core flaw in PRO’s case is its false premise that a digital ticket is full, alienable property. A ticket is a revocable, conditional license to attend a specific event on the organizer’s terms, not a fee simple interest in the event itself. Transfer restrictions are not arbitrary restraints on alienation—they are standard terms of service fans agree to at purchase, designed to protect event safety, accessibility, and fair access for all attendees.
[C1.2] The “narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits” PRO relies on are unworkable for peer-to-peer transfers. There is no feasible way to enforce face-value caps, purchase limits, or bot detection on transfers that happen off-organizer platforms, via social media, cash, or peer payment apps. The only mechanism to enforce transfer rules on entry is mandatory ID matching, which bars all legitimate transfers (a fan giving a ticket to a parent with a different legal name, for example) while doing nothing to stop scalpers who use fake IDs or mules to bypass checks.
[C1.3] The legal standard PRO proposes offers no real guardrail, only a fig leaf.
[C1.4] Worse, the proposed legal right would preempt existing fan protections and hand scalpers a new weapon. More than 20 U.S. states already have scalping laws that limit markups or ban resale entirely for high-demand events. This proposition would invalidate those rules unless organizers impose their own limits—but organizers often profit directly from secondary market scalping via official resale platforms that take 10-15% commissions, giving them no incentive to enforce strict rules. During the 2023 Eras Tour, for example, primary sellers and official resale platforms made hundreds of millions in markup fees when initial sales crashed, and no effective transfer limits were imposed.
[C1.5] A legal peer-to-peer transfer right would have let scalpers operate with zero accountability, pricing out ordinary fans at events that are supposed to be accessible to all.
