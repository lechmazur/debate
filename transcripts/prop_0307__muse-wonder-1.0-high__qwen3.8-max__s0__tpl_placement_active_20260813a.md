[P1.1] Elder care should start with a different question: not 'where is a bed?' but 'what would it take to keep this person safely at home?' That reversal protects autonomy, health, and public money.
[P1.2] First, home preserves the person. An older adult at home keeps their routines, neighbours, pets, privacy, and control over when to eat, sleep, bathe, and receive visitors. In an institution those ordinary choices are replaced by shifts, menus, and visiting hours. That loss is not sentimental.
[P1.3] Loss of control is linked to depression, withdrawal, and faster cognitive and physical decline, because people move and engage less. Keeping someone in a familiar environment also helps with dementia: familiar rooms, photographs, and streets reduce confusion in a way a new ward cannot replicate.
[P1.4] Second, targeted home support can meet most needs more precisely. Most care is not continuous intensive nursing; it is help with meals, medication, dressing, cleaning, transport, and a few hours of supervision. Visiting nurses, home aides, physiotherapy, telehealth check-ins, grab rails, and respite for family carers can be scaled up or down as needs change. An institutional place, by contrast, bundles housing, food, and round-the-clock staffing whether a person needs them or not — which is why it is typically far more expensive per person, and why infectious outbreaks, falls in unfamiliar surroundings, and understaffed nights hit so many residents at once.
[P1.5] Third, supporting home care supports carers instead of discarding them. Paid carer allowances, training, and regular respite keep families involved without burning them out, and keep older people woven into community life rather than removed from it.
[P1.6] None of this denies a role for residential care when needs become intensive, the home is unsafe, or no support network exists. The motion says 'when possible.' When it is possible, we should fund the rails, visits, and respite that make home work — not pay first to uproot the person.

[C1.1] The dangerous word in this motion is “possible.” A frail person can often remain at home in the bare physical sense: a bed, a bathroom, a meal delivery, an aide who comes for thirty minutes. That is not the same as being cared for safely. The central question in elder care is not where the person sleeps, but whether the system can respond at 2 a.m. when she falls, becomes confused, misses medication, or begins wandering. Home care, however well intended, is intermittent.
[C1.2] It creates gaps between visits, and those gaps are where crises happen.
[C1.3] The alternative is not institutions as the only answer. The error is making home care the default organizing principle, because that substitutes a location for a standard of care. Residential facilities can provide continuous supervision, rapid response, medication management, rehabilitation, and social contact under one roof. For people with advanced dementia, recurrent falls, incontinence, or no reliable family network, that continuity is not a luxury; it is protection.
[C1.4] A rotating set of aides cannot reproduce it.
[C1.5] The PRO case romanticizes autonomy while outsourcing risk. Home care often means family care—usually daughters—without adequate pay, training, or sleep. It also deepens inequality: wealthy households can hire round-the-clock help; poorer households patch together scraps of public support. If the state says “home first whenever possible,” the practical meaning is often “cheapest placement first.” The burden falls on women, low-wage workers, and older adults left alone between visits.
[C1.6] Institutions have real problems, but they are also visible, regulatable, and staffable in ways dispersed homes are not. Abuse, neglect, and isolation are easier to hide in private homes. A good elder-care system should not start from a presumption that home is always better. It should start from assessed need and provide residential care as a legitimate, adequately funded option when continuous care is required.

At night, institutions may have few staff for dozens of residents; bells go unanswered, people head to the bathroom alone anyway, and fall in unfamiliar rooms. A home package can target the actual risk: an overnight carer for a wanderer, sensors and alarms, medication dispensers, scheduled night checks.
[P2.3] If someone needs constant hands-on supervision that cannot be provided safely at home — advanced wandering dementia, repeated injurious falls — that is not a possible case, and residential care remains. My opponent only wins by defeating cases the motion excludes.

The motion is to organise around support: allowances, training, respite and paid aides. [P2.5] Visibility cuts both ways too. A locked ward can conceal neglect at scale among residents dependent on the same staff and afraid to complain. A supported home has nurses, aides, doctors, family and neighbours crossing the threshold — more independent eyes, not fewer.
[P2.6] Assessed need is not the alternative; it is how “possible” is judged. After assessment, do we build help around the person, or uproot the person to fit the institution? When home can work with help, help should come first.

[C2.1] PRO redefines “possible” as “safely supported,” but that turns the motion into a tautology and hides its operative force. The debate is not whether safe home care is good; it is whether the system should begin with the home and make institutions the fallback. That ordering matters because budgets, eligibility rules, and political pressure will always stretch “possible” toward cheaper, less intensive arrangements. Systems fund minimum visits, not the ideal package PRO describes.
[C2.2] Their answer to the 2 a.m. fall is sensors, alarms, and scheduled checks [P2.2]. But an alarm is not a responder. A sensor cannot lift someone, assess chest pain, manage acute confusion, or decide whether hospital is needed. If the honest answer is an overnight carer plus continuous monitoring plus rapid backup, PRO is trying to rebuild institutional staffing in scattered private houses—often at higher cost, with weaker supervision, and no second nurse when the lone aide is overwhelmed.
[C2.3] They also claim the motion excludes hard cases [P2.3]. In practice, the boundary is blurry. Many older adults are not obviously “impossible” until the third fall, the medication error, the night they wander out, or the family carer collapses. A home-first default puts the burden on families to prove failure before adequate placement is offered.
[C2.4] That produces delay, crisis-driven transfers, and avoidable harm.
[C2.5] On visibility, PRO says many eyes cross the threshold [P2.5]. But short visits are not surveillance. A private home can isolate an older person from neighbours, hide financial coercion, or make abuse by relatives or lone aides hard to report. A regulated facility has records, rosters, clinical oversight, and colleagues who can notice patterns.
[C2.6] The real clash is this: PRO treats location as the organizing principle. We should treat reliable response capacity as the organizing principle. When continuous presence is required, it should be provided directly—not simulated by gadgets and intermittent visits. The motion makes the home the test; we make need the test.

PRO’s safety valve proves the problem: home is “not possible” only if the package cannot be delivered, and escalation comes after a fall, hospital visit, or carer strain. That is not preventing crisis; it is making older people and families absorb the first harms before residential care becomes legitimate.
[C5.2] PRO asks what must justify itself, support or uprooting. But the real default should be response capacity, not location.
