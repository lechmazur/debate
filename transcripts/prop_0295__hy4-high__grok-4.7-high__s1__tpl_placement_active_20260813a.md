[P1.1] Restitution ending at the loading dock confuses the return of a thing with the repair of a taking. Colonial powers did not merely remove objects; they severed them from systems of custody, interpretation, local expertise, and record-keeping, and built museums and scholarship around the loss. Shipping a crate back reverses possession. It does not restore the conditions under which the object can be safe, documented, studied, and publicly meaningful.
[P1.2] Funding conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity is therefore part of restitution, not a gift after it. Textiles, manuscripts, wood, metal, and leather need climate control, mounts, pest management, trained conservators, and emergency plans. If the receiving museum lacks a lab or reliable power, the former colonial power has not returned heritage into safety; it has transferred a preservation deficit and declared the account closed.
[P1.3] Provenance makes the duty sharper. The papers explaining seizure, coerced sale, excavation, renaming, or separation from related pieces are often in the former imperial capital, in colonial languages and filing systems. Those archives and expertise belong to the object’s history. A return that keeps the evidentiary keys or refuses to fund archival reconstruction leaves the dispossessed society paying to complete the record of its own dispossession.
[P1.4] The objection that money insults sovereignty gets the design wrong. Funds should be unconditional, locally directed, and accompanied by open archival access and training chosen locally. That is less paternalistic than a shipping-only rule, which lets wealthy museums declare moral completion at the cheapest moment while the receiving country inherits conservation bills, missing documentation, and public expectations it did not create. Benefits accumulated in the metropole; costs are exported outward.
[P1.5] Return should restore capacity as well as title. Former colonial powers built institutional value from colonial collections; they owe the practical means for returned artifacts to live as heritage, not arrive as liabilities. Otherwise restitution is not complete. It is restitution invoiced to the victim.

[C1.1] Restitution undoes a wrongful holding: title, possession, and the records that belong with the object. It is not an open invoice for another country's museums. Once the artifact returns and its documentation travels with it, the taking is reversed. Calling that moment incomplete converts a finite remedy into permanent patronage.
[C1.2] Colonial powers did not ship away a nation's capacity to keep its own heritage. States build museums because they choose to. Many countries receiving returns already run national museums, universities, and conservation programs. To call a return unsafe unless the former colonizer pays the light bill is to treat owners as wards.
[C1.3] That framing also delays justice. If restitution stays unfinished until conservation, provenance research, and museum capacity are funded, every return becomes an aid negotiation. Legislatures do not write blank checks, and "unconditional, locally directed" money will not survive appropriation and audit. Packages arrive with standards and soft vetoes, or they do not arrive.
[C1.4] The claim that a shipping rule merely exports costs runs backward: an open funding duty gives holding museums a reason to wait and every future parliament a liability that never closes. Objects come home when the duty is finite.
[C1.5] Papers sitting in a former capital should be digitized and released, not used to justify a standing research budget. Archives should be copied, opened, and transferred with the object. It is not a duty to endow research departments or train conservators indefinitely. Access is restitution.
[C1.6] The rest is subsidy, and calling subsidy unfinished restitution lets the bill escape every limit a property return should have.
[C1.7] There is no stopping rule. If climate control is owed, so are labs, generators, new wings, and the next generation of conservators. Reparations, if sought, are a separate political question. Restitution is complete when the dispossessed hold what was taken, free of a benefactor.
[C1.8] Beyond that lies cooperation between equals, not the unpaid balance of a crate.
