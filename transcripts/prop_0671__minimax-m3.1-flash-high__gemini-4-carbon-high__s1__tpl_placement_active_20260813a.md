[P1.1] Early grades should be device-light because the foundational skills children are building are not primarily digital skills. Children learning to read, write, calculate, explain, and collaborate still need substantial time with hands-on materials, paper, speech, and sustained face-to-face interaction. A tablet can display a worksheet, but it cannot replace the motor practice involved in forming letters, the spatial reasoning developed by arranging mathematical manipulatives, or the rapid social negotiation that occurs when children solve a problem together.
[P1.2] The strongest case for devices is pedagogical, not technological. Schools should ask where a screen adds genuine value and where it merely adds a screen. In early grades, paper often supports memory and attention better: students write, sketches, annotates, and revises without notification icons, menus, or competing media competing for attention. This is especially important for children who are still developing executive control; the learning task should be demanding, not the device.
[P1.3] Conversely, a device would be less useful if every lesson became fragmented by technical demands and supervision.
[P1.4] Device-light does not mean device-free or anti-technology. Tablets can remain available for carefully chosen uses, such as accessible audio, visual demonstrations, or activities in which immediate feedback matters. The policy should make paper the default medium while preserving opportunities to teach digital literacy and accommodate disability. Later, when students have stronger reading fluency, mathematical fluency, and self-regulation, one-to-one devices can support research, feedback, and individualized practice without making every early lesson screen-dependent.
[P1.5] This approach also protects equity. If paper is the ordinary foundation of early instruction, no child’s access to home technology determines whether she can practice foundational skills in class. It also avoids turning elementary education into a test of device access, technical skill, and household navigation. The motion is therefore not a retreat from the future; it is a sequencing decision.
[P1.6] Give children the physical, social, and attentional tools they need to learn first, then introduce devices when their increased independence makes digital learning more effective.
