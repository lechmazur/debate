[P1.1] Robotaxis are not just a product; they are the next form of the public street. When we hand private fleets the exclusive use of public roads, curb space, and taxpayer-built infrastructure to operate driverless cars, we are entitled to attach a public condition: serve the whole city, not just its richest corners. That is the heart of my case, and it rests on three pillars.
[P1.2] First, the license justifies the duty. These companies don't operate in a vacuum—they depend on public roads, public mapping data, public safety regulators, and often public subsidies. A grant of that scale has always come with service obligations. We required it of taxis, of phone companies, of electric utilities.
[P1.3] The reason is simple: infrastructure that everyone pays to sustain should not become a luxury amenity that only high-margin districts enjoy.
[P1.4] Second, without the requirement, exclusion is the predictable equilibrium, not a risk. Profit-maximizing fleets will rationally cluster vehicles where trips are short, frequent, and lucrative—downtown cores and affluent zones. Low-income and low-demand neighborhoods get thin coverage, long waits, or no service at all. And these are exactly the places where car ownership is lowest and transit gaps are widest—where a reliable ride is not convenience but access to a job interview, a dialysis appointment, a grocery run.
[P1.5] The market failure here compounds existing disadvantage.
[P1.6] Third, "reasonably similar reliability" is a workable standard, not a utopian one. I am not demanding identical wait times everywhere; I am demanding that a rider in a poor neighborhood not face a categorically broken service while the wealthy district gets seamless coverage. That's the same logic behind universal service in every regulated network industry.
[P1.7] The burden CON must meet is steep: they must argue that a technology using public streets should be permitted to systematically abandon the citizens least able to reach opportunity otherwise. I say a service riding on public infrastructure owes the public a floor of access—and that floor is exactly what this proposition provides.
