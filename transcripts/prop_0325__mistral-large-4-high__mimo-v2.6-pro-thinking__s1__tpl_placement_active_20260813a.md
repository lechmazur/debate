[P1.1] The current streaming model treats every sports fan as if they have identical tastes. You want one Premier League club? Subscribe to a bundle that includes cricket, rugby, and seventeen other leagues you will never watch. This is not just annoying; it is economically irrational and fundamentally unfair.
[P1.2] Bundles exist to extract maximum revenue from the most dedicated fans while pricing out everyone else. A casual supporter who follows only the Chicago Bears should not need to pay for access to MLB, the NBA, and international soccer just to watch eight Sunday games. When broadcasters force that choice, they create deadweight loss: millions of potential customers sit on the sidelines because the entry fee exceeds their willingness to pay, even though their marginal cost of streaming one more game is essentially zero.
[P1.3] Team-specific passes would capture that demand. Lower price, higher volume, larger total revenue.
[P1.4] This is not charity for fans; it is standard price discrimination that grows the market. Netflix does not force you to buy an all-entertainment package to watch one show. Spotify lets you follow one artist without subscribing to every genre. Sports broadcasters cling to bundles because they fear cannibalization, but that fear is misplaced.
[P1.5] A ten-dollar monthly pass for one team converts non-subscribers into paying customers while letting superfans stack passes for the two or three teams they actually follow. The broadcaster wins, the fan wins, and the sport grows.
[P1.6] The alternative—forcing fans to subsidize leagues they ignore—breeds resentment and piracy. When legitimate access feels like a protection racket, fans find workarounds. Offering targeted, affordable passes respects consumer choice and builds loyalty. It is time broadcasters treated fans as individuals, not as a captive audience for a content firehose.
