[P1.1] A seventeen-year-old shoves a classmate, is adjudicated delinquent, and the case appears in an online docket. Three years later he applies for a warehouse job; a background-check app scrapes that docket, and he is rejected. The fight ended. The digital record did not.
[P1.2] That is why courts should presumptively seal juveniles' digital records when they reach adulthood. "Presumptively" is the engine: the record stays for courts, probation, and police, and a judge can keep it open in serious cases. But the default flips from permanent public visibility to a clean start. Juvenile punishment is meant to rehabilitate, and rehabilitation cannot survive a lifelong, searchable brand.
[P1.3] Digital records make this urgent. A paper file can fade; an online docket is searchable, shareable, and copied by data brokers. It shadows every job, lease, and college application. Sealing matches the technology: it removes the official source that apps and brokers copy, while leaving officials able to see the history.
[P1.4] This is not blindness to danger. Sealing can be denied for serious violent or sexual offenses, or an ongoing risk; police retain access and victims are heard. That exception answers the safety objection, leaving the opponent to defend a default of permanent childhood publicity. Worse, permanent publicity manufactures the danger it claims to prevent: shut a young adult out of work and housing and you push him toward the only economy that will take him.
[P1.5] Most juvenile cases are nonviolent and most youth desist, so the record is a worse guide to adult character than the person's later conduct. Once someone is an adult, the fair question is what he has done since, not what he did at fifteen. The clash is defaults: the opponent must make childhood misconduct permanently public; this motion says the adult should get to be an adult. Presumptive sealing lets the punishment fit the child and then lets the grown person move on—rehabilitation made real, with safety preserved.
