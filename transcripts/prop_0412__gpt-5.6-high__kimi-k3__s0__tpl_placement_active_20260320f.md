[P1.1] When a television host promotes a product for payment, viewers are entitled to know that the praise is advertising. The same entitlement does not disappear because the pitch arrives through a phone screen. A large influencer can reach an audience comparable to a broadcaster’s, shape purchasing decisions, and earn substantial income from sponsorships. Equal commercial power should carry equal disclosure duties.
[P1.2] Disclosure matters even more in influencer marketing because the advertisement is deliberately woven into personal life. A polished commercial announces itself through format and context. An influencer may present a paid supplement, investment app, or beauty product as a spontaneous recommendation from a trusted friend. That parasocial trust is commercially valuable precisely because followers may not recognize the sales pitch.
[P1.3] Without a clear, prominent disclosure, consumers cannot properly judge the speaker’s incentives.
[P1.4] The rule should concern function, not occupational labels. If money, free products, affiliate commissions, or other valuable benefits influence an endorsement, that relationship should be disclosed clearly, at the point where the endorsement appears, in language an ordinary viewer can understand. Large audiences justify strict enforcement because one hidden sponsorship can mislead vast numbers at once. Platforms and advertisers should also share responsibility, preventing brands from outsourcing deception to supposedly independent creators.
[P1.5] This is not a restriction on opinion or creativity. Influencers remain free to praise, criticize, entertain, and advertise. They simply may not disguise paid persuasion as independent judgment. Adding “paid partnership” or an equally clear statement imposes a trivial burden compared with the consumer’s risk of wasting money, adopting unsafe habits, or trusting conflicted financial advice.
[P1.6] Any weaker standard creates an irrational loophole: the more advertising migrates from recognizable media into intimate, personalized feeds, the less transparent it becomes. Regulation should follow influence, not nostalgia about where media happens. If two personalities possess comparable reach and receive comparable commercial benefits, consumers deserve comparable protection. Large influencers should therefore face disclosure rules at least as strict as those governing traditional media personalities.
