[P1.1] Emergencies make executives stronger, and executives know it. That is the central problem this proposition addresses. When a crisis hits—war, pandemic, financial collapse—legislatures hand over broad powers in days, under fear, with little scrutiny. But the fear fades slowly while the powers fade even more slowly.
[P1.2] Emergency authority has a ratchet: it is granted fast and surrendered almost never voluntarily. Automatic sunset hearings and renewal votes break that ratchet. They force the question "do we still need this?" onto the calendar, rather than leaving it to the executive's discretion to decide when its own power expires.
[P1.3] The status alternative is not neutrality; it is drift. Congress has delegated sweeping authority through vaguely worded statutes that persist for decades, invoked for purposes their drafters never imagined. Authorizations passed for one conflict become legal cover for operations years later against actors who barely existed when the ink dried. Pandemic-era powers to restrict gatherings, spend without normal process, and override licensing rules lived long past their moment because repeal required affirmative legislative action—an uphill battle against an executive that controls the agenda and the veto.
[P1.4] Sunsets flip that default. Continuation becomes the thing requiring justification, and the burden lands on the branch with every incentive to keep the power.
[P1.5] This is a democratic argument, not an anti-government one. Some emergencies genuinely last years, and renewal votes can say so—nothing in this proposition caps executive capacity at a moment of real danger. What it caps is unilateral executive judgment about when the danger ends. And hearings create a record: legislators must listen, in public, to what the power actually did, whom it touched, and whether it worked.
[P1.6] That is the minimum epistemic discipline a democracy owes itself before extending extraordinary authority.
[P1.7] The deepest principle: legitimacy in a democracy is borrowed, not owned. Emergency powers are loans against the constitution. A loan with no repayment date is not a loan; it is a transfer of ownership. Requiring a vote to renew is simply the collection notice.

[C1.1] Automatic sunset hearings and renewal votes do not end emergency power ratchets—they institutionalize them, replacing quiet drift with a predictable, exploitable process that delivers less accountability, not more. Fixed, mandatory renewal dates do not force sober, evidence-based review. When a sunset lands during a spike in media-amplified public fear—whether a new pandemic wave, a high-profile terror attack, or a sudden financial shock—legislators do not dig into misuse or overbreadth. They grandstand for cameras, then vote to reauthorize sweeping powers with even less scrutiny than the original grant, because voting “no” is instantly framed as choosing chaos over safety.
[C1.2] The hearings become a stage for executive officials to parade selectively declassified threat assessments, warn of imminent catastrophe if powers lapse, and tie renewal to unrelated must-pass disaster or relief funding, turning the vote into a foregone conclusion that rubber-stamps overbroad authority with a false democratic seal of approval. These votes rarely produce clean, up-or-down judgments on whether power is still needed, because legislative leadership bundles them with high-stakes measures no legislator dares vote against, from flood relief to military pay raises.
[C1.3] Worse, the rigid calendar distorts emergency response itself. Slow-burn, unglamorous crises—public health supply chain preparedness, ongoing cross-border wildfire coordination, targeted sanctions against hostile transnational groups—do not align with legislative scheduling. Agencies facing automatic expiration will hoard power in advance, writing broader rules before the sunset to avoid future legislative fights, frontloading disruptive enforcement actions to beat the deadline, and diverting resources from on-the-ground response to preparing lobbying and testimony for renewal. The reform does not shift power back to legislatures; it shifts leverage to the legislators best at extracting campaign concessions from executive agencies in exchange for their vote, while leaving ordinary people facing more erratic, more aggressively enforced emergency rules.
