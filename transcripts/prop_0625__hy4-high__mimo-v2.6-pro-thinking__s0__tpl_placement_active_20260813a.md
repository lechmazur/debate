[P1.1] Governments should impose a temporary moratorium on solar geoengineering field experiments. The issue is not whether the underlying science deserves study. It is whether anyone should cross from models, labs, and observations into deliberate outdoor tests before a binding international governance framework defines authority, consent, risk thresholds, monitoring, liability, and shutdown. The only defensible answer is no experiments until that framework is in place.
[P1.2] Small physical footprints do not make small political facts. A balloon releasing reflective particles into the stratosphere, or a ship spraying sea salt into clouds, acts on a shared medium, not a fenced test site. Large volcanic eruptions show that material high in the atmosphere can affect weather far away. Without a framework, there is no settled answer to who must be notified, how affected states object, what evidence stops a trial, or who pays if rainfall shifts and someone claims injury.
[P1.3] Even a harmless trial can look like a hostile probe or a rehearsal for deployment.
[P1.4] The precedent is the core danger. Once a state, coalition, or private funder demonstrates the ability to alter sunlight outdoors, it creates a first mover, a constituency for more testing, and an incentive for rivals to match it. Voluntary codes and domestic review bind the cautious, not the ambitious. They cannot compensate outsiders, verify dual-use intent, or prevent a larger trial from being labelled “research.” “Trust us, it is small” is the rule a future unilateral program would most want to inherit.
[P1.5] The urgent-data argument reverses the proper sequence. Data from nonconsensual tests will be politically contested, and the tests normalize the very conduct governance must control. The moratorium is not anti-science: modelling, laboratory work, satellite observations, and governance design continue. It also creates leverage to negotiate authorization rules, public registries, environmental assessment, liability, and enforcement.
[P1.6] Pause outdoor experiments first; then experiment under law. That protects research, cooperation, and the atmosphere at once.

A gram-scale release is not a private measurement when the atmosphere is the laboratory. The first question is who authorized it, who can object, who monitors, who stops it, and who pays if harm is alleged. A framework answers those questions; openness and domestic review do not.
[P2.2] The “indefinite” objection [C1.1] proves too much. A temporary moratorium can have review periods and one off-ramp: make the framework binding and experimentation resumes.
