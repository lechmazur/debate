[P1.1] Weddings once marked a community’s recognition of a union. Today they too often function as an expensive spectacle—social media-ready staged moments, hashtag-driven production values, vendor packages priced by the guest count, and familial pressure to turn a personal commitment into public theater. When the ritual becomes performance, obligation should weaken, not harden. Couples should not feel bound to traditions whose main function has shifted from meaning to display.
[P1.2] Two harms make this more than taste. First, cost pressure pushes newlyweds into debt before their shared life begins, and the sums involved have become ordinary rather than exceptional: deposits, venue minimums, floral design, photography, dress, travel, and the quiet arms race of hospitality. Second, performative expectations distort consent. Couples absorb costs they never chose because the event is read as a public verdict on love, gratitude, and family status.
[P1.3] When declining an upgrade is interpreted as indifference, tradition stops being a shared language and becomes leverage.
[P1.4] The strongest defense of tradition is symbolic continuity, but that defense cuts our way. Continuity matters when forms serve meaning. A courthouse signing, a small meal with witnesses, or a backyard vow preserves commitment while rejecting the assumption that affection must be documented for an audience. If tradition is about honoring commitment, it cannot demand that couples spend beyond their means to prove it.
[P1.5] This is not a call for couples to be careless with people they love. It is a call to relocate obligation: owe honesty, care, and communication to a partner; owe hospitality and courtesy to guests; do not owe a marketplace an imitation of a magazine spread. Culture has changed the wedding from a marker of union into a costly production. Couples should be free to follow only those traditions that still mean something to them.

[C1.1] The case for abandoning obligation rests on a sleight of hand: conflating modern commercial trends with traditional expectations. Hashtags, staged social-media moments, and luxury vendor upgrades ([P1.1], [P1.3]) are not traditions; they are recent consumer fads. Actual traditional expectations—gathering the extended family, honoring parents, performing religious or ancestral rites, and sharing a celebratory meal—predate the bridal marketplace by centuries. Pointing to inflated floral minimums to absolve couples of their obligations to family and heritage is like using the price of luxury restaurants to argue we owe little obligation to family dinners.
[C1.2] Couples should feel a genuine obligation to traditional expectations because marriage is never solely about two atomized individuals. It is the joining of two families and a claim on a community’s lifelong support. Parents, grandparents, and extended kin invest years of care, sacrifice, and identity into raising a person. Traditional wedding customs—whether a religious blessing, a cultural tea ceremony, or inviting the aunts and uncles who watched you grow up—are acts of reciprocity and gratitude.
[C1.3] Insisting that couples follow only traditions "that still mean something to them" ([P1.5]) endorses a self-centered standard where generational continuity and an aging grandparent’s hope to witness a family milestone carry little weight unless the couple personally curates them.
[C1.4] Finally, dismissing public ritual as "performative" ([P1.1], [P1.2]) misunderstands why human beings have rituals at all. Public performance is the mechanism of social accountability. Standing before a gathered community to make vows transforms a private feeling into a witnessed covenant. Yes, hosting that community requires effort and expense, and navigating family expectations involves compromise.
[C1.5] But traditions scale to any budget—from church basements and community halls to family-cooked feasts—without erasing the rites themselves. A private courthouse signing ([P1.4]) may avoid commercialism, but when it shuts out the community that formed the couple, it confuses frugality with virtue. Rejecting commercial excess never justifies defaulting on our obligations to tradition and kin.

[P2.2] The couple inherits the obligation, but the marketplace sets the terms. When declining an upgrade is read as indifference, the inherited form has become leverage. The restaurant analogy fails because we do not judge a family’s love by whether they can afford the tasting menu, nor make the entrée tier a condition of belonging.
[P2.3] The reply that traditions “scale to any budget” [C1.5] misunderstands the harm. The pressure is not only monetary; it is interpretive. A modest basement ceremony still carries the social verdict that simplicity equals neglect, that fewer guests means fewer blessings, that the wedding is a referendum on gratitude. Even the most sincere relative can wield tradition as a test.
[P2.4] Frugality is not the enemy—coercion is. And coercion thrives precisely because tradition has been framed as non-negotiable duty rather than chosen meaning.
[P2.5] Marriage does join families, and kinship matters. But reciprocity is not ritual compliance. Parents are honored through sustained care, honesty, and presence—not through a staged spectacle that mortgages a couple’s future before it begins. The strongest covenant is not witnessed by an audience; it is maintained daily.
[P2.6] If tradition exists to strengthen commitment, it cannot demand that couples prove love by spending beyond their means or performing for an audience.
[P2.7] Keep what still means something. Refuse what has become theater. That is not abandoning obligation; it is restoring it to where it belongs—between the couple and those they choose to share their lives with.
