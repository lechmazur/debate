[P1.1] This proposition does not ban behavior-based car insurance. It bars insurers from forcing drivers to surrender to continuous in-car surveillance to access a legally required product, unless they opt in on terms that are actually fair. The status quo it targets inflicts three overlapping, unjust harms that demand correction.
[P1.2] First, continuous in-car surveillance collects far more data than legitimate driving risk metrics. Telematic dongles, built-in infotainment trackers, and insurance apps log every trip destination, every stop at a doctor’s office, place of worship, or domestic violence resource center, even who rides in the car with you. None of this information has any bearing on auto accident risk, and there is no justified interest in forcing drivers to hand it over to buy insurance.
[P1.3] Second, current “opt-in” models are not genuine choices. Most usage-based insurance programs impose 20% or higher rate penalties on drivers who decline surveillance, a cost many low-income drivers cannot absorb. A choice made under threat of financial harm is not a free choice, and the proposition’s requirement for truly fair opt-in terms eliminates this coercion.
[P1.4] Third, unregulated surveillance-based pricing is inherently discriminatory. Black-box algorithms that use unrestricted location data consistently penalize drivers in low-income neighborhoods and marginalized racial groups, even when their individual driving records are flawless. Drivers have no recourse when a rate hike is triggered by factors outside their control: braking suddenly to avoid a pedestrian, driving at night to a second job, or living in a neighborhood insurers arbitrarily mark as high-risk.
[P1.5] The proposition preserves the right of drivers to share limited, relevant driving data if they so choose, while barring insurers from imposing mass, unfair surveillance as a condition of basic financial security.
