[P1.1] Peer review is the only quality-control step in science that is run on unpaid, invisible, unaccountable labor, and it shows. Editors routinely send a dozen invitations to find two reviewers. Papers sit for months. Reviews arrive late, thin, or hostile, and nobody can tell which, because the report vanishes into a drawer.
[P1.2] We ask reviewers to be the gatekeepers of the scientific record, then treat the work as a favor nobody has to do well.
[P1.3] Payment fixes the supply problem and the standards problem at once. Nobody argues that auditors, referees, or external examiners should work free because money would corrupt them; payment is what lets you set a deadline, a scope, and a quality bar and actually enforce them. A paid review is a deliverable. An unpaid one is a hope.
[P1.4] And the money exists: commercial publishers earn margins that software companies envy, built on articles and reviews they receive for nothing.
[P1.5] Publication of reports fixes the accountability problem. Today a reviewer can reject a competitor's paper with two lazy sentences, demand five citations to their own work, or wave through a friend's flawed study, and no one outside the editor ever sees it. When the report sits beside the paper, readers see exactly what was scrutinized and what was not. Graduate students finally get worked examples of how to review.
[P1.6] Reviewers get citable credit for careful work. Several journals already publish reports, and the sky has not fallen; what has happened is that the reports got more careful and more polite.
[P1.7] The deepest point is that these two reforms reinforce each other. Payment without publication buys quantity; publication without payment demands rigor from volunteers. Together they turn reviewing from an anonymous favor into visible, compensated professional work.
[P1.8] The measure that matters is whether review is faster, more thorough, and more honest. Secrecy and unpaid labor deliver none of those. They deliver a system everyone complains about and no one will defend on its merits.

[C1.1] The motion treats two protections as defects: the reviewer is not a vendor, and the report is not a press release. Peer review works best as a candid, fallible judgment for an editor, not a billable public performance. “faster, more thorough, and more honest” [P1.8] are not equally weighted: speed and visibility are worth little if candor collapses.
[C1.2] “A paid review is a deliverable” [P1.3] gives away the problem. Money buys compliance with visible metrics: turnaround, length, boxes ticked. It does not buy the slow act of noticing a wrong control or an overstated inference. A paid reviewer is a repeat contractor with an incentive to please the payer.
[C1.3] Publisher margins are not neutral funding; the actor most interested in throughput controls the paycheck. If authors fund reviews, friendly opinions become shoppable; high fees push publication costs toward wealthy institutions. The unpaid norm is not sentiment: it recruits expertise through reciprocity and keeps judgment outside the price system.
[C1.4] Publishing reports turns confidential criticism into performance. Even with names withheld, small fields let authors guess; with citable reports, a postdoc saying a famous lab’s statistics are incoherent is writing for hiring committees and complainants. Candor becomes diplomacy. The abusive reviewer can still demand citations to his own work, now in courteous prose; the crony can still wave a paper through, now with a public blessing.
[C1.5] Nor does publication create the accountability promised in [P1.5]. The worst abuses occur in rejections, where no paper appears beside the reports. For accepted papers, reports address earlier drafts. “Sample size inadequate” may sit next to a revised article whose sample size was expanded, giving stale objections permanent authority and letting readers mistake process debris for current evidence.
[C1.6] Together, payment and publicity reward careful-looking, creditable, billable output rather than independent review. Better fixes are editorial triage, conflict rules, internal audits, and sanctions for abusive reports.

[C2.2] The predictable result is safer criticism: softer charges, omitted suspicions, ceremonial praise. Opt-in journals [P2.2] self-select people comfortable with exposure; less cruelty may mean more diplomacy. Citable credit rewards public vindication, not quietly preventing error.
[C2.3] The auditor analogy [P2.3] misses the job. Audit checks accounts against rules; peer review requires saying a fashionable question is trivial or a famous group’s inference is wrong. That is judgment under uncertainty, not a checklist. Money enforces what managers count: turnaround, length, boxes.
[C2.4] It makes the journal a client interested in predictable throughput, not a neutral truth-machine. A two-line midnight review [P2.4] is a failure, but not evidence that buying reports buys insight.

[C2.6] Without that machinery, publication freezes process debris and gives resolved objections permanent authority.
[C2.7] [P2.6] confuses labor with payment per review, and records with publicity. Salaried editors can audit; confidential files can support sanctions and appeals; due process need not be public theatre. [C2.8] The comparison is decisive: a late thin review is bad; a prompt, polished, public review that never says “the central claim is probably false” is worse, because it launders weak work with apparent scrutiny.

[C5.1] The choice offered—“checked” over “trusted”—mistakes a public document for an audit of judgment. A published report can be checked for courtesy, length, speed, and whether it mentions controls. It cannot be checked for the sentence that was never written: “the central claim is probably false.” To spot that omission, a reader must already know the flaw, possess the earlier draft, and know what a candid referee would have said. Otherwise typeset silence does not expose laundering; it completes it.
[C5.2] That is why the proposed asymmetry fails. A weak confidential review is visible to the editor, who can discard it, demand another, stop inviting that reviewer, and compare report to manuscript. A weak paid public review has already satisfied the client and produced a citable artifact. The incentive is not to be caught being hollow; it is to produce hollow work outsiders cannot identify without doing the review themselves.
[C5.3] The existing failures are correctable editorial failures. The motion turns them into professional outputs for an audience beyond the editor. Publication checks the transcript while changing the testimony: restraint becomes safer, candor riskier. On truth-detection, that is the losing trade.
