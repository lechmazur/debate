[P1.1] A cure nobody can afford is not a medical breakthrough; it is a press release. The first approved CRISPR therapy treats sickle cell disease, and its list price runs into millions of dollars per patient. Sickle cell is concentrated in India, Nigeria, and among poor patients in rich countries. The science arrived; the medicine did not.
[P1.2] Nothing here is radical. Compulsory and government-use licensing are ordinary features of patent law, explicitly preserved in the Doha Declaration, and used for HIV and hepatitis C drugs. Often the lever worked without being pulled: credible threats of licensing produced voluntary price cuts. The motion asks countries to make that lever mandatory, bounded to essential therapies, with royalties paid to the inventor.
[P1.3] That is a bargain, not expropriation.
[P1.4] The innovation objection collapses on arithmetic. Compulsion bites in markets that were never going to pay a million dollars. Licences there convert zero revenue into modest royalty revenue; they do not shrink the research budget that produced the therapy in the first place. And taxpayers already funded much of the underlying CRISPR science through public grants.
[P1.5] Paying twice — first for discovery, then indefinitely for access — is not an incentive system; it is a toll booth.
[P1.6] The alternatives usually offered are tiered pricing, donations, and voluntary pools: all discretionary, all revocable, all conditioned on the patent holder's goodwill. Rights cannot rest on charity. And the objection that gene therapies are too complex to copy proves our case — a licence must reach know-how and technology transfer, not merely suspend a patent. That is precisely why the pathway must be written into law.
[P1.7] A child born with sickle cell in Lagos does not need a better press release. She needs the cure that already exists, at a price her country can pay.

[C1.5] And "essential" is not a scientific category. Once the pathway exists, politics will widen it from lethal, one-time cures toward anything scarce and expensive. [C1.6] The credible threat PRO treats as a success is the damage: capital exits before any licence is pulled.
[C1.7] Advance commitments and outcome-based contracts can cut prices without teaching rivals the process. The child in Lagos needs a system that can deliver a living therapy, and a pipeline that still produces the next one.
