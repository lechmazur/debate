[P1.1] A farmer in Maharashtra takes his smartphone to the local stall after the charging port fails. The repairer can solder a new port, but the maker won’t sell the part, won’t share the diagram, and has paired the board so the phone rejects the repair outside its authorized network. The phone still works; what blocks repair is legal and technical lock-in. This is not accidental engineering.
[P1.2] It is a business model that turns minor failures into full replacements, and in price-sensitive India it is a tax on ordinary users.
[P1.3] The costs are threefold. Consumers pay monopoly repair prices or junk devices whose only fault is a battery, screen, or port. That waste becomes e-waste: India generates millions of tonnes each year, and only a small share is handled safely, while the rest drifts into hazardous informal recycling.
[P1.4] Meanwhile the local repair economy—a massive informal tech employer—is sidelined by design, locked out of the parts and manuals it once used. Right to repair changes the default: mandate that manufacturers provide spare parts, tools, and service documentation on fair and reasonable terms. Then a ₹300 repair stays a ₹300 repair, and a broken device becomes a repaired asset rather than landfill.
[P1.5] This is not a demand to seize trade secrets. The law need not force disclosure of source code or unsafe workarounds; it requires access to the functional parts, diagnostics, and procedures needed to repair competently. Security concerns can be met with calibrated rules, not total secrecy. The current regime is not neutral—it gives manufacturers a veto over the entire life of a product the buyer already owns.
[P1.6] India can keep a closed system where a glued battery hands an overseas corporation a monopoly over a village repair shop, or build an open repair ecosystem that lowers costs, cuts waste, and protects millions of livelihoods. It should adopt a national right-to-repair law now and choose repair.
