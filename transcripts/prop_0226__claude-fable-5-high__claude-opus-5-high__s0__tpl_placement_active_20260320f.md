The courts turned him away — not because his claim was false, but because the law had no category for him. The 1951 Refugee Convention protects people fleeing persecution by governments and militias. [P1.2] That gap is the whole debate.
[P1.3] Here is the core reality: displacement from sea-level rise and extreme heat is not hypothetical or preventable by asylum policy. Low-lying states like Tuvalu and parts of Bangladesh face literal territorial loss; heat in parts of South Asia and the Gulf increasingly approaches limits of human survivability. These people will move. The only question countries actually control is whether that movement happens through legal channels or through smugglers, capsized boats, and permanent limbo.
[P1.4] So the affirmative case rests on three pillars.
[P1.5] First, coherence. Asylum law already embodies a principle: we do not return people to conditions that destroy them. A person whose village is underwater faces harm as absolute as many we already protect. Refusing recognition isn't caution — it's arbitrariness.
[P1.6] Second, responsibility. Climate displacement is not a natural disaster falling from the sky; it is caused, overwhelmingly, by the historical emissions of wealthy states. When your conduct renders someone's homeland unlivable, offering a legal pathway is the minimum corrective justice demands — far cheaper than reparations, far more humane than walls.
[P1.7] Third, order. The word "limited" matters. We propose defined criteria — uninhabitability, no viable internal relocation — not open borders. Structured legal pathways let states screen, plan, and distribute responsibility.
[P1.8] Denial doesn't reduce arrivals; it just converts them into irregular ones, which is worse for migrants and host societies alike.
[P1.9] Expect the other side to warn of floodgates and line-drawing problems. But every asylum category requires line-drawing. The alternative to imperfect lines is no lines — and that is the truly unmanageable world.
