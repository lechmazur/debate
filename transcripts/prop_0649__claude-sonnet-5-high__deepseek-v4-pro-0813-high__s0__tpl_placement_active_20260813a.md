[C1.1] The gap peer-to-peer trading celebrates is not an inefficiency; it is the price of reliability. The spread between a low export rate and the retail rate covers poles, transformers, line losses, voltage control, metering, billing, and the guarantee that power is still there when rooftop output disappears. A bilateral trade inside that spread does not create new value. It captures grid services while shifting their cost elsewhere.
[C1.2] A simple example makes the problem plain. A solar owner might sell surplus to a neighbor at 12 cents when the utility charges 18 cents. The seller beats a 6-cent export rate, the buyer saves 6 cents, but the utility must still provide the wires, frequency control, and evening supply. The missing money does not vanish.
[C1.3] It shows up in higher fixed charges or higher rates for everyone not in the trading arrangement—renters, apartment dwellers, and low-income households who cannot buy panels or choose their seller. Peer-to-peer trading thus becomes a regressive cross-subsidy, not a neutral market.
[C1.4] The physics also fails the "neutral platform" claim. Electricity does not flow by contract from the seller's roof to the buyer's meter. Both remain embedded in one shared pool, and the grid operator must balance their fluctuations. Rooftop output peaks midday, while residential demand rises in the evening.
[C1.5] Far from removing the need for peaker plants, private trading leaves utilities still obliged to supply instant shortfall, often without recovering the cost from those creating it.
[C1.6] The property-rights argument also misstates the asset. Panels are private, but injecting power into an interconnected grid is not like selling furniture. It imposes safety, voltage, and planning obligations on every other user. If the transport fee were set high enough to cover those obligations, the arbitrage gap would largely disappear.
[C1.7] If it is not, that is the subsidy the proposition claims does not exist.
[C1.8] Countries serious about clean energy should pay solar owners fairly for delivered value. They should not allow a parallel market that shifts grid costs onto those least able to escape them.
