[P1.1] When public pensions were designed, many systems set retirement around 65, and the typical retiree could expect maybe a decade or so of benefits. Today, a 65-year-old in most rich countries can expect closer to twenty years. The system did not break because workers got lazy or taxes got low. It is strained because we succeeded at living longer.
[P1.2] The right fix targets that cause: keep the share of adult life spent in retirement roughly stable by moving the retirement age as longevity rises.
[P1.3] The payroll-tax path fails three tests.
[P1.4] First, it never ends. Life expectancy keeps rising and the ratio of workers to retirees keeps falling as large cohorts retire. A tax hike buys a few years, then the gap reopens and the next hike comes. Indexing the retirement age adjusts automatically, so the system stays sound without a fresh political crisis every decade.
[P1.5] Several European countries, including Denmark and Finland, already tie retirement ages to longevity for exactly this reason.
[P1.6] Second, it loads the cost onto the wrong people. Payroll taxes fall on today's workers, often young families paying rent and childcare, who also face the most uncertainty about whether they will ever collect the same deal. Raising those taxes drives a wedge between what employers pay and what workers take home, which discourages hiring and pushes work into the informal economy. And because many systems cap taxable earnings, the burden often falls hardest on ordinary wage earners, not top earners.
[P1.7] Third, it misreads fairness. Under indexation, someone who lives longer works somewhat longer but still collects for longer. Each generation gets a comparable retirement, instead of each generation of workers paying more to fund ever-longer retirements for the one before.

[C1.1] The proposition’s central mistake is to treat a longer average life as a longer supply of sellable labor. It is not. Longevity gains are uneven by income, occupation and health, and extra years often bring arthritis, caregiving, chronic illness, or poor hiring prospects. Keeping “the share of adult life spent in retirement” stable [P1.2] sounds actuarially tidy; in practice it tells a 63-year-old cleaner with bad knees that because a 63-year-old consultant is likely to live longer, the cleaner’s pension must start later too.
[C1.2] Age indexation also hides the cost. Raising the normal retirement age is not mainly an invitation to work; for many it means fewer benefit years, lower monthly benefits if claiming early, or a forced gamble that an employer will keep them. Older job-losers struggle to find new work; manual workers cannot simply retrain into spreadsheets at 64; caregivers cannot easily add paid years. The “targeted” disability and arduous-work routes [P1.8] require medical proof, bureaucratic classification, and clean boundaries real lives rarely have.
[C1.3] Chronic pain and obsolete skills fall through the cracks.
[C1.4] Payroll-tax increases are at least adjustable and shareable. Lift earnings caps, include compensation now outside the base, and give low-wage credits; the burden follows capacity to pay, not capacity to survive another year of work. The claim that taxes load the cost onto young families [P1.6] ignores the opposite concentration: age hikes hit people near retirement, with the least time to rearrange savings or health. Contributions affect take-home pay, but the cost is visible, can be phased with wages, and falls across the insured economy.
[C1.5] Nor is contribution financing an endless hole [P1.4]: automatic age indexation is the real one-way ratchet. The examples in [P1.5] show only that a formula can be written, not that it is fair. Public pensions should not index retirement to averages; they should preserve access to retirement and finance it through higher, fairer payroll contributions.
