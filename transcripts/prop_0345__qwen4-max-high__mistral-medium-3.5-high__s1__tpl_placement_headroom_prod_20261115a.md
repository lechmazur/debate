[P1.1] This motion does not ban return-to-office. It asks that when a large employer moves thousands of lives, it can state a reason tied to the work those people actually do. That is not a veto over management. It is a demand that management have a reason.
[P1.2] Notice how easy that is when the reason is real. A hardware team needs benches and prototypes. A surgical service needs hands in the building. New analysts learn by overhearing senior people handle a difficult call.
[P1.3] Each of those is specific, checkable, and different in scope — and none of them justifies dragging a remote support engineer who has shipped alone for four years across a metro area three days a week.
[P1.4] "Culture" does the opposite work. It is unfalsifiable, uniform, and infinitely elastic: whatever the mandate's actual effect, culture can be said to have improved. That is why it so often serves policies whose real purpose lies elsewhere — trimming headcount without severance, or honoring a lease and an executive's preference. A reason that can never fail is not a reason; it is an alibi.
[P1.5] The costs are not symmetric. An employer that guesses wrong loses some slack time. A worker who guessed wrong on a house, a school, a spouse's job, or a caregiving arrangement loses far more, and a disabled worker who built a functioning life around remote work may lose the job itself. Where one side holds nearly all the power and the other absorbs nearly all the risk, requiring an explanation is the mildest possible check.
[P1.6] If the answer is that a large employer owes no explanation at all, say so plainly — that is an argument about power, not culture, and it deserves to be defended as one.

[C1.1] The motion turns a routine management decision into a legal test. Employers already weigh costs and benefits when setting workplace policies. If a mandate is arbitrary or unpopular, the labor market punishes it: top talent leaves, recruiting becomes harder, and productivity flags. High-profile tech firms have reversed RTO mandates after backlash and attrition.
[C1.2] The proposed rule adds no value—it merely shifts the cost of justification from the market to the courtroom, inviting lawsuits over whether a reason is “role-specific” enough.
[C1.3] Moreover, the demand for role-specific justifications is arbitrary. Many benefits of in-office work—spontaneous collaboration, institutional knowledge transfer, alignment on complex projects—are network effects that accrue to the organization as a whole. Requiring each role to have a distinct, verifiable link to these benefits forces managers to manufacture artificial distinctions or to drop broad, genuine reasons in favor of narrower, less accurate ones. That is the opposite of good governance.
[C1.4] The rule also ignores practical reality. Large employers often have hundreds of roles, with responsibilities that evolve rapidly. A justification written today may be obsolete tomorrow. The legal risk of non-compliance would push companies toward rigid, one-size-fits-all policies that are easier to defend in court, reducing flexibility for both employer and employee.
[C1.5] Finally, the motion rests on a false premise: that employers currently owe no explanation. In truth, they explain policies constantly—to investors, to the public, and especially to their own teams. The difference is that these explanations are tailored to the audience and the context, not to a bureaucratic standard. Imposing a single, judicial standard for a business decision is a step toward treating employers like regulated utilities, not like dynamic organizations competing for talent.
[C1.6] This is overreach. The market already disciplines bad mandates; the law should not.
