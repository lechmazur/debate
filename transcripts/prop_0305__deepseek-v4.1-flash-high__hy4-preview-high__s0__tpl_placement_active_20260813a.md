[P1.1] The decisive question is not whether trophy hunting is tasteful. It is what happens to wildlife and rural people when a transparent, regulated hunting economy is banned.
[P1.2] The proposition does not defend corruption or secret deals. It defends a narrow class of programs: legal hunting whose revenue is traceable and shared—permits and quotas funding rangers, anti-poaching patrols, habitat, compensation for livestock lost to predators, and jobs for local trackers, guides, and cooks.
[P1.3] Where that condition holds, hunting changes incentives. A live elephant or lion becomes valuable to the community that must live with it. A dead one taken legally under a quota generates money that can protect the rest. Ban the legal trade, and demand does not disappear; it moves underground.
[P1.4] Poachers do not pay conservancy fees, respect quotas, or compensate farmers. The choice is rarely “hunting or perfect protection.” It is often “regulated hunting with local buy-in, or illegal killing with none.”
[P1.5] Livelihoods matter for the same reason. Communities near wildlife bear real costs: trampled crops, lost cattle, children kept from school by fear, sometimes death. If conservation is pure sacrifice, resentment grows. Transparent hunting revenue can make tolerance rational.
[P1.6] Community conservancy models in southern Africa show the logic: when wildlife pays, communities protect it; when it only costs, they often eliminate it.
[P1.7] No one has to like trophy hunting. Many find it distasteful; grant that. But distaste is not a conservation plan. The critic must answer the counterfactual: what replaces the revenue, the jobs, and the local consent?
[P1.8] Vague hope that tourism or donations will fill the gap is not serious for remote areas with few roads and dangerous animals.
[P1.9] Transparency is the safeguard. Where money is stolen, punish theft and fix governance. Outlawing the transparent cases punishes the communities and species the system can protect. The motion asks only that legal, accountable, locally beneficial hunting remain an option.
[P1.10] That is not moral endorsement; it is hard-headed conservation.

[C1.1] The motion invites us to take a bloodless bargain: shoot an elephant or a lion so the money can protect the rest. I reject the bargain even on its own terms. Clean accounting does not redeem a dead animal, and remote areas are not forced to choose between hunting and nothing.
[C1.2] PRO claims animals become valuable because a legally shot one can fund protection [P1.3]. But this hides what kind of value is created. Trophy hunting teaches that a lion's highest purpose is future taxidermy, and it cashes in on precisely the spectacular individuals—prime males, big tuskers—whose removal can fracture prides and erase genetic lineages. The model rewards maintaining numbers as huntable stock, not protecting animals as wild beings.
[C1.3] That cannot ground a durable conservation ethic.
[C1.4] Nor does transparency fix this. Hunting depends on permits, quotas and veterinary certifications, all vulnerable to corruption even when the final ledger glows. Worse, legal trophy trade legitimizes commodification. Because a hunted lion or elephant already enters commerce with paperwork, illegal traffickers inherit a legal template; the captive-bred lion trade shows how fast supposedly sporting hunts curdle into canned killing and suspect paperwork.
[C1.5] A paper trail does not purge the vice—it professionalizes it.
[C1.6] PRO demands what replaces the money [P1.7]. The answer is the same governance we are asked to trust. If a state or conservancy can route a hunting fee transparently to a village, it can equally capture tourism levies, conservation trust funds, and donor results-based finance. Pretending the choice is "bullets or abandonment" lets the easy payments crowd out harder alternatives.
[C1.7] Finally, note what the PRO calls hard-headed conservation is actually a surrender of moral authority [P1.10]. Law is not merely price; it teaches. A statute saying it is fine to pay to mount an endangered animal's head tells rural communities, tourists and poachers alike that wildlife is ultimately ours to consume. That norm, not squeamishness, is why trophy hunting should remain banned even where the books balance.
