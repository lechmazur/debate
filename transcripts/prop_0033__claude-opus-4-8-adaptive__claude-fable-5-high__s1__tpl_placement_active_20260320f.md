[P1.1] The core question is whether a democracy can survive when campaigns stop persuading citizens and start exploiting them. Behavioral micro-targeting based on inferred psychological and emotional profiles does exactly that, and that is why it should be prohibited.
[P1.2] Start with what this practice actually is. It is not a candidate making an argument in public that anyone can hear and rebut. It is a machine inferring that you are anxious, lonely, impulsive, or fearful—usually from data you never knowingly handed over—and then crafting a message engineered to trip that specific lever. The persuasion is private, individualized, and invisible to everyone except its target.
[P1.3] That design is the problem.
[P1.4] My case rests on three pillars.
[P1.5] First, autonomy. Democratic consent assumes voters reason about claims they could in principle evaluate. A message reverse-engineered from your emotional vulnerabilities bypasses reasoning by design; its whole value to the campaign is that it works on you before you can think. Manipulation that succeeds precisely because you can't see it coming is not persuasion—it is the engineering of a choice while pretending to ask for one.
[P1.6] Second, accountability. Public campaigning is self-policing because lies can be caught and challenged. Micro-targeted emotional appeals are dark by nature: a campaign can tell ten thousand frightened people one thing and ten thousand resentful people the contradictory opposite, and no journalist, opponent, or fact-checker ever sees the full picture. You cannot rebut an argument you were never allowed to witness.
[P1.7] That breaks the basic feedback loop democracy depends on.
[P1.8] Third, exploitation of the vulnerable. These profiles are sharpest precisely where people are most fragile—the grieving, the addicted, the isolated. A system that lets political power flow to whoever can most efficiently locate and squeeze human weak points rewards the wrong skill entirely.
[P1.9] The clean line the proposition draws is this: campaigns may argue to citizens, but they may not weaponize inferred psychological profiles against them. Defending the motion means defending the difference between convincing a free mind and quietly hacking one.

[C1.3] There is no principled line between "psychological inference" and ordinary audience targeting—which means enforcement will be arbitrary, selective, and inevitably wielded by incumbents against insurgents. [C1.4] Second, the manipulation story is mostly mythology. [C1.5] Third, micro-targeting is the cheapest tool small actors have. Broadcast advertising belongs to billionaires and incumbents. Targeted digital outreach is how a school-board candidate, a union, or a minority-language community gets heard at all—how campaigns find the disengaged renter or the first-time voter that television ignores. Prohibit it, and money doesn't leave politics; it flows back to those who can afford to blanket the airwaves with the same emotional appeals, just less efficiently and less accountably.
[C1.6] The genuine problems here—covert data harvesting, undisclosed ads, contradictory messaging—have direct remedies: privacy law, mandatory public ad libraries, disclosure rules.
