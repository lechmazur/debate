[P1.1] A prison sentence takes years from a person's life. The only thing that can justify that taking is what it accomplishes — and that is where this debate is decided. Retribution looks backward at a harm already done and answers suffering with suffering. Rehabilitation looks forward and asks how we ensure the harm never happens again.
[P1.2] A society that prizes the first over the second is choosing the feeling of justice over the fact of it.
[P1.3] Start with the most concrete stake: future victims. Most prisoners will one day be released. What walks out the gate is either someone equipped to live lawfully or someone angrier, less employable, and better schooled in crime than when they entered. Purely punitive systems produce the latter: in the United States, roughly two-thirds of released prisoners are rearrested within a few years.
[P1.4] Systems built around rehabilitation — education, treatment, work, humane conditions, as in Norway — see far lower reoffending. Every point of recidivism is not an abstraction; it is a new assault, a new burglary, a new family harmed. Retribution has no answer to that ledger. It balances a past pain with a present one and calls it even, while the next victim's name is already being written.
[P1.5] Second, notice what retribution actually delivers. It cannot undo the crime or restore the victim. It expresses condemnation — but condemnation is already fully expressed by the conviction and the loss of liberty itself. The sentence is the retribution.
[P1.6] Once that is satisfied, everything punishment does beyond it — warehousing, brutalizing, or rebuilding — is a choice, and rehabilitation is the only choice that produces anything of value for anyone.
[P1.7] Note what the motion requires: not that desert is irrelevant, but that it ranks second. Proportionality can still limit how much we punish; rehabilitation should govern why and how. The state is not a bookkeeper of pain. It is a guardian of the public, and the public is protected by fewer criminals, not by more suffering.

[C1.2] My opponent claims punishment is justified solely by forward-looking utility. But when rehabilitation is elevated above retribution, the moral architecture of justice collapses.
[C1.3] First, rehabilitation cannot explain who ought to be punished or how much. Consider a calculating corporate fraudster who steals life savings, or a deposed tyrant who poses zero future danger. [C1.4] Conversely, a petty offender with stubborn antisocial traits could face indefinite confinement until state experts declare them "cured." When reform supersedes desert, punishment is no longer tied to the gravity of the crime, but to the state's power to enforce psychological conformity.
[C1.5] Second, retribution is what anchors human dignity. For victims, retribution vindicates their worth by establishing that a real moral wrong was committed and answered. [C1.6] Rehabilitation is a humane, worthwhile secondary aspiration, but it is contingent on retributive justice. Retribution determines who deserves punishment and sets its limits.
