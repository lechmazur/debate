[P1.1] The front door to routine care is the most congested, most delayed part of modern health systems. Phone queues, same-day booking ladders, emergency departments filling with coughs, rashes and sprains. Meanwhile nearly every patient already carries a symptom in their pocket at 2am and types it into a general search engine or an unvetted chatbot. That is the real status quo here: not patients waiting patiently for professional guidance, but self-triaging badly and unpredictably before arriving somewhere anyway.
[P1.2] Approved AI symptom-checkers are not the idealist's replacement for a clinician. They are the regulated replacement for an unregulated search box. "Approved" is doing heavy lifting in this motion and it should: these tools would carry pre-market evaluation, audit trails, mandatory escalation rules, and a duty to refer. When a tested tool says "this sounds self-limiting, self-care and recheck in three days," that wording was safety-evaluated and the interaction was logged.
[P1.3] When a search result says it, nobody designed the failure mode and nobody is accountable for it.
[P1.4] Three consequences follow.
[P1.5] First, demand management. Most people with self-limiting illness do not need a clinician, but they do need enough reassurance to stay home — and enough structure to return if things change. An approved tool supplies both, alongside red-flag routing. That frees genuinely scarce appointments for the people who cannot wait.
[P1.6] Second, access equity. The weekday receptionist gate works terribly for shift workers, carers, and anyone who cannot sit on hold at 8:30am. An always-available front door with interpreter support and plain-language output narrows that gap rather than widening it.
[P1.7] Third, it is a learnable surface. A supervised front door generates structured evidence about what patients ask and where routing goes wrong, so it can be corrected. Unguided searching teaches no one anything.
[P1.8] My burden is narrow: that letting patients use approved tools beats discouraging them. Discouragement does not erase demand — it only removes supervision over it.

[C1.3] A fluent answer based on an incomplete account can look authoritative without being clinically adequate. Clinicians also make mistakes, but they can probe uncertainty and arrange examination. The relevant comparison is between care pathways, not between perfect humans and imperfect machines.
[C1.4] PRO’s demand-management promise exposes the central trade-off. To release appointments, the tool must persuade some patients not to seek professional assessment. [C1.5] Neither “approved” nor “mandatory escalation” establishes that the resulting balance beats existing access routes.
