An automation tax simply makes the firm internalize the full cost of the disruption it creates instead of dumping it onto stranded taxpayers, devastated families, and hollowed-out towns.
[P1.2] The revenue must flow to retraining and adjustment support because the alternative is economic decay. Without targeted funds, displaced workers do not magically teleport into new careers; they slide into long-term unemployment or precarious gig work that further strains Medicaid, food assistance, and collapsing local retail.

[P1.4] Opponents will cry that this stifles innovation. That confuses a modest tax with a ban. The levy merely forces a firm to automate only when the efficiency gain is large enough to cover both the machine and the social cost of the humans it replaces. Currently, we implicitly subsidize reckless displacement by letting companies externalize adjustment costs onto the state and community while they monopolize the gains.
[P1.5] Consider a major retail distribution center converting to robotic palletizers. Without this policy, former forklift operators are abandoned to credential inflation and dwindling prospects. With it, the facility contributes to sector-specific programs that move those workers into robotic maintenance, logistics coordination, or care economies where human judgment remains irreplaceable and demand is growing.
