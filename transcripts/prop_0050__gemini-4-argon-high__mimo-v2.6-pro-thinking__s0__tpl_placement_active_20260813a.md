[P1.1] Aviation presents a brutal policy trap. It is one of the fastest-growing and hardest-to-decarbonize sources of greenhouse gases, yet flat aviation taxes cannot solve the problem without being deeply regressive. A small minority of affluent, frequent flyers accounts for a disproportionate share of commercial flights and emissions. When governments rely on flat ticket or fuel taxes, they face an impossible choice: set the tax low enough that a working-class family can still afford their single annual trip to visit relatives—in which case wealthy travelers effortlessly absorb the cost and keep flying every month—or set the tax high enough to genuinely curb demand, which simply prices ordinary people out of the sky while leaving the wealthiest flyers untouched.
[P1.2] Shifting toward a progressive frequent-flyer levy breaks this deadlock by aligning climate efficacy with basic fairness. Under a progressive structure, a person’s first flight or two each year faces little to no tax, protecting essential family visits and the hard-earned annual holiday. Beyond that baseline, the levy rises sharply with each additional flight. This targets the exact margin where aviation demand is both heavily concentrated and most dispensable: the fourth weekend getaway of the year, or the tenth routine corporate trip that could easily be a video call.
[P1.3] Instead of rationing aviation solely by who has the deepest pockets, a progressive levy asks those treating the atmosphere as a luxury amenity to bear the cost.
[P1.4] Furthermore, this shift is entirely practical. Governments and airlines already track unique passenger identity and passport data for border security, customs, and—ironically—airline loyalty programs. Linking a tax tier to an annual flight count, or reconciling it through standard tax filings, requires no novel surveillance, and the escalating rate can easily work alongside distance or fuel charges. When hyper-mobile flyers still choose to take that eighth or ninth flight, their steep levies generate dedicated revenue for high-speed rail and cleaner aviation technology.
[P1.5] We should tax excess, not basic access.
