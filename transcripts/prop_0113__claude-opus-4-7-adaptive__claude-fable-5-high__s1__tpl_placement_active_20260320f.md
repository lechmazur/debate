[P1.1] Walk down any city street the morning after a hot weekend. The plastic bottles in the gutter, the crumpled cans, the shrink-wrap blowing against the fence — every one of those items was designed, branded, and sold by a company that booked the revenue and walked away from the cleanup. The bill lands on a municipal sanitation budget, which means it lands on you, in property taxes and utility fees, whether you drank the soda or not.
[P1.2] That is the core injustice this motion fixes. The producer chose the package. The producer chose PET over refillable glass, multi-layer pouches over recyclable mono-materials, black plastic that optical sorters cannot read. Those are unilateral design decisions whose downstream costs — collection trucks, sorting facilities, landfill space, ocean cleanup, microplastic remediation — are currently socialized.
[P1.3] Making producers pay is not a new tax; it is ending a hidden subsidy.
[P1.4] Second, this is the only intervention that actually changes behavior upstream. Consumers can rinse and sort all they like; if Coca-Cola ships a bottle that costs more to recycle than virgin plastic costs to make, recycling will keep losing. The moment a producer's own balance sheet reflects end-of-life cost, packaging gets lighter, simpler, more recyclable, and more refillable — fast. We have seen this with extended producer responsibility schemes in Germany, British Columbia, and South Korea, where recycling rates for covered packaging sit far above the taxpayer-funded American norm.
[P1.5] Third, the cost argument cuts our way. Total system cost is lower when the entity that can redesign the package pays for handling it, because that entity has both the information and the leverage to cut waste at the source. Taxpayers have neither. Asking households to subsidize Nestlé's packaging choices is like asking neighbors to pay for a factory's smokestack scrubbers.
[P1.6] The principle is simple: if you profit from putting it on the shelf, you pay to get it off the street. That is what PRO defends.

[C1.2] The only question is *how*: through a transparent, progressive-leaning municipal budget they can vote on, or through an invisible grocery surcharge that hits the poorest families hardest, because low-income households spend the largest share of income on bottled drinks. The motion doesn't shift the burden off taxpayers; it converts it into a regressive, unaccountable consumption tax and calls that justice.
[C1.3] Second, the governance trade is terrible. Municipal sanitation is imperfect, but it answers to voters and integrates trash, recycling, and street cleaning in one accountable system. Producer-pays schemes hand collection to industry-run compliance organizations whose incentive is to *minimize* what they spend and *maximize* what they report. Where these schemes operate, the recurring pattern is fees set below true costs, "recycling rates" that count material collected rather than actually recycled, and cities still sweeping the gutters PRO describes — now with less budget control than before.
[C1.4] Third, the celebrated design incentive is mostly mush. Fees are pooled and averaged across thousands of producers, so any one company's redesign saves it pennies while the benefit spreads to competitors. If you want lighter, recyclable packaging, target the design directly — recyclability standards, virgin-plastic taxes, deposit-return on bottles — narrow tools that work without nationalizing the waste system into corporate hands.
[C1.5] So the comparison for this debate: PRO offers a moral slogan — "polluter pays" — that in practice means consumers pay more, regressively, to an opaque industry body with weak incentives. CON defends paying for a public service the public, transparent way, while regulating design directly. Cleaner streets come from accountability, not from accounting tricks about whose name is on the invoice.
