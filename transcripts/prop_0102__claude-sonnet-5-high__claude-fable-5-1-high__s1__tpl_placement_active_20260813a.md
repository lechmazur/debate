[P1.1] The timeline alone should give CON pause. Teen depression, self-harm, and suicidal ideation in the US and UK began climbing sharply around 2012, precisely when smartphones and platforms like Instagram and Snapchat became near-universal among adolescents. The rise is steepest in the exact group hit hardest by social media use: teenage girls. Boys, who use social media differently and less intensively, show a much flatter curve.
[P1.2] A "deeper problems" story struggles to explain why the timing and the demographic gradient line up so neatly with a specific technology's adoption curve rather than with, say, economic recessions, family structure, or academic pressure, all of which existed before and didn't move in lockstep this way.
[P1.3] The mechanism is not mysterious. Social media replaces unstructured, face-to-face time with peers—the kind that builds real social skills and resilience—with hours of passive scrolling and social comparison. Platforms are engineered around metrics: likes, follower counts, streaks. That turns adolescence, already a period of identity formation and social sensitivity, into a public performance scored in real time.
[P1.4] A teenage girl posting a photo isn't just sharing a moment; she's submitting herself to quantified peer judgment, at 2 a.m., alone in her room, with no adult mediation. That is a qualitatively new stressor, not just a new venue for old ones.
[P1.5] We also have interventional evidence, not just correlation. When researchers have run experiments having people cut back social media use for a few weeks, measures of loneliness and depressive symptoms improve. That's the strongest kind of evidence available short of banning the platforms outright, and it points to causation, not mere visibility.
[P1.6] CON will say troubled teens were always troubled, and social media just makes their distress visible to us. But visibility doesn't explain why sleep loss, in-person time with friends, and reported wellbeing all move in the same direction as time-on-device increases. The pattern isn't teens finding a mirror for pre-existing pain—it's a machine generating new pain at scale.

Time spent on devices explains a sliver of the variance in teen mood; the vast majority of what's making kids unhappy lies elsewhere.
[C1.3] Next, geography. Smartphones and Instagram arrived nearly everywhere in the developed world on the same schedule. [C1.4] If a universal technology produces a localized crisis, the technology isn't the cause—something local is.
[C1.5] Then measurement. [C1.6] Finally, direction. Anxious, lonely teenagers reach for their phones at 2 a.m. because they are anxious and lonely.
