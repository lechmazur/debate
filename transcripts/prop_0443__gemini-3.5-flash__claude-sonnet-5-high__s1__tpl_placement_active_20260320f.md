[P1.1] Publicly funded science belongs to the public. When taxpayers invest billions in scientific inquiry, the return on that investment should not be locked behind private paywalls or proprietary databases. Major science funders have a fiduciary and moral duty to ensure that the outputs they finance—papers, data, and code—are released under open licenses by default. This mandate is essential for economic fairness, scientific integrity, and global progress.
[P1.2] First, it corrects a massive market failure. Currently, taxpayers fund research, academic peers review it for free, and then commercial publishers lock the results behind exorbitant subscription fees. This forces public institutions to buy back the very research they funded. Mandating open licensing by default breaks this parasitic cycle, ensuring that everyone from doctors to local policymakers has instant, free access to cutting-edge knowledge.
[P1.3] Second, modern science requires the "triple threat" of open access: the paper, the raw data, and the code. A scientific paper without its underlying data and code is merely an advertisement for the research, not the science itself. In an era plagued by replication crises, transparency is the ultimate quality control. Open licenses allow independent researchers to instantly verify results, run sensitivity analyses, and reuse code, preventing costly duplication of effort and accelerating breakthroughs in fields like medicine and climate science.
[P1.4] Third, only funder mandates can solve the academic coordination problem. Individual researchers operate under intense pressure to publish in high-prestige, closed-access journals to secure tenure and promotion. They cannot change the system alone. By making open licensing a non-negotiable condition of funding, major grant-makers reshape the incentive structure overnight.
[P1.5] Publishers will adapt to where the money is, normalizing open science globally.
[P1.6] To maximize the human and economic return on scientific investment, we must unlock the vault. Mandating open licenses by default is the only way to align scientific practice with the public interest.

[C1.1] The word "default" in this motion is doing a lot of quiet work, and PRO never examines it. A blanket, funder-imposed default is a blunt instrument being applied to a landscape that is anything but uniform, and that mismatch is where this policy does real damage.
[C1.2] Start with the human cost. Publicly funded research routinely involves patient health records, genetic data, locations of endangered species, or interviews with vulnerable populations. A default-to-open rule treats these the same as astronomy datasets. Even with "opt-out" provisions, defaults are sticky by design—that is the whole point of a default—and busy researchers under grant deadlines will under-request the exemptions they need.
[C1.3] The predictable result is not utopian transparency; it is deanonymized patients and poached wildlife.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's "vault" metaphor ignores the translational pipeline that actually turns public research into public benefit. Many breakthroughs—vaccines, diagnostics, materials—only reach patients because a university can patent early-stage findings and license them to a company willing to spend the hundreds of millions needed for trials and manufacturing. Mandatory open licensing on data and code, applied by default at the funding stage, torpedoes that filing window. You don't get more open science; you get fewer treatments developed at all.
[C1.5] Third, "correcting a market failure" cannot be done by unfunded fiat. Making outputs genuinely reusable—cleaning data, documenting code, clearing licenses—costs real money and staff time. Wealthy labs at Harvard or Max Planck absorb that cost easily; a small lab in Nairobi or a junior PI on a shoestring grant cannot. A mandate without proportional resourcing doesn't level the playing field PRO describes—it entrenches exactly the inequality it claims to fix, while the compliance burden falls hardest on those with the least capacity to bear it.
[C1.6] None of this means openness is bad. It means "mandatory by default" is the wrong mechanism—rigid where science needs judgment, and blind to the very people it claims to protect.

This is why "mandatory by default" widens the very gap PRO claims to close.
[C2.5] Weigh the asymmetry: the harms here—deanonymized patients, poached species, foreclosed trials—are irreversible. The benefits PRO wants are already arriving through voluntary open-access growth, repository incentives, and funder encouragement. You don't need a blunt mandate to get the upside; you very much need to avoid one to prevent the downside.
