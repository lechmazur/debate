[P1.1] Indefinite immigration detention gives the state one of its most severe powers—confinement—without an endpoint. It can hold a person for months or years not because a court has found guilt, but because removal is merely possible or delayed. That is detention by uncertainty, and it turns a civil administrative process into open-ended punishment.
[P1.2] The motion does not abandon enforcement. It replaces drift with a workable default: release unless there is an individualized reason to detain, paired with case management that addresses the government’s real concerns. Those concerns are identity, appearance, and removal—not custody for its own sake. Check-ins, legal support, document assistance, work and housing stability, and prompt adjudication manage those risks better than prolonged confinement.
[P1.3] A person with counsel, an address, and a scheduled hearing is far easier to track than a person held for years without resolution.
[P1.4] Detention often makes compliance worse, not better. Prolonged isolation can destroy records, contacts, evidence, and health, making eventual removal or integration harder. It also diverts money from adjudicators, interpreters, and legal aid into guards and beds, while backlog grows.
[P1.5] Liberty should not depend on bureaucratic delay. Narrow, time-limited detention may still be justified for genuine flight risk or danger, but the state should bear the burden of proving that continued confinement is necessary. Presumptive release plus case management preserves enforcement while ending indefinite uncertainty.

[C1.1] While indefinite immigration detention is a deeply flawed practice that inflicts unnecessary harm on people caught in bureaucratic limbo, the proposed replacement of presumptive release paired with case management is a dangerous overcorrection that sacrifices public safety, immigration enforcement integrity, and national security for the sake of administrative idealism. [C1.2] For individuals with final removal orders, serious violent criminal records, no verifiable identity, or ties to extremist networks, presumptive release is not a humane alternative—it is a dereliction of the state’s core duty to protect its citizens. Consider a non-citizen convicted of multiple counts of sexual assault who has exhausted all legal appeals, refuses to disclose their true nationality, and has a final removal order to a country that has refused to accept them. Under the motion’s framework, this person would be released into the community with only light case management support, with no ability to detain them even if they skip check-ins or reoffend.
[C1.3] PRO claims case management outperforms detention for compliance, but real-world experience contradicts this assertion, including PRO’s claim that detention undermines compliance. In jurisdictions that have sharply limited immigration detention, no-show rates for removal hearings have risen sharply, wasting public resources on scheduled proceedings that never occur and allowing people who should be removed to remain in the country indefinitely, often reoffending while free. PRO also argues detention diverts funds from adjudication, but case management programs are not cost-free: they require full-time caseworkers, monitoring technology, legal aid, and housing support, all of which add up to costs that often exceed detention for high-risk individuals, especially when participants abscond and require new apprehension efforts.
[C1.4] The motion’s one-size-fits-all default ignores the reality that some people pose risks that cannot be managed through voluntary check-ins and support services alone.
