[P1.1] Obesity is not a moral failing or cosmetic inconvenience; it is a complex, chronic metabolic disease driving our most catastrophic and expensive health crises—type 2 diabetes, stroke, heart failure, and renal disease. Modern FDA-authorized anti-obesity medications provide profound, clinically verified disease modification, dramatically reducing cardiovascular events and metabolic dysfunction. Denying coverage for therapies that treat the root cause of these conditions, while routinely funding open-heart surgery and dialysis down the road, is both medically irrational and economically short-sighted.
[P1.2] The inevitable objection is short-term pharmacy spending. Yet the proposition explicitly resolves this by pairing coverage with value-based pricing and outcomes-based contracting. Under these frameworks, manufacturers bear financial risk: reimbursement is directly linked to sustained clinical benchmarks—such as verified weight reduction and glycemic improvements—with mandatory rebates when therapies fail to deliver. This ends open-ended price gouging, forcing drug prices down to levels justified by measurable clinical value and real-world health outcomes.
[P1.3] Moreover, a coverage mandate is necessary to overcome a fatal market failure. In a fragmented private insurance market where patients frequently switch plans, individual insurers face a perverse incentive to deny coverage, hoping to externalize the long-term costs of chronic disease onto future payers or Medicare. A universal mandate eliminates this free-rider problem, ensuring all insurers invest in prevention while benefiting from an overall healthier population.
[P1.4] Mandating coverage under value-based contracts does not inflate spending; it disciplines it. It replaces the passive financing of end-stage organ failure with accountable, outcome-driven care that treats disease before it becomes catastrophic.

Even after "value-based" discounts, supplying this population means tens of billions of dollars annually flowing to a handful of manufacturers. Discounts on enormous volume are still enormous spending.
[C1.3] The proposition treats price as the only variable; volume is the actual driver of fiscal damage.
[C1.4] The value-based contract is more rhetorical than real. Meaningful outcomes for obesity drugs—cardiovascular events, mortality, kidney preservation—take years to manifest. Contracts binding reimbursement to those endpoints are administratively brittle: they either default to easily gamed surrogates like weight-loss percentages, or expire before outcomes arrive. The GLP-1 market is dominated by two manufacturers with parallel pipelines, so the bargaining leverage PRO imagines does not exist.
[C1.5] "Value-based pricing" in concentrated markets tends to mean modest rebates on stretched list prices.
[C1.6] PRO's "free-rider" framing overstates the problem [P1.3]. A mandate does not eliminate trade-offs; it hides them in higher premiums for the majority of commercial enrollees who do not meet clinical criteria, and in slower wage growth for workers on self-funded plans.
[C1.7] Finally, mandates crowd out other care. Every dollar on these drugs is a dollar not spent on primary care, mental health, or competing therapies. Clinicians lose flexibility; employers lose the ability to design benefits around their workforce; patients lose the option to weigh trade-offs. The proposition asks us to solve one affordability crisis by manufacturing a larger one.
