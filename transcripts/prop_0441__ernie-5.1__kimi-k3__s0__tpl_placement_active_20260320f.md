[P1.1] Cash bail is a wealth test, not a danger test. It jails people who are poor but low-risk while releasing people who are wealthy but high-risk. Replacing it with validated risk-assessment tools and supervised release corrects that inversion—keeping jails focused on people who genuinely threaten public safety.
[P1.2] The evidence supports this. New Jersey's 2017 bail reform, which largely eliminated cash bail in favor of risk assessment, led to a dramatic drop in the pretrial jail population—roughly a 40 percent reduction in the first years—without a corresponding spike in violent crime or failures to appear. Kentucky adopted a similar model and saw comparable jail population declines while maintaining public safety. These are not outliers; they reflect a pattern across multiple jurisdictions that have moved away from cash-based detention.
[P1.3] How does this work mechanistically? Validated risk-assessment instruments use factors like prior convictions, failure-to-appear history, and current charges to estimate who is actually likely to reoffend or flee. Supervised release—check-ins, electronic monitoring, drug testing—provides accountability without a jail cell. The combination means resources go to supervision rather than warehousing.
[P1.4] People who would have sat in jail for weeks simply because they couldn't afford $500 instead appear for court and comply with conditions.
[P1.5] The proposition has two halves, and both hold. Jail populations fall because the primary driver of pretrial detention—inability to pay—is removed. Violent crime does not increase because the people who are detained under the new system are those the tools correctly identify as higher risk, while low-risk individuals are managed in the community with supervision that prevents the desperation and instability that jail itself often creates.
[P1.6] The alternative—keeping cash bail—means continuing to jail the poor at enormous fiscal and human cost while doing little to target actual danger. Reform is not a gamble; it is a correction.
