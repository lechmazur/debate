[P1.1] The veto is not a safeguard of stability; it is a license for paralysis. When the Security Council holds the world’s primary mandate to confront mass atrocities, a single permanent member can convert conscience into deadlock. The result is predictable: vetoes shield perpetrators, delay humanitarian relief, and teach smaller states that international law bends to raw power. Syria offers the clearest recent illustration—years of Council inaction as civilians bore the cost, not because the institution lacked options, but because a few capitals could extinguish them.
[P1.2] Proponents of the status quo claim that major powers would withdraw without the veto. That threat deserves scrutiny. Permanent membership buys agenda-setting power, intelligence access, diplomatic prestige, and influence over the rules of trade and security. No major power abandons those benefits lightly.
[P1.3] The United States does not quit the UN when it loses a vote; it lobbies, pressures, and wins the next one. Great powers want outcomes, and they usually secure them more reliably inside a reformed Council than outside a weakened one.
[P1.4] Even if a power did storm out, the comparison favors reform. A Council without a veto can still marshal legitimacy, funding, and operational reach; it can act for the many rather than the few. A Council with a veto is structurally guaranteed to fail precisely when the stakes are highest. Legitimacy is not a luxury of small states—it is the operating capital of collective security.
[P1.5] Abolishing the veto would force permanent members to earn assent through persuasion and coalition-building rather than automatic obstruction. That is not weakness; it is the price of moral authority. The UN was built to prevent catastrophe, not to insulate five states from accountability. If major powers prefer a forum they can ignore, they should leave; the world is better served by a Council that acts than one that merely endorses.

Take the veto away, and those powers do not stay around to be lobbied or persuaded as pro claims—they walk out, and they stop treating Council resolutions as even a nominal constraint on their military action. A Council that excludes the United States, China, or Russia cannot deliver a single humanitarian convoy, fund a single peacekeeping mission, or deter a single cross-border invasion, because those states supply the overwhelming share of the UN’s budget, military assets, and diplomatic leverage.

[C1.4] Veto paralysis on atrocities like Syria is ugly, but that deadlock is a feature, not a bug: it guarantees the Council will never trigger direct war between nuclear states by authorizing action against a permanent member’s core interests. A deadlocked Council that keeps great powers at the table negotiating is infinitely preferable to a morally pure, unenforceable talking shop that stands by as those powers fight unconstrained wars outside its walls. The alternative to a Council with vetoes is not a Council that acts—it is no functional collective security system at all.

[P2.4] Smaller states comply with institutions they believe are just; when legitimacy erodes, resolutions lose their practical force even when adopted. Deadlock is not a firewall; it is a delay that lets atrocities accumulate while powers fight outside the rules.
[P2.5] Finally, the budget and access argument proves too much. Peacekeeping and humanitarian logistics depend on broad coalitions, not five capitals alone. A reformed Council that finally speaks for the many would command broader legitimacy, burden-sharing, and compliance. [C2.3] Nor can broad legitimacy from smaller states replace the great-power buy-in PRO dismisses.
