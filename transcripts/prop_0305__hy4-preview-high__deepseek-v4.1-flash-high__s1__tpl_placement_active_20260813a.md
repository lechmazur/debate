[P1.1] The proposition survives contact with disgust because it is deliberately narrow: keep trophy hunting legal only where revenue is transparently channeled into conservation and local livelihoods. The question is not whether posing beside a dead lion is edifying. It is whether, under those conditions, prohibition leaves wildlife and rural communities better off. It does not.
[P1.2] Wildlife endures where it pays its way. On communal or marginal lands, a small, audited stream of hunt fees can be the difference between habitat maintained and habitat ploughed under. Namibia’s communal conservancies illustrate the mechanism: when villages derive income and jobs from wildlife, including strictly limited hunting, game guards are paid, problem animals are tolerated, and conversion to cattle or crops loses appeal. Remove that stream and the same elephant that once generated revenue becomes a costly pest.
[P1.3] Bans aimed at satisfying distant conscience instead often punish the stewards. After Botswana suspended hunting in 2014, communities that had depended on the revenue reported loss and resentment where benefits evaporated. The opponent must show ecotourism or charity would replace those funds at comparable scale on those lands. Often it cannot.
[P1.4] Photographic tourism needs scenery, safety and infrastructure; a carefully managed take—often an older male past breeding prime—is a low-volume, high-value use of country most tourists will never visit.
[P1.5] The moral intuition against paying to kill is powerful, but it is asked to do policy work it cannot do. We already accept lethal control of overabundant or dangerous animals; conservation is triage, not sentiment. Where every rand or dollar is traceable and villagers gain, the choice is not purity versus cruelty. It is intact habitat and fed families versus moral vanity purchased with emptied wild places.

[C1.1] Transparent revenue does not make a bad policy good; it only makes the bookkeeping clearer. The proposition asks us to legalise the killing of wild animals for trophies and then trust a transparency condition to civilise it. That condition cannot carry the weight, and the killing is not necessary in the first place.
[C1.2] First, legal trophy hunting is not a scalpel you can confine to well-audited conservancies. It creates an international market, an outfitting industry, and a demand for trophies. Those channels are exactly where corruption, laundering and captive-bred “hunts” flourish. A transparent village fund does not stop a forged export permit or a quota padded by officials.
[C1.3] Prohibition is cruder but harder to game; legalisation requires perfect enforcement across borders, which we do not have.
[C1.4] Second, even where every dollar is traceable, the incentive is wrong. If conservation depends on selling animals to be shot, managers are rewarded for producing shootable animals, not for resilient ecosystems. Hunters select the largest males; removing them can disrupt social groups and leave younger males to fight over dominance, with costs for reproduction. The revenue stream is also fragile: a travel ban, a bad economy, or a change in taste can collapse it.
[C1.5] Communities are left with the bill and the resentment.
[C1.6] Third, the revenue argument is not unique to hunting. Transparent conservation finance can come from photographic tourism, park fees, carbon and watershed payments, and direct community funds. Those may need support, but the proposition’s own condition—traceable money for conservation and livelihoods—does not require a dead trophy.
[C1.7] So the choice is not purity versus habitat. It is whether we normalise and entrench a lethal market for a marginal, unstable revenue stream when better non-lethal financing exists. The condition is a promise; the policy is a market in killing. Reject it.
