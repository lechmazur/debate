[P1.1] Elite universities are engines of class reproduction, not mobility. Their admissions practices—legacy preferences that can double or triple an applicant’s chances, development cases for wealthy donors, and reliance on metrics like standardized tests and costly extracurriculars that strongly correlate with family wealth—systematically advantage the already privileged. Data consistently shows that at many top schools, students from the top 1% are dramatically overrepresented compared to their share of the population, while those from the bottom 50% are severely underrepresented. Even when low-income students gain admission, they enter environments where wealth is the norm.
[P1.2] Social networks are pre-formed among the affluent, informal mentorship and internship opportunities flow through family connections, and subtle cultural signals often favor those who already possess elite backgrounds. The mobility effects, while meaningful for individuals, are statistically marginal at the societal level: the number of low-income students who benefit is dwarfed by the thousands of wealthy students whose elite status is reproduced. Moreover, the downstream effects are stark and self-perpetuating. Graduates of elite universities disproportionately occupy the most powerful positions in society, and their children then benefit from the same preferential treatment in admissions.
[P1.3] These institutions reproduce class hierarchy through both selection and socialization. Policy must confront this reality. Treating elite universities as primary engines of social mobility is not just inaccurate—it legitimizes and obscures their role in perpetuating inequality across generations. The mobility narrative serves mainly to mask their function as institutions of inheritance.
