[P1.1] We send graduates into an economy built on compound interest, payroll withholding, and leveraged debt, yet we hand them a diploma without ever requiring them to read a W-2, compare an APR, or budget a monthly cash flow. That is not educational neutrality; it is institutional negligence dressed up as freedom. Mandating a serious financial-literacy course is the only reliable way to guarantee that every student leaves high school equipped for economic self-government regardless of zip code.
[P1.2] The status quo—electives and family background—is a rigged lottery. Electives self-select for students who are already affluent or curious, while those who most need the knowledge never enroll because no one told them what they do not know. Family transmission is even more inequitable: it privileges the children of accountants and stockbrokers and punishes the children of the working poor, turning financial survival into an inherited caste marker rather than a universally taught skill.
[P1.3] The damage is concrete, measurable, and avoidable. Young adults sign predatory car leases, carry credit-card balances at rates they cannot calculate, and leave need-based aid unclaimed because they cannot navigate a FAFSA form. Meanwhile, basic tax mistakes drain paychecks year after year. A required course breaks this cycle by standardizing baseline competence in budgeting, taxation, and debt—precisely the domains where ignorance compounds into lifelong penalty.
[P1.4] Critics will cry paternalism or plead curriculum overload. But we require algebra, biology, and history not because every graduate becomes a mathematician, physician, or historian, but because democratic society demands a floor of shared competence. If decoding a tax bracket, amortizing a loan, and balancing a household budget are not part of that floor, then the diploma is an empty promise sold to teenagers who will pay the interest on our neglect.

[C1.1] The belief that a high school classroom can immunize teenagers against predatory financial systems is a comforting illusion, but it ignores how human learning actually works. The core flaw of mandatory financial literacy courses is the "decay effect." High school students taught the mechanics of amortization schedules, FAFSA forms, or tax brackets forget these complex, dry concepts long before they actually use them. It is a psychological reality: abstract information taught years before it can be applied does not stick.
[C1.2] We do not teach people how to drive five years before they can touch a steering wheel; doing so with complex financial decisions is equally futile.
[C1.3] Furthermore, school curricula are a zero-sum game. Every new graduation requirement forces a trade-off. Mandating a semester of financial literacy means sacrificing a semester of foundational mathematics, science, or the humanities. Ironically, empirical evidence suggests that stronger general cognitive training—specifically rigorous math like algebra—is actually a far better predictor of long-term financial health than specific personal finance classes.
[C1.4] Algebra teaches abstract problem-solving and logical reasoning; a financial literacy course often degenerates into outdated instructions on how to balance a paper checkbook in an era of automated, digital banking.
[C1.5] Finally, the financial landscape changes far faster than public school curricula can adapt. By the time a state-approved textbook is written, printed, and taught, tax codes have shifted, new predatory financial instruments have emerged, and the digital economy has evolved. A mandate simply creates a bureaucratic, box-checking exercise that gives school districts a false sense of accomplishment while doing nothing to protect vulnerable students. If we want to protect young adults, we should focus on structural consumer protection and simplified financial systems, not dump the burden of navigating a broken economic system onto overcrowded classrooms and distracted teenagers.

The claim that curricula move too slowly to keep up is an argument for improving curriculum governance, not for withholding a graduation requirement entirely.
[P2.5] Finally, [C1.5]’s call to shift responsibility entirely to structural consumer protection is a false dichotomy. We should regulate predators and educate prey. An overconfident consumer who thinks they understand "risk diversification" because they passed a multiple-choice quiz is far easier for predatory lenders to exploit than one who knows what they do not know.
[C2.5] If we want to protect students, we must build actual quantitative competence through rigorous mathematics and push for structural consumer protections, rather than using graduation requirements to hand teenagers a shield made of paper.
