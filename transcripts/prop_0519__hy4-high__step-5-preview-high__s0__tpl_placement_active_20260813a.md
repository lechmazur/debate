[P1.1] The question is not whether higher wages are good; it is which institution can set minimum standards in markets scattered among small restaurants, care agencies, cleaning contractors, hotels and franchises. Fragmentation is the problem: employers are too small for firm-by-firm organizing, too easy to undercut by a lower-cost competitor, and too numerous for inspectors to police workplace by workplace. Changing one number every few years leaves that structure intact.
[P1.2] Sectoral bargaining sets industry-wide floors through joint employer-worker boards. That changes the unit of competition. If every cleaning contractor or fast-food outlet in a labor market must meet that floor, the low-road employer cannot undercut the decent one by shaving wages, tips, scheduling, training or safety. The real minimum is a bundle: base pay, tip rules, predictable hours, paid waiting time, protection against misclassification, and enforcement.
[P1.3] A wage hike reaches base pay and stops.
[P1.4] It also uses better information. A general minimum wage has to average across unlike trades and regions, then return through another political fight when conditions move. A sector board can examine the economics of home care, hospitality, or retail: margins, turnover, staffing patterns, shift premiums, and phase-in effects. Employers get predictability and a level playing field; workers get a voice before standards are set.
[P1.5] Adjustment becomes routine, not a recurring crisis.
[P1.6] Repeated hikes look simple; in fragmented services that simplicity is a liability. Each round invites exemptions, subminimum categories, tip-credit fights and last-minute lobbying, then years of erosion. Enforcement still falls on the lone dishwasher or home-care aide with the least power to complain. Sectoral standards can run through licensing, contracting, franchising, payroll records and joint compliance bodies, reaching the whole sector.
[P1.7] A statutory floor should remain a backstop. But a backstop is not a system. Sectoral bargaining sets broader, better-informed, more durable and more enforceable minimum standards than asking the same blunt wage increase to solve a structural problem again and again.

[C1.1] PRO assumes sectoral bargaining arrives with a balanced worker side and a clear employer side. In fragmented services, both are missing. Care agencies, cleaning contractors, and fast-food franchises often have no industry-wide union capable of sitting across from an employer association. Without that counterpart, the "joint board" is not partnership; it is either capture by the largest firms or a fig leaf for continued wage suppression.
[C1.2] Bargaining requires power on both ends, and the fragmented structure PRO describes destroys exactly the worker concentration that makes bargaining possible.
[C1.3] Worse, the sector boundary is imaginary. A hotel housekeeper may be employed by a cleaning subcontractor, a staffing agency, or a franchisee. A home-care aide may be classified as an independent contractor. Sectoral standards attached to the "sector" leak through every gap in that definition.
[C1.4] A statutory minimum wage follows the worker, not the corporate chart, and inspectors already know how to check pay rates.
[C1.5] PRO calls repeated hikes blunt. I call them accountable. When a legislature raises the floor, the public can see the vote, the trade-offs, and the effective date. Sector boards are quieter and more remote.
[C1.6] They can be dominated by incumbent chains that prefer a uniform standard precisely because it raises costs for smaller rivals—protection dressed up as labor policy.
[C1.7] Finally, PRO ignores implementation cost. Minimum-wage enforcement piggybacks on existing wage-and-hour machinery. Sectoral bargaining requires new boards, new definitions, new coverage rules, and new compliance regimes in industries that are precisely the hardest to police. The result is not faster standards but slower ones.
[C1.8] A backstop is not a system, PRO says. But a statutory floor is not merely a backstop when the alternative has no reliable way to set itself. In fragmented services, repeated wage hikes deliver more coverage, more transparency, and more enforceability than sector boards that lack the partners to make them fair.

A minimum wage builds neither side. It leaves the atomized dishwasher or home-care aide facing the same complaint form.
[P2.2] If concentration is missing, that argues for institution-building, not repeating one number and hoping enforcement appears.

Hotel housekeeping by a subcontractor remains hotel housekeeping, with joint liability up the chain. Home care can be covered by a service-based presumption, not the label.
[P2.4] CON’s rule “follows the worker” [C1.4] only if the worker is already recognized as an employee with visible hours, tips, waiting time and deductions. Misclassification, tip credits and subminimums are holes too; hikes invite fights over each.
[P2.5] Accountability cuts the other way. A legislative vote is visible, but reveals one blunt number, not whether home care, hospitality and retail need different phase-ins, shift premiums or scheduling rules. Sector boards can publish reasons, standards and compliance data; the public authority keeps the backstop and can reject capture. Incumbent chains lobby legislatures too, often to preserve low-cost subcontracting.
[P2.6] Implementation cost [C1.7] buys reusable capacity: licensing, contracting, payroll and joint compliance can enforce sector rules, while wage hikes restart lobbying and leave the same gaps. CON’s “more coverage, more transparency, and more enforceability” [C1.8] counts one nominal wage rule. Real coverage is a bundle that survives franchising, subcontracting and misclassification. Universality on paper is not superiority in practice.
[P2.7] Sectoral bargaining reaches the fissures; repeated hikes repaint the floor.
