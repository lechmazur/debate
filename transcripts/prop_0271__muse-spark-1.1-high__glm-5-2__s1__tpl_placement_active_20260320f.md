[P1.1] An algorithmic feed is not a library. It is a sales pitch that never stops rewriting itself to keep a child watching. When a 12-year-old watches one video about anxiety, acne, or a breakup, the system learns that fear holds attention and pours more of it in. That is not what she searched for; it is what the system decided will keep her scrolling.
[P1.2] Children are developmentally primed for exactly this exploitation. Their impulse control, identity formation, and sensitivity to social comparison are still maturing, which is why we already let parents decide bedtime, medication, and schooling. The parent is the only adult in this triangle with a fiduciary duty to the child. The platform has a fiduciary duty to advertisers and engagement.
[P1.3] App makers know this, and they offer the illusion of control. YouTube Kids still autoplays. TikTok's Family Pairing can limit screen time but doesn't let a parent turn off the For You Page entirely and lock the app to following-only or chronological. Instagram lets a teen reverse a supervision setting in two taps.
[P1.4] The off-switch either doesn't exist, is hidden three menus deep, or quietly resets. Parents are left with a nuclear option: take the phone away entirely, which punishes the child for the company's design choice.
[P1.5] Giving parents more power to block or delay these features fixes that trap. It is not censorship; no video is banned. It is de-amplification by choice - chronological feed only, no recommendations until 16, no infinite scroll before homework, no Shorts on school devices. It preserves access while stopping behavioral steering.
[P1.6] Because platforms profit every time that steering succeeds, they will never volunteer real friction on their own. Power must shift from the party that benefits from compulsive use to the party legally and morally responsible for the child's welfare.

[C1.1] The proposition sounds commonsensical but rests on a flawed assumption: that the gap between current parental controls and child safety can be closed by giving parents more toggles, more granular settings, more choices to navigate. It cannot.
[C1.2] The first problem is equity. A "more power to parents" framework rewards families with the time, literacy, and engagement to configure complex settings, and abandons everyone else. The parent working two jobs, the grandparent raising grandchildren, the household where nobody has heard of a For You Page—these families don't need more settings to manage. They need products that are safe by default.
[C1.3] Every granular control we celebrate is a control that must be discovered, understood, toggled, and re-checked. That is a tax on the least-resourced families, paid by their children.
[C1.4] The second problem is false confidence. When you give a parent twenty switches, they flip a few, feel they've handled it, and stop paying attention. Meanwhile, a thirteen-year-old who can clear a cache or create a new account in ninety seconds has already bypassed everything. More controls don't close this gap; they widen it by making parents believe the problem is solved when it isn't.
[C1.5] The more complex the control surface, the faster it drifts out of date—apps update, settings reset, workarounds spread through group chats.
[C1.6] The third problem is that this framing lets the actual responsible party off the hook. If algorithmic feeds exploit children's developmental vulnerabilities, the answer is not to give every parent a personal off-switch and call it protection. We don't solve unsafe cars by giving drivers more buttons. We set design standards at the manufacturer level.
[C1.7] The proposition keeps the burden on millions of individual parents rather than on the handful of companies that design, deploy, and profit from the harmful mechanism.
[C1.8] More parental power feels like action. It is actually a concession that the product is unsafe—and a willingness to distribute the cleanup work to families least equipped to do it.
