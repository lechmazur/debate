[P1.1] The tipping system is a fundamentally exploitative compensation model that offloads employer wage obligations onto low-income workers and guilt-ridden diners, and replacing it with higher base pay plus transparent service charges delivers fairer outcomes for all stakeholders.
[P1.2] First, the current system’s hidden labor costs are no accident—they are a deliberate subsidy for restaurant owners. The federal subminimum tipped wage sits at just $2.13 per hour, a rate unchanged in over 30 years, allowing employers to pay workers pennies per hour as long as customer tips cover the gap to full minimum wage. When shifts are slow or customers tip arbitrarily, workers are left to absorb the shortfall, with no guarantee their employer will make up the difference.
[P1.3] Well-documented data shows tipped workers face wage theft at rates far higher than non-tipped peers, and are disproportionately women, immigrants, and people of color who already face systemic barriers to stable pay.
[P1.4] Second, the system relies on customer guilt to function, not genuine reward for good service. Worker testimony and basic economic incentive structures confirm most tips are driven by social pressure, not service quality, tying workers’ pay to diners’ moods and biases rather than their actual performance. This forces customers to perform a compulsory, uncomfortable calculation at the end of every meal, with no transparency about how much of their payment actually goes to staff versus being skimmed by management.
[P1.5] The alternative proposed in this motion solves both flaws at once. Higher base pay guarantees workers a stable, living wage regardless of shift traffic or customer bias. Clearly listed service charges eliminate the awkward, guilt-driven tipping calculation entirely, making the total cost of dining transparent for customers while ensuring workers receive a fixed, guaranteed share of service revenue. States that have moved to abolish the subminimum tipped wage see higher average earnings for tipped staff, no meaningful rise in restaurant closures, and reduced income inequality across the hospitality sector.
[P1.6] This is not a radical overhaul—it is a correction of a broken system that has exploited workers and burdened diners for decades. [clipped]
