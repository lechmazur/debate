[P1.1] Newborns cannot wait for perfect parental paperwork. When a condition is silent at birth and treatable only if caught in the first weeks of life, the public health default should be to find it. Genomic newborn screening, tightly limited to conditions with effective early interventions, therefore belongs on an opt-out model, not an opt-in one.
[P1.2] This is not a bid to sequence every infant for curiosity or risk scores. It is the same justification already used for phenylketonuria, congenital hypothyroidism, severe combined immunodeficiency, and spinal muscular atrophy: a time-sensitive disease, a heel-stick test, and a treatment that prevents irreversible harm. Genomics can enlarge that list. Confine the panel to actionable conditions and the central objection—that families will be burdened with uncertain or untreatable findings—loses most of its force.
[P1.3] Opt-in fails the children who need this most. It turns a population screen into a service for the organized and well-informed. Parents exhausted after delivery, families facing language barriers, and those with the least time or institutional trust are the ones who miss a signature. Their infants then carry the same treatable diseases, only without the early diagnosis that would have made treatment work.
[P1.4] Opt-out still honors refusal. It does not treat parental inertia as a morally decisive veto when inertia costs a child immunity, mobility, or cognition.
[P1.5] We already accept this structure. Hearing screens, pulse oximetry for critical congenital heart disease, and metabolic panels run as near-universal programs because a missed case is worse than the modest burden of a default test. A curated genomic panel is that program updated, not a new invasion of family life. The child’s interest in an effective intervention should set the default.
[P1.6] Parents retain the right to decline. What they should not retain is an architecture that quietly withholds a treatable diagnosis from the infants least able to absorb that loss.

[C1.2] That distinction is not a technical footnote; it is the reason the default architecture matters more, not less, when genomics enters the picture.
[C1.3] Opt-out is not a neutral setting. Defaults exert enormous gravitational force on exhausted, postpartum parents. That is not informed participation.
[C1.4] It is administrative convenience dressed up as parental autonomy. The right to decline means little when the architecture is engineered so that most people never exercise it.
[C1.5] The equity argument reverses on PRO. Opt-in, done with proper counseling and accessible information, treats genetic testing as something requiring genuine engagement. Opt-out treats mistrust as a problem to be routed around rather than addressed.
[C1.6] That deepens the wound it claims to heal.
[C1.7] The "actionable conditions only" limit is also unstable in practice. Screening panels expand. Today's curated list becomes tomorrow's pressure to add conditions with weaker evidence, because the sequencing infrastructure already exists and the incremental cost is marginal. [C1.8] Opt-out makes expansion invisible—a bureaucratic update, not a democratic choice.
[C1.9] A child's treatable condition is a real and serious interest.
