[P1.3] Second, consent presupposes real alternatives. When a dying patient "agrees" to a tenfold markup on insulin, or a worker in a company town "agrees" to unsafe wages, the choice is between the offer and disaster. Coercion by circumstance is not violent, but it hollows out consent as a moral seal. The transaction can still exploit even when nobody held a gun.
[P1.4] Third, consent binds only the parties at the table. Two people can consensually agree to dump waste upstream of a village, to fix prices between them, or to sell a rainforest one of them owns. Consent between A and B does nothing to justify what the deal imposes on C. Externalities are the clearest case where a signed contract is morally incomplete.
[P1.5] Fourth, some things resist commodification regardless of willingness. Selling votes, selling a kidney under desperation, selling oneself into indentured labor — societies routinely refuse to enforce these contracts not because consent was faked, but because the object of the transaction implicates dignity, democratic equality, or future autonomy that a moment's agreement cannot legitimately trade away.
[P1.6] So the standard I ask the judges to use is simple: a transaction is ethical only when consent is joined by adequate information, meaningful alternatives, respect for third parties, and a subject matter fit for exchange.
