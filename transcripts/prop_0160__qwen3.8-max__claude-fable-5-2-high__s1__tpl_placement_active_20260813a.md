[P1.1] The proposition draws a bright line at devices that write to or stimulate the brain because they are not ordinary inputs like sound, text, or exercise. They intervene directly in the organ that produces thought, mood, memory, and self-control. Once stimulation becomes bidirectional and adaptive, the user is no longer merely choosing a product; the product can shape the chooser. A market optimized for comfort and novelty is not equipped to police changes to the neural basis of consent itself.
[P1.2] Consumer markets assume reversible, understandable risks: if a phone app harms you, you delete it, report it, seek a refund. That assumption collapses with neural stimulation. Harms can be subtle, delayed, and hard to attribute: changes in motivation, anxiety, sleep architecture, impulse control, or dependence on a device to feel normal. A consumer headset marketed for focus might overstimulate reward circuits, disrupt natural sleep, or create mood crashes after updates.
[P1.3] Users cannot calibrate these effects without medical diagnostics, and firms racing for engagement have every incentive to maximize perceived benefit while discounting long-term costs.
[P1.4] The second problem is control. A write-capable consumer device is an attack surface. Faulty firmware, predatory subscription features, or hacking could turn stimulation into coercion, harassment, or surveillance-adjacent manipulation. Medical regulation does not eliminate risk, but it imposes standards: defined indications, dose limits, clinical evaluation, informed consent, cybersecurity audits, adverse-event reporting, and recall authority.
[P1.5] It also protects people who are most vulnerable—children, people with depression, chronic pain, or cognitive disabilities—before they become test subjects in an unregulated marketplace.
[P1.6] This is not a ban on innovation. It channels high-risk neural intervention into settings where benefits can be demonstrated and monitored, while leaving low-risk read-only devices to ordinary rules. The real question is whether citizens should face invasive, preference-shaping technologies with no more protection than buying a gadget. Because mental integrity and agency deserve stronger safeguards, the proposition should prevail.
