[P1.1] The proposition is deliberately narrow, and that is its strength. It does not let religious schools or employers impose dress norms on a captive audience. It permits faith-based expectations only where participation is genuinely voluntary and exit is realistic. Within those bounds, dress is part of institutional identity, not a triviality law should flatten.
[P1.2] Religious communities express belief through appearance: modesty, head coverings, plain dress, symbols of membership. A school or employer that cannot ask voluntary participants to share even modest dress expectations must either abandon its ethos or exclude people who do not fit the secular default. That is not neutrality; it makes the majority’s dress code invisible and compulsory.
[P1.3] The conditions do the moral work. “Genuinely voluntary” rules out coercion, surprise rules, and pressure on dependents. “Realistic alternatives” rules out the theoretical exit: a lone religious employer in a small town, or a school with no comparable option, cannot use the escape hatch. If a worker truly has nowhere else to go, the proposition gives no room. If a child has no feasible alternative school, it gives no room. So the opponent’s strongest examples—the trapped employee, the child whose family has no choice—are cases where the safeguards switch the permission off, not counterexamples to the motion.
[P1.4] That is the central comparison. A blanket ban protects some from bad faith by denying all religious institutions the ability to shape their common life, including for willing members. Conditional room targets the actual wrong—coercion—while preserving pluralism. We can police voluntariness and alternatives case by case.
[P1.5] We cannot restore a school’s erased mission after forcing it to adopt secular dress norms.
[P1.6] A Catholic school may ask teachers to model modest dress; a Muslim-owned shop may ask customer-facing staff to dress modestly; both must show real choice. Where they can, the law should allow it. Where they cannot, it should not. That is the proposition, and it is the better rule.
