[P1.1] Essential insurance should protect people against bad luck, not make bad luck the reason they cannot afford protection. Actuarial pricing can accurately predict someone’s costs while producing an unacceptable result: the person who most needs coverage faces the highest barrier to obtaining it. Statistical accuracy is not a sufficient principle for distributing access to necessities.
[P1.2] Consider two people seeking basic health coverage. One has an inherited condition; the other does not. Charging the first more may balance each customer’s expected premiums and claims. But it treats an unchosen disadvantage as a financial obligation that society need not share.
[P1.3] Social solidarity means precisely the opposite: when fundamental needs are at stake, protection should not depend entirely on the biological lottery.
[P1.4] The legal response should be targeted, not indiscriminate. For essential benefits, law should prohibit or cap premium differences based on health status and comparable unchosen risks, guarantee access, and establish broad financing. This does not require identical prices for every insurance product, or prohibit proportionate incentives for genuinely preventable risks. It places a floor beneath competition: insurers may compete over service and efficiency, but not make essential protection unaffordable simply because someone is predictably expensive.
[P1.5] Nor does solidarity repeal arithmetic. Higher-risk claims must be funded through broad participation, shared contributions, and public support where necessary. [P1.6] These are complements to premium limits, not reasons to reject them.
[P1.7] The objection that healthier people pay more identifies the mechanism of solidarity, not its defeat. They help fund protection against circumstances they or their families could also face. That is how solidarity actually works — we help the unlucky through collective contribution calibrated to ability to pay. Premium caps do something quite different: they hide the cost inside the price everyone pays, which is a poll tax on insurance.
[C1.3] A young low-income worker with no health conditions then subsidizes a higher-risk enrollee with more income. Calling that "solidarity" dignifies a regressive transfer from poor healthy people to wealthy sick people as if it were a moral achievement. [C1.4] Worse, the caps are self-defeating on the motion's own goal of access. Healthy enrollees, facing artificially high premiums, drop out; the pool deteriorates and prices rise again. That is the standard adverse-selection spiral, and it does not announce itself as rationing — but the person who cannot find a carrier at all has lost access no less than the person priced out.
[C1.5] Finally, the motion's scope is incoherent. "Essential coverage" is a legislative label, not a natural kind, and every benefit placed inside the cap becomes a magnet for cost-shifting and a battlefield for lobbying. Actuarial pricing at least has the merit of transparency: costs are visible and can then be subsidized openly. The proposition would declare solidarity satisfied by making costs invisible.
