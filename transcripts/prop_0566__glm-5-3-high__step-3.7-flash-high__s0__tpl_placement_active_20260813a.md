[P1.1] Legacy and donor preferences are admissions decisions that reward students for what someone else did: a parent's diploma, a grandparent's check. That is the core problem, and no amount of institutional hand-waving about "community" changes it. Universities are gatekeepers to opportunity; when they allocate spots by lineage and money rather than merit and potential, they corrupt their own justification for existing.
[P1.2] Three arguments carry this case.
[P1.3] First, fairness. Every admissions cycle is zero-sum: a seat given to a less-qualified legacy applicant is a seat taken from a more qualified one—disproportionately from students without family wealth or family alumni, which in practice means poorer students and students of color. Legacy preference is affirmative action for the already advantaged. It is the one preference system that nobody can defend on diversity grounds, because it actively works against diversity.
[P1.4] Even a modest boost at a highly selective school flips outcomes, since most rejected applicants are fully capable of succeeding there; the tiebreakers decide everything.
[P1.5] Second, integrity. Donor preferences are worse, because they are openly transactional: the university sells access to its degree. The moment admissions decisions track expected donations, "holistic review" becomes a price list. We would never tolerate a court ruling influenced by a litigant's charitable giving; a university distributing society's most valuable credentials should be held to the same standard.
[P1.6] The harm isn't just to rejected applicants—it's to the credibility of every diploma the institution issues.
[P1.7] Third, the defenses don't hold. "Legacy builds community and giving"—private schools that have cut legacy preferences haven't collapsed, and donors motivated by genuine loyalty don't need a quid pro quo on their grandchild's application. If your generosity requires buying your way past the admissions committee, it was never philanthropy. And "universities need the money" is a confession, not an argument: it says the institution will trade fairness for funds whenever the price is right.
[P1.8] Sharp limits—narrow exceptions, strict transparency—are the only way to keep admissions from being quietly auctioned. Anything less leaves the auction running.

[C1.4] Third, PRO’s framing of donor preferences as a "price list" misrepresents university philanthropy.
