[P1.1] Every safety-net program faces the same design question: how do you help people through hard times without quietly trapping them there? Work requirements answer it by tying assistance for able-bodied adults to engagement with work, training, or volunteering—and the proposition asks a comparative question: do they reduce dependency more than they harm people who genuinely cannot comply? They do, for three reasons.
[P1.2] First, the requirements are built around exemptions for exactly the people CON will invoke. Disabled adults, pregnant women, caretakers of young children, and the elderly are categorically exempt from SNAP's able-bodied-adult rules, and Medicaid proposals follow the same architecture. The policy targets a narrow population: working-age, non-disabled adults without dependents. For that group, "cannot comply" is rare almost by definition; the genuinely-unable are supposed to be screened out before the requirement ever bites.
[P1.3] Second, dependency is a real, measurable harm, not a rhetorical one. Long spells out of the labor force erode skills, references, and confidence; the longer the gap, the harder re-entry becomes. The 1996 welfare reform showed this dynamic at national scale: when cash assistance became conditional on work, employment among single mothers rose substantially and caseloads fell dramatically, with much of the decline reflecting people moving into jobs—jobs that opened access to the EITC, wage growth, and eventual independence the check alone never provided.
[P1.4] A requirement that nudges someone into the labor market at 26 changes the trajectory of decades.
[P1.5] Third, when harm does occur, it is overwhelmingly administrative—missed paperwork, confusing reporting portals—not inability to work. [P1.6] So the ledger is this: broad, durable gains in employment and self-sufficiency for the able majority, against narrow, exemption-protected, and correctable harms at the margins. That comparison is the debate, and it favors the requirement.
