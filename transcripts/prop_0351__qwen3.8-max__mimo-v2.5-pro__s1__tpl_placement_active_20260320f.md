[P1.1] The central question is not whether junior employees can complete tasks at home; many can. It is whether they can become the professionals their careers require them to be. Career development for juniors is built less through formal training than through ambient apprenticeship: overhearing how seniors frame a client problem, asking a thirty-second question before a meeting, being noticed when they stay late to fix an error, or receiving the quick correction that prevents a bad habit from hardening. Remote work strips away that layer.
[P1.2] A junior working from home gets scheduled check-ins and screensharing, but misses the corridor, the post-meeting debrief, and the casual moments where trust is transferred.
[P1.3] This damage is systematically under-admitted. Employers often point to stable output, retention, and lower real-estate costs, because those measures look good in quarterly reviews. But career development compounds over years, and the losses appear later: weaker professional networks, thinner judgment, slower sponsorship, and fewer stretch assignments. Workers, meanwhile, rarely admit the cost because flexibility is real and valuable, and juniors especially fear sounding ungrateful or expendable.
[P1.4] The result is a comfortable silence: everyone praises remote arrangements while quietly worrying that younger staff are not being formed.
[P1.5] The strongest evidence is the kind of mistake that becomes visible only in hindsight. Consider a new consultant who can produce slides at home but never learns how a partner redirects a tense client conversation, because she is not in the room. Consider a junior developer who completes tickets but lacks the informal code reviews and lunch-table architecture talk that would have accelerated his judgment. Employers may call this a training problem; it is better understood as an environment problem.
[P1.6] Remote work can preserve individual tasks, but it weakens the career ecosystem around them. Because that weakening is delayed, hard to measure, and easy to rationalize, it is larger than most organizations are willing to admit.
