[P1.1] When Greek pensioners queued at shuttered ATMs in the summer of 2015, they were not witnessing European solidarity in action. [P1.2] The case rests on three pillars.
[P1.3] First, the response converted partners into creditors and debtors. The bailouts were not transfers but loans, initially at punitive rates, policed by a Troika that dictated pension cuts, wage levels, and privatizations in Athens, Lisbon, and Dublin. Whatever the macroeconomic merits, the political form was unmistakable: foreign officials overruling elected governments. When Papandreou proposed a referendum in 2011, he was effectively forced out; technocratic cabinets were installed in Greece and Italy.
[P1.4] Citizens learned that democratic choice stopped where eurozone discipline began. That is a recipe for distrust, not solidarity.
[P1.5] Second, the damage is measurable in politics itself. Trust in EU institutions collapsed in the debtor states — surveys throughout the decade showed dramatic drops in Greece, Spain, and Portugal. And distrust flowed both ways: the AfD was literally founded as an anti-bailout party in Germany; Syriza, Podemos, and Five Star surged in the South. The crisis manufactured a creditor narrative of lazy southerners and a debtor narrative of German domination — stereotypes that poisoned European politics for a decade and fed directly into later ruptures.
[P1.6] Third, the "solidarity" defense proves the point. Every rescue came late, grudgingly, after markets forced the issue, and wrapped in moralizing conditions. Keeping the euro intact preserved a currency; it did not preserve fellow-feeling. A solidarity that arrives at gunpoint, priced for punishment, is experienced as coercion.
[P1.7] The honest comparison: the euro survived, but the belief that Europeans would stand by each other was the crisis's deepest casualty. That is precisely what the motion asserts.
