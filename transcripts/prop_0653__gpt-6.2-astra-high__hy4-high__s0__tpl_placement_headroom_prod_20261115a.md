[P1.1] A company deciding whom to train can look at the work its employees have done and ask who wants the opportunity. It should not be able to buy a ranking of who will produce more, organize coworkers, or quit. The danger is not just that a forecast might be wrong. The company controls the conditions under which it appears to come true.
[P1.2] Consider two machinists with equally good work records. A model predicts lower future output for one because workers on her shift historically produced less. Her supervisor gives the other machinist training on the faster equipment. A year later, the output gap is real.
[P1.3] What would she appeal? Her recorded output is accurate. What she lost was the chance to show what she could do. A quit score works the same way: pass someone over because he might leave, and you give him a reason to go.
[P1.4] For unionization, even perfect accuracy is an objection, not a defense. Knowing which workers are likely to organize lets management concentrate pressure before coworkers can act together. A rule against retaliation asks a worker to prove why a supervisor changed her assignment. A ban on individual prediction asks whether the company commissioned or used the score, something an auditor can investigate without reading a supervisor’s mind.
[P1.5] Employers can still forecast staffing needs across a department, evaluate completed work, and offer retention benefits to everyone on a hard-to-fill shift. If training is scarce, use a work sample and publish the selection criteria. These alternatives give management information it needs and workers something they can answer. The ban draws the line where planning becomes a judgment about a particular person’s unwritten future, made by the institution with the power to write it.

[C1.3] Govern the use, not the instrument. Require scores to be provisional, job-related, contestable, and never the sole basis for adverse action. Used under those limits, AI can expose bias instead of laundering it. A quit forecast flagging a bad shift schedule tells an employer to fix schedules and offer retention benefits, not blacklist "flight risks."

[C1.5] A blanket ban cannot distinguish a retaliatory manager from the compliance officer trying to stop retaliation, while inviting evasion: call the score "engagement segmentation" and the model survives; ban the AI label and the same inference returns as consultant judgment. A better regime demands disclosure, purpose limits, impact audits, human review, and contestable decisions. It allows validated forecasts for training, staffing, workload repair, and universal retention support, while prohibiting adverse action based solely on a score. That attacks opacity and self-fulfilling treatment directly.
[C1.6] A ban attacks the tool, leaves managerial power untouched, and sacrifices worker protections that require early risk detection.
