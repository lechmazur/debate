[P1.1] When an employer demands forty hours a week of research, copywriting, or event coordination and pays nothing, that is not an educational opportunity—it is a minimum wage violation rebranded as a stepping stone. By exempting genuinely educational placements like nursing clinicals or supervised student teaching, this motion protects real pedagogy where trainees are primarily learning within an accredited curriculum. We are targeting a specific abuse: demanding unpaid productive labor as the price of admission to a profession.
[P1.2] First, these unpaid internships operate as a ruthless socioeconomic filter. In fields like journalism, politics, publishing, arts, and advocacy, internships are the de facto gateway to full-time hiring. Yet working for free in major cities requires a heavy subsidy: someone else must pay the intern’s rent, groceries, and transit. A talented graduate from a working-class family who needs immediate wages to survive is locked out, ceding the pipeline to wealthier peers bankrolled by their parents.
[P1.3] Restricting unpaid roles dismantles this pay-to-play barrier, ensuring that influential professions select for merit rather than family wealth.
[P1.4] Second, unpaid internships cannibalize paid entry-level jobs. Minimum wage laws exist because when bargaining power is unequal, desperate workers will accept exploitative terms just to get a foot in the door. Young graduates face a collective action trap: as long as unpaid labor is legal, refusing an unpaid post merely hands the resume line to a rival. Consequently, organizations replace paid junior staff with rotating cohorts of free interns, suppressing youth wages and undercutting ethical competitors who actually pay their trainees.
[P1.5] Opponents will claim that restrictions destroy opportunities. Yet if the work is genuinely needed, employers will pay at least the minimum wage to get it done. If the work is neither valuable to the firm nor genuinely educational for the trainee, it was a dead-end exploit from the start. Governments do not let warehouses pay workers in "experience," and white-collar employers deserve no special exemption.
