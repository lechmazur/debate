[P1.1] Publicly funded research should become a public resource, not a collection of permission barriers. Major science funders should require papers, data, and code to carry open licenses by default, because the public return on research depends on what others can do with it—not merely whether it gets published.
[P1.2] An open license matters beyond free reading. A paper might be readable while its figures cannot readily be reused, its underlying data remain inaccessible, and its software cannot legally be adapted. Releasing all three with clear reuse rights lets another laboratory check the analysis, a teacher adapt the explanation, and a small company build a useful application without negotiating separate permissions.
[P1.3] That improves both reliability and productivity. Imagine a publicly funded study whose conclusion depends on a data-cleaning decision. The paper alone may not reveal its significance; the data and code let others test it. Openness does not guarantee correctness, but it makes scrutiny possible and enables researchers to build on existing work rather than reconstruct it.
[P1.4] A mandate is necessary because voluntary openness leaves collective benefits dependent on individual incentives. Researchers face pressure to publish quickly, while documentation and sharing take effort. Funders can make that effort an ordinary, budgeted research obligation, supporting repositories and preparation costs. Compliance need not mean paying an expensive journal: openly licensed repository copies can provide access.
[P1.5] “By default” is crucial. Identifiable patient records, genuinely dangerous information, and documented legal restrictions require justified exceptions or controlled access—not indiscriminate publication. A specific commercialization need can warrant a narrowly limited delay, rather than indefinite exclusion. The rule should demand reasons for withholding, not reasons for sharing.
[P1.6] The alternative makes restricted reuse the starting point even when no concrete harm justifies it. That sacrifices opportunities across institutions, countries, and businesses to preserve gatekeeping. Public funders should finance discovery and ensure that its benefits can travel. Openness should be the rule; restriction should carry the burden of proof.

A mandate built on them would slow translation, distort collaboration, and dump risk onto the patients and partners funders claim to serve.
[C1.2] Access is not an open license. Taxpayers can already read the paper, inspect the report, and, where appropriate, obtain deposited data. That is the legitimate public return. An open license goes further: remixing, commercial reuse, downstream products without negotiation.
[C1.3] That extra step can extinguish patentability, collapse a startup’s exclusive path, and turn a hospital agreement into a compliance violation. Calling that “the public resource” redefines ownership until partnered work cannot survive.
[C1.4] Papers, data, and code are not one object. Figures may belong to a coauthor or journal; data may carry patient traces, community knowledge, or industry samples; code may embed libraries no one is free to relicense. [C1.5] The public also needs products, not just repositories. A compound or diagnostic often reaches patients only if a partner can take a limited exclusive stake. Open-by-default licensing makes that bargain rarer. The result is not more reuse; it is more abandoned translation.
[C1.6] Exceptions cannot carry this weight. Dual-use biology and identifiable genomics need caution first. A rule demanding reasons for withholding will produce dumped code, thinner datasets, and fewer co-funded projects—compliance theater in place of science. Funders should require publication, deposit, and justified sharing, not a default tax of open licenses on every output.
