[P1.1] A supermarket that throws away edible food is not managing inventory. It is converting remaining social value into private convenience. Bread still safe at closing, yogurt a day from date, bruised apples: all of it still feeds people. Treating that surplus as ordinary shrink is a policy choice, not a law of nature.
[P1.2] The store captures the gain from overstocking for full shelves and variety. The public pays for landfill gas, wasted water and farmland, collection costs, and hunger a few blocks away. Disposal looks cheap on the ledger because those harms sit off the books. Requiring donation or a sharp discount before disposal forces the firm to recover the last honest use instead of destroying food for appearance.
[P1.3] Donation already works where pickup and basic legal protection exist. Charities take surplus that still meets safety rules. Evening markdowns work even more cleanly, moving last-hour stock to customers who want it. Neither path sells spoiled food.
[P1.4] Both end the fiction that throwing calories away is just another line item.
[P1.5] Voluntary codes fail because waste remains cheaper than coordination. Managers rewarded for perfect shelves and few markdowns will keep over-ordering. Liability fears collapse once good-faith donors are protected, as they already are in many places. Logistics are no mystery to firms that run cold chains, warehouses, and forecasts.
[P1.6] If a chain can stock seasonal candy to the hour, it can route Tuesday bread to a pantry or a discount bin.
[P1.7] The status quo destroys edible food so a store never looks empty. That is not efficiency. It is a subsidy for cosmetic abundance, paid by the climate, the taxpayer, and people who need a meal. Require the last use—give it away or cut the price hard—before anyone may call it trash.

[C1.1] The proposition asks us to trade a flexible, already-functioning system for a rigid legal mandate, and that trade is a bad one. My opponent's own opening admits the fatal weakness: donation and markdown "already work" where infrastructure exists. If the mechanism already works voluntarily, the case for coercion collapses to a matter of degree, not principle, and a blunt legal requirement is the wrong tool to fix a degree problem.
[C1.2] Consider what "required" actually means in practice. A statute has to define edible, sharply discounted, and before disposal with enough precision to prosecute violations. That precision is impossible at the scale of a supermarket's daily churn: a dented can, a bruised melon, a yogurt six hours from its printed date all sit in a gray zone that store staff must judge in real time. Turn that judgment into a legal exposure and managers will not become generous, they will become risk-averse.
[C1.3] The safest response to an ambiguous mandate is to order less, not donate more, shrinking selection and availability precisely in the neighborhoods that rely on that store having stock at closing time.
[C1.4] Mandates also fall hardest where PRO's own vocabulary of "cosmetic abundance" matters least: thin-margin grocers in lower-income areas who cannot absorb sorting, packaging, tracking, and transport costs for compliance the way a national chain's logistics division can. The result is closures or consolidation, not more donated bread.
[C1.5] Liability is not a solved problem either. Good-faith donor protections vary and rarely cover every edge case, especially once a legal duty to donate exists rather than a voluntary act. A store forced to give away food it did not choose to give faces different liability exposure than one that volunteers it.
[C1.6] The honest fix is incentives, not command: tax credit for verified donations, standardized liability shields, and public infrastructure for pickup. That gets PRO's result without the enforcement mess, the closures, or the incentive to simply order less.
