When housing costs spiral upward, they do not merely reduce discretionary spending; they push working-class families to the brink of homelessness and permanently lock younger generations out of homeownership and wealth accumulation. While a recession causes acute, temporary spikes in unemployment, runaway housing inflation creates a permanent underclass.
[P1.3] It acts as a crushing, inescapable tax. Central banks cannot claim to protect public welfare while allowing the cost of basic survival to vastly outpace wage growth.
[P1.4] Second, unchecked housing inflation breeds catastrophic systemic risk. [P1.5] Finally, runaway housing costs suffocate long-term economic dynamism. The recent American experience is instructive: aggressive hikes cooled sales, yet payments on a typical new mortgage soared and construction thinned. House prices may soften; the cost of housing — the payment against income — often gets worse.
[C1.3] PRO's mechanism does not cure the disease; it compounds it.
[C1.4] Second, weigh the trade PRO actually proposes, not the one they describe. They call recession "temporary" and housing inflation a "permanent underclass." High rent stretches a budget; a lost paycheck obliterates it.
[C1.5] Recessions produce evictions, foreclosures, and homelessness: the precise harms [P1.2] invokes as justification. You do not make housing affordable by making people jobless.
[C1.6] Third, PRO claims hawkishness prevents systemic crisis. Deliberately courting recession in a housing-leveraged financial system is how you get one. Popping a bubble by engineering a downturn does not defuse it; it detonates it in your hands.
[C1.7] Finally, the actual cures for housing costs — zoning, land, construction — belong to legislatures, not monetary authorities. The motion recruits the wrong institution, armed with the wrong tool, paid for with the wrong people's jobs. It trades a chronic problem we can fix for an acute catastrophe we cannot control.
