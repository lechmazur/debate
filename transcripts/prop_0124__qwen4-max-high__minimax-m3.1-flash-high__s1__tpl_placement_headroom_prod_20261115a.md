[P1.1] The proposition is narrow, and its narrowness is the argument. Not anyone who is tired of life. A competent adult, with a terminal or degenerative condition, whose suffering they themselves judge unbearable, asking repeatedly, assessed by independent clinicians, with palliative care offered and refused or exhausted. Every safeguard CON wants can be written into the law — and the law is where they belong, because the alternative to a regulated death is not a protected life.
[P1.2] It is an unregulated death: a hoarded bottle, a firearm, a lonely hotel room, a family who finds the body and never recovers.
[P1.3] Consider the man with advanced ALS. He can already lawfully refuse a ventilator, refuse a feeding tube, and be sedated until he stops breathing. Medicine calls that a good death and calls it natural. He may not ask for the same endpoint on a Tuesday afternoon, surrounded by his children, with the certainty that it will work.
[P1.4] CON must explain why the slow version is dignity and the fast version is a crime — when the patient prefers the second and the diagnosis is identical.
[P1.5] That is the clash: not life versus death, but who decides how much suffering a competent person must absorb before they are permitted to stop. CON's position transfers that decision from the person living inside the body to the state. That is an enormous coercion, and it needs a justification stronger than unease.
[P1.6] Safeguards are not a hope; they are the mechanism: capacity assessment, waiting periods, independent second opinions, mandatory reporting, after-the-fact review. In jurisdictions that have legalized, the reported picture is not mass coercion but a small fraction of deaths, mostly older people with cancer or advanced neurological disease. Enforcement gaps argue for tighter enforcement, not for driving the practice back into the dark.

[C1.1] The proposition asks the state to authorize an irreversible act through judgments safeguards can never make infallible. “Competent” can be assessed, yet degenerative illness may fluctuate or bring depression, medication effects, and family dependence. “Terminal” describes a trajectory, not a date; “unbearable” may change when treatment, care, or support improves. Procedures can improve accuracy; they cannot prevent a temporary wish from becoming permanent death.
[C1.2] That danger differs categorically from medical decisions. Refusing treatment may allow time to reconsider; assisted dying cannot. If assessment is wrong, evidence cannot restore the patient’s life. The law would impose a permanent consequence through fallible judgments made by people other than the patient.
[C1.3] PRO says patients may already refuse life-sustaining treatment. But that analogy exposes the issue: refusing dialysis does not compel lethal assistance, and withdrawal often preserves reversal or stabilization. Assisted dying makes the state the guarantor of an outcome selected through institutional assessment. The question is therefore whether the state should possess such irreversible power at all, not merely whether procedures are well drafted.
[C1.4] Nor is refusal the only alternative to regulated death. Better pain control, sedation for refractory distress, specialist palliative care, and caregiver support can often reduce suffering without an irreversible decision. They may be harder and less attractive than a quick legal death, but difficulty is not sufficient reason to authorize permanent error.
[C1.5] Mercy makes the aim compelling, but it does not settle means, authority, or safeguards. Nor does it prove that less irreversible forms of care cannot meet the same need. Adding procedures administers risks rather than answering them.
[C1.6] Legislation cannot make coercion, bias, and mistaken judgments harmless. Because assisted dying is unrecoverable, the state should not acquire authority to impose it, even under safeguards, unless the central risk of permanent mistake is answered rather than merely regulated.
