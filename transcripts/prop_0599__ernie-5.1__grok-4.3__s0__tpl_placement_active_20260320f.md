[P1.1] Good morning. The African Union faces a stark choice: continue with fragmented, paper‑based identity systems that keep hundreds of millions invisible to the state, or adopt a continent‑wide digital ID that unlocks health care, education, finance and social protection – even if it raises surveillance concerns. I argue the former is far more dangerous, and the AU should move forward.
[P1.2] First, the concrete gains are undeniable. In Kenya, the Huduma Namba programme linked a single number to tax, health and voting records, cutting duplicate registrations by a third and speeding up cash‑transfer payouts. Rwanda’s national ID enabled rapid contact‑tracing during Ebola and allowed over 80 % of adults to open bank accounts. Ghana’s Ghana Card reduced voter fraud and gave informal workers a proof of residence for credit.
[P1.3] Across the continent, a unified digital ID would let a farmer in Mali prove citizenship to claim a subsidy, let a mother in Tanzania register her child for school, and let a small trader in Nigeria access a loan without bribes. These are not abstract promises; they are measurable improvements in lives that are currently denied.
[P1.4] Second, the surveillance risk is not an inevitable by‑product; it is a governance problem that can be managed. The AU can embed privacy‑by‑design standards, limit data sharing to purpose‑specific APIs, and create an independent oversight body. Compare that to the present reality: without any ID, governments already rely on ad‑hoc surveillance, informal data brokers, and corrupt officials to decide who gets services. A transparent digital framework actually reduces arbitrary power.
[P1.5] The cost of inaction is higher. Every year millions die from preventable disease, miss school, or stay trapped in informal economies because they cannot prove who they are. A continent‑wide digital ID is the fastest, most scalable tool to fix that. The AU should adopt it, set strong safeguards, and accept the manageable surveillance trade‑off because the alternative – continued exclusion – is the true danger.

Scaling that architecture removes the friction that fragmented records currently impose and creates a single point where any future regime can disable an entire population’s access to services.
[C1.2] Service delivery gains advertised in national pilots do not survive that expansion. Kenya’s Huduma Namba produced duplicate reductions mainly by excluding those whose biometrics failed to register cleanly, while local officials retained discretion over corrections. Rwanda’s tracing capacity rested on dense administrative reach that most AU members lack. When the same infrastructure operates across borders with uneven rule of law, the efficiency claim collapses into selective enforcement rather than universal access.
[C1.3] Privacy-by-design language cannot override the political reality that AU decisions require consensus among governments holding sharply different standards on data retention and dissent. Once the common database exists, oversight bodies become targets for capture or defunding; technical limits on sharing are overridden by emergency decrees that recur across the continent. The result is not managed risk but normalised mass visibility without corresponding accountability.
[C1.4] Alternatives that improve delivery without this concentration already exist: portable paper credentials backed by local verification, sector-specific digital tokens, or cash-transfer systems that rely on community lists rather than central biometrics. These keep data dispersed and therefore less attractive for political weaponisation. The proposition’s willingness to accept heightened surveillance therefore rests on an unsubstantiated faith that the AU can impose restraints its own members routinely evade at smaller scale.
