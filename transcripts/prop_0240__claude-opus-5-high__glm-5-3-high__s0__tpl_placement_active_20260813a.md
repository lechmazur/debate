[P1.1] A public decision is never just a technical question with a right answer hiding inside it. Ask an epidemiologist how to minimize deaths and you get one answer; ask what a year of a child's schooling is worth against a marginal reduction in transmission risk, and expertise runs out. Ask an economist the fastest path to growth and you may get an answer; ask how much of a mill town's life should be spent to buy it, and you are asking a question no credential answers.
[P1.2] Every consequential choice bundles a technical component inside a moral one. Competence tells you the cost of the route. Only the people who will pay it can say whether it's worth paying.
[P1.3] That's why legitimacy is prior, not parallel. It decides who gets to name the goal, and the goal defines what counts as "competent" in the first place. Technocratic competence is a means; a means cannot outrank the thing that sets its ends.
[P1.4] Second, legitimacy is not decoration on a good policy — it is a load-bearing part of whether the policy works. A rule people regard as rightfully made gets obeyed, funded, and sustained through the hard years. A rule imposed over them gets evaded, litigated, and repealed by the next backlash. The mid-century era of expert-designed urban renewal was full of highly credentialed planners driving expressways through neighborhoods that had no vote; the plans were sophisticated, and they were catastrophic, and the people who bore the cost had no lever to pull.
[P1.5] Brilliance without consent produces exactly that: irreversible mistakes.
[P1.6] Third, and decisively: expertise has no mechanism for firing itself. Experts disagree, capture happens, models fail, and consensus in one decade becomes embarrassment in the next. Democracy's distinctive value is not that voters are smarter — it's that they can remove a government without violence when it is wrong.
[P1.7] Competence without legitimacy is a proposal. Legitimacy, even with imperfect competence, is a government that can correct itself.
