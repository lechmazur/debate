[P1.1] A healthy democracy demands citizens capable of holding their government accountable. When public schools teach only national triumphs and victimhood, they replace education with state propaganda, cultivating blind allegiance rather than critical thought. [P1.2] First, teaching past abuses is essential for civic vigilance. Atrocities do not happen in a vacuum—they are systematically enabled by institutions, laws, and public complicity. If students do not learn how their own country historically justified systemic disenfranchisement, forced assimilation, or unjust violence, they cannot recognize the warning signs of state overreach today. A citizenry ignorant of its government's capacity for immense harm is inherently vulnerable to repeating it.
[P1.3] Second, historical honesty is a prerequisite for genuine social cohesion. In many countries, the victims of historical abuses—such as Indigenous populations or historically oppressed minorities—are still navigating the systemic fallout of those events. When a national curriculum erases or glosses over these atrocities, it gaslights marginalized communities, perpetuating the original harm and deepening societal divides. Formally acknowledging these dark chapters in public schools validates the lived realities of these citizens.
[P1.4] It transforms marginalized grievances into a shared national context, laying the groundwork for true reconciliation.
[P1.5] Finally, hyper-nationalism fueled by sanitized history is dangerous. A national identity built exclusively on exceptionalism and victimhood breeds chauvinism and a perpetual sense of entitlement, making countries more aggressive and easily manipulated by demagogues. True patriotism does not require historical amnesia. A nation demonstrates ultimate strength and maturity not by hiding its failures, but by confronting them openly to ensure they are never repeated.
[P1.6] We cannot protect our future by hiding from our past. By requiring the study of major national abuses, governments equip the next generation with the moral clarity and critical skepticism needed to build a more just and resilient society.
