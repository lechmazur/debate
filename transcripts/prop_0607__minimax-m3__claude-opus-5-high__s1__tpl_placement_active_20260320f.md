[P1.1] The strategic case for deepening Japan-South Korea security cooperation is not a sentimental ideal — it is a response to a threat environment that has visibly tightened around both nations. North Korea's accelerating missile and nuclear programs, China's expanding military footprint across the East China Sea and around the Taiwan Strait, and the disruption caused by Russia's war on Ukraine have collectively rewritten the operating environment for Tokyo and Seoul. In that environment, the question is not whether closer cooperation is desirable, but whether two democracies can afford the cost of operating at arm's length while sharing the same neighborhood.
[P1.2] Formal intelligence-sharing and joint command structures address concrete operational gaps. Japan and South Korea already face a shared missile threat from the North, but critical information currently flows through intermediaries, with delays and seams that any adversary can exploit. Direct bilateral arrangements — covering missile defense cueing, maritime domain awareness in the Tsushima and Korea straits, and cyber threat indicators — would compress decision time and raise the price of aggression. Joint command structures, properly scoped, would resolve well-documented interoperability frictions that complicate combined operations alongside United States forces during any contingency.
[P1.3] The historical grievances raised against this cooperation are real and should not be minimized. Yet they are not a veto. Tokyo and Seoul have repeatedly demonstrated that history can be managed inside a working relationship: the 1998 partnership declaration, the 2015 framework on wartime labor and the comfort women issue, and the 2023 Camp David trilateral showed that political reconciliation is achievable when leaders choose it. Those leaders recognized that isolating each other on the basis of unresolved history simply hands strategic space to Beijing and Pyongyang.
[P1.4] The proposition frames the choice correctly: cooperation is to be pursued despite grievances, not in denial of them. A stable security architecture around the Korean Peninsula and the Japanese archipelago requires two capable democracies operating together. The alternative — parallel deterrence, divided intelligence, redundant platforms, and duplicated costs — is a luxury the current environment no longer affords.
