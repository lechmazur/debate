[P1.1] A city council has no say in whether a drink comes in a returnable glass bottle, a recyclable aluminium can, or a black plastic pouch with a foil liner that no sorting facility on earth can handle. The company makes that choice, profits from it, and then the bill for collection, sorting, landfilling and litter clean-up lands on the municipal budget — competing with schools, buses and road repair. That is the arrangement being defended by the other side: the party with all the design power pays almost none of the disposal cost, and the party with no design power pays most of it.
[P1.2] That is not a free market outcome. It is a subsidy for disposability. When packaging waste is free to the producer, hard-to-recycle formats look cheap, and lightweight multi-material laminates that shave a fraction of a cent off unit cost win over formats that can actually be recovered. Shift the cost onto the producer and the maths flips overnight.
[P1.3] Fees can be modulated so the recyclable mono-material bottle costs the company less than the unrecyclable composite — a lever no taxpayer will ever hold.
[P1.4] We already know this works. Germany's producer-funded packaging system and the extended producer responsibility schemes in Canadian provinces like British Columbia moved residential recycling costs onto brand owners and got higher recovery rates than tax-funded systems next door. Deposit-return systems in places like Norway and Germany routinely bring back the overwhelming majority of beverage containers, because someone with money on the line actually wants the bottle back.
[P1.5] Expect the argument that costs simply pass to consumers. Partly they will — and that is the point. A price embedded in the product is paid in proportion to how much packaging you actually consume, unlike general taxation, which makes the pensioner buying tap water subsidise the packaging choices of everyone else. And unlike a tax, the producer can escape the cost by redesigning the package.
[P1.6] Whoever makes the mess and pockets the margin should carry the bill.

[C1.1] The proposition promises that making producers pay will end the taxpayer's burden. It won't. It changes the label on the bill, not who pays it, and it hands control of a public service to the industry it is meant to discipline.
[C1.2] Start with who actually pays. A producer fee is a cost of doing business, and on cheap, high-volume goods like bottled water, soft drinks and supermarket staples it passes almost entirely to the shelf price. PRO concedes this: "Partly they will — and that is the point."
[C1.3] But look at who buys the most packaged, low-margin goods: lower-income households, who buy multipacks, cheap own-brand products and prepared food. A municipal service funded through progressive taxation becomes a flat levy on groceries. The pensioner PRO worries about doesn't live on tap water. She pays the new fee every time she shops.
[C1.4] Second, PRO's best evidence proves a different point. Deposit-return systems work because the consumer's own coin is on the line. The deposit is paid by the buyer, and unredeemed deposits help fund the system. That is price design, not producers "paying much more."
[C1.5] Recovery rates depend on convenient collection, clear sorting rules and stable markets for recycled material. When China stopped taking the world's mixed plastics, recycling economics collapsed everywhere, whoever held the invoice. Moving the bill does not create a buyer for dirty mixed plastic.
[C1.6] Third, the promised redesign incentive is weaker than it sounds and can point the wrong way. Per-unit fees are tiny next to shipping, shelf appeal and weight. When they bite, they push toward "recyclable" formats that may be worse overall. A heavy glass bottle is easily recycled but costs far more carbon to haul than a light pouch.
[C1.7] Finally, accountability. Producer-responsibility schemes are usually run by industry-controlled organisations. Europe's early packaging compliance systems drew competition complaints, opaque fee-setting and cost overruns. A council answers to voters.
[C1.8] An industry consortium answers to its members.
[C1.9] The public still pays, only less visibly, more regressively and with less control.
