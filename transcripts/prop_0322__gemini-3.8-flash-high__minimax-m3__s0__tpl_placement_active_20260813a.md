[P1.1] When a podcast host pitches a product, the sponsor is not just buying airtime; they are renting that creator's personal credibility. The commercial power of the "host-read" endorsement rests on perceived authenticity: listeners assume the person they trust actually evaluated the script and chose to speak the words. When that performance is secretly replaced by an AI voice clone or synthetic likeness, the endorsement becomes fundamentally deceptive.
[P1.2] Truth-in-advertising law already recognizes that consumers have a right to understand commercial context. Regulatory frameworks require creators to disclose sponsorships and free products precisely because commercial relationships affect consumer judgment. Synthetic replication is a direct distortion of that reality. When an ad sounds indistinguishable from a trusted creator, listeners reasonably infer genuine human participation.
[P1.3] Concealing that the performance is algorithmic allows advertisers to exploit parasocial trust without the creator ever stepping up to a microphone.
[P1.4] A legal disclosure mandate does not ban synthetic media or restrict technological innovation. Creators and agencies remain free to use voice clones to scale production, save studio time, or adapt campaigns into multiple languages. It simply requires baseline honesty: a brief audio disclaimer or on-screen label informing the audience that the likeness is AI-generated.
[P1.5] The compliance burden is virtually nonexistent, but the cost of inaction is severe. Without a legal requirement, the commercial incentive is to deceive, eroding consumer trust and enabling low-accountability advertising where synthetic hosts endorse products they have never seen. Commercial speech rules exist precisely to ensure that endorsements do not falsify the nature of their delivery. Mandating disclosure is a necessary, proportionate rule to protect listeners and preserve market integrity.

[C1.7] PRO's case treats the technology as the harm. It isn't. We should not build a new regulatory infrastructure on a speculative injury to solve a problem the FTC already handles.
