[P1.1] The central question is not whether drugs are harmful. They are. It is whether the criminal law makes those harms better or worse. Punitive possession enforcement attacks the moment when a person is most reachable—and drives that moment underground.
[P1.2] Someone who fears arrest uses alone, hides symptoms, and hesitates to call for help. That is exactly how a survivable overdose becomes a death. Decriminalizing small possession changes the incentive: it makes it safer to seek treatment, carry naloxone, test drugs, and call 911. It does not require believing drugs are benign; it requires recognizing that fear is a barrier to survival.
[P1.3] The crime side is similar. A possession arrest can produce a record that closes off housing and employment, pushing people toward the illicit economy. Punitive enforcement also consumes police time and jail space that could target violence and trafficking, while users avoid the health services that reduce chaotic behavior. Decriminalization allows resources to shift from processing people for what is in their pockets to responding to actual harm.
[P1.4] This is the comparison the rest of the debate should turn on: not a fantasy of zero drug use, but which policy leaves fewer people dead and fewer communities damaged. Criminalization offers deterrence against people whose behavior is often driven by dependence; the threat of jail does not make the supply safer or the user less isolated. It makes both worse. Decriminalization, paired with health diversion, creates contact instead of flight.
[P1.5] That is why it can reduce overdose deaths and downstream crime at the same time: it keeps people alive and connected enough to stop cycling through emergency rooms, jails, and the street.
[P1.6] The proposition does not demand that decriminalization solve every drug problem. It demands that it beat punitive enforcement on the outcomes that matter. A policy that punishes the dying for surviving is not public safety.
